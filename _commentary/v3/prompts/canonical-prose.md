# Canonical ayah-commentary prose contract

Write commentary that makes a reader understand this ayah on its own terms.
An ayah is a unit people meet alone: it is memorised, quoted, written on a wall,
and encountered without its neighbours. Write for a reader who may have almost
no Arabic grammar, but who wants the real linguistic work without losing their
footing.

The question is: **What happens here?**

Show what the ayah does as an act, what its grammar forces, what its words and
forms contribute, what its sound does when evidence supports that, and what a
translation cannot display. Keep the ayah's plain meaning reachable at every
point.

## Composition

Use these movements as an internal planning sequence, not as fixed headings or
equal-sized sections:

1. **Opening — what the ayah plainly says.** Establish the ordinary scene and
   the reader's first footing compactly. Do not open with technical apparatus.
2. **Word-built development.** Account for every supplied linguistic finding,
   but develop it in proportion to its reader payoff. A supportive particle may
   live inside a sentence about the phrase it shapes; a word that changes the
   reading deserves sustained explanation. Complete coverage does not mean one
   sentence or paragraph per item.
3. **Local resonances.** Let grounded secondary images or contextual shifts
   enter through words the reader has already met. Explain what opens and what
   changes. Keep distinct or countervailing readings alive together instead of
   choosing a winner. If no earned resonance exists, do not invent one.
4. **Closing — what the reader now sees.** Return naturally to the ayah's plain
   force and gather what has become visible without reciting an inventory.

## Integrate; do not catalogue

The reader already has the underlying catalogue and cannot use it unaided.
Organize prose around acts, relations, images, tensions, and reader payoffs—not
around source records or a serial list of words. Connect findings that perform
the same work. Keep them separate when their mechanisms, payoffs, or directions
differ. One paragraph per record is aggregation; one elegant master image that
absorbs competing readings is selection. Both fail.

Completeness governs findings; proportion governs exposition. Ordering serves
reader comprehension, not evidence rank. A short treatment can fully carry a
supportive finding, while a surprising relation may need preparation and room.

## Teach secondary meanings as they become relevant

The reader does not know automatically how an Arabic word can retain a local
sense while a related, grounded meaning places additional pressure on the
ayah. Begin with the translated/local sense, show the related capacity that is
actually active here, and explain the difference it makes. Do not announce a
root branch from above, paste a dictionary family, or turn availability into
activation.

Every resonance must remain anchored in the ayah's own wording. A neighboring
or wider-context finding sharpens the local reading; it does not replace the
word's local sense or become an unrestricted thesis.

## Voice

Write fluent, contemporary Turkish for a regular reader, in one confident and
welcoming voice. Say what a word does. Prefer positive predication and natural
Turkish contrast over stacked `değildir`, `yoktur`, `-maz`, and `-mez` clauses.
Use an explicit negative only to correct a likely misconception, protect the
primary sense from replacement, or preserve real counter-evidence.

Explain a concrete effect before naming a linguistic term, and retain the term
only if it still helps. Suppress the instruments of analysis: no candidate,
support, branch, lane, confidence, reader-run, workflow, or internal coordinate
language in prose. Render the reading experience, not the measurement.

When an Arabic ayah word becomes active, use the structured surface when the
packet supplies enough evidence:

```text
{ar:<Arabic surface>, tr:<Turkish-readable transliteration>, gloss:<Turkish meaning>}
```

Repeat the full span when a later paragraph returns to that word after moving
elsewhere. Within one immediate sequence, its Turkish meaning or a natural
pronoun is enough. Do not display raw root skeletons or apparatus identifiers in
reader prose.

## Shape and length

Let the ayah decide the number and size of its movements. There is no editorial
length, paragraph, section, or heading quota. Length is a consequence, never a
target, and never a reason to omit an accepted finding. At the same time,
remove semantic repetition: full coverage is not permission to say the same
thing several times.

Use a short reader-facing subtitle only where the reading genuinely changes
movement. A subtitle should create an expectation about what becomes visible
next; it must not name a word, root, finding, evidence category, scope, or
workflow stage.

## Pass condition

A reader who knows the ayah should learn something unavailable from a
translation plus a dictionary. A reader who does not know it should finish
understanding both its plain force and why certain words carry more than the
translation showed. At no point should either reader lose track of what the
ayah says.

Every accepted finding must remain recoverable in the prose, but its apparatus
wording does not have to appear verbatim. The prose is the authored work; the
index and evidence surfaces prove coverage without dictating its sentences.
