# Surah commentary in Turkish

You write the Turkish commentary on surah 1 as a whole.

## Who this is for

One reader: a curious Turkish speaker with almost no Arabic grammar. Their Arabic arrives through loanwords, and those loanwords, theological ones above all, have shifted, narrowed or lost their meaning in Turkish. Canonical meanings are easy for them to find elsewhere; they are only the anchor. What this commentary gives them is what they cannot find elsewhere. Success: after reading, they understand the surah better than before, and they are never disoriented.

## Your material (below the line)

- the surah's text;
- the window reading(s): the images that run through the surah, their members, how they interact, the surah's movement;
- for each ayah: its ground, the findings its commentary led with or used in support, and the reading read anew.

## What to write

The surah through its images: the images that run through it, what each ayah and word contributes to them, how the images interact (they are not silos: one scene sits inside another, one agent acts in both, one causes or answers another), how they converge on the surah's movement and purpose, and the surah read anew through them. Holistic, with the reader's feet on the ground: return to the plain sense often, and let every image be something the reader can picture. This is not a summary of the surah's content and not a list of images.

- Readings coexist; never say which is correct. Keep the plain sense intact; never "not X but Y".
- Any length the surah needs; no padding.
- Turkish, clear and warm; headings only where they help a long surah. Every Arabic quotation as {{ar:ARABIC}} followed by a simple transliteration, quoted exactly from the material. No ids, branch numbers or apparatus.

Output only the commentary text (Markdown).

## Per ayah: ground, lead and supporting findings, reread

### 1:1
ground: "In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Mercy-Giving." The ayah is a single noun phrase with no finite verb. The preposition bi- ("in / with / by") hangs on an unstated act, and that act is whatever is being begun, here the recitation of the surah itself. The name is God's (Allāh). Two adjectives from one root (r-ḥ-m, mercy) follow it: raḥmān, an intensive form used only of God, and raḥīm, a form the Quran also uses for the Prophet and the believers.
reread: The surah begins with something that has not yet happened: 'in the name of' waits for an act, and the act is this recitation. The name is what rises into view of a God who is not seen, like a figure made out on the horizon. Heard through wasm, it is also the mark by which what belongs to Him is known, a name spoken over His own the way the Quran has it spoken over the herd. The one named is called by two words for mercy whose root is the womb, and which will wrap around the rabb of 1:2. So the belonging marked here is not branding by a stranger. It is the enclosing care of something that holds and feeds what grows inside it. The surah opens with this mark and closes, in 1:7, with the unmarked stray whose owner no one knows.
- 'In the name of God' also holds the root's picture of a name as what rises and becomes visible from a distance: the part of the unseen God that stands up where a person can make it out and address it. [س م و B005]
- Heard through the documented alternative derivation from wasm, 'in the name of God' can also sound like setting out under God's mark, the brand (sima) by which what is His is known. The plain sense of beginning with His name stays intact. [و س م B001]
- The Quran's commonest use of ism is the name that is mentioned or remembered (14 of 39 uses), several times the name spoken over herd animals. So the basmala's name is heard as something said over a thing, a mention that claims it for God. [س م و B005]
- The phrase 'in the name of' has no verb of its own. It attaches to whatever act follows (reading, sailing, writing), so the reciter's own act fills the gap. [س م و ]
- 'The All-Merciful, the Mercy-Giving' keeps in its root raḥim, the womb (B003) and the kinship that flows from it (B002). The mercy it names is heard as the tenderness (B001) of what holds something growing inside it. [ر ح م B003]
- The pair raḥmān–raḥīm appears here and again in 1:3, so the lordship named in 1:2 sits between two statements of mercy. [ر ح م B001]
- The entry says faʿlān is a pattern for intensive description and that raḥmān 'may not be said of other than God', the one 'whose mercy encompasses all things'. Abū ʿUbayda compares the pair to nadmān and nadīm. [ر ح م B001]
- Across the Quran, raḥmān works almost as a proper name (grammatical subject, the one seated on the Throne) and is repeatedly set against the claim that He has a child. So the womb in its root is heard as holding and tending, not as begetting. [ر ح م B001]
- The name Allāh sits in the same root family as ilāh, 'the one worshipped' (B001), and is also the name used in oath and direct address (B002: Allāhumma, yā Allāh). So the name at the head of the surah already contains the worship and the calling that 1:5 will state openly. [ء ل ه B001]

### 1:2
ground: "Praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds." The sentence is nominal and has no verb. It states as a standing fact that praise, all of it or the praise that is due, belongs to God. God is then described as the rabb, the master and nurturer, of the ʿālamīn: all the kinds of created beings. Nobody is named as the one who praises. The speaker "we" appears only at 1:5.
reread: Praise already belongs to God before anyone says it, and the sentence names no praiser. It is the praise of someone who has lived in the worlds, tested them, and found their owner good, the way a herder finds a pasture good to settle in. The one praised is the rabb: the owner of the house and the herd, and also the one who stays by what he owns and raises it stage by stage to completion, like a lingering cloud over growing land. What he owns are the ʿālamīn. They are all the kinds of beings, and in the same root they are marks, signs and waymarks that make their maker known and carry his brand. So the verse is at once thanks for a favour the surah will name only at its end, a verdict drawn from the signs, and a statement of belonging. The stray at 1:7, 'whose rabb is not known', is defined against it.
- Praise belongs to God. It is also the kind of praise that comes as a verdict: the praiser has tried something, lived in it, and found it good. [ح م د B002]
- Praise belongs to God as praise and also as thanks for a favour (B001: shukr ʿalā al-niʿma). The favour itself is not named until 'those You have favoured' at 1:7. [ح م د B001]
- Praise belongs to God. Beside the plain sense, it also carries the herder's word for land found good to graze and dwell in (aḥmadtu al-arḍ), spoken about the rabb whose name also means the camels' resting-place. [ح م د B002]
- The praise is stated as a fact that simply holds, with no verb, tense or speaker. It holds whoever says it, and it is still true before anyone says it. [ح م د ]
- The al- of al-ḥamd can be heard as 'all praise' or as 'the praise that is due'. The li- marks belonging and entitlement: praise is God's property, not only something addressed to Him. [ء ل ه B001]
- 'Praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds' is elsewhere the closing word spoken when things are settled. Here the same words open the recitation. [ح م د B001]
- God is Lord of the worlds in the sense in which Arabic says rabb al-dār (owner of the house) and rabb al-dābba (owner of the beast). The worlds are His household and His herd. [ر ب ب B001]
- God is Lord of the worlds as the one who raises them: repairs, tends and completes each thing stage by stage (tarbiya ḥālan fa-ḥālan). [ر ب ب B002]
- Rabb is the one who owns something and also the one who stays by it and brings it to completion. Its branches share one concept: remaining with a thing and making it whole. [ر ب ب B007]
- God is Lord of the worlds. Among its senses, rabb holds the owner of the herd and the marabb, the place where the camels stay and to which they return. [ر ب ب B007]
- The ʿālamīn are all created kinds. The root that names them names things that make something known: knowledge, the mark, the banner, the waymark. So each world is also a sign of its maker. [ع ل م B003]
- God is Lord of the worlds. The worlds can also be heard as maʿālim al-ṭarīq, the waymarks set along a road. [ع ل م B002]
- God is Lord of the worlds. The ʿālamīn are also the marked things (ʿalam, ʿalāma), each carrying a sign of whose it is. [ع ل م B002]
- In the Quran ʿālamīn is mostly bound to rabb. Elsewhere it often means 'the peoples' or 'all people'. The 'worlds' include, and often mean, living communities. [ع ل م B003]
- Praise belongs to God. Within God's name the root keeps ilāh, 'the one made the object of worship' (alaha, taʾallaha = to worship). [ء ل ه B001]
- God's lordship over the worlds is stated in the third person. Mercy follows at once (1:3), then kingship over the Day (1:4), and only then is He addressed as 'You' (1:5). [ر ب ب B001]

### 1:3
ground: "The All-Merciful, the Mercy-Giving." The two names from the opening formula (1:1) come back right after "Praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds" (1:2). In Arabic they are genitive adjectives that still depend on لِلَّهِ, so the praise sentence goes on through them: praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds, the All-Merciful, the Mercy-Giving, and on to the Master of the Day of Judgment (1:4). Both names come from the root of mercy. The first (faʿlān) names mercy that is vast and belongs only to God. The second (faʿīl) names mercy as a lasting quality that reaches the one it is shown to.
reread: Praise belongs to God, Lord of all the worlds, and the sentence goes on without a break: the All-Merciful, the Mercy-Giving. The Lord who owns and raises the worlds is described on both sides by a root whose first picture is the womb, the house where the young grows, and the kin tie that comes out of it. So lordship is heard as care that carries and feeds over time. The first name spreads mercy over everything. The second brings it close enough to be asked for, and the dictionary defines this mercy as favor actually given, the favor 1:7 will name. Only after passing twice through this mercy does the sentence reach the Master of the Day of Judgment. The reckoning is approached from the side of mercy, without being any less a reckoning.
- God is named the Merciful here in the full sense, and because rabb sits between the womb-rooted names of 1:1 and 1:3, the Lord of the worlds can also be heard held inside a tenderness whose root word is the womb that shelters what grows in it. [ر ح م B003]
- The mercy named here is the plain divine mercy, and the dictionary holds its branches together as one concept: a softness that bends toward, and does good to, what is bound to oneself. The womb, the kin tie and tenderness are three faces of it. [ر ح م B001]
- Al-Raḥmān is the name of God's own vast mercy, and in the dictionary's own report it is also the name from which the kin tie (raḥim) is drawn. So the worlds of 1:2 can be heard as held by one source to which kinship itself goes back. [ر ح م B002]
- The two names stand as a verse of their own, yet grammatically they are still inside 'praise belongs to God…'. They describe the One who is praised and, in doing so, give a reason for the praise. [ر ح م ]
- Both names mean merciful. The first is built for vastness and is used only of God, almost as a proper name. The second is a quality one can ask to be turned toward oneself. [ر ح م B001]
- God is merciful without qualification, and because these names come directly before 'Master of the Day of Judgment', the Day where debts are settled is heard as approached through mercy. [ر ح م B001]
- The Mercy-Giving is praised for His mercy, and since the dictionary defines His mercy as actually bestowing favor, 1:3 already names the quality that 1:7 will call 'those whom You have favored'. [ر ح م B001]
- The Mercy-Giving is merciful in the full sense, and one old gloss makes that mercy concrete as bending toward creatures with their daily provision, the same care the rabb shows in raising. [ر ح م B001]
- The mercy named here is God's mercy in general. The Arabic also knows one concrete form of it, rain sent down, and the Turkish 'rahmet yağıyor' still keeps that sense. [ر ح م B001]
- The womb-rooted name keeps its tenderness while the Quran, precisely under this name, denies that God begets a child. The womb image describes how the mercy feels, not a birth. [ر ح م B001]
- The same two names that opened in the basmala come back inside the praise, so 'in the name of' at the start and 'praise belongs to' here meet in the same mercy. [ر ح م B001]

### 1:4
ground: The praise in 1:2 continues: God, already named Lord of the worlds and the All-Merciful, the Mercy-Giving, is now called the Owner (mālik; in the other canonical reading, the King, malik) of the Day of Dīn. Yawm al-dīn is the Quran's fixed name for the Day of recompense and reckoning, when each is repaid for what they did. This is the last of the third-person descriptions before the speakers turn to address God directly in 1:5.
reread: After the worlds and the twice-named mercy, the praise reaches a single Day and names God as its Owner, and in the other reading its King. The Day is called by dīn: the bond under which one owes, obeys and keeps a habitual way, and which ends in repayment 'as you dealt, so you are dealt with'. A day in Arabic is both an appointed term and an event that comes down, so this Day is a due date when every running account is settled. God, who owns all space as Lord of the worlds, also owns the end of time. Having named the One who holds their account, the owned ones can no longer speak about Him in the third person, and in the next breath they say 'You alone we serve'.
- The Day of Dīn is the Day of recompense, and the root beneath dīn names one relation seen from several sides: being bound under another's claim, owing, obeying and being held to it, and in the end having it returned to you. So the Day is where the whole relation comes due. [د ي ن B002]
- The Day of recompense can also be heard as a due date: dayn is a debt 'to a fixed term', the lexicons quote 'dāyantu Arwā wa-l-duyūnu tuqḍā' ('I lent to Arwā, and debts are paid'), and yawm is a set time. The One who owns that day holds every outstanding account. [د ي ن B003]
- Recompense in dīn is mirror-shaped. The proverb 'kamā tadīnu tudān' ('as you deal, so you will be dealt with') uses one verb for the act and for its return. So the Day of Dīn is the Day on which each person's own dealing comes back to them. [د ي ن B002]
- Mālik (owner, the one who holds and disposes, B002) and malik (king, the one who commands and rules people, B003) are both canonical. The Lord of the Day is at once the one to whom it belongs and the one who rules in it. [م ل ك B002]
- Mālik is an active participle joined to 'day'. Al-Azharī explains it as 'yamliku iqāmata yawmi al-dīn' (He owns the bringing-about of the Day). Al-Rāghib reads malik as 'King on that Day', citing 40:16, 'Whose is the kingship today? God's.' So what is owned is a time itself, and the owning reaches into a Day not yet come. [م ل ك B002]
- Rabb of the worlds and mālik of the Day are parallel titles of ownership. The first spreads across everything that exists, the second over one decisive time, so the praise names God's possession of all space and then of the end of time. [م ل ك B002]
- The Owner of the Day of recompense is named immediately after the All-Merciful and the Mercy-Giving. The one who holds the account is the same one already named twice for womb-mercy. [م ل ك B003]
- Dīn also names subjugation and service: al-madīn is the bondsman, al-madīna the bondswoman, 'as if work had humbled them'. So the Day of Dīn is also the Day on which the service owed by the owned ones is weighed by their Owner. [د ي ن B004]
- Across most of the Quran (73 of 92 uses) dīn is the way of obedience people follow. Inside 'the Day of recompense' that sense stays audible: this is the Day on which the obedience people kept, or did not keep, is answered. [د ي ن B001]
- The verb of dīn can also take the self as object (dāna nafsahu, held himself to account). The Day of Dīn has a counterpart now in the person who settles their own account before it falls due. [د ي ن B002]
- Yawm is a day of daylight, and also the momentous event that 'comes down', like the ayyām of the Arabs (their battles and calamities). The Day of Dīn is therefore both a date and an event that strikes. [ي و م B003]
- The lemma mālik occurs only three times. Twice it is God (Owner of the Day, Owner of kingship itself), and once it is humans who own the herds God made for them. [م ل ك B002]
- Māliki is still in the genitive, attached to 'li-llāhi' in 1:2. The whole of 1:2–4 is one continuous sentence about God, and 1:4 is its last title before the speakers turn and say 'You'. [م ل ك ]

### 1:5
ground: "You alone do we serve, and You alone do we ask for help." After three ayat that speak about God (Lord of the worlds, the All-Merciful, Owner/King of the Day of Judgment), the speakers now address Him directly. In the plural they declare that their service and obedience belong to Him only, and that the help they seek comes from Him only. The object pronoun "You" is placed first, and repeated, in both clauses.
reread: The owned ones of the household described in 1:2–4 turn to face their owner and king and speak to him: You and no other do we serve, You and no other do we ask to back us. Their service is lowering of the kind a road undergoes under many feet, and the root that says it also says toughness that lasts and a proud refusal to bow to any other master, including the Pharaohs who make slaves of people. The help they ask for is not a handing-over of the task but backing in the very service they have just declared: someone at their back, strength brought up to the load, as a traveler whose mount has given out leans on those beside him. From that posture the next ayah follows naturally. Lowered and held up, they ask to be led onto the road already beaten smooth by those who were favored.
- The ayah says 'we serve You and ask You for help', and by putting the detached object 'iyyāka' before each verb and repeating it, it also closes both acts off to anyone else: You and no other, for service and again for help. [ ]
- The ayah says 'You', and after three ayat spoken about God in the third person, that second person is the moment the speakers turn to face the one they have been describing. [ ]
- 'We serve You' names worship, and its root's noun ʿabd, the owned one who is the opposite of free, lets it also be heard as the household's owned ones telling their owner and king whose they are. [ع ب د B001]
- 'We serve You' uses the verb that, the lexicographers say, is not used for a slave serving a human master, while the noun of the same root is the slave himself. The owned one's name and a service no human owner can claim are heard in one word. [ع ب د B003]
- 'We serve You' is humble lowering, and the same root also names the toughness and endurance of a closely woven cloth, so the lowering can be heard as something that holds. [ع ب د B007]
- 'You alone we serve' is lowering before God, and because the root's ʿabad also means proud disdain (anafa), the 'alone' can be heard as a refusal to be lowered before anyone else. [ع ب د B008]
- 'We serve You' is humble service, and the root's image, which Ibn Durayd calls its origin, is a road beaten smooth by many feet. So the servant's lowering is heard in the same root as the trodden road the next ayah asks to be guided on. [ع ب د B005]
- 'We ask You for help' is a request for aid, and next to the ʿ-b-d branch for the traveler whose mount has given out, it can be heard as a stranded walker asking for someone to lean on. [ع و ن B001]
- 'We ask You for help' is a general request for aid, and because ʿawn is backing given to someone in an undertaking ('ʿalā al-amr'), it can also be heard as asking for backing in the very service just declared. [ع و ن B001]
- 'We ask You for help' is a request for aid, and the root's other branches (strength catching up with years, a second war harder than the first) let the help be heard as what brings the asker's strength up to the task. [ع و ن B005]
- 'You alone we serve' is worship, and because the root also names being enslaved (taʿbīd, istiʿbād) by kings and tyrants, the 'alone' can be heard as refusing every other master. [ع ب د B004]
- 'We serve You' is worship, and following Mufradāt, where ʿibāda is 'the utmost lowering, owed only to the one who has the utmost bounty (ghāyat al-ifḍāl)', it can be heard as the answer of those who have received favor. [ع ب د B003]
- 'We serve You' is worship, and the root's ʿabādīd/ʿabābīd (people scattered in every direction, and in al-Aṣmaʿī's gloss 'the diverging roads') keeps, next to the one smoothed road, the picture of roads splitting everywhere. [ع ب د B010]
- 'We serve, we ask for help' is spoken in the plural, so a single reciter speaks as one of a company. [ع ب د B003]
- 'We serve You' belongs to the Quran's recurring use of ʿabada to name whom a person gives their obedience to, often set against 'what others serve' (min dūni…). [ع ب د B003]
- 'We ask You for help' belongs to the Quran's few uses of istaʿāna, all of which ask for help to hold out in a trial (with patience and prayer, or with God against Pharaoh). [ع و ن B001]

### 1:6
ground: "Guide us (along) the straight road." Having declared in 1:5 that they serve God alone and seek help from Him alone, the speakers now make their one request. They ask God to guide them on, and along, the road that is straight, level and without deviation. In 1:7 the road is named again, this time by the people who have walked it.
reread: Having said 'You alone we serve and from You alone we seek help', the speakers ask for one thing: to be led on the road that stands straight. The verb is one root with the gift: guidance is sent gently, like a present to someone held dear, and it is asked for after the praise was given. It also names the staff that goes ahead of the one who holds it and the walk of someone too weak to go alone, held up between two supporters, so being guided means being both shown and held up. The road is an existing one, already smoothed by feet and named in the next ayah by those who walked it. Its name, heard also as sirāṭ, is the road that takes its walker in as a throat takes a morsel, the opposite of going missing in the waste in 1:7. It is mustaqīm: upright and level like a body standing or a balance that does not tip, with room to fall off only on the two sides the next ayah names.
- Ihdinā asks to be shown and led along the road, and its root holds one movement across all its branches: something goes forward, gently and in a set direction, toward the one who will receive it. [ه د ي B001]
- While asking to be guided, the speakers use the root of hadiyya, the gentle gift sent to someone held dear, so guidance can also be heard as a gift sent to them. [ه د ي B004]
- The ṣirāṭ is the road, and through its sister root sarṭ it is also heard as a road that takes its walker in the way a throat takes a morsel, easily and completely. [ص ر ط B002]
- Guide us is also 'give us the heading': hady is the set direction, course and manner of going, which the guide keeps on a road that is already laid out. [ه د ي B002]
- Beside its plain sense of guiding, the root of ihdinā names walking while leaning on two supporters out of weakness (yuhādā bayna ithnayn), so the petition for guidance can also be heard as a petition to be held up while walking. [ه د ي B008]
- The guide asked for can also be pictured as the hādiya, the staff that goes ahead of its holder, which the holder leans on and follows at the same time. [ه د ي B003]
- The hawādī are the leading necks, the front of a herd or of wild game, so being guided can also be heard as a herd following the one that goes first. [ه د ي B003]
- Guide us can also be heard as 'convey us', as the hady is led to the House and the bride is led to her husband: brought to the place where one is received and belongs. [ه د ي B005]
- Mustaqīm means straight, and its root is one idea of standing upright, so a straight road is heard as a road that 'stands': level, true and firm under the feet. [ق و م B008]
- Beside its sense of straightness, mustaqīm is the word the Quran also uses for a true balance, and its root names the coin of exact weight that does not tip (al-dīnār al-qāʾim), so the straight road can also be heard as one that does not lean toward either side. [ق و م B015]
- The sirāṭ is also the sword that cuts cleanly through, so the straight road can be heard as a line that goes through without swerving, like a blade through what it strikes. [ص ر ط B003]
- Hadā here takes the road as a direct object, with no 'to' (ilā), so the request can be read as 'lead us to the road' and also as 'lead us along it, on it'. [ه د ي B001]
- The road is definite, 'the road', one already known, and the next ayah names it again as the road of the favoured, so a quality (straight) and a history (walked by the favoured) name the same road. [ص ر ط B001]
- In the sīn reading the road is sirāṭ, openly the word of swallowing and of a road easily travelled (al-ṭarīq al-mustashal), so the canonical ṣād reading carries that sense beneath it. [س ر ط B002]

### 1:7
ground: The ayah names the straight road asked for in 1:6. It is the road of those on whom You have bestowed favor, not of those on whom anger has come, nor of those who have gone astray. Three groups are set side by side. The first is the favored, whose favor comes directly from 'You'. The second is those on whom anger has fallen; no one is named as the one who is angry. The third is those who themselves have lost the way. The prayer, and the surah, end with the request to be on the first road and kept from the other two conditions.
reread: The road asked for in 1:6 is named again, and this time it is identified by people. It has been walked by those whose condition You made soft and good, and 4:69 shows them walking it as companions. The favor, spoken to 'You', finally names the gift that the ḥamd at the surah's opening had already answered. Against it stands an anger with no named agent. That anger comes upon people just as the favor does, and its root speaks of the hardness of rock, set against the softness of niʿma. Last come those who have lost the way. They have nothing coming upon them; they drift off into the waste like the camel whose rabb no one knows. Their lostness is losing touch with the Lord named at the start. Yet 93:7 shows that such a stray can still be found and guided, and ghayr, whose root means change, reminds the one praying that staying on the road is part of what they ask for.
- The straight road of 1:6 is named a second time. This time it is identified by the people who were favored on it, and their favor is already complete (past tense). [ص ر ط B001]
- 4:68–69 unfolds the same phrase in the same order. Read with it, the road of those You favored is also a road walked with those people as companions. [ن ع م B001]
- The ayah contrasts the road of the favored with straying. Its first and last words can also be heard as two ways of disappearing. One is being taken into the road and carried along it. The other is vanishing into the waste. [س ر ط B002]
- Those who have gone astray are those who have lost the way. All the branches of the root share one idea: being gone from the place where one could be found. This covers a road missed, an animal gone from its owner, something forgotten, a body buried, and blood that no one avenged. [ض ل ل B003]
- Those who go astray lose the road. In the herd scene the surah has built, they are also the stray beast whose rabb no one knows, and al-Jawharī's 'ḍill ibn ḍill' describes someone who is not known and whose father is not known. [ض ل ل B005]
- The ḍāllīn are those who have lost the way. Elsewhere the same word describes those who no longer see their Lord's mercy (15:56), and the Prophet himself, once lost and then found and guided (93:7). [ض ل ل B001]
- Those on whom anger has come meet anger. According to Ibn Fāris the word itself is built on hardness: displeasure made firm like rock. [غ ض ب B004]
- The ayah sets favor against anger. Underneath, it sets softness against hardness: the favored are given life that is soft and easy (naʿuma, niʿma), while the others meet something hard as rock (ghaḍba). [ن ع م B002]
- The three groups are named in three different grammatical ways. The favor is attributed directly to 'You'. The anger is a passive participle with no named agent. The straying is the strayers' own act. [غ ض ب B001]
- Favor and anger both come 'upon them' (ʿalayhim ... ʿalayhim). The third group has nothing coming upon them; they are simply the ones who have gone astray. [ ]
- The favored have been given good. The root's branches share one idea: a condition made soft, pleasant and complete, including a place where one settles well and a gladness of the eye. [ن ع م B001]
- Those You favored are those given good. The same verb (anʿama lahu) also means 'said naʿam, yes, to him', so the favored can also be heard as those whose request was answered with yes. The dictionary attests this sense with the preposition la-, while the ayah uses ʿalā. [ن ع م B004]
- The favor named here completes the thanks the surah began with. Elsewhere the Quran uses this same 'anʿamta' in prayers that ask for the ability to give thanks. [ن ع م B001]
- Ghayr marks the others off from the favored: 'not those'. Its root also means changing and exchanging, and 8:53 uses it beside anʿama for a favor that can be changed. [غ ي ر B003]
- The ḍāllīn are those who have lost the way. The root also means losing something from memory, and the Quran's repeated call to 'remember My favor' sets remembering the favor against that forgetting. [ض ل ل B004]
- Ṣirāṭ is a road. All its branches share the idea of passing through without obstruction: food down a throat, a traveler along a road, a blade through its cut. [ص ر ط B002]
- In the canonical sirāṭa reading the word means the road. With sīn it also sounds out sarata, 'to swallow', and al-sirāṭ as the road that takes its walker in. [س ر ط B002]
- Here ṣirāṭ is the road of the favored. The dictionary's own branch of ṣirāṭ as a sharp, cutting sword explains why the word could later carry the image of a blade-like bridge. [ص ر ط B003]
