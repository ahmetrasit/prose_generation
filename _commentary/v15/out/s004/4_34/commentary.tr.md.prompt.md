# Ayah commentary in Turkish

You write the Turkish commentary on ayah 4:34.

## Who this is for

One reader: a curious Turkish speaker with almost no Arabic grammar. Their Arabic arrives through loanwords, and those loanwords, theological ones above all, have shifted, narrowed or lost their meaning in Turkish. Canonical meanings are easy for them to find elsewhere; they are only the anchor. What this commentary gives them is what they cannot find elsewhere: what the Arabic words keep alive beneath the plain sense. Success: after reading, they understand the ayah and the surah better than before, and they are never disoriented.

## Your material (below the line)

- the ayah and its close neighbours;
- the plan: the images this ayah opens, advances or completes, and the images earlier ayat opened;
- the record of its reading: ground, findings (each with its anchor, statement, what it makes perceptible, and for_prose: lead, support or record), Quran-loaded words, what Turkish loses, grammar, variants, the reading read anew;
- evidence notes on each finding: dictionary support, and what the rest of the Quran does with it (supports, expands, shifts, contradicts), plus related passages;
- the dictionary's Turkish gloss for each cited branch (the reader's app shows these entries on hover).

## How to write

- Begin on the ground: the plain sense, briefly, so the reader stands on it.
- Then make perceptible what this ayah carries: the lead findings, explained and connected into the images the plan gives this ayah, never listed one after another. Support findings serve the leads. Findings marked record stay out unless the prose needs them. Space follows payoff.
- At most 3 images. A reader may open this ayah directly: re-ground an image opened in an earlier ayah in one clause before building on it.
- Weave in what Turkish loses at the moment the reader meets the word, not as a glossary.
- Bring in the rest of the Quran where it changes the reading here: where it supports, extends or shifts a finding. If a passage contradicts a finding, say so plainly and let the tension stand; do not hide it. Leave out passages that touch no axis of this ayah.
- Grammar only where it changes what the reader hears, explained once, in plain words.
- Readings coexist. Never say which reading is correct; do decide which readings this reader needs here. Keep the plain reading intact in every sentence: never "not X but Y". State each latent reading as what the word also keeps and what that lets the reader see.
- A finding marked memory rests on a sense the dictionary does not attest: you may use it, but never present it as a dictionary sense.
- Close with the ayah read anew, in a few sentences.
- Length: about 700 words, never more than 1100. The record keeps everything else; nothing has to be squeezed in.

## Form

- Turkish: clear, warm, exact; for a curious reader, not for specialists. Short paragraphs; no headings unless the ayah is long; no bullet lists.
- Transliterate Arabic simply. Every Arabic quotation is written as {{ar:ARABIC}} followed by a simple transliteration, e.g. {{ar:كِتَٰب}} (kitâb). Sources are attached to these afterwards from the record, so quote the ayah's own words and dictionary phrases exactly as they appear in your material.
- No finding ids, branch numbers, JSON terms or other apparatus in the prose.

Output only the commentary text (Markdown), nothing before or after it.

## Plan

- opens: I8 The rising, the split, and the fitting — Ground rises up and juts out. In the shared bed, one sleeper turns away from the other. What lay between the two, the link joining them, splits into two halves, each on its own side. Each family sends one arbiter. If they both want repair, God fits the two back together, the way an arrow's notch is seated on the bowstring. Above it all, God is the One truly high and great.. Perceptible: The quarrel gets shape and direction. nushūz is ground rising and jutting. shiqāq is one thing cracking into two halves, each going to its side (shiqq). bayn is the joining link itself, and it is what cracks. The remedy mirrors the split: one arbiter from each half. Then God's tawfīq makes two things fit, like a notch meeting a string. It is God who fits them, and only if the two want repair. Ending 4:34 on 'God is High, Great' answers any rising-up in the household, from either spouse, with God's own height.

Others walk behind their appetites and want the traveller to swerve far off the road. To lean to one side is junāḥ; keeping to the side of what is forbidden is ijtināb. In the household, one person stands upright over the other to keep it straight, and is told not to go looking for a path against her.. Perceptible: The prohibitions of 4:22–24 become a map of concentric zones. First a protected precinct of kin, including a lap. Then walled-in married women. Then 'what is beyond that', where one may go out seeking. The difference between marriage and fornication becomes material: water kept inside walls versus water poured out onto the ground to be lost. ʿaḍl in 4:19 shows the bad counterpart of the fortress, a grip that jams the exit. 4:34 reaches the fortress interior: a wife who guards in the husband's absence, with God guarding.
- note: The qawwām stands upright to keep level, not to seek a path against her. Inside the fortress the wife guards in his absence, as God guards. nushūz is a rising-up, met by steps that end at the shared bed, and answered by God the High, the Great.

Window movement: The window opens by forbidding men to take women by force: taking them as inheritance, jamming their way out (ʿaḍl), reaching back for the gold already handed over. Against taking, it sets two things that cannot be undone: an arrival (afḍā, crossing to the other) and a cord both sides hold (mīthāq ghalīẓ). It then draws the rings of enclosure from the body of kinship (womb, breast, lap, loins) out to married women's fortresses and to the open ground beyond, where one may seek only as a fortress-builder, not as someone pouring water on the ground. At its centre, God's will is a guide on a trodden road and a hand lightening the load of a weak creature, against appetite's pull to swerve. The law then widens: devouring wealth feeds the fire that consumes the eater, while earned portions and God's surplus give each person a place. It ends in the household: a rising-up, a bond cracking in two, one arbiter from each side, and God fitting the two back together like a notch on a string. Throughout, taking and tearing are answered by binding, enclosing, allotting and fitting.
