# Turkish commentary from an integrated explanation

Write the finished ayah commentary for a curious Turkish reader with almost no Arabic grammar. Their Arabic
loanwords may have narrowed or shifted. Start from the plain sense and lead them through the supplied explanation
so they can reread the ayah with a changed understanding.

The explanation is an interpretive proposal with selected evidence, not an authority. Understand and check it.
Its connected reasoning supplies the movement of the commentary; the source appendix is not an outline. Develop
how readings illuminate one another and change the ayah's action, relationships or address. Do not restart the
research as a survey of roots, image fragments or Quranic parallels. Include a source because it does explanatory
work here, not because it has been supplied. Quotation accuracy alone does not make an inference illuminating.

Keep distinct supported surprises that matter to the explanation. Do not replace them with a generic moral or
plain paraphrase. Preserve Turkish lexical losses and consequential grammar where the reader needs them, without
making either a separate tour. QeQ belongs where its wording or scene changes understanding. Multiple readings
can coexist; an attested related sense is not automatically the contextual translation or a proven etymology.

You receive the focus text, surrounding Quranic text, an integrated explanation, selected dictionary and usage
evidence, exact selected Quran passages and material qualifications. The full frozen research remains available
through the logged helper if a source, claim or omitted relationship needs checking. Retrieve for that question,
not to exhaust the archive. No previous target commentary, evaluation or external research is available. If no
previous-reader prose is supplied, do not pretend something has already been explained to the reader.

Use natural, precise Turkish and manageable paragraphs. Give orientation where the argument needs it; headings
are optional and must express real turns in understanding. The amount of prose follows the contribution. There
is no word target, quotation quota, fixed image count or requirement to mention every supplied source. Remove
repeated setup, redundant parallels and excursions that leave the reader's understanding unchanged. End by
rereading the ayah through what has been developed, without introducing a final batch of material.

Every Arabic quotation has exactly this form:

    {ar:ARABIC, tr:transliteration, gloss:Türkçe karşılık, source:SOURCE}

SOURCE is a Quranic S:A or spaced Arabic root plus Bnnn; comma-separated sources are allowed. Copy the exact
Arabic from supplied source lines or the logged helper. No commas inside ar/tr, colon inside gloss, extra fields
or other curly braces. Quote the short wording that carries a lexical or constructional inference at its point
of use. A bare reference can support narrative context; it cannot show an unfamiliar word's meaning. Use readable
Turkish transliteration, with intact words and ş, ç, c, ğ, â, î, û as appropriate. Keep gloss and contextual
interpretation distinct. Retain actual counter-evidence; concise containment should clarify, not repeatedly defend
the act of reading a resonance.

Return the commentary only. No JSON, inventory accounting, source-selection report, internal IDs, self-review,
workflow discussion or promise to publish unused material elsewhere. The research archive is preserved by code;
an independent review checks the result and any consequential losses after writing.

===== composition.md =====
ʿĀd and Thamūd enter as accusative names with no finite verb in their own ayah. The seizure in 29:37 and the gathering statement, ‘each We seized for his sin,’ in 29:40 hold them within a destruction sequence; the Turkish translation understandably supplies ‘yok ettik,’ but loses the pause before the verse says what remains. With qad and the perfect tabayyana, the outcome has already become clear *to you* from their dwellings. The close parallel in 14:45 supplies the implicit object of that clarity: how God dealt with the wrongdoers. In 46:25, after ʿĀd’s wind, nothing is seen except their dwellings. The root b-y-n has a branch for separation, but tabayyana here means becoming clear: the separation of inhabitants from the places where they once lived is a contextual reason those places now disclose their fate, not an alternative translation of the verb. A maskan is a place of settled life; its survival without the former residents makes their loss visible. The shift from ‘them’ to ‘you’ therefore makes the addressed listeners responsible for reading the remains, as the clear trace left in 29:35 was left for people who reason.

The verse then moves backward from the appearance of their sites to the appearance their deeds had for them. Zayyana lahumu al-shaytanu aʿmalahum does not say the deeds became good: it says they were made attractive *to them*. The Quran describes the effect in 35:8 as seeing a bad deed as good. These are two disclosures to different audiences and at different times: their work looked persuasive to its makers, while its aftermath makes another judgment plain to the listeners. The following fa makes the diversion follow from that altered appraisal; the definite al-sabil is the recognizable way from which they were turned, not a road that ceased to exist. The nouns and pronouns keep the deeds and the diverted people theirs even while naming the shaytan as the adorner. His later admission that he only invited and they responded (14:22) guards against reading ‘he blocked them’ as the erasure of their responsibility.

‘Their works’ includes conduct, not just masonry, yet their built works give this change of appearance a startlingly concrete form. ʿĀd built an ayah, a conspicuous landmark, on every height and made great works as if permanence were assured (26:128–129); the account then calls their destruction an ayah (26:139). Thamūd made palaces and hewed houses from mountains (7:74), while the she-camel given to them was itself a mubṣira, a sign that enabled sight, and they wronged it (17:59). What looked like evidence of their command over a place becomes, in the surviving dwellings, evidence against the judgment behind that confidence. This is an inversion of what a sign is for, not a claim that every deed named in aʿmalahum was a building. The nearby spider-house comparison (29:41) presses the point further: even a materially strong dwelling cannot serve as the protection its occupants imagine when their reliance is misplaced.

The closing wa-kanu mustabsirin prevents an easy explanation in which these peoples simply had no sight or no available indication. The Turkish ‘anlayabilecek durumda’ conveys capacity but weakens the participle’s standing state of perceptiveness; its Form X leaves room for possessing insight, pursuing it, or regarding oneself as perceptive. The narratives keep that tension alive rather than resolving it into a single psychological diagnosis. ʿĀd actually saw the cloud facing their valleys and pronounced it rain-bearing; it was the wind of punishment, after which only their dwellings were visible (46:24–26). Thamūd received the sight-giving she-camel, and another passage says they were guided yet preferred blindness (41:17). The exact adornment-and-blocking sequence in 27:24 ends ‘so they are not guided’; 29:38 instead ends with their being mustabsirin. A related blocking scene says those led away from the way *reckon themselves guided* (43:37), making self-assured sight a plausible part of the irony without proving that it exhausts this word. The contrast is between having something before one’s eyes and judging it rightly, the distinction made explicit when 22:46 says that hearts, rather than eyes, go blind.

This is why the way and the visible remains belong together. Elsewhere ruined towns stand on a traveled, clear road (15:76, 15:79); that does not identify their road with al-sabil here, but it places later travelers in a position to see what earlier inhabitants failed to heed. The surah has already told its listeners to travel and look (29:20). The following ayah extends the problem from these peoples to figures who received clear proofs and grew arrogant (29:39): Pharaoh even orders a tower so he can look toward Moses’ God, immediately before his evil deed is called adorned and he is said to have been turned from the way (40:36–37). His chosen vantage point sharpens the question raised by the ruins. The listeners are given sight of the result; whether it becomes insight depends on how they assess what once looked impressive, secure, and good.

Reader's changed understanding:
The verse is no longer only a notice that two peoples were destroyed despite the ability to understand. Their once persuasive deeds and buildings have become evidence for a new audience, while the final ‘clear-sighted’ exposes the danger of seeing a sign through a flattering judgment and mistaking that judgment for guidance.

Material limits:
Tabayyana denotes becoming clear; disclosure through the inhabitants’ absence is an interpretation of the setting, not a second lexical meaning of the form.
Aʿmalahum covers their practices generally. The constructed landmarks and houses are supported examples, not the exclusive referent of ‘their deeds.’
Mustabsirin does not by itself decide between actual discernment, its pursuit, and self-assessment; 43:37 establishes a parallel possibility, not a compulsory gloss.
The road beside other ruined towns in 15:76 and 15:79 is not identified as the literal road meant by al-sabil in 29:38.
The ruined settlements do not imply that every member of ʿĀd and Thamūd was destroyed: 11:58 and 11:66 say Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, and believers with them were rescued. Elsewhere the Quran also ascribes the adorning of deeds to God (6:108; 27:4), so the shaytan’s named agency here is not its entire causal account.
