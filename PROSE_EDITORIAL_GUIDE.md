# Prose editorial guide

## Purpose and use

Use this guide when planning, writing, revising, or reviewing prose. It is designed to be usable by a fresh Sol or Luna session with no previous conversation. It also applies to other writers. Read it completely before undertaking a substantial piece; apply the principles in proportion to the assignment.

The target is fluent prose with substantial content: the reader understands difficult ideas without doing unnecessary work, encounters surprising details at the moment they become useful, and can follow how the evidence supports the interpretation. The editorial benchmark is the combination of fluency, depth, and integration admired in an Opus reading. Everything needed to understand that benchmark is defined here. No example output or knowledge of a model's personality is required.

This is an editorial standard, not a claim that a prompt can guarantee equal performance across models. Better organization cannot supply missing evidence or knowledge. Fluency must not conceal an unsupported conclusion.

The assignment determines the audience, language, genre, length, voice, source boundaries, citation format, and deliverable. Follow those requirements and the applicable instruction hierarchy. Use this guide for editorial choices within them. Do not override a required technical format, silently discard required content, or add research, extra agents, or separate model calls merely because this guide describes planning and review.

The guide's lists are working aids. The finished prose should use the form its reader needs. Do not reproduce the guide's terminology, checks, or planning fields in the deliverable unless requested.

## 1. The standard in observable terms

A successful piece has these properties:

- **A clear purpose.** The reader can tell what the piece helps them understand, decide, do, or experience.
- **Progression.** Each substantial passage changes what the reader knows, sees, expects, or can do. Later passages use earlier discoveries.
- **Depth.** Important claims have enough explanation, evidence, distinctions, and consequences to be understood and assessed.
- **Proportion.** Central relationships receive development. Smaller details remain available without taking over the piece.
- **Useful surprise.** An unexpected connection has both support and a reason to appear here. The reader can explain why it matters.
- **Continuity.** References are clear, terms remain stable, and transitions follow relationships in the subject.
- **Natural language.** The prose sounds written for a person in the target language. Its rhythm follows the thought.
- **An earned ending.** The close leaves the reader with the result, implication, decision, changed perspective, or narrative consequence the piece has established.

Readability and depth should support each other. Organizing several details around a meaningful relationship can make a passage both richer and easier to read. Removing the relationship to make the passage shorter can make it harder to understand.

## 2. Establish the assignment before drafting

Identify the following from the request and supplied material:

1. **Reader:** What do they already know? Which terms or assumptions need explanation?
2. **Purpose:** What should be different for them after reading?
3. **Genre:** Explanation, interpretation, analysis, narrative, instructions, proposal, reference, or a mixture?
4. **Scope:** Which questions belong here? What is the requested level of detail?
5. **Evidence:** What sources are available, authoritative, required, uncertain, or outside the permitted scope?
6. **Delivery:** What language, length, structure, quotations, tags, and citations are required?

Use reasonable assumptions for ordinary editorial choices. Ask for clarification only when a missing fact would materially change the result and cannot be resolved from context. Avoid making the reader complete an editorial questionnaire before useful work begins.

For a short answer, these decisions may be brief and implicit. For a substantial work, prepare a compact outline or editorial note. Do not inflate planning to satisfy a ritual.

## 3. Read for relationships before choosing a structure

Read the material needed to understand the subject, including the surrounding context of passages you intend to use. An index, extracted phrase, evidence ID, or summary is a pointer to content; it may omit the feature that makes that content important.

As you read, distinguish:

- What the material explicitly establishes.
- What becomes apparent through comparison, sequence, contrast, repetition, cause, or consequence.
- What remains ambiguous, contested, incomplete, or unsupported.
- What the reader is likely to find difficult or surprising.

In an interpretive piece, look for real questions: an unexplained change of address, a contrast between knowledge and conduct, an unexpected word choice, or a pattern that changes across contexts. In a technical explanation, look for the mechanism, boundary conditions, and failure cases. In a narrative, look for changes in desire, situation, knowledge, or relationships.

Do not manufacture a paradox or impose a quota of tensions. Some subjects require a clear sequence or a careful distinction rather than a central conflict. Let the material determine the kind of understanding the piece offers.

If evidence is missing, identify the gap accurately. Research only when the assignment and available tools permit it. Otherwise narrow the claim, mark the limitation where it matters, or request the essential source. Do not invent a supporting passage to complete an attractive argument.

## 4. Choose the organizing question, claim, or movement

For explanatory and analytical prose, state the question the piece answers and the main answer in plain language. For an interpretation, identify what the proposed reading reveals and what supports it. For a narrative, identify the movement the reader will follow; it need not be declared as a thesis. For instructions, identify the outcome and the prerequisites.

This decision should organize the material without forcing every detail to prove the same point. A complex subject may need several connected claims or an unresolved question. Preserve tensions the evidence does not resolve.

Choose sections by their contribution to the reader's understanding. Source files, evidence categories, keywords, and discovery order do not automatically deserve their own sections. Several source groups may support one section. One source may support different sections when each uses a genuinely different feature.

For each substantial section, know:

- What it establishes or changes.
- Which specific evidence or scene makes that possible.
- What the reader needs to understand first.
- What it adds beyond the preceding sections.
- Where it leads next, if the piece continues.

Choose the number and length of sections to suit the subject and assignment. Do not force every piece into a fixed number of headings. Prefer titles that orient the reader to the subject or discovery. An image belongs in a title when it helps name that discovery.

## 5. Preserve substance while controlling prominence

Treat these as separate decisions: whether a detail belongs, where it belongs, and how much development it needs.

Give a detail sustained development when it carries an important inference, explains a mechanism, resolves a real difficulty, complicates the main claim, or is itself central to the assignment. Give it a sentence or clause when the relationship is clear without more explanation. Group details when their combination reveals something that separate treatment would obscure.

Use notes or an appendix for relevant material that would interrupt the main progression, when the requested format allows them. Optional material may be omitted when it duplicates another point or has no defensible contribution. Explicitly required material must have a place; if the requested length makes that impossible, expose the conflict instead of silently dropping it.

When revising, preserve:

- Distinct substantive findings and relevant surprising readings.
- Evidence needed to establish them.
- Qualifications that change their meaning or scope.
- Differences between plausible interpretations.
- The connections that explain why they matter.

Reduce repeated definitions, duplicated conclusions, scenic restarts, and quotations that do the same work. Never equate compression with deleting the explanation between evidence and conclusion.

For a source-heavy assignment, a brief coverage note can track important findings and their destinations. Keep it separate from the prose unless requested. An inventory helps prevent accidental loss; it should not decide paragraph count.

## 6. Develop a useful surprise

An unusual detail earns its place through support and consequence. Before using it, be able to answer:

1. What exactly is the surprising observation?
2. What source establishes it?
3. What feature of the present subject makes the connection relevant?
4. What understanding changes when the connection is made?

These are editorial checks, not a required four-sentence pattern. Explain the relationship naturally, in the space it needs. Several observations may share one explanation.

For example, a dictionary's rare sense establishes that a usage exists. It does not by itself establish that a particular passage activates that sense. Identify the local word, scene, construction, or wider usage that makes the association meaningful. A sound resemblance may support literary wordplay without establishing common origin. A shared grammatical form may invite comparison without giving different words identical meanings.

In other fields, make the corresponding distinctions: a correlation needs further support for a causal explanation; two similar events do not automatically share a mechanism; a vivid anecdote does not establish prevalence.

Do not suppress a well-supported discovery because it is unusual. Do not require every section to contain a surprise. Novelty is useful when it increases understanding.

## 7. Make paragraphs perform coherent work

An analytical paragraph usually performs one coherent task: establishes a relationship, explains a mechanism, compares alternatives, develops a consequence, or resolves a difficulty. It may need several pieces of evidence and more than one related idea to do that work.

The useful question is: **What does the reader understand after this paragraph that they did not understand before it?**

When inference is involved, make the connection between observation and conclusion available to the reader. Do not expect a quotation, a shared keyword, or the word “therefore” to supply that connection.

Paragraph length follows the task. Combine adjacent planned steps when they form one natural movement. Divide a step when the reader needs time to understand a substantial distinction. There is no paragraph allowance per source, finding, quotation, or evidence ID.

Vary the mode of development. One paragraph may compare two phrases; another may follow a scene; another may explain a grammatical consequence; another may gather several observations. Avoid repeating the same opening, citation placement, and final sentence across the piece.

A paragraph can end on a result, question, tension, action, image, or necessary qualification. Choose the ending that completes or advances its work. Do not require a miniature summary or an ornamental final image.

For narrative and descriptive prose, a paragraph's contribution may be experiential: revealing a character, changing the pace, making a place tangible, or redirecting attention. It need not state an argument.

## 8. Build continuity at every scale

Connect sentences through their actual relationship. A new sentence can take up a person, term, finding, action, or unresolved question introduced by the previous one. Put necessary orientation before the information that depends on it.

Use transitions accurately. “Therefore” announces a supported consequence; “however” introduces a real qualification or contrast; “for example” supplies an instance. These words cannot repair a missing relationship. Sometimes repeating a precise noun is clearer than adding a transition word.

At a section boundary, carry forward what has been established and introduce the next question it makes possible. Avoid repeatedly returning to the original premise as though the reader has learned nothing.

Keep terms stable when the distinction matters. Do not rotate through near-synonyms merely for variety if they imply different mechanisms or categories. Make pronoun references explicit when several possible referents are nearby.

Manage the reader's load. Introduce unfamiliar people, symbols, foreign terms, or concepts with enough explanation to understand their present role. If a paragraph requires several new concepts, order them by dependency and show their relationship. Do not hide complexity behind vague nouns such as “this,” “the dynamic,” or “the process.”

## 9. Use evidence and quotations deliberately

For each quotation or reference, identify the particular feature that contributes: wording, sequence, omission, speaker, contrast, outcome, numerical result, or another concrete detail. Read enough context to avoid changing its meaning.

Quote the amount needed for that contribution. Paraphrase accurately when exact wording does no additional work and the assignment permits paraphrase. Keep prescribed source text, citations, transliteration, and machine-readable tags intact.

Several references may belong together when they perform distinct roles. A representative example may suffice when others would only repeat it. A list of citations does not demonstrate the relationship among them.

Repeat a key quotation, word, or phrase when a new passage examines a different aspect of it. There is no universal repetition cap. Avoid reintroducing the same definition or explanation unless the genre requires a reminder. For a non-specialist reader, translate or explain the part of a foreign-language quotation that does the work.

Separate what the source says from your interpretation. Put factual qualifications near the claims they qualify. Do not let a final disclaimer carry limits the main prose repeatedly ignores.

## 10. State claims with precise confidence

Match the wording to the kind and strength of support:

| Kind of claim | Appropriate wording | What to preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Directly observed or stated | “The report records…”; “The sentence addresses…” | Exact scope, context, and attribution. |
| Attested usage or association | “The dictionary also records…” | Attestation is distinct from the meaning in this occurrence. |
| Supported inference | “Together, these details suggest…”; “This sequence explains…” | The relationship that supports the inference. |
| Plausible alternative | “On this reading…”; “A second interpretation treats…” | Its evidence and how it changes the result. |
| Analogy | “An analogy helps here…” | What is being compared and where the comparison ceases to help. |
| Unresolved question | “The supplied evidence does not establish…” | The actual missing information. |

Use ordinary expressions of uncertainty when they are accurate. Avoid stacked, nonspecific hedges that weaken a sentence without identifying the uncertainty. An absolute ban on words such as “may” or “perhaps” can force false certainty.

When alternatives matter, explain what each accounts for and why one is preferred or why both remain open. Do not list possibilities merely to sound comprehensive. Do not collapse an unresolved distinction to produce a tidier ending.

## 11. Write natural, precise sentences

Use familiar language where it conveys the meaning accurately. Introduce technical terms when they give the reader useful precision, and explain them at the point of need.

Prefer identifiable agents, actions, and objects. Use active voice when agency matters and is known. Passive voice is appropriate when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately withheld by the source.

Let a sentence carry one manageable movement of thought. Related clauses may belong together when their relationship is the point. Breaking every sentence into short statements can destroy rhythm and make the reader reconstruct the connection.

Vary sentence length according to function. A longer sentence can hold a comparison or unfolding qualification; a short sentence can establish a result. Do not alternate lengths mechanically or make every paragraph end with a slogan.

Replace inflated abstractions with the actual relation. If you write “a transformation in the configuration of understanding,” ask whose understanding changed, about what, and why. Preserve necessary abstract terms; the goal is clarity, not a prohibition on abstraction.

Avoid canned openings, generic praise, announcing that something is “important” without showing why, repeated rhetorical questions, and decorative contrasts. Contrast is useful when two real alternatives need distinguishing. Do not invent a weak alternative just to make your preferred claim sound decisive.

Aim for a thoughtful, assured voice. Avoid speaking as though the reader is being tested, scolded, or impressed by the writer's vocabulary. Match the formality and emotional register to the subject.

## 12. Let images clarify the subject

Use an image when it makes a relationship, experience, or mechanism easier to perceive. It may come from the source, a relevant scene, or an analogy introduced as such.

Keep the image internally coherent. If the prose introduces a vessel, a road, a mirror, and a threshold in quick succession, the reader must repeatedly reconstruct the scene. Retain only the images that contribute distinct understanding, and give each a clear role.

Do not invent historical atmosphere or source details as though they were documented. A wind at a ruined doorway may be an illustrative scene; it is not automatically evidence about the event under discussion.

Return to an image when it develops. A building first presented as protection may later become evidence of failed protection. That return changes its significance. Repeating a door, a footstep, or a ray of light at every paragraph ending usually adds no such development.

For literary narrative, atmosphere and sensory detail can be substantive: they may establish character, mood, pacing, or viewpoint. Judge them by their function in that genre. An analytical essay should not acquire an unrelated fictional scene merely to become vivid.

## 13. Open and close at the right scale

An opening gives the reader enough orientation to enter the piece. It may state the answer, identify a genuine question, establish a scene, or introduce a problem. Choose the entry appropriate to the genre. Avoid a long generic preamble and avoid announcing every section before anything has happened.

The ending follows from what the piece has established. In analysis, it may show the consequence of the combined findings. In interpretation, it may change how the reader returns to the source. In a recommendation, it may make the decision and its conditions clear. In narrative, it may complete a movement while leaving appropriate uncertainty.

Do not force a moral instruction, a personal challenge, or a lyrical image onto every ending. “The reader's position” can mean a new understanding or a decision they can now make; it need not mean an obligation.

A recap is useful when the genre or length requires one. Make it selective and consequential. Avoid closing with a list of headings in sentence form, a new major unsupported claim, or a grand statement that could end almost any piece.

## 14. Adapt to genre and language

| Genre | Give priority to | Avoid imposing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interpretation or commentary | Textual detail, contextual relationships, defensible readings, meaningful alternatives. | A paragraph for every word or an obligation to make every lexical association a claim. |
| Explanation or teaching | Prerequisites, mechanism, well-chosen examples, limits of the explanation. | Surprise or ornament where the reader needs a direct account. |
| Research synthesis or analysis | Agreement, disagreement, evidence quality, scope, implications. | A source-by-source summary that leaves synthesis to the reader. |
| Technical or procedural prose | Correct sequence, explicit conditions, observable outcomes, usable examples. | Essay transitions or imagery that slows execution. |
| Proposal or recommendation | Decision, reasons, alternatives, tradeoffs, actionable conditions. | Manufactured certainty or a narrative that hides the recommendation. |
| Narrative, memoir, or literary prose | Viewpoint, causality, scene, rhythm, meaningful detail, earned emotion. | Explicit claims and evidence explanations in every paragraph. |
| Reference or comprehensive catalogue | Consistency, findability, complete requested coverage. | A single essay argument that makes facts harder to locate. |
| Short correspondence | Purpose, relevant context, clear request or response, suitable tone. | Long planning scaffolds or comprehensive background. |

Write idiomatically in the requested language. Preserve the concept rather than translating the syntax of an English outline word for word. Keep the technical vocabulary stable across languages.

For Turkish in particular:

- Prefer natural verb placement and clear relationships between clauses.
- Untangle long chains of possessives and nominalizations when they obscure who does what. Do not remove every noun ending in `-ma`, `-ış`, or `-lık`; many are natural or necessary.
- Make the referent of “bu,” “o,” and “burada” clear.
- Avoid repeating “böylece,” “bu yüzden,” “bir başka,” or “yalnızca … değil” as default paragraph connectors.
- Explain Arabic or other foreign-language material at the point where it contributes, preserving any required quotation and transliteration format.
- Keep precision and a natural speaking rhythm together. Simplicity should not turn a substantial interpretation into a sequence of elementary statements.

## 15. Worked editorial examples

The examples below are invented teaching material. They are not historical claims, quotations from sources, or reusable evidence. Transfer the editorial principle to the assignment; do not copy their imagery or wording as a template.

### A. Group evidence into an explanation

**Before**

> The town's wall could withstand an attack. The town imported its grain along a single road. That road often flooded. These facts show that the town was vulnerable.

**After**

> The wall protected the town from attack, but its food still arrived along a road that often flooded. Security therefore depended on access as well as fortification: the town could withstand an enemy at its gates and still run short of grain.

**What changed:** Each detail now has a role in one explanation. The distinction between protection and supply produces the insight. The revision preserves the relevant details and supplies their relationship.

### B. Replace decorative movement with meaningful development

**Before**

> The polished cup shines in the market. Its crack becomes a road, and the road falls across the traveller's eye.

**After**

> The polish draws attention to the cup's surface while leaving its crack unrepaired. As an analogy, it clarifies how an attractive appearance can change a judgment without changing the condition being judged.

**What changed:** One image remains coherent, is explicitly an analogy, and explains a distinction. If the surrounding passage already explains that distinction clearly, the analogy may be unnecessary.

### C. Make a quotation contribute a specific relationship

**Before**

> “I will keep the bridge open,” the governor said. He left town before the crossing was secured. This also concerns trust.

**After**

> The governor promised to keep the bridge open, then left before the crossing was secured. The promise matters because it could encourage people to wait for protection that his departure made less likely.

**What changed:** The revision identifies the relationship between promise, possible reliance, and withdrawal. “Could” preserves the fact that the supplied scene does not establish how people actually responded.

### D. Preserve uncertainty without vague hesitation

**Before**

> Perhaps the repeated word may in some sense suggest a change of attitude.

**After**

> The repeated word links the two speeches. Whether it also marks a change of attitude depends on how we read the second speaker's reply.

**What changed:** The established observation is stated directly. The uncertainty is located in a particular interpretive decision.

### E. Improve Turkish by supplying the missing relationship

**Before**

> Bilginin davranış üzerindeki etkisinin gerçekleşmemesi, kavrayışın yönlendiricilik niteliğinin eksikliğini görünür kılar.

**After**

> İnsan neyin doğru olduğunu bilebilir; yine de seçimini bu bilgiye göre yapmayabilir. Buradaki sorun, bildiğini davranışına taşıyamamasıdır.

**What changed:** The revision gives the abstract claim a recognizable subject and action. The contrast becomes available on the first reading. A specialist text may retain more technical wording if it makes a needed distinction.

### F. Make the ending deliver the argument's result

**Before**

> We have considered the walls, the grain, the road, and the flood. All these show the importance of security.

**After**

> Raising the wall would leave the town's dependence on a single road intact. If the aim is to withstand the next flood, stored grain and a second route address the failure the wall cannot prevent.

**What changed:** The ending uses the developed distinction to support a concrete decision. It does not merely name the topics again.

### G. Sustain a discovery across paragraphs without losing its details

**Supplied facts for this invented example:** Three electric clocks in one building stopped at 03:12. All three depended on the same electrical supply. A maintenance record places a power failure at 03:12. A witness places a loud crash at approximately 03:20. The cause of the crash is unknown.

**Developed passage**

> Binadaki üç saatin de 03.12'de durmuş olması, ilk bakışta birbirini doğrulayan üç ayrı kayıt gibi görünür. Oysa saatlerin üçü de aynı elektrik hattına bağlıdır. Bakım kaydı da elektriğin 03.12'de kesildiğini söyler. Saatlerin ortak gösterdiği şey, binada olup biten her olayın zamanı değil, onları birlikte durduran kesintinin zamanıdır.
>
> Bu ayrım, büyük bir gürültüyü yaklaşık 03.20'de duyduğunu söyleyen tanığın anlatısını nasıl değerlendireceğimizi değiştirir. Saatler onunla zorunlu olarak çelişmez: elektrik kesintisini ve gürültüyü iki ayrı olay olarak düşünürsek kayıtlar birbiriyle bağdaşır. Yine de tanığın verdiği saat yaklaşıktır; bu bilgilerden tam sekiz dakikalık bir aralık çıkaramayız.
>
> Böylece üç saatin aynı zamanı göstermesi anlamını korur, fakat kanıtladığı şey daralır. Kesintinin zamanı için birbirini tamamlayan kayıtlarımız vardır. Gürültünün nedenini bulmak içinse yeni kanıt gerekir.

**What changed:** The first paragraph groups three details into a discovery about shared dependence. The second uses that discovery to reassess apparent disagreement while preserving the approximate time. The third states what the combined evidence establishes and what remains unknown. Every supplied detail has a role; the paragraphs develop different tasks and do not repeat one citation-and-payoff template. The analogy to separate records is introduced briefly and gives way to the actual mechanism.

## 16. A practical workflow for a fresh session

### When planning and writing in one session

1. Establish the assignment and read the relevant evidence.
2. Identify the central question, claim, or movement and the strongest supporting relationships.
3. Make a compact outline that records what each part contributes and the source details it needs.
4. Draft in a natural sequence, adjusting the outline when the material warrants it.
5. Revise structure and substantive gaps before polishing sentences.
6. Review the final piece against the assignment and the checks below.

These stages need not become separate files, outputs, or model calls. For a short piece, they may take very little space.

### When a planner hands work to a separate writer

The handoff should preserve editorial decisions and supporting information. For each section, provide a concise record of:

- Its claim or narrative contribution.
- The ordered developments it needs, without prescribing a fixed paragraph pattern.
- The evidence IDs or locations and the exact feature each contributes.
- Important alternatives, limits, or unresolved source gaps.
- What it adds beyond earlier sections.
- Required minor details and their intended placement.
- An optional image, only if it helps.

The writer also needs access to the relevant source material, not only IDs. A full scene may reveal a relationship an extracted phrase misses. Preserve that context where feasible.

Treat a supplied plan as editorial guidance subject to the assignment's rules. Combine or divide steps for readability. Deepen a connection using the authorized evidence. Correct an unsupported inference rather than polishing it into apparent certainty. When a major change requires permission under the task's actual constraints, identify the issue precisely; otherwise use ordinary editorial judgment.

### When revising existing prose

First identify the important content and distinctions to preserve. Then diagnose the main problem: missing explanation, weak order, disproportion, redundancy, unsupported interpretation, or sentence-level friction. Fix that problem at its own scale.

A paragraph with a missing inference needs reasoning and evidence. A section that repeats an earlier section needs restructuring. A tangled sentence needs rewriting. Applying only shorter sentences or stronger verbs to every problem will leave the main weakness intact.

Do not replace a distinctive, supported observation with a generic summary merely because the generic version reads smoothly. Preserve the author's intended voice when that is part of the assignment.

## 17. Editorial review and acceptance

Review the whole piece once for structure and substance, then revise the passages that create actual difficulty. Repeat a focused review when those changes create new dependencies. Avoid endless polishing after the requirements are satisfied.

Use the following checks. They are diagnostic questions, not a scoring formula or a required report.

| Check | Evidence of success | Repair when it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | The reader can identify the piece's question, use, or movement. | Clarify the opening and organize around that purpose. |
| Progression | Each section adds something distinct and later sections use earlier results. | Merge repetition, reorder dependencies, or supply the missing connection. |
| Depth | Major conclusions have understandable support and relevant limits. | Develop the mechanism, inference, counterexample, or distinction. |
| Detail preservation | Required and important findings remain present with their meaning intact. | Restore omitted content or move it to a suitable place. |
| Useful surprise | An unusual detail has a source, a contextual connection, and a consequence. | Supply the connection, narrow the claim, or omit optional unsupported material. |
| Proportion | Attention follows significance and explanatory need. | Shorten minor treatment or expand an underexplained central relationship. |
| Continuity | Pronouns, terms, transitions, and prerequisites are clear. | Name the referent, stabilize terminology, or change the order. |
| Natural prose | Sentences can be read aloud without repeated stumbling or mechanical cadence. | Untangle syntax and vary development according to the thought. |
| Evidence | Quotes are faithful and each source does identifiable work. | Correct the source use and explain its particular contribution. |
| Ending | The result or consequence follows from the body. | Replace generic recap or an unsupported flourish with the earned result. |

For a final reader pass, ask where someone without your notes would need to stop and reconstruct the meaning. Repair avoidable friction. Keep productive difficulty when the subject requires a real distinction or unresolved question.

Do not claim the prose has achieved a particular model's quality merely because it passed format checks. A representative reader's judgment of clarity, depth, and preserved substance remains the relevant standard.

## 18. Reusable launch instruction

Use the following with a future assignment. Fill in the task and provide the source material; the writer should not need this repository's conversation history.

> Read `PROSE_EDITORIAL_GUIDE.md` completely and apply it to the assignment below. Write for the specified reader and genre. Preserve required content, substantive findings, useful surprises, and meaningful qualifications. Organize the material so that each part advances understanding; explain the relationships that make the evidence useful. Give details space according to their contribution. Use natural language and imagery that serves the subject. Adapt paragraph shapes to the work they perform. Before returning the result, check progression, depth, evidence, detail preservation, and reading friction. Keep planning and review notes out of the final prose unless requested. Follow the assignment's source boundaries, output format, and length requirements.

For a separate planner, request the compact handoff described in section 16. For a separate writer, provide both that handoff and its supporting sources. For a reviewer, request concrete passages, the reader's difficulty, and the smallest substantive correction; request a full rewrite only when the structure requires one.
