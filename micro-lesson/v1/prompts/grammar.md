# Engine: grammar

Apply common.md. Ask what feature of this expression the reader can learn to
recognize or interpret elsewhere. Explore the full grammar coverage in ONTOLOGY.md:
word pieces, noun/verb morphology, pronouns, case and mood, phrases, clauses,
particles, participant relations, agreement, order and speech function. These are
directions for discovery, not a lesson checklist.

Ground the observation in the expression and available analysis. Morphology can
support a form claim without proving a rhetorical or theological inference.
Distinguish Arabic nominal case, verbal mood, derivation and Turkish case suffixes.
Not every genitive ends in -i; not every nâ is an object; not every fronted element
means only; not every imperative is a command in its speaking context.

Separate what each part contributes before explaining the whole expression.
For example, in لَمْ يُذْكَرِ the negative meaning requires lam; the verb alone
does not mean “anılmadı.” In nafs-i-hi, nafs supplies “self” and -hi supplies the
third-person reference; do not attribute “self” to the suffix. Use the supplied
morpheme analysis to settle such local questions, not Turkish translation alone.

Teach the difference that matters here, including a minimal comparison already in
the paragraph when helpful. Keep one coherent observation even if it teaches
several concepts across grammar, discourse and Turkish mapping. For example,
recognizing the imperative in ihdinâ and its prayer function can form one lesson.
The object suffix can be a separate lesson if it has independent learning value.

Introduce prerequisite knowledge locally when this passage supplies a natural
example. Otherwise a compact shared introduction can help. Do not turn a simple
reading observation into a full paradigm or terminology course.

Annotate by the taught content, not by this engine's name. Form-family meaning
belongs mainly to semantics and gloss tradeoffs mainly to mapping, but preserve
useful overlaps for the assembler to judge against the wording and evidence.
