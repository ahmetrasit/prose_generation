[RAZI-FULL:v1p166/r7] verses 1:1 | Al-Shafi‘i’s count is preferable: the pause after an‘amta ‘alayhim does not match the sura’s verse endings, and verse endings elsewhere are either near-rhyming or matching. «مقطع قوله صراط الذين أنعمت عليهم لا يشابه مقطع الآيات المتقدمة»
[RAZI-FULL:v1p166/r8] verses 1:1 | Starting a verse with ghayr separates a modifier or exception from what it qualifies, though each belongs syntactically with the preceding phrase. «هذا اللفظ إما أن يكون صفة لما قبله أو استثناء عما قبله»
[RAZI-FULL:v1p166/r9] verses 1:1 | The request to guide us to the path of the blessed is incomplete without excluding those under anger or astray; the whole qualified phrase should therefore be one verse. «ما لم يضم إليه قوله غير المغضوب عليهم ولا الضالين لم يصح قوله اهدنا الصراط المستقيم»
[RAZI-FULL:v1p167/r1] verses 1:1 | The verse-count objection disappears if the basmala and the words after it form one verse; a complete verse in al-Fatiha can be part of a longer verse elsewhere, and al-Kawthar’s count covers its distinctive content. «إذا قلنا بسم الله الرحمن الرحيم مع ما بعده آية واحدة فهذا الإشكال زائل»
[RAZI-FULL:v1p167/r2] verses 1:1 | Ahmad ibn Hanbal is reported to count the basmala as a verse of al-Fatiha while reciting it quietly in every rak‘a; al-Shafi‘i counts it and recites aloud, while Abu Hanifa does not count it but reads it quietly. «التسمية آية من الفاتحة إلا أنه يسر بها في كل ركعة»
[RAZI-FULL:v1p167/r3] verses 1:1 | Razi holds loud recitation to be Sunna: if the sura is recited aloud, its opening basmala should be too, rather than making only part of a sura audible. «السورة الواحدة إما أن تكون بتمامها سرية أو جهرية»
[RAZI-FULL:v1p167/r4] verses 1:1 | Because the basmala praises and glorifies God, making its remembrance public is fitting; the Qur’an commands abundant remembrance, as one would publicly honor a beloved father. «قوله بسم الله الرحمن الرحيم لا شك أنه ثناء على الله وذكر له بالتعظيم»
[RAZI-FULL:v1p167/r5] verses 1:1 | Open remembrance publicly shows pride in honoring God and indifference to objection; reason commends that, supported by the reported maxim about what Muslims deem good. «الجهر بذكر الله يدل على كونه مفتخرا بذلك الذكر غير مبال بإنكار من ينكره»
[RAZI-FULL:v1p168#2/r1] verses 1:1 | The implied verb with the basmala means beginning acts with God’s help; hearing it teaches dependence on divine protection from sin and aid for good, making its recitation a call to the best good rather than an innovation. «يتعلق بفعل لا بد من إضماره والتقدير بإعانة اسم الله اشرعوا في الطاعات»
[RAZI-FULL:v1p168#2/r2] verses 1:1 | Anas reports that the Prophet, Abu Bakr, Umar, and Uthman began prayer with al-hamdu lillahi rabb al-alamin; Muslim versions say they did not mention, or were not heard saying, the basmala aloud. «صليت خلف رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم وخلف أبي بكر وعمر وعثمان وكانوا يستفتحون القراءة بالحمد لله رب العالمين»
[RAZI-FULL:v1p168#2/r3] verses 1:1 | Ibn al-Mughaffal says his father warned him against introducing the basmala, recalling that the Prophet and first three caliphs began with al-hamdu lillah. «يا بني إياك والحدث في الإسلام فقد صليت خلف رسول الله»
[RAZI-FULL:v1p168#2/r4] verses 1:1 | Opponents cite Qur’anic commands to invoke God humbly and remember Him inwardly as proof that the basmala should be quiet; jurists infer this from the verses and earlier reports. «وبسم الله الرحمن الرحيم ذكر الله فوجب إخفاؤه»
[RAZI-FULL:v1p168/r1] verses 1:1 | Concealment suits a defect, not the greatest honor of remembering God; reports praise dying with a tongue moist in remembrance and Ali’s saying that God’s mention ennobles those who remember Him. «طوبى لمن مات ولسانه رطب من ذكر الله»
[RAZI-FULL:v1p168/r2] verses 1:1 | Ali is reported to recite the basmala aloud in all prayers, a practice Razi says strongly confirms public recitation. «كان مذهبه الجهر ببسم الله الرحمن الرحيم في جميع الصلوات»
