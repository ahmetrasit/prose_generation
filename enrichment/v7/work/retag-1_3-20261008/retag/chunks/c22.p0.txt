# Notes

## 1:1
[SULAMI:v1p31/r5] verses 1:1 | The four letters form a complete, unique name: alif is the pillar of oneness, the two lām letters signify the Tablet of the Pen and of Prophecy, and hāʾ is the utmost indication; God exceeds apprehension. «سمعت منصورا بإسناده عن جعفر أنه قال في قوله ' الله ' : إنه اسم تام»
[SULAMI:v1p32/r1] verses 1:1 | All enduring graces for believers issue through al-Rahman, and no creature can bear that name because creatures cannot give what lasts. «باسم الرحمن خرجت جميع الكرامات للمؤمنين مثل الإيمان والطاعات والولاية»
[SULAMI:v1p32/r2] verses 1:1 | Al-Rahman's mercy lets seekers detach from everything below Him; its worldly bounty reaches friend and enemy alike. «وأيضا فإن رحمة الرحمانية للمريدين بها ينفصلون عما دون الرحمن»
[SULAMI:v1p32/r3] verses 1:1 | Al-Rahman signifies the sweetness of benefaction, witnessed nearness, and careful service. «وقيل في اسمه الرحمن : حلاوة المنة ومشاهدة القربة ومحافظة الخدمة»
[SULAMI:v1p32/r4] verses 1:1 | Lovers, the fearful, repentant, and sinners each receive a distinct spiritual fruit from the meanings of al-Rahman: intimacy, security, purification, or remorse and seeking forgiveness. «والخائفون يتلذذون في قلوبهم في معاني اسمه الرحمن ويتزودون منها حلاوة السكون»
[SULAMI:v1p32/r5] verses 1:1 | Al-Rahman means God's aid and victory. «وقال ابن عطاء : في اسمه الرحمن عونه ونصرته»
[SULAMI:v1p32/r6] verses 1:1 | One approaches al-Rahman only through His all-encompassing mercy, while al-Rahim is approached through obedience; the Prophet also bears the latter name (9:128). «وقال الواسطي : الرحمن لا يتقرب إليه أحد إلا بصرف رحمانيته»
[SULAMI:v1p33/r1] verses 1:1 | Al-Rahim denotes mercy for creatures' livelihood and bodily welfare, so creatures may be called rahim, unlike the uniquely divine Rahman. «يقال : إن معنى الرحيم هو ما يخرج من الرحمة الرحيمية لمعاش الخلق»
[SULAMI:v1p33/r2] verses 1:1 | The Prophet, called compassionate and merciful toward believers, is a means by which people reach God and the two divine names; the Merciful accepts the returning person despite faults and preserves even one who turns away. «وقيل : إن معنى الرحيم أي بالرحيم وصلتم إلى الله وإلى الرحمن»
[SULAMI:v1p33/r3] verses 1:1 | Al-Rahim signifies God's affection and mercy. «قال ابن عطاء : في اسمه ' الرحيم ' مودته ورحمته»
[SULAMI:v1p33/r4] verses 1:1 | Al-Rahman suits those sought by God, immersed in lights and realities; al-Rahim suits seekers remaining with themselves and tending outward practice. «هو واقع على المريدين والمرادين ، فاسم الرحمن للمرادين لاستغراقهم في الأنوار»
[SULAMI:v1p33/r5] verses 1:1 | Al-Rahman's mercy reaches back into pre-eternity and has no endpoint, whereas al-Rahim connects mercy with worldly life, guidance, and provisions. «والرحمن المنتهى بكرامته إلى ما غاية له لأنه قد أوصل الرحمة بالأزل»
[TAB-FULL:v1p111#3/r1] verses 1:1 | God taught Muhammad to put His beautiful names before every action, establishing a practice for people opening speech, letters, books, and requests. «أدب نبيه محمدا صلى الله عليه وسلم بتعليمه تقديم ذكر أسمائه الحسنى أمام جميع أفعاله»
[TAB-FULL:v1p111#3/r2] verses 1:1 | The prefixed bāʾ requires a governing verb omitted from the wording; the listener recovers it from the immediately accompanying action or speech. «مقتضية فعلا يكون لها جالبا، ولا فعل معها ظاهر»
[TAB-FULL:v1p111#3/r3] verses 1:1 | At Qurʾān recitation the implicit verb is read, while in other acts it is rise, sit, or the relevant action, all begun with God’s name. «أراد بقيله {بسم الله} [الفاتحة: 1] : أقوم بسم الله»
[TAB-FULL:v1p113/r1] verses 1:1 | Ibn ʿAbbās reports Gabriel first taught seeking refuge and then instructed Muhammad to read, rise, and sit in remembrance of his Lord. «يا محمد يقول: اقرأ بذكر الله ربك، وقم واقعد بذكر الله»
[TAB-FULL:v1p113/r2] verses 1:1 | The phrase means beginning by naming and remembering God, rather than claiming that the act itself is performed through God’s power. «أبدأ بتسمية الله وذكره قبل كل شيء، أو أقرأ بتسمية الله»
[TAB-FULL:v1p113/r3] verses 1:1 | Arabic can use a verbal noun in a form normally associated with a noun; thus bismillah can express beginning with the act of naming. «العرب قد تخرج المصادر مبهمة على أسماء مختلفة»
[TAB-FULL:v1p115/r1] verses 1:1 | Poetic usage confirms verbal nouns can take other noun forms: ʿaṭāʾika means iʿṭāʾika, ṭūl means iṭāla, and muṣāb means iṣāba. «أكفرا بعد رد الموت عني … وبعد عطائك المائة الرتاعا يريد: إعطائك»
[TAB-FULL:v1p115/r2] verses 1:1 | Accordingly, the name stands for naming: the phrase means that one begins an action or recitation by naming God. «فجعل الاسم مكان التسمية، كما جعل الكلام مكان التكليم، والعطاء مكان الإعطاء»
[TAB-FULL:v1p116/r1] verses 1:1 | Ibn ʿAbbās reports Gabriel’s instruction that Muhammad read and undertake actions in remembrance of God his Lord. «يا محمد اقرأ بذكر الله ربك، وقم واقعد بذكر الله»
[TAB-FULL:v1p116/r2] verses 1:1 | Ṭabarī adopts the interpretation that recitation starts with God’s naming and remembrance, using His beautiful names and exalted attributes. «أقرأ بتسمية الله وذكره، وأفتتح القراءة بتسمية الله، بأسمائه الحسنى، وصفاته العلى»
[TAB-FULL:v1p116/r3] verses 1:1 | The wording does not mean simply “by God”; the consensus on slaughter treats saying bi-llāh without bismillah as omitting the prescribed invocation. «أن قائلا لو قال عند تذكيته بعض بهائم الأنعام: بالله»
[TAB-FULL:v1p116/r4] verses 1:1 | The present question concerns whether the attached “name” is a noun or a verbal noun meaning naming; the broader identity of name and named is outside this discussion. «وليس هذا الموضع من مواضع الإكثار في الإبانة عن الاسم، أهو المسمى أم غيره أم هو صفة له»
[TAB-FULL:v1p118/r1] verses 1:1 | Ṭabarī rejects taking Labīd’s “name of peace” simply as “peace”; that reading would license impossible Arabic such as eating the name of honey. «وفي إجماع جميع العرب على إحالة ذلك ما ينبئ عن فساد تأويل من تأول قول لبيد»
[TAB-FULL:v1p118/r2] verses 1:1 | One possible reading makes al-Salām a divine name: “adhere to God’s name” after a year, with the command for adherence delayed in the wording; Ṭabarī finds this likelier. «أن السلام اسم من أسماء الله؛ فجائز أن يكون لبيد عنى بقوله: ثم اسم السلام عليكما»
[TAB-FULL:v1p118/r3] verses 1:1 | A second possible reading is a protective invocation: may God’s name be upon the two addressed people against harm. «ثم تسميتي الله عليكما، كما يقول القائل للشيء يراه فيعجبه: واسم الله عليك»
[TAB-FULL:v1p118/r4] verses 1:1 | Of the two defensible readings of Labīd, the first—taking al-Salām as God’s name and an exhortation to keep His remembrance—is more apt. «وكأن الوجه الأول أشبه المعنيين بقول لبيد»
[TAB-FULL:v1p120/r1] verses 1:1 | A report says Jesus explained the letters bāʾ, sīn, and mīm as God’s beauty, splendor, and dominion when a teacher asked about bism. «فقال عيسى: الباء: بهاء الله، والسين: سناؤه، والميم: مملكته»
[TAB-FULL:v1p120/r2] verses 1:1 | Ṭabarī suspects a transmitter mistakenly joined the beginner’s alphabet letters b-s-m into bism; the explanation is impossible in ordinary Arabic when the whole basmala is recited. «فأخشى أن يكون غلطا من المحدث»
[TAB-FULL:v1p121/r1] verses 1:1 | Ibn ʿAbbās defines God as the one whom everything worships and every creature serves. «عن عبد الله بن عباس، قال: «الله ذو الألوهية والمعبودية على خلقه أجمعين»»
[TAB-FULL:v1p121/r2] verses 1:1 | The proposed derivation of Allāh from ilāh is an inference rather than an attested form heard directly from Arab speech. «أما سماعا من العرب فلا، ولكن استدلالا»
[TAB-FULL:v1p121/r3] verses 1:1 | Arab usage of taʾallaha for worship and seeking God supports the root’s devotional sense; a line by Ruʾba uses taʾalluhī in that sense. «سبحن واسترجعن من تألهي يعني: من تعبدي وطلبي الله بعمل»
[TAB-FULL:v1p121/r4] verses 1:1 | Ibn ʿAbbās reads the Pharaoh passage’s ilāhataka as worship, explaining that Pharaoh worshipped rather than being worshipped. «عن ابن عباس أنه قرأ: (ويذرك وإلاهتك) قال: عبادتك»
[TAB-FULL:v1p121/r5] verses 1:1 | Mujāhid likewise glosses ilāhataka in the Pharaoh passage as worship, a reading also attributed there to Ibn ʿAbbās. «عن مجاهد، قوله: (ويذرك وإلاهتك) قال: «وعبادتك»»
[TAB-FULL:v1p121/r6] verses 1:1 | These explanations establish that alaha means worship and ilāha is its verbal noun, supporting the interpretation of God as the worshipped one. «أن أله: عبد، وأن الإلاهة مصدره»
[TAB-FULL:v1p124#2/r1] verses 1:1 | Al-Raḥmān follows the faʿlān pattern from raḥima, while al-Raḥīm follows faʿīl, a praise form that can occur despite the root’s vowel pattern. «أما الرحمن، فهو فعلان، من رحم، والرحيم فعيل منه»
[TAB-FULL:v1p124#2/r2] verses 1:1 | In Arabic, the more distant a praise name is from its ordinary verbal pattern, the stronger its commendation; thus al-Raḥmān adds intensity beyond al-Raḥīm. «أن قول القائل الرحمن عن أبنية الأسماء من فعل يفعل أشد عدولا من قوله الرحيم»
[TAB-FULL:v1p124#2/r3] verses 1:1 | Al-ʿAzramī distinguishes al-Raḥmān as mercy extending to all creation and al-Raḥīm as mercy toward believers. «الرحمن بجميع الخلق. الرحيم قال: بالمؤمنين»
[TAB-FULL:v1p124/r1] verses 1:1 | A prophetic report quotes Jesus telling his teacher that Allāh is the god of gods. «إن عيسى أسلمته أمه إلى الكتاب ليعلمه»
[TAB-FULL:v1p124/r2] verses 1:1 | Ṭabarī proposes that Allāh derives from al-ilāh: the initial hamza is dropped and the adjacent lām sounds assimilate into one doubled lām. «فكذلك الله، أصله الإله، أسقطت الهمزة، التي هي فاء الاسم، فالتقت اللام»
[TAB-FULL:v1p124/r3] verses 1:1 | The contraction is comparable to the Qurʾānic form lakinnā from wa-lākin anā, illustrated by a poet’s similar elision and assimilation. «فكذلك الله، أصله الإله، أسقطت الهمزة»
