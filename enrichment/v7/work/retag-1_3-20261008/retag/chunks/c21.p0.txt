# Notes

## 1:1
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1#7/r6] verses 1:1 | On the al-ilah derivation, hamza is dropped through frequent use, like anās becoming nās; an alternative account transfers its vowel before deletion. «ثم حذفت الهمزة لكثرة الاستعمال كما حذفت في ناس»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1#8/r1] verses 1:1 | Al-Khalil derives Allah from walah, because all creatures long for God; philosophers say all things love God, supported by the verse that everything glorifies Him. «ويعزى هذا القول للخليل، فعلى هذين القولين وزن إلاه: فعال، وهو بمعنى مفعول أي: معبود أو متحير فيه كالكتاب بمعنى مكتوب»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1#8/r2] verses 1:1 | This derivation changes initial waw to hamza, like ishaah and i'aah from wishah and wi'aah; the name then has the pattern fa'al meaning worshipped or wondered at. «فأصله: ولاه ثم أبدلت الواو همزة كما أبدلت في إشاح وإعاء»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1#8/r3] verses 1:1 | The author rebuts al-Khalil: if hamza replaced waw, the original forms should remain pronounceable, and the word should pluralize as awliha; neither occurs. «ورد قول الخليل بوجهين، أحدهما: أنه لو كانت الهمزة بدلا من واو لجاز النطق بالأصل»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1#8/r4] verses 1:1 | Al-Khalil can answer that the replacement became obligatory because this name has unique grammatical properties and its plural preserves that replacement. «وللخليل أن ينفصل عن هذين الاعتراضين بأن البدل لزم في هذا الاسم لأنه اختص بأحكام لم يشركه فيها غيره»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1#8/r5] verses 1:1 | Whether the article in Allah is original depends on whether the name is derived; if derived it is original, otherwise added. «وأما الألف واللام فيترتب الكلام فيها على كونه مشتقا أو غير مشتق»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1#8/r6] verses 1:1 | The alif was omitted in writing to distinguish Allah from the idol al-Lat; another explanation invokes the word al-lāh, while a third calls the spelling a rare form fixed by frequent use. «قالوا: وحذفت الألف التي قبل الهاء خطا لئلا يشبه بخط " اللات»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1#8/r7] verses 1:1 | The lām of Allah is emphatic except after kasra; al-Zamakhshari's unqualified statement should be understood this way, while Abu al-Baqa's report of always-light pronunciation is rejected. «وحكم لامه التفخيم تعظيما ما لم يتقدمه كسر فترقق»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1#8/r8] verses 1:1 | Readers disagree about the lām after an imāla-like open vowel; al-Susi has one reading with a light lām in hatta nara Allah jahratan. «ونقل أهل القراءة خلافا فيما إذا تقدمه فتحة ممالة»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1#9/r1] verses 1:1 | Al-Suhayli and Ibn al-Arabi unusually say the article is original; the author objects that this would make the pattern fa'al regularly take tanwin, which Allah does not. «ونقل السهيلي وابن العربي فيه قولا غريبا وهو أن الألف واللام فيه أصلية غير زائدة»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1#9/r2] verses 1:1 | Abu Zayd al-Balkhi reports that Allah is a Syriac loan from laha; a poem preserves the supposed original form lahi. «ونقل ذلك أبو زيد البلخي»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1#9/r3] verses 1:1 | A strange proposal derives the name from the pronoun hu plus a possessive lām and article; the author says it resembles mysticism, not linguistic scholarship. «ومن غريب ما نقل فيه أيضا أن الأصل فيه الهاء التي هي كناية عن الغائب»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1#9/r4] verses 1:1 | Al-Zamakhshari rejects treating Allah as an adjective: it is used as a noun, while adjectives require a noun they describe and cannot all remain without one. «وقد رد الزمخشري هذا القول بما معناه أنك تصفه ولا تصف به»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r1] verses 1:1 | The verb basmala means to say bismillah, like hawqala, haylala, and hamdala for saying their respective formulas. «مصدر بسمل، أي قال: بسم الله، نحو: حوقل وهيلل وحمدل»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r10] verses 1:1 | Ba can mean exchange or substitution, as in the prophetic saying about red camels and in a poem wishing for replacement companions. «والبدل كقوله عليه السلام:
" ما يسرني بها حمر النعم»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r11] verses 1:1 | Ba also marks an oath and location, as in swearing by God and saying Zayd is in Mecca. «والقسم: أحلف بالله لأفعلن، والظرفية نحو: زيد بمكة أي فيها»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r12] verses 1:1 | Ba can make a verb transitive, as in God taking away their light. «والتعدية نحو:
ذهب الله بنورهم»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r13] verses 1:1 | A poet's ba in drinking from seawater means from it, showing the partitive use. «والتبعيض كقول الشاعر:
9 شربن بماء البحر ثم ترفعت»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r14] verses 1:1 | Ba can mark price or equivalence in buying something for a thousand. «والمقابلة: " اشتريته بألف " أي: قابلته بهذا الثمن»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r15] verses 1:1 | Ba can mean away from, as in the sky splitting away from clouds; some restrict this sense to contexts of asking, as in ask an expert about it. «والمجاوزة مثل قوله تعالى:
ويوم تشقق السمآء بالغمام»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r16] verses 1:1 | Alqama's line uses ba with asking women to mean asking about them, illustrating the restricted sense of departure. «وقول علقمة:
10 فإن تسألوني بالنساء فإنني
خبير بأدواء النساء طبيب»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r2] verses 1:1 | These clipped verbs resemble nisba coinages that combine names, as in hadrami, abqasi, and abshami from tribal names; a poem uses abshamiyya. «وهذا شبيه بباب النحت في النسب، أي إنهم يأخذون اسمين فينحتون»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r3] verses 1:1 | Because basmala and haylala are non-analogical, some classify them as later colloquial coinages. «وهو غير مقيس، فلا جرم أن بعضهم قال في: بسمل وهيلل إنها لغة مولدة»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r4] verses 1:1 | Al-Mawardi calls the person who says bismillah mubasmil and labels that usage a later coinage. «قال الماوردي: يقال لمن قال: بسم الله: مبسمل وهي] لغة مولدة»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r5] verses 1:1 | Ibn Abi Rabi'a's verse attests basmala as established poetic usage, while Tha'lab and al-Mutarriz transmit it without calling it a late coinage. «قال عمر بن أبي ربيعة:
7 لقد بسملت ليلى غداة لقيتها»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r6] verses 1:1 | The preposition ba here expresses seeking aid: the implied sense is that I recite while relying on God. «والباء هنا للاستعانة كعملت وبالقدوم، لأن المعنى: أقرأ مستعينا بالله»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r7] verses 1:1 | Ba can mark real or figurative attachment, illustrated by wiping the head and passing by Zayd. «وهي: الإلصاق حقيقة أو مجازا، نحو: مسحت برأسي، مررت بزيد»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r8] verses 1:1 | Ba can mean cause, as in God forbidding things because of the wrongdoing of the Jews. «والسببية: [نحو]
فبظلم من الذين هادوا حرمنا عليهم»
[SAMIN-DURR:1:1/r9] verses 1:1 | Ba can mark accompaniment, as in Zayd going out with his clothes. «والمصاحبة نحو: خرج زيد بثيابه، أي مصاحبا لها»
[STUDYQURAN:1:1/r1] verses 1:1 | Every sūrah except one opens with the basmalah, also found in 27:30. «known as the basmalah in Arabic»
[STUDYQURAN:1:1/r10] verses 1:1 | The Prophet says an important matter not begun in God’s Name will be cut off. «the Prophet said, “Any important matter not begun with the Name of God shall be cut off”»
[STUDYQURAN:1:1/r11] verses 1:1 | The preposition bi may govern an implied verb such as begin or seek help, or a noun, with the phrase either preceding or following it. «could imply a verb at the beginning of the sentence»
[STUDYQURAN:1:1/r12] verses 1:1 | Another interpretation makes God’s opening in His Name an invitation for people to begin, unite, and commence through His Name as well. «so you too begin,” as if God were saying»
[STUDYQURAN:1:1/r13] verses 1:1 | Some consider Allāh a unique word without a root; others derive it from al-Ilāh, meaning the Divine. «God renders Allāh, which according to some is a unique word with no root and according to others derives from the word al-Ilāh»
[STUDYQURAN:1:1/r14] verses 1:1 | The Quran shows pre-Islamic Arabs knew the name Allāh as creator, although most idol worshippers denied His supreme sovereignty in the Age of Ignorance. «The Quranic usage of Allāh indicates that it was already known to the pre-Islamic Arabs, as in 43:87»
[STUDYQURAN:1:1/r15] verses 1:1 | Allāh is the most universal and all-embracing Divine Name, yet uniquely applies only to God, unlike other Divine Names. «Allāh is not only the most universal and all-embracing Name, but also the most specific of the Divine Names»
[STUDYQURAN:1:1/r16] verses 1:1 | Some call Allāh the All-Encompassing Name, comprising all divine Names and Attributes. «Some referto Allāh as the All-Encompassing Name»
[STUDYQURAN:1:1/r17] verses 1:1 | The order Allāh, al-Raḥmān, al-Raḥīm can symbolize Essence, unity of Attributes, and unity of divine acts, respectively. «The ordering of the three Divine Names in the basmalah can be seen as an allusion to the levels of Divinity in relation to the created order»
[STUDYQURAN:1:1/r2] verses 1:1 | Scholars disagree whether the basmalah is a verse of each sūrah or a marker beginning a new sūrah. «There is a debate as to whether the basmalah is a verse of each sūrah or an indication of the beginning of a new sūrah»
[STUDYQURAN:1:1/r3] verses 1:1 | Except in Shāfiʿī and Jaʿfarī law, most schools do not recite the basmalah with sūrahs during daily prayers. «with the exception of the Shāfiʿī and Jaʿfarī schools»
[STUDYQURAN:1:1/r4] verses 1:1 | Anas reports that the Prophet and first three caliphs began prayer recitation with Praise be to God, omitting the basmalah. «Anas ibn Mālik (d. 91/709), a close Companion of the Prophet, is reported to have said, “I have prayed behind the Prophet, Abū Bakr»
[STUDYQURAN:1:1/r5] verses 1:1 | Abū Hurayrah recited the basmalah while leading prayer and said his prayer most resembled the Messenger’s. «Abū Hurayrah (d. 61/681), another of the Prophet’s Companions, is reported to have recited the basmalah»
[STUDYQURAN:1:1/r6] verses 1:1 | Jaʿfar calls the basmalah the greatest Quranic verse; Shiite scholars consequently treat it as a verse of the Fātiḥah and every sūra
