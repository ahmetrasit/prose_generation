# Verses
- 1:1: بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ
- 1:3: ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ

# Notes

## 1:1
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r134] verses 1:1 | The preposition bi in the basmala may attach to an implied noun, “my beginning,” or verb, “I begin”; Qur'anic usage supports both. «هل هو اسم أو فعل متقاربان وكل قد ورد به القرآن»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r135] verses 1:1 | The implied action varies with the activity begun—standing, sitting, eating, drinking, reciting, ablution, or prayer—and the basmala seeks blessing, aid to completion, and acceptance. «وذلك بحسب الفعل الذي سميت قبله ، إن كان قيامًا أو قعودًا أو أكلا أو شربًا»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r136] verses 1:1 | In a report on the first revelation, Gabriel tells Muhammad to say the basmala and explains it as beginning, standing, and sitting in remembrance of God; Ibn Kathir elsewhere calls the report weak and disconnected. «قال له جبريل : قل : باسم الله يا محمد ، يقول : اقرأ بذكر الله ربك»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r137] verses 1:1 | One position identifies a name with the named object; Abu ʿUbayda and Sibawayh hold this view, as do al-Baqillani and Ibn Furak. «أن الاسم هو المسمى ، وهو قول أبي عبيدة وسيبويه»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r138] verses 1:1 | Al-Razi reports that the Hashwiyya, Karramiyya, and Ashʿariyya identify name and named but distinguish both from naming, while the Muʿtazila distinguish name from named and equate it with naming; he chooses a third view. «قالت الحشوية والكرامية والأشعرية : الاسم نفس المسمى وغير التسمية ، وقالت المعتزلة : الاسم غير المسمى ونفس التسمية»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r139] verses 1:1 | Al-Razi's preferred view is that the name differs from both the named thing and the act of naming. «والمختار عندنا : أن الاسم غير المسمى وغير التسمية»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r14] verses 1:1 | Ibn ʿAbbas, Qatada, and Abu al-ʿAliya hold that the surah was revealed in Mecca. «ابن عباس وقتادة وأبو العالية»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r140] verses 1:1 | Al-Razi argues that names can exist without their referents, one thing can have many names, one name can refer to many things, and a word is an accident while its referent may be an entity. «قد يكون الاسم موجودًا والمسمى مفقودًا كلفظة المعدوم»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r141] verses 1:1 | Al-Razi says God's many names refer to one God and that calling God by those names indicates their distinction from Him. «فهذه أسماء كثيرة والمسمى واحد وهو الله تعالى»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r142] verses 1:1 | Those who equate name and named cite “blessed is the name of your Lord”; al-Razi answers that honoring the name honors the sacred essence, and a divorce utterance refers to the person bearing that name. «والجواب : أن الاسم معظم لتعظيم الذات المقدسة»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r143] verses 1:1 | Allah is the proper name of the Lord and is called the Greatest Name because all the other attributes are predicated of Him. «على الرب تبارك وتعالى ، يقال : إنه الاسم الأعظم»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r144] verses 1:1 | A sound hadith says God has ninety-nine names; whoever comprehends them enters Paradise, though the enumerated lists in al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah differ. «إن لله تسعة وتسعين اسما ، مائة إلا واحدًا من أحصاها دخل الجنة»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r145] verses 1:1 | Allah is a name not used for anyone else, and Arabic does not clearly derive it from an ordinary verb. «وهو اسم لم يسم به غيره تبارك وتعالى ؛ ولهذا لا يعرف في كلام العرب له اشتقاق من فعل ويفعل»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r146] verses 1:1 | These scholars regard Allah as an underived, fixed proper name; al-Khattabi argues that the vocative “ya Allah” works, whereas “ya al-Rahman” does not. «فذهب من ذهب من النحاة إلى أنه اسم جامد لا اشتقاق له»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r147] verses 1:1 | Ruʾba's line uses taʾalluh, worship, as evidence that Allah is derived from the root for worship. «سبحن واسترجعن من تألهي»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r148] verses 1:1 | Ibn ʿAbbas's reading “wa-yadharuka wa-ilahatak” is explained as “your worship”; Mujahid gives the same gloss. «كما روي أن ابن عباس قرأ»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r149] verses 1:1 | Those who derive Allah from divinity cite that God is worshipped in heaven and earth and is a god in both realms. «أي : المعبود في السماوات والأرض»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r15] verses 1:1 | Abu Hurayra, Mujahid, ʿAta ibn Yasar, and al-Zuhri hold that the surah was revealed in Medina. «أبو هريرة ومجاهد وعطاء بن يسار والزهري»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r150] verses 1:1 | Al-Khalil and Sibawayh derive the word from ilah, with the article replacing an initial hamza, by analogy with al-nas from unas; another proposal takes it from lah for exalted. «أن أصله : إلاه ، مثل فعال ، فأدخلت الألف واللام بدلا من الهمزة»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r151] verses 1:1 | Al-Kisaʾi and al-Farraʾ derive it from al-ilah, with the hamza dropped and the two lams assimilated, like lakinna from lakin ana. «أصله : الإله حذفوا الهمزة وأدغموا اللام الأولى في الثانية»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r152] verses 1:1 | Al-Razi lists proposed derivations from bewilderment, repose in God, concealment, a calf's attachment to its mother, and seeking refuge in God. «ثم قيل : هو مشتق من وله : إذا تحير»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r153] verses 1:1 | Al-Razi says God alone is the refuge and provider, citing that He protects and cannot be protected against, gives every blessing, feeds without being fed, and all things come from Him. «فالمجير لجميع الخلائق من كل المضار هو الله سبحانه»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r154] verses 1:1 | Al-Razi chooses Allah as an underived proper name; he argues that if it were derived, others could share its meaning, while the other names function as attributes. «وقد اختار فخر الدين أنه اسم علم غير مشتق البتة»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r155] verses 1:1 | Al-Razi also cites 19:65, “Do you know anyone like Him?” as evidence for an un-derived name, but Ibn Kathir finds that argument debatable. «وفي الاستدلال بهذه على كون هذا الاسم جامدًا غير مشتق نظر»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r156] verses 1:1 | Al-Razi rejects the claim that the name Allah is Hebrew rather than Arabic. «وحكى فخر الدين عن بعضهم أنه ذهب إلى أن اسم الله تعالى عبراني لا عربي ، ثم ضعفه»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r157] verses 1:1 | Al-Khalil explains that all creation is captivated by God, using a form of the root “to worship.” «لأن الخلق يألهون إليه بنصب اللام وجرها لغتان»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r158] verses 1:1 | A further proposal derives Allah from elevation: Arabs called something high lahan and said lahat when the sun rose. «وقيل : إنه مشتق من الارتفاع ، فكانت العرب تقول لكل شيء مرتفع : لاها»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r159] verses 1:1 | The proposed form begins as al-ilah; deleting the hamza brings two lams together, which assimilate and are pronounced as one emphatic doubled lam. «وأصل ذلك الإله ، فحذفت الهمزة التي هي فاء الكلمة»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r16] verses 1:1 | A third report says it was revealed twice, but Ibn Kathir prefers the Meccan account, connecting it with the seven oft-repeated in 15:87. «ومرة بالمدينة ،   والأول أشبه»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r160] verses 1:1 | Al-Rahman and al-Rahim are intensive forms derived from mercy, with al-Rahman more emphatic; Ibn Jarir's discussion suggests agreement. «اسمان مشتقان من الرحمة على وجه المبالغة»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r161] verses 1:1 | A report attributed to Jesus distinguishes Rahman as merciful in this life and the next, and Rahim as merciful in the next life. «والرحمن رحمن الدنيا والآخرة ، والرحيم رحيم الآخرة»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r162] verses 1:1 | Some grammarians claim Rahman is not derived or is Hebrew while Rahim is Arabic; al-Zajjaj rejects this as an objectionable view. «وقال أحمد بن يحيى : الرحيم عربي ، والرحمن عبراني ، فلهذا جمع بينهما»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r163] verses 1:1 | Al-Qurtubi cites the hadith, graded sound by al-Tirmidhi, in which God says He is Rahman and derived the womb's name from His own; he treats it as explicit evidence of derivation. «أنا الرحمن خلقت الرحم وشققت لها اسمًا من اسمي»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r164] verses 1:1 | Al-Qurtubi says Arabs denied the name Rahman through ignorance of God and what is due to Him. «وإنكار العرب لاسم الرحمن لجهلهم بالله وبما وجب له»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r165] verses 1:1 | Abu ʿUbayd treats Rahman and Rahim as synonymous, like two parallel forms; others distinguish the intensive faʿlan form from faʿil. «هما بمعنى واحد كندمان ونديم قاله أبو عبيد»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r166] verses 1:1 | Abu ʿAli al-Farisi says Rahman encompasses every kind of mercy and is exclusive to God, while Rahim describes His mercy toward believers. «الرحمن : اسم عام في جميع أنواع الرحمة يختص به الله تعالى ، والرحيم إنما هو من جهة المؤمنين»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r167] verses 1:1 | Ibn ʿAbbas calls the two names tender and says one is more tender—that is, more merciful—than the other. «وقال ابن عباس : هما اسمان رقيقان ، أحدهما أرق من الآخر»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r168] verses 1:1 | Ibn al-Mubarak says Rahman gives when asked, while Rahim becomes angry if not asked, linking the distinction to the hadith that God is angered by one who does not supplicate. «الرحمن إذا سئل أعطى ، والرحيم إذا لم يسأل يغضب»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r169] verses 1:1 | Al-ʿArzami says Rahman covers all creation, while Rahim specifically describes mercy to believers. «الرحمن لجميع الخلق ، الرحيم ، قال : بالمؤمنين»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r17] verses 1:1 | Abu al-Layth al-Samarqandi says half the surah was revealed in Mecca and half in Medina; Ibn Kathir calls this very strange, as transmitted by al-Qurtubi. «وحكى أبو الليث السمرقندي أن نصفها نزل بمكة ونصفها الآخر نزل بالمدينة ، وهو غريب جدًا»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r170] verses 1:1 | This distinction is supported by verses that name Rahman at God's establishing the Throne and Rahim for believers, although a traditional supplication calls Him Rahman and Rahim of both worlds. «فدل على أن الرحمن أشد مبالغة في الرحمة لعمومها في الدارين لجميع خلقه»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r171] verses 1:1 | Rahman is a name specific to God; Musaylima's self-title Rahman of Yamama became a byword for lying. «واسمه تعالى الرحمن خاص به لم يسم به غيره»
[IBNKATHIR:1:1/r172] verses 1:1 | The claim that Rahim must intensify Rahman because it follows it is mistaken: the second word is another adjective, not emphatic repetition. «وإنما هو من باب النعت [ بعد النعت ]»
