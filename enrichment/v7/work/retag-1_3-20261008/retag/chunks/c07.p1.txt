[ELMALILI:1:1#4/r3] verses 1:1 | Tanrı, hudā, ilāh, rabb, and maʿbūd are common nouns that can refer to false or multiple gods, so these terms cannot translate the unique proper name Allah. «demek olsa yine ism-i hâs değildir»
[ELMALILI:1:1#4/r4] verses 1:1 | Pluralizing Allah is false and irreverent; unlike common names for gods or revered human names, the name signifies divine unity. «ism-i celâlinde böyle bir tâbir bilakis maksad-ı ta‘zîme münâfî olduğundan hem hilâf-ı hak»

[ELMALILI:1:1#4/r6] verses 1:1 | Linguistic and religious historians ask whether Allah is Arabic, borrowed, derived, or primordial; the author turns to those questions while acknowledging their limits. «Bu kelime esasen Arabî mi değil mi? Menkul mü mürtecel mi, müştak mı, gayr-i müştak mı?»
[ELMALILI:1:1#4/r7] verses 1:1 | Human knowledge cannot encompass God’s essence or proper name, and Arabic has no comparable word from which its origin can be securely identified. «ilmimiz Ma‘bûd-ı hakk’ın zâtını muhît olmadığı gibi ism-i hâssına karşı da öylece kāsırdır»
[ELMALILI:1:1#4/r8] verses 1:1 | The Qurʾān shows that Arabs knew the name Allah and answered that Allah created the heavens and earth; its use dates at least to Ishmael, while the author finds no evidence it was borrowed from Hebrew. «وَلَئِن سَأَلْتَهُم مَّنْ خَلَقَ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ لَيَقُولُنَّ ٱللَّهُ»
[ELMALILI:1:1#4/r9] verses 1:1 | Although the name’s presence before Abraham may invite a Hebrew or Syriac borrowing theory, its circulation among ancient Arab peoples and earlier prophets supports an old Arabic history, with no proof of foreign borrowing. «çıkan bir tarihi bulunduğu zâhirdir»
[ELMALILI:1:1#5/r1] verses 1:1 | The distinctive Arabic use of the name Allah supports its status as a unique proper name, though whether the initial alif-lām is an inseparable article and how its hamza behaves remain debated. «ism-i şerîfinin lisân-ı Arab’daki hususiyeti ve binâen‘aleyh bir ism-i ‘alem olduğu anlaşılıyor»
[ELMALILI:1:1#5/r10] verses 1:1 | Unlike idols, fire, the sun, rulers, or other things made into gods by worshippers, God remains intrinsically worthy of worship whether people recognize Him or not; even deniers are compelled to serve Him unknowingly. «Allah mâbud olduğu için Allah değil, Allah olduğu için mâbuddur»
[ELMALILI:1:1#5/r11] verses 1:1 | By inductive reasoning proper names precede common nouns, which later arise from shared traits; therefore the claim that Allah’s name was transferred from a common noun or attribute is untenable. «ism-i ‘alemler velev kısmen olsun ism-i cinslerden evvel vaz‘olunur»
[ELMALILI:1:1#5/r12] verses 1:1 | A proposed compound of Hebrew El and Syriac or Arabic laah fails linguistic tests: a foreign or compound word should be indeclinable, and the proposed hamza and vowel do not match Allah’s form. «kelimenin ‘ucme ve ‘alemiyetten veya terkib ve ‘alemiyetten dolayı gayr-i munsarif»
[ELMALILI:1:1#5/r13] verses 1:1 | Borrowing or derivation of Allah from another language cannot be determined; like its referent, the name remains veiled in eternity, and the most basic response to it is awe and greatness. «bu ism-i celâl, lisan nokta-i nazarından da müsemmâsı gibi bir ezeliyet nikābı içindedir»
[ELMALILI:1:1#5/r2] verses 1:1 | ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib invoked God at the Kaaba during the Elephant event with laahumma, illustrating pre-Islamic use of the name; the author points readers to Sūrat al-Fīl. «Abdulmuttalib hazretleri Vak‘a-i Fîl’de Kâbe kapısının halkasına yapışarak»
[ELMALILI:1:1#5/r3] verses 1:1 | Abū Yazīd al-Balkhī regards laah as foreign because Jews and Christians say laha, and some relate it to Hebrew Elohim; the author says chronology cannot establish borrowing and cognate forms may share an older source. «Yehûd ve Nasârâ لاها derler»
