## BINTSHATI: ʿĀʾisha ʿAbd al-Raḥmān (Bint al-Shāṭiʾ), d. 1419 AH
[BINTSHATI:v2p75/r2] author | holds | The root sense of asr is pressing to extract juice, as in squeezing grapes; related words name the press, place of pressing, and stones used for it.
[BINTSHATI:v2p75/r3] author | holds | Arabic also calls rain-bearing clouds mu'sirat, says a people asaru when they bring rain, and uses i'sar for a strong wind driving clouds.
[BINTSHATI:v2p75/r4] author | holds | Arabic also uses asr metaphorically for confinement and extraction by pressure, and derives words for age and lineage from the image of pressing out an essence.
[BINTSHATI:v2p75/r5] author | holds | The age is called asr because experience and hardship press out a person's essence; late afternoon is also asr as the day is brought to its close, and the prayer takes its name from that time.
[BINTSHATI:v2p75/r6] author | holds | Quranic uses of the root include squeezing grapes in Yusuf 12:36 and rain clouds in al-Naba 78:14, alongside the verb in Yusuf 12:49. | mentions 12:36, 78:14, 12:49
[BINTSHATI:v2p76/r1] author | holds | The root also appears for a whirlwind in al-Baqara 2:266 and for asr in the surah itself. | mentions 2:266
[BINTSHATI:v2p76/r2] الطبري | prefers | Among the meanings of the oath, al-Tabari reports both the age and a particular time of day, then prefers the age.
[BINTSHATI:v2p76/r3] الرازي | reports | Al-Razi lists the afternoon prayer, understood with an omitted noun, and the age of Prophethood among the proposed meanings, without an explicit preference.
[BINTSHATI:v2p76/r4] الزمخشري | prefers | Al-Zamakhshari appears to prefer the afternoon prayer because of its merit and difficulty during the busy end of the workday; he cites the report comparing a missed prayer to losing one's family and property. | mentions 2:238
[BINTSHATI:v2p76/r5] محمد عبده | reports | Muhammad Abdu says Arabs gathered at afternoon to talk, so the oath corrects the belief that time itself is blameworthy when harmful conduct may occur then.
[BINTSHATI:v2p76/r6] ابن قيم الجوزية | prefers | Ibn Qayyim prefers reading asr as the age, as do most exegetes.
[BINTSHATI:v2p77/r1] author | holds | The oath's waw retains its basic linguistic force of magnifying what is sworn by; al-Tabari did not construct elaborate arguments for the greatness of asr.
[BINTSHATI:v2p77/r2] الرازي | reports | Al-Razi assembles six reasons for the greatness of the age, three for a particular time of day, six for the afternoon prayer, and a further case for the Prophetic age.
[BINTSHATI:v2p77/r3] محمد عبده | rejects | The author rejects Muhammad Abdu's claim that afternoon was the customary time for gatherings: evening may fit better, and objectionable talk is possible at any time.
[BINTSHATI:v2p77/r4] الرازي | reports | Al-Razi's reasons for magnifying the age include its extraordinary changes, the value of the final moment of life, and time's rank over space.
[BINTSHATI:v2p77/r5] author | prefers | The author follows al-Tabari, Ibn Qayyim, and most exegetes in reading asr as the age, and confines discussion to the arguments offered for that meaning.
[BINTSHATI:v2p78/r1] الرازي | reports | Al-Razi calls time a wonder encompassing fortune and hardship, health and illness, and wealth and poverty, while resisting simple judgments that it either exists or does not.
[BINTSHATI:v2p78/r2] الرازي | reports | Al-Razi says even a final instant can secure eternal paradise after a wasted lifetime, making time one of the greatest blessings.
[BINTSHATI:v2p78/r3] الرازي | reports | Al-Razi regards time as nobler than space and says the oath answers people who blame the age for calamities: the fault lies in the human being, not time.
[BINTSHATI:v2p78/r5] ابن القيم | reports | Ibn Qayyim sees the ordered alternation of night and day, their changing light and temperature, and time divisions as signs of God's power and wisdom; the oath links human actions in time to their judgment.
[BINTSHATI:v2p78/r6] النيسابوري | reports | Al-Nisaburi compares evening to the world's destruction and morning to resurrection, urging people to use their remaining lifespan for a trade without loss.
[BINTSHATI:v2p79/r1] النيسابوري | reports | Al-Nisaburi says the world's remaining life is like the time between afternoon and sunset, so people should hasten a trade without loss.
[BINTSHATI:v2p79/r2] author | reports | The author cites the prophetic report against blaming time, since God is al-dahr.
[BINTSHATI:v2p79/r3] author | reports | A report describes time as strangely both existent and like nothing, and compares a person's lifetime to a small part of it.
[BINTSHATI:v2p79/r4] author | rejects | The author rejects loading asr with philosophical and mystical interpretations; the Quran explains signs of night and day in accessible terms, as in al-Qasas 28:71-72. | mentions 28:71, 28:72
[BINTSHATI:v2p80/r1] الرازي | reports | Al-Razi suggests the Quran chose asr instead of dahr because unbelievers were fond of invoking and exalting the latter word.
[BINTSHATI:v2p80/r2] النيسابوري | reports | Al-Nisaburi explains the oath as honoring the age and cites the report that people should not curse time because God is al-dahr.
[BINTSHATI:v2p80/r4] author | holds | The oath's waw moves from a perceptible reality to a moral truth, a pattern the author also discusses in other surahs. | mentions 93:1, 100:1, 79:1

## CORPUSCORANICUM: Nicolai Sinai (Frühmekkanische Suren), with Nora K. Schmid, using preparatory work by Angelika Neuwirth; later parts Dirk Hartwig, Angelika Neuwirth et al. (author line per sura in segments)
[CORPUSCORANICUM:103:1-3:kommentar/r1] author | holds | The three-rhyme sura is an opening single-member oath by late afternoon, followed by a rebuke; verse 3 exempts believers and differentiates good from bad people.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:103:1-3:kommentar/r2] Neuwirth | reports | Neuwirth argues that oaths by prayer times preserve the recipient's liturgical experience and give the sura a hymn-like tone.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:103:1-3:kommentar/r3] author | rejects | The commentator finds no hymn-like resonance in this sura and points to a critical discussion of Neuwirth's hypothesis elsewhere. | mentions 93:1, 93:2
[CORPUSCORANICUM:103:1-3:kommentar/r4] Robinson | prefers | Robinson's secular reading makes ʿaṣr the time when merchants count a day's earnings, linking the oath's image of profit to the eschatological loss in verse 2.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:103:1-3:kommentar/r6] Neuwirth | reports | A prayer-time meaning for afternoon nevertheless remains possible.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:103:1:anmerkung/r1] author | reports | Arabic lexica offer competing senses for ʿaṣr: time or period, a time of day, morning, late afternoon or evening; the dual can mean day and night.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:103:1:anmerkung/r2] author | prefers | Since other opening oaths name specific daytimes or night phases, ʿaṣr probably denotes a particular time rather than time in general. | mentions 74:33, 81:17, 89:1, 89:4, 91:1, 91:4, 92:1, 93:1, 93:2
[CORPUSCORANICUM:103:1:anmerkung/r3] Cuypers | reports | Cuypers reads ʿaṣr as a general span of time, though the commentator notes that he gives no further justification.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:103:1:anmerkung/r4] author | holds | If ʿaṣr means late afternoon, this oath differs from all other early Meccan oaths naming night or stages of morning and forenoon.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:103:1:anmerkung/r5] author | holds | Qur'anic oaths likely draw on a characteristic pre-Islamic seer expression, a relationship that has not yet been adequately studied. | mentions 100:1, 100:2, 100:3, 100:4, 100:5
[CORPUSCORANICUM:103:1:anmerkung/r6] Spitaler | reports | Medina II joins the oath verse to verse 2 in its verse division. | mentions 103:2
[CORPUSCORANICUM:103:1:anmerkung/r7] Neuwirth | prefers | Neuwirth treats the oath as an independent verse, in keeping with other sura-opening oaths.

## ISLAHI-TADABBUR: Amin Ahsan Islahi (d. 1997)
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p433/r1] author | holds | Al-ʿAṣr follows al-Takāthur’s warning against wasting life amassing wealth; people would have used life wisely if they knew they must answer for this great blessing.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p433/r2] author | holds | This sūrah explains life’s real value and the factors that make it a path to salvation or a cause of eternal punishment.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p433/r3] author | holds | The oath points to a lifespan as a person’s real investment: using it well earns God’s favor, while wasting it brings Hell; time can serve either the person or Satan.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p433/r4] author | holds | Only the present is available to shape; the past cannot return and the future is hidden, with its own demands.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p434/r1] author | holds | A person cannot defer a present obligation to an uncertain future.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p435/r1] author | holds | The opening particle wa means that time bears witness, and ʿaṣr means a period of time.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p435/r2] Ḥam īd al-Dīn Far āhī | reports | Farāhī distinguishes ʿaṣr from dahr: ʿaṣr emphasizes time moving swiftly forward and is commonly used for time that has passed.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p435/r3] author | reports | Imruʾ al-Qays’s verse uses ʿaṣr for a past age, asking whether those who lived then now enjoy good fortune.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p435/r4] author | reports | ʿUbayd ibn al-Abras describes an ʿaṣr when he saw himself riding a young, beautiful she-camel.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p436/r1] Im ām Far āhī | reports | Farāhī says ʿaṣr evokes both past events and their swift passage, prompting belief in divine recompense and diligence in using time.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p436/r2] author | holds | The oath recalls historical examples of divine reward and punishment and urges people to use fleeting moments to gain an eternal kingdom rather than a curse.
