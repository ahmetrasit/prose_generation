## TABRISI: al-Ṭabrisī, d. 548 AH
[TABRISI:16:66-67/r10] قتادة | reports | Qatāda says the verse was revealed before wine was prohibited and that its prohibition came later in Sūrat al-Māʾida.
[TABRISI:16:66-67/r11] أبو مسلم | rejects | Abū Muslim says there is no need to place the verse before the prohibition: it addresses polytheists and counts their blessings, including wine as one of their drinks.
[TABRISI:16:66-67/r12] الشعبي | reports | Al-Shaʿbī and al-Jubbāʾī interpret ‘intoxicant’ as permissible kinds of drink and ‘good provision’ as food, the pleasant things people eat.
[TABRISI:16:66-67/r13] الجبائي | reports | Al-Shaʿbī and al-Jubbāʾī interpret ‘intoxicant’ as permissible kinds of drink and ‘good provision’ as food, the pleasant things people eat.
[TABRISI:16:66-67/r14] author | rejects | The verse does not authorize nabīdh: it reports what people did with the fruits, just as another verse reports making oaths a means of deception; describing a use does not license it. | mentions 16:92
[TABRISI:16:66-67/r15] author | holds | God created these fruits for people’s benefit, but people also made forbidden things from them.
[TABRISI:16:66-67/r5] الحسن | reports | One construction means that people have a lesson in what God produced from the fruits of palms and grapes.
[TABRISI:16:66-67/r6] author | reports | Another construction supplies the omitted relative ‘what’: from the fruits of palms and grapes, you make what becomes an intoxicant; Arabs often omit the relative pronoun. | mentions 83:23
[TABRISI:16:66-67/r7] author | reports | A further construction supplies an omitted noun: ‘something’ from the fruits of palms and grapes that you make into an intoxicant.
[TABRISI:16:66-67/r8] author | holds | The intoxicant is wine or any intoxicating drink, while good provision is lawful products such as vinegar, raisins, syrup, fresh dates, and dried dates, according to several early authorities.
[TABRISI:16:66-67/r9] ابن عباس | reports | In a report preserved by al-Ḥākim, Ibn ʿAbbās says ‘intoxicant’ is what is forbidden from the fruits and ‘good provision’ what is lawful.
[TABRISI:16:66-70#2/r2] author | reports | In ‘from it you make an intoxicant,’ the pronoun can refer to the fruits already mentioned or to thamarāt as a collective equivalent to thamar.
[TABRISI:16:66-70/r7] author | reports | Sukr has four senses: an intoxicating drink; food or taste, as in the poet’s line about making nobles’ faults a taste; stillness, as in a quiet night or wind; and the verbal noun of sakira, including confusion of sight. | mentions 15:15
[TABRISI:16:66-70/r8] author | holds | Dhulul is the plural of dhalul, while radhil and radhāl mean low or poor quality.
[TABRISI:16:67-69/r1] author | holds | The variety of useful fruit products is a sign for people who reason; God’s extraction of juice from mixed fruit points to His ability to recover the dispersed dead from soil.

## IBNJAWZI-ZAD: Ibn al-Jawzī, d. 597 AH
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v4p464/r1] author | holds | The construction omits “something”: from date palms and grapes you take a thing for drink and good provision; minhu refers to the omitted noun.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v4p464/r2] الأخفش | reports | Al-Akhfash says the pronoun is singular because an understood “thing” is omitted; it therefore need not be dual.

[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v4p464/r7] أبو عبيدة | reports | Abu Ubayda interprets sukar as taste or flavor, supporting the usage with poetry.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v4p464/r8] author | reports | If sukar means vinegar or flavor, the verse remains operative rather than abrogated.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v4p464/r9] author | holds | Good provision means lawful products such as dates, grapes, raisins, and vinegar.

## RAZI-FULL: Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, d. 606 AH
[RAZI-FULL:v20p55#2/r1] author | reports | To explain wine's mention as a blessing, one answer says the sura is Meccan and wine was not forbidden until the Ma'ida revelation.
[RAZI-FULL:v20p55#2/r2] author | prefers | Another answer needs no abrogation: wine was useful to the polytheist audience, but separating السكر from good provision signals that it is not good in religious law and is therefore forbidden.
[RAZI-FULL:v20p55#2/r3] أبو حنيفة | reports | A second interpretation takes sugar as nabīdh: grape, raisin, or date juice boiled until two-thirds evaporates and left to ferment; Abu Hanifa permits it until it intoxicates.
[RAZI-FULL:v20p55#2/r4] أبو حنيفة | reports | This view argues that the verse presents sugar as a blessing and that the report wine is forbidden in itself means sugar is something other than wine, namely cooked nabīdh.
[RAZI-FULL:v20p55#2/r5] أبو عبيدة | reports | A third interpretation takes السكر as food; Abu Ubayda supports it with a poetic line where slandering the honorable is made one's food.
[RAZI-FULL:v20p55#2/r6] الزجاج | reports | Al-Zajjaj says the poetic usage better resembles wine: the speaker is metaphorically intoxicated by slandering honorable people.
[RAZI-FULL:v20p55#2/r7] author | holds | The production of these benefits is evidence and blessing; a rational person recognizes that only God can bring about these states and infers a powerful, wise deity.
[RAZI-FULL:v20p55/r3] author | holds | After animal benefits, the passage turns to benefits from plants.
[RAZI-FULL:v20p55/r4] author | holds | The phrase from dates and grapes attaches to an omitted نسقيكم, meaning their juice, which is omitted because the preceding verb supplies it; تتخذون منه سكرًا explains the drink.
[RAZI-FULL:v20p55/r5] الواحدي | reports | Al-Wahidi says الأعناب coordinates with الثمرات, not النخيل, since grapes themselves are fruit and do not produce another fruit.
[RAZI-FULL:v20p55/r6] author | reports | One interpretation takes السكر as wine, a verbal noun of sakira; رزق حسن means other date and grape products such as syrup, vinegar, molasses, dates, and raisins.
