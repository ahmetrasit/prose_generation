## STUDYQURAN: Seyyed Hossein Nasr (ed.-in-chief), Caner K. Dagli, Maria Massi Dakake, Joseph E. B. Lumbard, Mohammed Rustom
[STUDYQURAN:22:11/r1] Ṭ | reports | “Upon a brink” can mean worshipping in a state of doubt.
[STUDYQURAN:22:11/r2] R | reports | The phrase can describe superficial belief located at the edge of consciousness rather than in the heart.
[STUDYQURAN:22:11/r3] al-Ṭabarī | reports | Al-Ṭabarī applies the verse to opportunistic Bedouins in the Prophet's time: they embraced Islam for material gain, affirmed him when wealthy with sons, then reverted when hardship and daughters came.
[STUDYQURAN:22:11/r4] Ṭ | reports | Others read the verse as a general description of hypocrites whose religion serves their superficial self-interest.
[STUDYQURAN:22:11/r5] R | reports | Those on the brink are toppled from faith by the slightest discomfort.
[STUDYQURAN:22:11/r6] Ṭ | reports | They lose this world when expected material goods fail to arrive and lose the Hereafter through neglect of faith's true nature and rewards.
[STUDYQURAN:22:11/r7] the Prophet | reports | When a man complained that his new religion had brought him no good, the Prophet replied that Islam is tested and refined like metals in fire.
[STUDYQURAN:22:intro/r1] IK | reports | One dating view holds that the surah is Madinan except verses 52–55, which are Makkan.
[STUDYQURAN:22:intro/r2] Q | reports | Another dating view holds that the surah is Makkan except verses 19–22, which are Madinan.
[STUDYQURAN:22:intro/r3] Q | reports | Most authorities regard the surah as containing both Makkan and Madinan verses.
[STUDYQURAN:22:intro/r4] Q | reports | A tradition calls the surah remarkable because it came at night and by day, on journeys and at home, in war and peace, and includes abrogating and abrogated as well as clear and allegorical verses.
[STUDYQURAN:22:intro/r7] author | holds | The passage contrasts the misguided and righteous through a garden parable and their respective afterlife reward or punishment.
