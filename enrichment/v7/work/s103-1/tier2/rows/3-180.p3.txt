## RAZI-FULL: Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, d. 606 AH
[RAZI-FULL:v9p91#2/r1] author | holds | The passage shifts from urging self-sacrifice in jihad to urging financial sacrifice and warning those who withhold wealth in God's cause.
[RAZI-FULL:v9p91#2/r2] الزجاج | reports | For Hamza's second-person reading, the construction means do not consider the stinginess of the stingy good for them; a noun for stinginess is elided because the verb supplies it.
[RAZI-FULL:v9p91#2/r3] author | reports | With the other readers' third-person form, the subject may be the Prophet or anyone in general, or the stingy themselves with the object their stinginess omitted.
[RAZI-FULL:v9p91#2/r4] author | holds | The omitted object is licensed by context, as in the saying about lying being bad and the phrase about a fool running toward folly; al-Farra's verse uses به for kingship, inferable from kings.
[RAZI-FULL:v9p91#2/r5] author | holds | Basrans call هو a separating pronoun and Kufans a support; it marks the added relation in which the subject is predicated of the predicate.
[RAZI-FULL:v9p91#2/r6] author | holds | The condemned stinginess can concern any good or benefit, including either wealth or knowledge.
[RAZI-FULL:v9p92#2/r1] أكثر العلماء | reports | Most scholars define stinginess as withholding what is obligatory, not declining voluntary giving: the severe threat and blame fit neglected duties, not optional generosity. | mentions 2:3, 2:5
[RAZI-FULL:v9p92#2/r2] author | holds | Other proofs include that God cannot give without limit, so refusing any extra favor cannot make Him stingy; a hadith calls stinginess a dreadful disease, and withholding voluntary gifts would not warrant that label.
[RAZI-FULL:v9p92#2/r3] author | holds | If every refusal of a voluntary gift were stinginess, even the owner of all wealth could escape the charge only by giving everything; 2:3 praises spending only part of God's provision and 2:5 calls such people guided and successful. | mentions 2:3, 2:5
[RAZI-FULL:v9p92#2/r4] author | holds | Obligatory spending includes one's own needs and dependents, zakat, defense when Muslims face an attack on lives or property, and enough aid to keep a desperate person alive; withholding these is stinginess.
[RAZI-FULL:v9p92/r1] author | reports | One view applies the warning to withholding wealth: the withheld money will become punishment, while the money itself will not remain with them because God inherits all.
[RAZI-FULL:v9p92/r2] author | reports | A second view applies it to withholding knowledge: Jews concealed Muhammad's description and the glad tidings of his coming in the Torah and Gospel, although knowledge is God's gift. | mentions 4:113
[RAZI-FULL:v9p92/r3] author | prefers | Razi prefers wealth: the collar threat fits money literally, whereas knowledge requires metaphor; reading wealth also continues the preceding exhortation to spend in jihad, unlike the knowledge reading.
[RAZI-FULL:v9p93#2/r1] author | reports | If stinginess means concealing knowledge, the collar is a fiery bridle on the mouths and tongues that failed to speak the truth; a prophetic report threatens such a bridle for concealing known knowledge.
[RAZI-FULL:v9p93#2/r2] author | holds | The knowledge interpretation is plausible because the Quran also condemns Jewish stinginess, and the next verse cites a Jewish statement; the warning may cover withholding both knowledge and wealth. | mentions 4:53, 4:37, 3:181
