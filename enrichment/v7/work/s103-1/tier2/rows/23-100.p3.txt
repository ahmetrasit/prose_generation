## BURSEVI: İsmāʿīl Ḥaqqī Bursevī, d. 1127 AH
[BURSEVI:v6p74#3/r4] author | holds | He imagines being asked whether he wants to return to gather wealth, plant, build, or dig canals, then says he wants to do righteous work with the faith he left behind.
[BURSEVI:v6p74#3/r5] author | holds | He does not frame faith itself as a hope because he presents it as definite, while righteous action is what he hopes to perform.
[BURSEVI:v6p74#3/r6] الجلالين | reports | Al-Jalalayn interprets the hoped-for righteous work as testifying to God's oneness, which he left undone in worldly life.
[BURSEVI:v6p74#3/r7] بعضهم | reports | One view addresses the Angel of Death and his helpers; the mention of Lord is an oath, and the man appeals first to God and then to the angels.
[BURSEVI:v6p74#3/r8] النبي عليه السلام | reports | A report says a believer who sees the angels declines return to the world and welcomes going to God, whereas an unbeliever asks to be returned.
[BURSEVI:v6p74#3/r9] الكبير | prefers | Al-Kabir considers فيما تركت broader: it includes neglected worship and both bodily and financial duties or rights.
[BURSEVI:v6p75#3/r1] author | holds | The barzakh lasts until resurrection and wholly rules out returning to worldly life; the return at resurrection is to the afterlife.

## ALUSI-FULL: al-Ālūsī, d. 1270 AH
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63#2/r1] author | reports | ‘In what I left behind’ may mean abandoned faith; laʿalla may express hope about both work and faith despite knowing return will not happen, or only work because faith on return is certain.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63#2/r10] author | rejects | Al-Ālūsī rejects the claim that the dying person says kallā to himself; kallā and what follows are God’s speech.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63#2/r11] author | holds | Warāʾahum means ‘before them’; the pronoun refers to one dying person in wording but to all in meaning.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63#2/r12] author | holds | Barzakh is a barrier between the dead and return, lasting until they are raised.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63#2/r2] author | reports | Alternatively, what was left is wealth or worldly life; laʿalla may also express purpose.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63#2/r3] البغوي عن الواقدي | reports | Al-Baghawī cites al-Wāqidī that every Qur’anic laʿalla signals purpose except ‘perhaps you will live forever’ (26:129); al-Suddī’s route from Abū Mālik says similarly. | mentions 26:129
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63#2/r4] ابن عباس | reports | The wish to return is not confined to unbelievers: Ibn ʿAbbās says one who withheld zakāt or could afford pilgrimage but did not do it asks to return at death.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63#2/r5] جابر بن عبد الله | reports | A Prophetic report through al-Daylamī from Jābir says at death things obstructing truth appear before a person’s eyes, prompting the plea to return; this supports reading what was left as wealth.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63#2/r6] author | holds | Kallā rebukes and dismisses the request to return as impossible.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63#2/r7] author | holds | ‘It is a word he says’ means the dying person inevitably utters it in regret; fronting the subject gives emphasis or exclusivity.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63#2/r8] author | holds | The lone-word phrase depicts futile speech receiving no answer, like the retort to occupy oneself alone with pointless speech; kalimah means speech.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63#2/r9] author | reports | Grammarians treat kalimah meaning ‘speech’ as figurative, while lexicographers consider it literal or familiar metaphor.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p64/r1] author | holds | The barzakh lasts from the graves until resurrection, making worldly return impossible like a camel passing through a needle’s eye. | mentions 7:40
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p64/r2] ابن زيد | reports | Ibn Zayd defines the barzakh as a barrier between death and resurrection, spanning the wait before people rise from graves.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p64/r3] author | reports | A further view makes the barzakh a barrier to complete requital that remains until Judgment Day, when full recompense comes.

## IBNASHUR-FULL: Ibn ʿĀshūr, d. 1393 AH
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v18p122/r7] author | holds | The clause of hope gives the reason for the request to be returned.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v18p122/r8] author | reports | Tark may retain its literal sense of leaving: what the speaker left behind is the world.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v18p122/r9] author | reports | Alternatively, figurative rejection means the faith in God and His messenger that the unbeliever refused; the plea promises obedience and admits past error.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v18p123/r1] author | holds | Kallā rebukes the listener and signals that the unbeliever’s request is invalid.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v18p123/r2] author | holds | The clause that it is merely a word is a Quranic, proverbial construction: the request is only speech and will not be answered.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v18p123/r3] author | holds | Since it is obvious he says the word, the description means it has no quality beyond being uttered; inna emphasizes that point.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v18p123/r4] author | holds | Kalima means utterance, as in the Prophetic report quoting Labīd and in the formulas for the testimony and Islam; 9:74 uses it similarly. | mentions 9:74
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v18p123/r5] author | holds | Warāʾ is a metaphor for something inevitably approaching and reaching someone unaware, like a pursuer catching up; examples occur in 85:20, 45:10, 14:17, and 18:79. | mentions 85:20, 45:10, 14:17, 18:79
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v18p123/r6] لبيد | reports | Labīd’s verse uses warāʾ for what lies ahead as one’s death approaches.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v18p123/r7] author | holds | Barzakh means a barrier between two places; reported interpretations are the grave, the remaining span of worldly life, or an intermediate realm where souls see their future station, favored by Sufis.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v18p123/r8] السيد | reports | Al-Sayyid’s Definitions calls barzakh the perceptible realm between abstract meanings and material bodies, or the imaginal world in ancient philosophy.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v18p124/r1] author | holds | Until they are raised means the dead will not return to life before resurrection, extinguishing their hope because they know resurrection brings no return to worldly life.

## TABATABAI: Muḥammad Ḥusayn al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, d. 1402 AH
[TABATABAI:23:99-118#5/r1] أبو عبد الله | reports | Al-Qummi reports from al-Sadiq that barzakh is an intermediate state of reward and punishment between worldly life and the hereafter, and that the Imam fears for people concerning barzakh.
[TABATABAI:23:99-118#5/r2] علي بن الحسين | reports | Al-Sajjad says a grave is either a garden from the gardens of Paradise or a pit from the pits of Fire.
[TABATABAI:23:99-118#5/r3] أبو عبد الله | reports | Al-Sadiq rejects the claim that believers' spirits are inside green birds around the Throne: believers are too honored for this and dwell in bodies like their own.
[TABATABAI:23:99-118#5/r4] أبو عبد الله | reports | One report says believers' spirits dwell in a tree in Paradise, eating and drinking while asking God to hasten the Hour and join later believers with earlier ones.
[TABATABAI:23:99-118#5/r5] أبو عبد الله | reports | Another report places spirits in body-like forms in a Paradise tree, where they recognize one another and ask about those still living or dead.
[TABATABAI:23:99-118#5/r6] author | holds | Reports detailing barzakh and the conditions of believers and others there are numerous and mutawatir, with some already discussed in earlier sections.
[TABATABAI:23:99-118/r1] author | holds | “Behind them” means ahead and surrounding them: the future is said to be behind a person because it approaches and overtakes him. | mentions 55:20, 18:79
[TABATABAI:23:99-118/r2] author | holds | The barzakh is the grave realm, an imaginal life after death until Resurrection, supported by the context, other verses, and abundant Shiite and Sunni reports.
[TABATABAI:23:99-118/r3] بعضهم | rejects | Some say the barrier merely prevents return to worldly life until Resurrection; al-Tabataba'i rejects this because the context places the barrier between death and Resurrection and the time limit would be pointless.
