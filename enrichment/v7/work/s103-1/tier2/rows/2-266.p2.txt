## TABRISI: al-Ṭabrisī, d. 548 AH
[TABRISI:2:266#2/r1] author | holds | مِن in من نخيل explains the garden's type; the clause about running rivers can modify either the garden or the date palms, depending on the pronoun's antecedent.
[TABRISI:2:266#2/r2] author | holds | The parable imagines an orchard of date palms and grapes, with water and every fruit, possessed by an aging person with weak, young children.
[TABRISI:2:266#2/r3] author | holds | إعصار is a powerful, upward-spinning wind column, commonly called a whirlwind; fire in it burns the garden.
[TABRISI:2:266#2/r4] السدي | reports | Al-Suddi sees the ruined garden as the show-off in spending: he enjoys its benefit now but loses it when he most needs the reward in the hereafter.
[TABRISI:2:266#2/r5] مجاهد | reports | Mujahid reads the garden as the person who neglects obedience for worldly pleasures and thus faces especially bitter regret when he needs righteous deeds in the hereafter.
[TABRISI:2:266#2/r6] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas interprets it as someone whose work ends in corruption; the author says all three interpretations fit the verse.
[TABRISI:2:266#2/r7] author | holds | The explanation points back to charity, Abraham's story, the passerby at the ruined village, and all prior disclosures; God sets out religious proofs so people will reflect and understand. | mentions 2:258, 2:259
