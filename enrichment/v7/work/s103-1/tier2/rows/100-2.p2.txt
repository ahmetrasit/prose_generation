## SAMIN-DURR: al-Samīn al-Ḥalabī, d. 756 AH
[SAMIN-DURR:100:2/r1] author | reports | ‘Qadhan’ may be a verbal noun reinforcing the sparks made by striking, or a circumstantial describing horses striking stones with their hooves to make fire.
[SAMIN-DURR:100:2/r2] الزمخشري | reports | Al-Zamakhshari says ‘qadhan’ is governed as ‘dabḥan’; he allows an omitted verb, the preceding active participle, or a circumstantial as its governor.
[SAMIN-DURR:100:2/r3] author | holds | The fire produced by hooves is called ‘nar al-habahib’; a poem uses that name for the sparks from struck blades.
[SAMIN-DURR:100:3/r3] author | reports | One view takes all the racing, sparking, and raiding descriptions as horses; their descriptions are coordinated in sequence as in a cited poetic example.
[SAMIN-DURR:100:3/r4] أمير المؤمنين | reports | A view attributed to the Commander of the Faithful takes the descriptions as camels running from ‘Arafa to Muzdalifa and Mina; a poem by Safiyya bint ‘Abd al-Muttalib supports this interpretation.
[SAMIN-DURR:100:3/r5] author | reports | Some interpret ‘al-muriyat’ as a group scheming in war; the idiom ‘I will light you up’ means ‘I will plot against you’. 

## IBNKATHIR-FULL: Ibn Kathīr, d. 774 AH
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p465#2/r1] author | holds | Ibn Kathir understands the oath as horses running in God's cause: their hooves strike stone and make sparks, and their morning raids raise dust across a battlefield before they enter the enemy ranks.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p465#2/r4] عبد الله بن مسعود | reports | Ibn Masud interprets the panting runners as camels; Ali also says they are camels.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p465#2/r5] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas identifies the runners as horses; when Ali objected that no horses were at Badr, Ibn Abbas clarified that he meant a military detachment.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p465#2/r6] علي | reports | In Ibn Abbas's account, Ali argues that the runners were camels traveling from Arafat to Muzdalifah and Mina; Ibn Abbas withdraws his earlier view.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p466#2/r4] رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | A report from Ibn Abbas says the Prophet sent horses on an expedition and heard no news for a month; the surah then revealed, and al-Bazzar calls the report very unusual.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p466/r1] ابن عباس | reports | Al-Awfi transmits Ibn Abbas's view that the oath refers to horses; many named Successors agree, and Ibn Jarir chooses this view over Ali's camel interpretation.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p466/r2] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas and Ata say only a horse or dog makes the panting sound called ضبح; Ibn Abbas imitates it as ah ah.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p466/r3] أكثر هؤلاء | reports | Most who interpret the runners as horses say the sparks come from their hooves; Qatadah says they inflame warfare among the riders.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p466/r4] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas and Mujahid also interpret the sparks as men's stratagems; others say the phrase describes night fires, tribal fires, or fires lit at Muzdalifah.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p466/r5] ابن جرير | prefers | Ibn Jarir prefers that horses make sparks by striking stone with their hooves.

[BURSEVI:v10p383/r3] author | reports | Grammatically, dabh may be an accusative of the implied verb as a circumstantial phrase, a verbal noun governed by al-‘adiyat, or a circumstantial noun meaning “panting.”
[BURSEVI:v10p383/r4] author | holds | The horses strike stony ground with their hooves and shoes, producing sparks; the image uses qadh, striking, for striking stones, and this follows the running whereas panting accompanies it.
