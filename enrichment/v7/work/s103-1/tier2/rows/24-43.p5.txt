## IBNKATHIR-FULL: Ibn Kathīr, d. 774 AH
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v6p72#2/r1] author | holds | God first gently drives newly formed clouds, gathers their scattered parts, piles them atop one another, and sends rain through them.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v6p72#2/r2] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Ḍaḥḥāk read the phrase as rain emerging through the cloud's openings.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v6p72#2/r3] عبيد بن عمير الليثي | reports | ʿUbayd ibn ʿUmayr describes winds that sweep the earth, raise clouds, assemble them, and fertilize them with rain-bearing water; Ibn Abī Ḥātim and al-Ṭabarī transmit this account.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v6p72#2/r4] author | reports | Grammarians parse the three min particles as origin, partitive, and explanatory if hail comes from mountain-like formations in the sky; on the cloud interpretation, the second min is also origin and apposition to the first.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v6p73/r1] author | reports | The hail and rain may strike as mercy and be withheld as delayed rain; alternatively, hail may punish by scattering fruit and destroying crops and trees, while its being withheld is mercy.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v6p73/r2] author | holds | The lightning's intense light nearly snatches sight when someone follows or looks directly at it.

## BIQAI-FULL: al-Biqāʿī, d. 885 AH
[BIQAI-FULL:v5p272/r2] أبو حيان | reports | Abu Hayyan says izjāʾ is used for gently driving a heavy thing; sḥāb is a generic noun whose singular is saḥāba, and the phrase means driving one cloud toward another.
[BIQAI-FULL:v5p272/r3] author | holds | God creates clouds from nothing, sometimes low and sometimes high, initially thin and scattered, then joins their parts and piles them into a massive mass.
[BIQAI-FULL:v5p272/r4] القزاز | reports | Al-Qazzaz explains al-wadq as rain, while another view takes it as the abundance of rain.
[BIQAI-FULL:v5p272/r5] author | holds | Rain emerges through openings formed in the piled cloud as its parts press and condense against one another.
[BIQAI-FULL:v5p272/r6] author | holds | The mountains in the sky are clouds amassed until they resemble mountains; hail is frozen water, and God sends or withholds rain and hail by choice as mercy or punishment.
[BIQAI-FULL:v5p272/r7] author | holds | Lightning nearly blinds with its brilliant flashes; its strength signals dense clouds and heavy rain and warns of possible lightning strikes.
[BIQAI-FULL:v5p272/r8] author | holds | God alternates night and day, light and darkness, increase and decrease, rain and clear skies, producing heat, cold, growth, and dryness; these changes show power, comprehensive knowledge, and oneness to reflective observers.

## DURR-FULL: al-Suyūṭī, d. 911 AH
[DURR-FULL:v6p211/r1] الضحاك | reports | Al-Dahhak glosses al-wadaq as rain; Mujahid glosses it as drops of rain, while Abu Bujayla’s father says it is lightning.
[DURR-FULL:v6p211/r2] ابن زيد | reports | Ibn Zayd says ‘through it’ means through the cloud; Ibn Abbas read the phrase with an open khāʾ and no alif.
[DURR-FULL:v6p211/r3] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas reads lightning’s sana as its light; Nafiʿ ibn al-Azraq asks about this meaning, which Ibn Abbas supports with a line by Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith witnessing the word sanāh.
[DURR-FULL:v6p211/r4] قتادة | reports | Qatada glosses the lightning’s sana as its flashing brightness.
[DURR-FULL:v6p211/r5] كعب | reports | Kaʿb says hail falling from the fourth heaven would destroy anything it passed; he also told ʿAbd Allah ibn ʿAmr that lightning is what precedes hail.

## BURSEVI: İsmāʿīl Ḥaqqī Bursevī, d. 1127 AH
[BURSEVI:v6p119#2/r3] author | holds | يزجي means gently and easily driving something along, especially an insignificant thing; this hints that clouds are negligible relative to God's power. Clouds are named for their movement through air, and here the plural parts are intended.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#2/r4] author | holds | The verse describes seeing God visually guide clouds wherever He wills.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#3/r1] author | holds | God joins separate cloud portions into one, then piles them on top of one another; ركام means a gathered mass.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#3/r2] أبو الليث | reports | Abu al-Layth defines الودق as all rain, whether heavy or light.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#3/r3] المفردات | reports | Al-Mufradat reports an alternative definition of الودق as the dust-like material visible among raindrops, also used for rain itself.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#3/r4] author | holds | The gap phrase is circumstantial with visual seeing; cloud openings are the spaces between things and here are passages through which drops emerge.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#3/r5] author | holds | The rain comes out from the cloud's gaps and openings created by the accumulation and compression of its parts.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#3/r6] كعب | reports | Ka'b says clouds act as a sieve for rain; without them rain would ruin what it struck.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#3/r7] author | holds | Here السماء means the clouds, since anything above you is called sky, and the sky is the highest part of anything.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#4/r1] author | holds | The hail comes from immense chunks resembling mountains, located in the sky; sky is grammatically feminine here.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#4/r2] author | holds | The preposition من before hail marks the object as partitive, while the earlier instances mark source and the second is an appositive relation; hail is frozen water.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#4/r3] بعضهم | reports | One view says God made mountains of ice and snow in the sky and assigned an angel to release measured hail or snow onto a region by His command.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#4/r4] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas says every spring running on earth ultimately comes from snow and ice.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#4/r5] قيل | reports | Another report says angels are half ice and half fire; by flapping their wings they send down the snow that falls from them.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#4/r6] قيل | reports | An alternative takes the sky as the encompassing celestial sphere containing mountains of hail, like the stone mountains on earth; reason does not rule it out.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#4/r7] المشهور | reports | The familiar natural account says rising vapor forms cloud in cold air: moderate cold yields rain, stronger cold yields hail before vapor joins, and extreme cold can condense air into cloud and rain or snow; all remains governed by divine will and wisdom.
[BURSEVI:v6p119#4/r8] إخوان الصفاء | reports | Ikhwan al-Safa explain that fine cloud differs from dense cloud; rain falls when water particles join, cool, and grow heavy, hail freezes after leaving cloud, and snow freezes within it.
[BURSEVI:v6p120/r1] ابن التمجيد | reports | Ibn al-Tamjid says sunlight on dry ground breaks off fiery particles that combine with earthy particles to form smoke.
[BURSEVI:v6p120/r2] شرح القانون | reports | Sharh al-Qanun distinguishes smoke, made from earth and fire, from vapor, made from water and air; vapor is finer.
[BURSEVI:v6p120/r3] author | holds | God directs hail toward whom He wills, potentially harming people, crops, livestock, and fruit, and protects others from its danger.
[BURSEVI:v6p120/r4] author | holds | سنا with a short final vowel means bright light, while the extended form means height; lightning is the cloud's flash.
[BURSEVI:v6p120/r5] القاموس | reports | Al-Qamus defines lightning either as a flash of cloud or as an angel striking and moving the cloud so that fires appear.
[BURSEVI:v6p120/r6] إخوان الصفاء | reports | Ikhwan al-Safa describe lightning as fire sparked by friction among smoky particles inside the cloud.
[BURSEVI:v6p120/r7] author | holds | The lightning nearly takes sight because of its extreme brightness and speed.
