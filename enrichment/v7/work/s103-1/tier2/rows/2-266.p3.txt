## BAYDAWI-FULL: al-Bayḍāwī, d. 685 AH
[BAYDAWI-FULL:2:266/r1] author | holds | The opening hamza is interrogative for repudiation; the garden is said to consist of palms and vines for their excellence and abundance, with all fruits indicating the other kinds or benefits too.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:2:266/r2] author | holds | The old age makes poverty and dependence more difficult, while weak offspring are too young to earn; the conjunction may be circumstantial or coordinated by sense.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:2:266/r3] author | holds | An i'sar is a violent whirlwind rising from earth toward the sky like a column; the garden's fiery destruction pictures the anguish of finding good deeds nullified by ostentation or harm.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:2:266/r4] author | holds | The parable also evokes someone who rises spiritually toward the divine realm, then turns back to worldly illusion and sees the effort scattered like dust.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:2:266/r5] author | holds | God explains the signs so people will think and draw a lesson from them.

## NASAFI-FULL: al-Nasafī, d. 710 AH
[NASAFI-FULL:v1p130#2/r1] author | holds | The interrogative hamza expresses denial of the prospect of such a loss.
[NASAFI-FULL:v1p130#2/r2] author | holds | The garden is described with dates and grapes because they are the noblest, most beneficial trees; all fruits are then added by generalization.
[NASAFI-FULL:v1p130#2/r3] author | holds | The conjunction before old age is circumstantial, as is the clause about weak offspring; the garden is the aged owner's livelihood and theirs.
[NASAFI-FULL:v1p130#2/r4] author | holds | An iʿṣār is a whirlwind that rotates over the ground and rises skyward like a column; fire is raised by the ظرف because the ظرف functions as an adjective of the whirlwind.
[NASAFI-FULL:v1p130#2/r5] author | holds | The garden's destruction depicts ostentatious good deeds found null at resurrection, causing the anguish of an elderly person whose weak children depend on the ruined garden.
[NASAFI-FULL:v1p130#2/r6] author | holds | As with the preceding explanation of monotheism and religion, the signs are clarified to prompt reflection and awaken people.

## QASHANI: ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Qāshānī, d. 736 AH
[QASHANI:2:266-267/r1] author | holds | The garden depicts any good deed, including charity, done for God's pleasure when the ego later emerges; conflicting ego motions become a whirlwind seized by Satan for whispering.
[QASHANI:2:266-267/r2] author | holds | Self-regard or ostentation is like fire that consumes the deed precisely when it is most needed.
[QASHANI:2:266-267/r3] أمير المؤمنين علي عليه السلام | reports | ʿAlī asks God to forgive acts of devotion whose intentions his heart later contradicted.
