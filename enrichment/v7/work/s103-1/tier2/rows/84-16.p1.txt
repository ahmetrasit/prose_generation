## SAMIN-DURR: al-Samīn al-Ḥalabī, d. 756 AH
[SAMIN-DURR:84:16/r1] الراغب | reports | Al-Raghib defines twilight as daylight mingling with night’s darkness at sunset; ishfaq combines care and fear, with ‘min’ emphasizing fear and ‘‘ala’ emphasizing care.
[SAMIN-DURR:84:16/r2] الزمخشري | reports | Al-Zamakhshari defines twilight as the redness in the west after sunset; its disappearance ends Maghrib and begins ‘Isha’ for most scholars, while Abu Hanifa reportedly once said it was the whiteness and later recanted according to Asad ibn ‘Amr.
[SAMIN-DURR:84:16/r3] author | holds | There are two twilights, red and white; shafaq and shafaqa are also names for ishfaq, and twilight’s name is linked to delicacy, as is compassion to a tender heart.
[SAMIN-DURR:84:16/r4] الشاعر | reports | The poem uses ‘shafaqan’ for fear or tenderness while speaking of loving a life and its death, witnessing the lexical relation to ishfaq.

## IBNKATHIR-FULL: Ibn Kathīr, d. 774 AH
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p358/r1] علي | reports | Ali, Ibn Abbas, Ubadah ibn al-Samit, Abu Hurayrah, and many others identify the twilight as redness.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p358/r2] أبو هريرة | reports | A report from Abu Hurayrah defines the twilight as whiteness.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p358/r3] author | holds | The twilight is the horizon's redness, said by Mujahid to occur before sunrise and conventionally by language scholars after sunset; al-Khalil places it through the evening prayer time.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p358/r4] الخليل بن أحمد | reports | Al-Khalil defines twilight as the redness from sunset until the time of the late evening prayer, after which it is said to have disappeared.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p358/r5] الجوهري | reports | Al-Jawhari describes twilight as the sun's remaining light and redness at the start of night until near deep evening.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p358/r6] رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | The Prophet says the time for Maghrib lasts until twilight disappears; Ibn Kathir takes this as evidence for al-Jawhari's and al-Khalil's definition.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p358/r7] مجاهد | reports | Mujahid also interprets twilight in this verse as the whole daytime, and in another report as the sun, because the next oath mentions night. | mentions 84:17
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p358/r8] ابن جرير | reports | Ibn Jarir reads the paired oaths as daytime departing and nighttime arriving. | mentions 84:17
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v8p358/r9] آخرون | reports | Other authorities say twilight names both redness and whiteness and is therefore a word with opposite senses.

## BURSEVI: İsmāʿīl Ḥaqqī Bursevī, d. 1127 AH
[BURSEVI:v10p294/r1] author | reports | Most scholars define shafaq as the red afterglow; when it vanishes, Maghrib ends and the time for Isha begins. Others define it as the white glow that follows, after which Isha begins.
[BURSEVI:v10p294/r2] author | reports | The white-twilight view is one report from Abu Hanifa, though he is said to have returned to the majority view; the name suits both meanings because of their delicacy, especially the white light as the Sun’s glow weakens.
[BURSEVI:v10p294/r3] عكرمة ومجاهد | reports | Ikrima and Mujahid interpret shafaq as daytime, understood as the Sun’s trace, so the oath covers night and day, the periods of rest and work that sustain the world.
[BURSEVI:v10p294/r4] الراغب | reports | Al-Raghib defines twilight as daylight mixing with the darkness of night at sunset.
[BURSEVI:v10p294/r5] القاشاني | reports | Al-Qashani reads twilight as the remaining light of human primordial nature after it sets into the body and becomes mixed with the darkness of the self; this light still permits spiritual perfection.
