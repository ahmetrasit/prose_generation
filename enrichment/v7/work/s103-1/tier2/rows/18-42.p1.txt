## TABRISI: al-Ṭabrisī, d. 548 AH
[TABRISI:18:39-42/r6] author | holds | The garden's destruction means its trees and palms were ruined completely; the report says God sent fire and the water disappeared.
[TABRISI:18:39-42/r7] ابن عباس | reports | Turning the palms over each other expresses grief and regret; Ibn Abbas explains it as striking one hand against the other over the money spent.
[TABRISI:18:39-42/r8] author | reports | The garden lying fallen on its trellises means its roof collapsed first and its walls then fell over it; another gloss takes the structures as empty houses with trees gone.
[TABRISI:18:39-42/r9] author | reports | One view says the owner regretted his associationism only because his wealth vanished and gained nothing; another says he truly repented of shirk and believed.

## IBNJAWZI-ZAD: Ibn al-Jawzī, d. 597 AH
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p146/r1] author | holds | God's punishment encompassed the garden's produce; turning his palms means striking one hand against the other in regret at what he spent.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p146/r2] author | holds | Here في means على; خاوية means empty and fallen over its trellises, with the walls standing and roofs collapsed inside.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p146/r3] author | holds | After God removed his blessing and fulfilled his brother's warning, the man regretted associating partners with God, when regret could no longer help him.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p146/r4] author | reports | Another view is that he will say this only at the Resurrection.

## RAZI-FULL: Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, d. 606 AH
[RAZI-FULL:v21p108#2/r19] author | holds | The fruit being surrounded is a metaphor for its total destruction, extended from an enemy encircling and taking control.
[RAZI-FULL:v21p108#2/r20] author | reports | The same idiom appears in the account of Jacob requiring his sons to be surrounded, meaning besieged by an enemy. | mentions 12:66
[RAZI-FULL:v21p108#2/r21] author | holds | Turning his palms is a metaphor for regret and grief: a deeply sorrowful person claps or rubs one hand against the other over his lost investment.
[RAZI-FULL:v21p108#2/r22] author | reports | The collapsed trellises may be grape arbors beneath which the walls fell, or roofs that fell onto the walls; either way the phrase means complete ruin.
[RAZI-FULL:v21p108#2/r23] author | holds | The ruined man recalls the believer's declaration of God's oneness and regrets having associated a partner with his Lord.
[RAZI-FULL:v21p108#2/r24] author | rejects | The ruin does not prove that believers are spared affliction: most trials also befall believers, and a prophetic report says prophets, saints, then the best are most tested. | mentions 43:33
[RAZI-FULL:v21p108#2/r25] author | rejects | His regret does not establish sincere repentance: he wanted monotheism only because he thought it would preserve his garden, so God did not accept it.
[RAZI-FULL:v21p108#2/r26] author | holds | His grief is severe because he spent his life seeking worldly gain and neglecting religion; now both his worldly gain and religious opportunity are lost.

## QURTUBI-FULL: al-Qurṭubī, d. 671 AH
[QURTUBI-FULL:18:42/r1] author | holds | Aḥīṭa bi-thamarihi has an implicit verbal noun as its grammatical subject, though the genitive form may also be construed as nominative; the whole fortune was destroyed, fulfilling the companion’s warning.
[QURTUBI-FULL:18:42/r2] author | reports | He turns his hands over in regret; alternatively, he turns over his dominion and sees nothing left to compensate what he spent, since hand can mean ownership.
[QURTUBI-FULL:18:42/r3] author | holds | Asbaḥa indicates the destruction happened at night, as in the story of the garden visited while its owners slept and left blackened by morning.
[QURTUBI-FULL:18:42/r4] author | reports | Khāwiyat ʿalā ʿurūshihā describes it as empty and fallen upon its roofs; the root can describe stars setting, barren land, or a collapsed house.
[QURTUBI-FULL:18:42/r5] author | holds | The garden fell onto its trellises, destroying both crop and structure; this great calamity answers his transgression.
[QURTUBI-FULL:18:42/r6] author | holds | He wishes he had not associated anyone with his Lord, now recognizing that his blessings came from God’s power; the regret comes too late.

## BAYDAWI-FULL: al-Bayḍāwī, d. 685 AH
[BAYDAWI-FULL:18:42/r1] author | holds | Being encompassed means his property was ruined; the expression comes from an enemy surrounding and overpowering someone, as with the idiom for destroying something.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:18:42/r2] author | holds | Turning the palms over and over expresses regret; the phrase syntactically means he regretted what he spent on the garden.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:18:42/r3] author | holds | The garden lies fallen on its trellises, with vines collapsed above them.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:18:42/r4] author | reports | The owner recalls his brother's warning and thinks his association caused the loss; his lament may be repentance and remorse for prior shirk.

## NASAFI-FULL: al-Nasafī, d. 710 AH

[NASAFI-FULL:v3p15#3/r3] author | holds | The vines' trellises collapsed onto the ground and the vines fell over them; “on what he spent in it” means its cultivation.
