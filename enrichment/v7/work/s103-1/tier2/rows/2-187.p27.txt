## ELMALILI: Elmalılı Muhammed Hamdi Yazır, d. 1361 AH
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r1] author | holds | At true dawn, stop eating and intercourse, then abstain completely until night; legal fasting is intentional abstention from food, drink, intercourse, and analogous bodily intake from dawn to the end of the legal day.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r10] Acemler (Ca‘feriyye mezhebinde bulunan) | reports | Persians of the Jaʿfarī school wait to see the stars before breaking the fast.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r11] author | holds | The lexical sense of fasting as abstention also suggests restraint by all outward and inward faculties: tongue from lies, slander, and idle speech; eye from doubtful sights; ear from entertainments; soul from desires; heart from love of the world; spirit from afterlife pleasures; and inner secret from witnessing anything besides God.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r12] author | holds | Although the verse can indicate these broader forms of abstinence, Elmalılı does not claim that every one is obligatory.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r13] author | holds | Given the preceding focus on eating, drinking, and intercourse, obligatory restraint is limited to these and analogous things entering the body; other restraint is recommended virtue and fasting etiquette.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r14] author | holds | Sexual relations are forbidden both day and night during mosque retreat, though otherwise lawful on fasting nights.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r15] author | holds | Linguistically iʿtikāf is staying confined in a place; legally it means remaining in a mosque with the intention of retreat, as the verse’s mosque qualification specifies.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r16] İmam Muhammed | reports | According to the apparent narration from Imam Muhammad, even a stay of one hour could count as legal iʿtikāf, which would mean fasting is not a condition and voluntary retreat can be done fasting or not.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r17] author | prefers | From the verse’s context, Elmalılı infers that iʿtikāf entails fasting: the ban on intercourse particularizes the night permission, making fasting a condition and the retreat at least one day; this is proper legal iʿtikāf.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r18] author | holds | The hadith “There is no iʿtikāf except with fasting” supports making fasting a condition.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r19] İmam-ı A‘zam | reports | A report from Abū Ḥanīfa says iʿtikāf is valid only in a congregational mosque or, at minimum, a mosque where the five daily prayers are held; a hadith’s mention of congregational mosque means mosques in their fullest sense.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r2] author | holds | This legal fast is a particular kind of lexical abstention defined by the additional limits and boundaries established by sacred law.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r20] author | holds | Women may perform iʿtikāf only in their home prayer place; the Prophet prohibited their retreat elsewhere.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r21] author | holds | A conditional or unconditional vow makes iʿtikāf obligatory; retreat during Ramadan’s last ten days is an emphasized Sunnah, and other times are recommended.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r22] author | reports | The two Ṣaḥīḥ collections report that the Prophet consistently observed iʿtikāf in Ramadan’s last ten days from his arrival in Medina until his death.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r23] author | reports | When ʿĀʾisha, Ḥafṣa, and Zaynab successively came to retreat in tents in the mosque, the Prophet stopped them, left iʿtikāf that Ramadan, and made it up in Shawwāl’s first ten days.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r24] Zührî | reports | Zuhri wondered how people could abandon iʿtikāf when the Prophet sometimes did and omitted other practices but never abandoned iʿtikāf until death.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r25] author | prefers | The Prophet’s continued practice might suggest obligation, but companions who omitted it without rebuke show it is Sunnah rather than obligatory; his omission one Ramadan also indicates it was not individually obligatory, while making it up shows no year passed without it.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r26] author | holds | Without the evidence of companions’ omissions going unrebuked, the Prophet’s never missing iʿtikāf within a year would imply that at least one retreat each year was obligatory.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r27] Katâde | reports | Qatada reports that in an earlier religious law a man could leave iʿtikāf, have intercourse with his wife, and return; this verse prohibited that practice.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r28] author | holds | In Islamic law, intercourse with women invalidates iʿtikāf; the intended meaning of “mubashara” here is intercourse.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r29] İmam Şâfiî | reports | One report from al-Shāfiʿī takes the second occurrence of “mubashara” more broadly as skin-to-skin contact, so even touching a woman with desire invalidates iʿtikāf.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r3] author | holds | Fasting has three requirements: the proper time, intention, and abstention.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r4] author | holds | The fast lasts from true dawn to sunset, the legal day, rather than from sunrise to sunset as in ordinary or astronomical reckoning.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r5] author | holds | Intention must be associated with most of the day, while abstention must last through the entire day; eating after dawn makes the fast invalid.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r6] author | reports | The hadith “Actions are only by intentions” supports intention as a general legal condition for acts of worship.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r7] author | holds | No part of the night is fasting time, and every fast-breaking act is permitted throughout it.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r8] author | rejects | Some wrongly infer that because legal morning starts at dawn, fasting must continue after sunset until twilight or even stars; the verse begins the day “from dawn” but ends it “at night,” and night begins with eastern darkness after sunset.
[ELMALILI:2:187#2/r9] author | holds | Applying the dawn rule by analogy to twilight would alter the explicit text; the revelation context also indicates the former fast did not include the time from sunset to the evening prayer.
[ELMALILI:2:187#3/r1] author | holds | The mentioned rulings are either the limits God established or prohibited limits; believers must not even approach them, much less transgress them.
[ELMALILI:2:187#3/r2] author | reports | A hadith compares God’s prohibitions to a ruler’s protected pasture: those who graze near it risk entering it; jurists derive the principle of blocking the means from this warning.
[ELMALILI:2:187#3/r3] author | holds | God explains the signs that guide people to His legislation clearly and eloquently so they may guard themselves by avoiding disobedience to His commands and prohibitions.
[ELMALILI:2:187#3/r4] author | holds | After completing the fasts and learning not to approach forbidden limits, believers return to the permission to eat and drink for the festival, though the passage then continues.
[ELMALILI:2:187/r1] author | holds | Sexual relations with one’s wife are lawful at any hour of the fasting night; the earlier restriction after the evening prayer or sleep no longer applies.
[ELMALILI:2:187/r10] author | reports | A third interpretation of “seek what God wrote for you” is to seek the Night of Decree.
[ELMALILI:2:187/r11] author | holds | Mubashara literally denotes skin touching skin and is conventionally used for intercourse; intercourse is unanimously the intended meaning here.
[ELMALILI:2:187/r12] author | holds | The command to have relations, like the command to eat and drink, grants permission rather than imposing an obligation.
[ELMALILI:2:187/r13] author | holds | The following command to seek what God decreed indicates that the aim of intercourse should be offspring rather than mere gratification, since lawful sex and desire serve procreation and the continuation of the species.
[ELMALILI:2:187/r14] author | holds | Some interpret “seek what God wrote” as prohibiting coitus interruptus, but the text does not make this certain because its object is unstated; hadith nevertheless suggest disapproval.
[ELMALILI:2:187/r15] author | holds | Eating and drinking remain permitted until the white thread of true dawn becomes distinct from the black thread; this is the first light of dawn.
[ELMALILI:2:187/r16] author | holds | The phrase “until” excludes its endpoint, so fasting must already begin when the white thread becomes distinct; if uncertain, refraining is recommended, but eating does not require a make-up fast because distinction means certain knowledge.
[ELMALILI:2:187/r17] author | reports | A report says the clarification “from dawn” was revealed after some people used actual black and white threads to decide when to stop eating; it explains the familiar metaphor of the first dawn light and the legal day’s start.
[ELMALILI:2:187/r18] author | prefers | Legal theory debates whether clarification came after it was needed; Elmalılı holds that delaying a clarification that changes the rule is impermissible, so on this report the verse must be abrogation rather than mere clarification.
