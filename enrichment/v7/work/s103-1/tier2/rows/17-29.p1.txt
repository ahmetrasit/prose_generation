## TABRISI: al-Ṭabrisī, d. 548 AH
[TABRISI:17:29/r1] author | holds | A hand tied to the neck portrays refusing to give; spreading it fully portrays giving away everything and becoming wasteful.
[TABRISI:17:29/r2] author | holds | The image of a fully spread hand is a metaphor for extravagance; ‘blamed’ means one blames oneself and others blame one.
[TABRISI:17:29/r3] السدي | reports | ‘Exhausted’ means left with nothing and no means.
[TABRISI:17:29/r4] ابن عباس | reports | ‘Exhausted’ means left with nothing and no means.
[TABRISI:17:29/r5] قتادة | reports | Alternatively, it means incapable and regretful.
[TABRISI:17:29/r6] أبو عبد الله ع | reports | Alternatively, it means stripped of clothing and naked.
[TABRISI:17:29/r7] الجبائي | reports | Al-Jubbāʾī explains the outcomes together: if stingy, one sits blamed; if extravagant, one remains distressed and regretful.
[TABRISI:17:29/r8] الكلبي | reports | Al-Kalbī says that giving everything leaves nothing for later requesters, who then blame the giver.

## IBNJAWZI-ZAD: Ibn al-Jawzī, d. 597 AH
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p29#2/r1] ابن مسعود | reports | Ibn Masud reports that a boy asked the Prophet for a shirt on his mother's behalf; the Prophet gave his own shirt and remained bare, after which the verse was revealed.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p29#2/r2] جابر بن عبد الله | reports | Jabir's version adds that the call to prayer came, the Prophet did not emerge, and companions entered to find him naked; the verse was revealed.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p29#2/r3] author | holds | The verse forbids both complete withholding and spending or giving everything, which leaves the giver blamed and exhausted.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p29#2/r4] ابن قتيبة | reports | Ibn Qutayba says maḥsūr is exhausted by the gift, like a camel worn out by travel and left behind.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p29#2/r5] الزجاج | reports | Al-Zajjaj says maḥsūr is someone who has reached the utmost fatigue and exhaustion.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p29#2/r6] القاضي أبو يعلى | reports | Abu Ya'la says the warning is aimed at people who may regret what they give, not the Prophet, who saved nothing, or the confident Companions who gave all they owned.

## QURTUBI-FULL: al-Qurṭubī, d. 671 AH
[QURTUBI-FULL:17:29#2/r1] author | holds | These instructions concern charitable spending; spending on corruption is forbidden whether little or much.
[QURTUBI-FULL:17:29#2/r2] author | holds | The verse discourages exhausting one's resources in response to the first needy person, lest later people or one's dependents be left without support; a maxim says every extravagance has a neglected right, and the ruling depends on each person's circumstances.
[QURTUBI-FULL:17:29#2/r3] ابن عرفة | reports | Ibn ʿArafa says one who squanders wealth ends up exhausted and unable to spend or act, like a spent camel; 67:4 uses ḥasīr for a tired, depleted gaze. | mentions 67:4
[QURTUBI-FULL:17:29#2/r4] قتادة | reports | Qatāda interprets maḥsūran as regretting what one has spent; al-Qurṭubī objects that the active form for regret is ḥasir or ḥasrān, not maḥsūr.
[QURTUBI-FULL:17:29#2/r5] author | holds | A person is malūm if blamed for wasting wealth or if denied assistance by someone who reproaches him.
[QURTUBI-FULL:17:29/r1] author | holds | A hand shackled to the neck is a metaphor for the miser whose will cannot release anything from his wealth, like a shackle restraining the hand.
[QURTUBI-FULL:17:29/r2] أبو هريرة | reports | In Bukhārī and Muslim, Abū Hurayra reports the Prophet's parable of miser and giver in iron cloaks: charity loosens the giver's clothing while the miser's rings tighten; Abū Hurayra saw the Prophet gesture to show it.
[QURTUBI-FULL:17:29/r3] author | holds | Stretching the hand fully pictures losing one's wealth: a closed fist retains what it holds, while an open hand lets it go.
[QURTUBI-FULL:17:29/r4] author | holds | Although addressed to the Prophet, the verse means his community, since scripture often uses their leader as their representative in Arab usage.
[QURTUBI-FULL:17:29/r5] author | holds | The Prophet saved nothing for the next day and endured hunger, while some Companions spent all their wealth; he did not condemn them because of their certainty and insight.
[QURTUBI-FULL:17:29/r6] author | holds | The prohibition targets excessive spending by someone who fears regret over what he gave away; one who trusts God's promise and reward is outside the intended warning.
[QURTUBI-FULL:17:29/r7] قيل | reports | Another view makes the address specific to the Prophet, teaching him moderation in spending.
[QURTUBI-FULL:17:29/r8] جابر | reports | Jābir and Ibn Masʿūd relate that a boy requested a shirt for his mother, so the Prophet gave his own and remained at home without clothes until people entered and found him naked; the passage says the verse was revealed then.

## NASAFI-FULL: al-Nasafī, d. 710 AH
[NASAFI-FULL:v2p285/r1] author | holds | Bound hand and hand spread fully illustrate miserliness and extravagance; kull is an accusative verbal noun, and the command is moderation between the two.
[NASAFI-FULL:v2p285/r2] author | holds | The squanderer is blameworthy before God, people, and himself when later need brings regret; maḥsūr means depleted and unable to go on, or bareheaded.
[NASAFI-FULL:v2p285/r3] author | reports | A Muslim woman wagered with her Jewish co-wife that Muhammad was more generous than Moses; her daughter asked for his shirt, he gave it and sat naked, missing prayer, whereupon the verse was revealed.
