## BIQAI-FULL: al-Biqāʿī, d. 885 AH
[BIQAI-FULL:v7p615#2/r1] بعض العارفين | reports | A gnostic says God inspires the servant at birth that life and the self are a trust, then asks at death whether the trust was kept.
[BIQAI-FULL:v7p615#2/r2] author | holds | Assimilation in the verbal form may signal that charity is concealed, or it may abbreviate speech because the dying person has no time.
[BIQAI-FULL:v7p615#2/r3] author | holds | Most readers other than Abū ʿAmr read “wa-akun” in the jussive, linked to an implied conditional: if granted time, the speaker would give charity.
[BIQAI-FULL:v7p615#2/r4] الخليل | reports | Sībawayh transmits from al-Khalīl that the jussive follows the position of an implied conditional, even though no explicit conditional particle appears.
[BIQAI-FULL:v7p615#2/r5] author | reports | Other grammarians explain that lawlā combines exhortation and conditional meaning, allowing the answer to be jussive by syntax or position.
[BIQAI-FULL:v7p615#2/r6] author | holds | Abū ʿAmr reads “wa-akūna” in the accusative, attaching it to the apparent wording of the response to the wish expressed by lawlā.
[BIQAI-FULL:v7p615#2/r7] author | holds | The written omission of wāw does not invalidate Abū ʿAmr’s reading, which treats it as abbreviation and fits the rule that a reading agree with the consonantal text at least potentially.

[BIQAI-FULL:v7p615/r2] author | holds | Believers should prepare for death before it arrives; each passing moment is already a sign and reminder of its approach.

[BIQAI-FULL:v7p615/r5] بعض العارفين | reports | The report says the door of repentance closes as death throes begin; the soul’s end is either faith and a good ending or doubt and an evil ending, by prior decree.
[BIQAI-FULL:v7p615/r6] author | holds | Delaying repentance risks sins becoming an indelible seal on the heart or sudden illness and death leaving no time to cleanse it.

## BURSEVI: İsmāʿīl Ḥaqqī Bursevī, d. 1127 AH
[BURSEVI:v9p437#2/r1] author | holds | Fa-aṣṣaddaq has a first-person hamza, a doubled ṣād from assimilating the original tāʾ, and an accusative ending after an implicit particle following the wish; wa-akun is jussive, coordinated with its syntactic position.
[BURSEVI:v9p437#2/r2] author | holds | The inward lesson links charity with righteousness and withholding with corruption; charity is for the needy out of compassion, while gifts are for beloved people out of affection.
[BURSEVI:v9p437#2/r3] author | holds | The Prophet accepted gifts but not obligatory or voluntary charity.
[BURSEVI:v9p437#2/r4] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbās says one who owes zakat or can afford pilgrimage will ask to return at death; he recited this verse when challenged that only disbelievers ask, and set thresholds of 200 dirhams or provision and a mount.
[BURSEVI:v9p437#2/r5] author | holds | The verse addresses believers and Muslims, though it reproaches disbelievers too; every negligent person may wish to return.
[BURSEVI:v9p437#2/r6] بعض العلماء | reports | Some scholars infer immediate zakat and other duties because death may come any time; the author suggests prompt action is generally recommended.
[BURSEVI:v9p437#2/r7] author | holds | Some jurists chose prayer’s earliest time to hasten toward God’s pleasure, since a person may not live to its end.
[BURSEVI:v9p437/r6] author | holds | ‘Spend from what We provided’ means some of God’s gracious provision, not something acquired by the recipients’ own power, saved for the hereafter; the apparent command is obligatory, though broader spending better fits the context.
[BURSEVI:v9p437/r7] author | holds | Death’s arrival means seeing its signs; the dying plea is either an entreaty for brief reprieve or a wish to return to the world.
[BURSEVI:v9p437/r8] أبو الليث | reports | Abū al-Layth glosses the plea as asking God to return the speaker to the world for a short additional time.
[BURSEVI:v9p438/r6] القاشاني | reports | Give wealth away while healthy and still needing it: spending benefits when it expresses established generosity, whereas at death wealth belongs to heirs and only regret remains.

## ALUSI-FULL: al-Ālūsī, d. 1270 AH
[ALUSI-FULL:v28p117#2/r1] author | holds | Spend some of the wealth God granted before death approaches, understood as its signs and preliminaries.
[ALUSI-FULL:v28p117#2/r10] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbās takes “give alms” as paying zakāt and “be among the righteous” as performing ḥajj.
[ALUSI-FULL:v28p117#2/r11] ابن عباس | reports | A report through al-Tirmidhī, Ibn Jarīr, and al-Ṭabarānī attributes to the Prophet the statement that one able to perform pilgrimage or owing zakāt who fails will ask to return at death.
[ALUSI-FULL:v28p117#2/r2] author | holds | The dying person asks for a short extension so that he can give alms.
[ALUSI-FULL:v28p117#2/r3] أبي، عبد الله، ابن جبير | reports | Ubayy, ʿAbd Allāh, and Ibn Jubayr read fa-at ṣaddaq, spelling out “so I may give charity.”
[ALUSI-FULL:v28p117#2/r4] أبو علي الفارسي، الزجاج | reports | Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī and al-Zajjāj parse the jussive wa-akun as coordinated with the syntactic position of fa-aṣṣaddaq: if delayed, I would give charity and be righteous.
[ALUSI-FULL:v28p117#2/r5] الخليل | reports | Al-Khalīl, as reported by Sībawayh, explains the jussive by an implied condition signaled by the wish, though al-Ālūsī likes the analysis but dislikes calling it supposition.
[ALUSI-FULL:v28p117#2/r6] author | holds | The difference between coordination to syntactic position and to an imagined condition is verbal: in the former the governor exists but its effect is absent, while in the latter the governor is absent but its effect appears.

[ALUSI-FULL:v28p117#2/r8] عبيد بن عمير | reports | ʿUbayd ibn ʿUmayr reads wa-akūnu in the indicative; grammarians explain it as a new nominal clause with an omitted subject, though the author questions that rationale.

[ALUSI-FULL:v28p118#2/r4] الكيا | reports | Al-Kiyā uses the command to spend as evidence that zakāt must be paid immediately, without delay.
[ALUSI-FULL:v28p118#2/r5] الزمخشري | reports | Al-Zamakhsharī says the warning against neglecting these obligations is severe because death may arrive at any time, requiring constant care.
