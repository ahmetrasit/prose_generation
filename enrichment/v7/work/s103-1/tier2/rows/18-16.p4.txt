## TABATABAI: Muḥammad Ḥusayn al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, d. 1402 AH
[TABATABAI:18:26#4/r5] أبو عبد الله عليه السلام | reports | Al-Sadiq says they concealed belief and outwardly showed disbelief, so God rewarded them twice; other reports say they wore zunnar belts and attended festivals.
[TABATABAI:18:26#4/r6] author | holds | The youths’ departure was migration from a society that prevented open monotheism and threatened stoning or forced conversion; their declaration at some point does not rule out taqiyya earlier.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#28/r4] author | reports | Accounts differ on whether the ruler learned of their opposition before or after escape, whether they planned together or met by chance in the wilderness, and whether a shepherd or only his dog followed them.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#28/r5] author | reports | Some reports say the ruler searched unsuccessfully, others found them asleep and walled up the cave until a shepherd broke in; still others say they met the king and died, or died before his arrival and were hidden, after which a mosque was built.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#29/r1] author | holds | Their coordinated flight and abandonment of family, property, and homeland showed opposition, but their first declaration can be understood as private resolve rather than public defiance; either way, the verses allow taqiyya earlier.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#29/r2] أبو عبد الله عليه السلام | reports | A report says they left without knowing one another’s faith or arranging a meeting, exchanged pledges in the wilderness, then disclosed and found they shared one belief.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#29/r4] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas says the youths left separately from an idolatrous city and cautiously questioned one another before making pledges, agreeing that God alone is Lord and seeking refuge in the cave.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#32/r10] author | holds | The cave story circulated in detailed reports from al-Qummī, Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿIkrima, Mujāhid, Ibn Isḥāq, Wahb ibn Munabbih, and al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr, among others.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#33/r1] author | holds | The transmitted accounts disagree even on major features, while reports about dates, rulers, lineage, appearance, names, and the title أصحاب الرقيم diverge still more.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#33/r2] author | holds | One cause is the story’s prominence among People of the Book: Quraysh learned it from them, and its Christian images and monuments spread it widely, so communities reshaped it to fit their beliefs.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#33/r3] author | holds | A second cause is Muslims’ broad collection of outside reports, especially through converts such as Wahb ibn Munabbih and Kaʿb al-Aḥbār; later transmitters treated such material as prophetic reports.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#33/r4] author | holds | The Qurʾān selects key lessons that serve guidance instead of supplying a complete historical account; it is a book of guidance, not history.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#33/r5] author | holds | The Qurʾānic narrative first shows the youths’ truth-bearing withdrawal and long sleep, then their awakening and exchange that exposes them, and finally their discovery and the mosque built over them.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#34/r1] author | holds | The Qurʾān omits names, ancestry, birth, upbringing, occupations, social standing, dates, rulers, cities, persecutors, and the dog’s identity because guidance does not depend on them; historians and early exegetes filled these gaps, producing divergent accounts.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#34/r3] author | holds | Seeing that remaining would force them to conceal truth or conform to idolatry, the youths chose to affirm God as Lord of heaven and earth, reject other gods as false, and withdraw to the cave trusting in divine mercy and guidance.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#36/r1] أهل الرواية والتاريخ | reports | Most historians date the story between Jesus and Muḥammad; it is absent from the two Testaments, though some Muslim reports say Quraysh heard it from Jews, while Christians preserved it extensively.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#36/r2] author | holds | Christian accounts resemble Ibn Isḥāq’s report from Ibn ʿAbbās in some respects but differ from Muslims on the number of sleepers, whether they had a dog, and the length of their sleep.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#38/r4] author | prefers | After centuries as a ruined, forgotten site, Jordanian excavation uncovered Rujayb; the author considers its archaeological signs and some Muslim reports the strongest fit for the Qurʾānic cave.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#39/r1] author | holds | Amman stands on the site of Philadelphia, a major and beautiful city before Islam that remained under Roman control from the early second century until the Muslim conquest.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#39/r2] author | reports | Other proposed locations include a cave on Mount Qāsiyūn near Damascus, one at Petra, a Scandinavian cave with seven uncorrupted Roman-looking bodies, and a cave near Nakhchivan.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#39/r3] author | prefers | No evidence identifies any proposed cave as the Qurʾānic one; the Scandinavian and Caucasus claims conflict with the story’s Roman setting and the limits of Roman power.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#8/r1] author | prefers | They most likely rose during an assembly enforcing idol worship, publicly declared monotheism, and fled under mortal threat; a report that six were the king's advisers supports this.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#8/r2] author | holds | Public confession does not conflict with reports that they had concealed their faith: they could have practiced caution before suddenly declaring belief and leaving.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#8/r4] author | holds | Iʿtizāl is withdrawal, nashr is spreading, and mirfaq, with the cited vowelings, means gentle treatment or kind benefit.
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#8/r5] author | holds | After leaving the people and their gods, they advise hiding in the cave, confident God will extend mercy and provide ease; these are the same gifts they requested on entering. | mentions 18:10
[TABATABAI:18:9-26#8/r6] author | rejects | The exception in “what they worship except God” is disconnected: the passage does not imply idolaters worshipped God alongside idols, contrary to other interpretations.

## ASAD-NOTES: Muhammad Asad, d. 1412 AH
[ASAD-NOTES:18:13#n19/r1] author | holds | Mirfaq means anything beneficial, concrete or abstract; here it is spiritual provision associated with abandoning the world and withdrawing into seclusion.

## ISLAHI-TADABBUR: Amin Ahsan Islahi (d. 1997)
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-4-surah-yunus-10-surah-maryam-19:p450/r2] author | holds | Marfaq means something useful to a person.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-4-surah-yunus-10-surah-maryam-19:p450/r3] author | holds | The account suppresses the escalating conflict: imminent stoning led the youths to the cave they had chosen, where God assured them of mercy and provision if they took the initiative to leave their people and idols. | mentions 18:20
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-4-surah-yunus-10-surah-maryam-19:p450/r4] author | holds | When servants fear God and set out toward Him, He provides the means they need, as also taught in al-Talaq. | mentions 65:2, 65:3
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-4-surah-yunus-10-surah-maryam-19:p450/r5] author | holds | The guidance to seek refuge is a particular kind of divine inspiration, comparable to God's instruction to Moses' mother to place him in a box and set him afloat.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-4-surah-yunus-10-surah-maryam-19:p450/r6] author | rejects | Islahi rejects the Sufi inference that this verse praises world-isolation: the youths fled mortal persecution, as the Prophet once sought refuge in Thawr, rather than pursuing monasticism.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-4-surah-yunus-10-surah-maryam-19:p451/r1] author | rejects | Against exegetes who treat the promise of mercy and provision as the youths' own speech, Islahi says its certainty marks it as God's promise; a humble person could voice it only as prayer or yearning, as in verse 10. | mentions 18:10
