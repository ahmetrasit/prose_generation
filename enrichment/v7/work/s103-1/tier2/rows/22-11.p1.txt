[WAHIDI-ASBAB:v1p307/r3] author | reports | The Prophet compares Islam’s testing of people to fire separating the impurities of iron, silver, and gold.

## WAHIDI-BASIT: al-Wāḥidī, d. 468 AH
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p282/r5] أكثر المفسرين | reports | Most exegetes understand worshipping God “on an edge” as worship based on doubt.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p283/r1] مجاهد، والسدي، وقتادة، وأبو إسحاق، وأبو زيد، وابن الأعرابي | reports | These authorities take “on an edge” to mean in doubt; al-Wahidi distinguishes this as the contextual meaning rather than the word's literal lexical sense.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p283/r10] أبو الفتح | reports | This returns to instability: the doubter stands at the edge of faith like someone on a cliff, ready to fall at the slightest disturbance; firm belief would not waver.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p283/r2] ابن اليزيدي عن أبي زيد | reports | Abu Zayd glosses the phrase as doubt.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p283/r3] ابن الأعرابي وأحمد بن يحيى | reports | Ibn al-Arabi and Ahmad ibn Yahya also explain the phrase as doubt.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p283/r4] author | holds | The literal lexical meaning of harf is an edge, end, side, or margin; the word for deviation also means turning toward a side.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p283/r5] أبو الفتح الموصلي | reports | Abu al-Fath says the root h-r-f conveys a boundary or sharpness: an object's edge is its limit or side, and food called harif is pungent.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p283/r6] أبو الفتح الموصلي | reports | He explains “turned away from me” as setting a boundary through distance, and the pungent plant as named for its sharpness.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p283/r7] author | holds | One etymology derives edge from inclination away from the center; Abu al-Fath instead derives it from sharpness.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p283/r8] أبو إسحاق | reports | The worshipper stands on the edge of religion's path without entering it as someone firmly settled.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p283/r9] أبو عبيدة | reports | Abu Ubayda says the person does not persevere; saying someone is “on an edge” means he cannot be trusted.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p286/r1] بعض أهل المعاني | reports | Some say doubt arises from weakness and lack of mastery of the proofs: a slight objection leads the person astray instead of being resolved.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p286/r2] المبرد | reports | Al-Mubarrad says a person “on an edge” may show a preference for one group while privately harboring reservations about another; the image is an unstable boundary or footing.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p286/r3] author | holds | These explanations all return to the same underlying idea.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p286/r4] ابن قتيبة | reports | Ibn Qutayba takes “one edge” to mean one aspect, one way, or one school of thought.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p286/r5] الأزهري | prefers | Al-Azhari prefers the one-sided-worship interpretation: prosperity is one side and adversity another, and a servant should worship the Creator in both.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p286/r6] author | holds | On this view, someone who worships only in prosperity is on one edge, while worship in both ease and hardship shows acceptance of the Creator's direction.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p286/r7] الحسن | reports | Hasan identifies the person as a hypocrite who worships with his tongue but not his heart; “on an edge” then means doubt.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p287/r1] author | holds | The good that brings reassurance means prosperity, health, abundant crops, and wealth; the pronoun in “he is reassured by it” refers to that good.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p287/r2] author | holds | The trial means drought and loss of wealth.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p287/r3] أبو إسحاق | reports | To turn on one's face means to leave religion for disbelief and idol worship.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p287/r4] المبرد | reports | Al-Mubarrad says he turns away from the religion he had followed; “face” may mean the path from which he came, namely disbelief.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p287/r5] الكلبي وغيره من المفسرين | reports | The verse was revealed about Bedouins who came to Medina: prosperity made one praise the faith, but illness, loss, or delayed charity led Satan to make him renounce it.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p287/r6] author | holds | The wavering worshipper loses worldly life by failing to obtain the wealth he sought and the afterlife by apostasy; this is the manifest loss.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v15p287/r7] author | holds | The verb “he lost” itself indicates the noun “loss.”

[WAHIDI-WAJIZ:22:8-12/r5] author | holds | Worshiping God on an edge means entering religion without settled commitment; prosperity and increased wealth reassure such people, while drought and poverty make them turn back to unbelief.

## JISHUMI: al-Ḥākim al-Jishumī, d. 494 AH
[JISHUMI:v7p4920/r2] يعقوب | reports | Yaqub reads خاسر as an active participle with alif and الآخرة in the genitive; the seven canonical readers use past خسر and accusative الآخرة.
[JISHUMI:v7p4920/r3] author | holds | Harf, طرف, and جانب are synonyms; harf is an edge or limit, as with a sword's edge, and deviation is turning to the side.

## BAGHAWI-FULL: al-Baghawī, d. 516 AH
[BAGHAWI-FULL:v5p368#2/r1] author | holds | Worship on an edge is insecure doubt; the metaphor is someone unstable on an edge. Gratitude in ease and patience in hardship would make worship firm.
[BAGHAWI-FULL:v5p368#2/r2] الحسن | reports | The person is a hypocrite, worshipping with the tongue rather than the heart.
[BAGHAWI-FULL:v5p368#2/r3] author | holds | Good fortune is health and ample livelihood, which reassures him; bodily affliction or constrained livelihood makes him apostatize. He loses worldly hopes and the Hereafter through loss of religion and eternal Hell.
[BAGHAWI-FULL:v5p368#2/r4] author | reports | Yaʿqūb reads خاسر with an alif and reads الآخرة in the genitive; خسران مبين means manifest loss.
[BAGHAWI-FULL:v5p368/r8] author | holds | The verse was revealed about Bedouin migrants who embraced Islam when health, a foal, a son, and wealth came, but called the religion bad and turned back when illness, a daughter, miscarriage, or poverty struck.
