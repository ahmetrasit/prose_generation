## IBNJAWZI-ZAD: Ibn al-Jawzī, d. 597 AH
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v4p21/r1] author | holds | The fading worldly life is compared to rain that mixes with plants, making abundant crops for people and pasture for animals.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v4p21/r2] ابن قتيبة | reports | The earth's ornament is its greenery; the word originally means gold and came to denote decorated engraving, blossoms, flowers, and whatever is adorned.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v4p21/r3] الزجاج | reports | Zakhraf means the perfection of a thing's beauty.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v4p21/r4] الجمهور | reports | Most readers use the geminated form wazzayyanat, meaning “became adorned” through assimilation; Sa'd, Abu Abd al-Rahman, al-Hasan, and Ibn Ya'mar use a lighter form meaning “came with its adornment,” while Ubayy and Ibn Masud read “and adorned itself.”

## RAZI-FULL: Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, d. 606 AH
[RAZI-FULL:v17p59#2/r1] القاضي | reports | One analogy is that people most attached to worldly life often die just when hope of enjoying it is greatest, like a crop ruined at its peak; 6:44 gives a parallel. | mentions 6:44
[RAZI-FULL:v17p59#2/r2] القاضي | reports | A second analogy is that the worldly lover, like the doomed crop, reaches no praiseworthy outcome.
[RAZI-FULL:v17p59#2/r3] القاضي | reports | A third analogy is that worldly strivers' efforts are rendered void, like the farmer's labor when disaster destroys the crop; 25:23 is cited. | mentions 25:23
[RAZI-FULL:v17p59#2/r4] القاضي | reports | A fourth analogy is the farmer's grief after costly labor becomes the cause of future misery, like the person whose effort for worldly gain becomes a source of afterlife suffering.
[RAZI-FULL:v17p59#2/r5] القاضي | reports | A fifth analogy addresses deniers of resurrection: as a barren-looking earth can become adorned again, the one able to renew it can restore the dead and recompense their deeds.
[RAZI-FULL:v17p59/r1] author | holds | After warning that wrongdoing returns upon its perpetrators (10:23), the verse likens worldly attachment to a luxuriant garden that can be ruined suddenly, leaving its owner heartbroken. | mentions 10:23
[RAZI-FULL:v17p59/r2] author | reports | The phrase about vegetation mixing with the rain may mean varied plants sprout because of the rain, or that rain revives young growth until it becomes beautiful and full.
[RAZI-FULL:v17p59/r3] author | holds | The earth taking its adornment is compared to a bride dressed in fine garments and decorated in many colors; the owner's hopes rise as the garden flourishes.
[RAZI-FULL:v17p59/r4] author | holds | A sudden frost, wind, or flood can destroy the flourishing orchard by night or day, just as the loss of worldly pleasures makes the person attached to them grieve.

## QURTUBI-FULL: al-Qurṭubī, d. 671 AH
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24#2/r1] author | reports | Labid’s line supplies a linguistic example for ghaniya, ‘to inhabit’ or dwell in a place.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24#2/r2] قراءة العامة | reports | The common reading uses the feminine verb taghn because its grammatical subject is the earth.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24#2/r3] قتادة | reports | Qatada reads yaghnu with yā’, referring to the adornment; the crop’s sudden destruction illustrates the same fate of worldly life.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24#2/r4] author | holds | To detail the signs means to make them clear for people who reflect on God’s signs.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r1] author | holds | The parable portrays worldly life as fleeting, vanishing, and of little value or pleasure; its condition resembles water from the sky, with the comparison particle in a nominative construction.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r10] الشعبي وقتادة | reports | Al-Sha‘bi and Qatada read the verb as ‘azayyanat,’ on the af‘alat pattern.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r11] أبو عثمان النهدي | reports | Abu Uthman al-Nahdi transmits three additional forms: az-zayyanat, zayānat, and azya’ant with hamza.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r12] author | holds | ‘Its people thought they had power over it’ means they were certain they could harvest and benefit from it; the pronoun may refer to the earth, its yield, or its adornment.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r13] author | reports | God’s command arriving means either His punishment or His order that the land be destroyed; night and day are temporal adverbs.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r14] author | holds | ‘Harvested’ means cut down with nothing remaining; it is masculine because fa‘il has the sense of maf‘ul, and Abu Ubayda defines it as uprooted completely.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r15] author | holds | ‘As though it had not flourished yesterday’ comes from ghaniya, to reside and inhabit; al-maghani are inhabited homes, while Qatada glosses it as not having enjoyed prosperity.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r2] author | holds | The phrase ‘which We sent down from heaven’ describes the water; Qurtubi promises fuller treatment of this comparison in al-Kahf.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r3] نافع | reports | Nafi‘ pauses after ‘it mixed’: water mixes into the earth, then the vegetation of the earth grows by that water; vegetation is the subject of the next clause.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r4] author | reports | Without Nafi‘’s pause, the vegetation is the subject of ‘mixed’: it mixes with the rain, drinks it, and becomes moist, beautiful, and green; ikhtilat means interpenetration.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r5] author | holds | People eat grain, fruit, and vegetables from the vegetation, while livestock eat pasture, straw, and barley.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r6] author | holds | The earth takes its full beauty and adornment, hence gold is called ornament; ‘and it was adorned’ derives from tazayyanat with assimilation and a joining hamza.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r7] ابن مسعود وأبي بن كعب | reports | Ibn Mas‘ud and Ubayy read the unassimilated original form, ‘and it adorns itself.’
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r8] الحسن والأعرج وأبو العالية | reports | Al-Hasan, al-A‘raj, and Abu al-A‘liya read ‘and it brought forth its adornment,’ using the verb in its original form.
[QURTUBI-FULL:10:24/r9] عوف بن أبي جميلة الأعرابي | reports | ‘Awf ibn Abi Jamilah says his elders read the form waziyānat; al-Muqaddami transmits wazāyanat, from tazāyanat with assimilation.

## BAYDAWI-FULL: al-Bayḍāwī, d. 685 AH
[BAYDAWI-FULL:10:24/r1] author | holds | The parable captures worldly life: it passes quickly, and its pleasures vanish after people become attached and deceived by it.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:10:24/r2] author | holds | Rain mingles and interweaves plants eaten by people and livestock, including crops, vegetables, and pasture.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:10:24/r3] author | holds | The earth takes on its beauty and becomes decorated in varied colors like a bride wearing fine garments and ornaments.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:10:24/r4] author | holds | Several forms explain izzayyanat: assimilation from tazayyanat, an unaltered ifta'ala form, or a form meaning it became adorned.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:10:24/r5] author | holds | The owners think they can harvest the crop; God's command strikes it with what it needs, leaving it cut down as though it had never flourished.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:10:24/r6] author | holds | The omitted noun in 'as though it had not flourished' is the crop, and the omission in both places intensifies the image; one reading uses the original ya form.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:10:24/r7] author | holds | The comparison is a composite image of flourishing vegetation suddenly becoming debris, not a comparison with the rain itself.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:10:24/r8] author | holds | God explains the signs for people who reflect, since they are the ones who benefit from them.
