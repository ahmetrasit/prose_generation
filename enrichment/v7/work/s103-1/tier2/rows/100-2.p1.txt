## WAHIDI-WAJIZ: al-Wāḥidī, d. 468 AH
[WAHIDI-WAJIZ:100:1-8/r2] author | holds | The horses strike sparks with their hooves over stony ground at night.

## JISHUMI: al-Ḥākim al-Jishumī, d. 494 AH
[JISHUMI:v10p7511/r1] الحسن وعكرمة وقتادة | reports | Al-Hasan, ʿIkrimah, and Qatadah say the surah is Meccan; another view says it is Medinan and was revealed around a raid.
[JISHUMI:v10p7511/r11] author | holds | Al-mūriyāt is the plural of māriyah and derives from appearing; it describes sparks shown by the horses’ hoof tips.
[JISHUMI:v10p7511/r2] النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | A report promises ten good deeds for reciting the surah for every person who spent the night at Muzdalifah and witnessed Jamʿ.

## BAGHAWI-FULL: al-Baghawī, d. 516 AH
[BAGHAWI-FULL:v8p508/r1] عكرمة وعطاء والضحاك ومقاتل والكلبي | reports | They identify the spark-producing runners as horses striking stones with their hooves and making fire.
[BAGHAWI-FULL:v8p508/r14] author | holds | These descriptions are the objects of the oath.
[BAGHAWI-FULL:v8p508/r2] قتادة | reports | Qatada says the horses kindle warfare and the fire of enmity between their riders.
[BAGHAWI-FULL:v8p508/r3] سعيد بن جبير عن ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas says these are horses raiding in God's cause, then returning at night to kindle a fire and prepare food.
[BAGHAWI-FULL:v8p508/r4] مجاهد وزيد بن أسلم | reports | Mujahid and Zayd interpret the sparks as men's stratagems in warfare, invoking an Arabic threat meaning that one will kindle a scheme against another.
[BAGHAWI-FULL:v8p508/r5] محمد بن كعب | reports | Muhammad ibn Kab says the phrase refers to fires coming together.

## KASHSHAF: al-Zamakhsharī, d. 538 AH
[KASHSHAF:100:2/r1] author | holds | The horses strike stones with their hooves and kindle sparks, called the fire of al-ḥabāḥib.
[KASHSHAF:100:2/r2] author | holds | Qadḥ means striking, while īrāʾ means producing fire; one may strike and kindle or strike without a spark.
[KASHSHAF:100:2/r3] author | holds | The accusative qadḥan is governed by the same element that governs ḍabḥan in the preceding verse. | mentions 100:1

## KASHSHAF-FULL: al-Zamakhsharī, d. 538 AH
[KASHSHAF-FULL:v4p793/r4] author | holds | Al-mūriyāt are horses striking stones with their hooves and drawing the fire called nār al-ḥabāḥib.

## IBNATIYYA-FULL: Ibn ʿAṭiyya, d. 542 AH
[IBNATIYYA-FULL:v5p513#2/r10] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas identifies the sparks as those made by horses' hooves striking stones.
[IBNATIYYA-FULL:v5p513#2/r9] علي بن أبي طالب | reports | Ali and Ibn Masʿud say the camels spark fire as stones they kick strike one another.
[IBNATIYYA-FULL:v5p514/r1] author | holds | Muwriyat qadhan may mean eloquent tongues, a metaphor for arguments that spark and reveal proofs.
[IBNATIYYA-FULL:v5p514/r2] مجاهد | reports | Mujahid interprets the phrase as men's cunning.
[IBNATIYYA-FULL:v5p514/r3] قتادة | reports | Qatada says these are horses kindling war, also metaphorically.
[IBNATIYYA-FULL:v5p514/r4] ابن عباس وجماعة من العلماء | reports | Ibn Abbas and others generalize it to anything that strikes fire from flint, a widespread divine benefit linked to 56:71. | mentions 56:71
[IBNATIYYA-FULL:v5p514/r5] عدي بن زيد | reports | A poem by Adi ibn Zayd describes striking flint and making a fire.

## TABRISI: al-Ṭabrisī, d. 548 AH
[TABRISI:100:1-4/r10] محمد بن كعب | reports | Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb says the phrase means fires kindled at Jamʿ.
[TABRISI:100:1-4/r11] عكرمة | reports | Another interpretation attributed to ʿIkrimah takes the sparks as men's tongues whose speech kindles fire.
[TABRISI:100:1-4/r8] عكرمة | reports | ʿIkrimah and al-Ḍaḥḥāk say hooves strike stones to make sparks; Muqātil and Ibn ʿAbbās compare it to flint.
[TABRISI:100:1-4/r9] مجاهد | reports | Mujāhid figuratively interprets sparks as men's battle stratagems, using an idiom about kindling a spark against someone.
[TABRISI:100:1/r6] author | holds | Awra is producing fire by striking flint; a weak spark is the fire of al-Ḥabāḥib.
[TABRISI:100:1/r7] النابغة | reports | Al-Nābighah's line uses ‘fire of al-Ḥabāḥib’ for weak hoof sparks; the name evokes a miser's dim fire.

## IBNJAWZI-ZAD: Ibn al-Jawzī, d. 597 AH
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v9p206#2/r5] ابن عباس | reports | In the expedition account, the horses snort through their nostrils, spark with their hooves on stones, attack at dawn, raise dust, and reach the enemy together.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v9p208/r1] الجمهور | reports | Most interpreters say the oath is by horses whose hooves strike sparks from stones as they gallop at night.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v9p208/r2] ابن عباس | reports | Alternatively, the sparks are fires lit by fighters.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v9p208/r3] مجاهد وزيد بن أسلم | reports | Alternatively, the sparks are the schemes men make in war.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v9p208/r4] القرظي | reports | Alternatively, they are the pilgrims' fires at Muzdalifa.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v9p208/r5] عكرمة | reports | Alternatively, they are tongues bringing forward proofs of truth and exposing falsehood.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v9p208/r6] مقاتل | reports | A further included statement identifies the third interpretation as the afternoon prayer.

## RAZI-FULL: Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, d. 606 AH
[RAZI-FULL:v32p60#2/r4] author | reports | On the camel interpretation, the hooves strike stones and spark, or pilgrims riding them light fires at Muzdalifah.
[RAZI-FULL:v32p60#2/r9] أبي عن النبي | reports | A report attributed to Ubay and raised to the Prophet promises a reward measured by the number who spend a night at Muzdalifah and witness Jamʿ.
[RAZI-FULL:v32p61#3/r1] author | holds | Irya is bringing out fire and qadh is striking or clashing; the verse has several proposed subjects for the sparks.
[RAZI-FULL:v32p61#3/r2] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbas says horses strike mountains with their hooves and produce sparks like flint.
[RAZI-FULL:v32p61#3/r3] مقاتل | reports | Muqatil says the horses' hooves spark against stones like the fire of al-Habhab, a miser who lit fires only after people slept and extinguished them when they woke.
[RAZI-FULL:v32p61#3/r4] author | prefers | A related explanation says iron horseshoes strike stone; the first view is stronger because it makes the horses' own hooves like iron.
[RAZI-FULL:v32p61#3/r5] قوم | reports | Others say the horses kindle the fires of war between their owners and enemies, as in the verse about God extinguishing the fire of war. | mentions 5:64
[RAZI-FULL:v32p61#3/r6] author | reports | Another view identifies the sparks with fires lit by raiders at night for cooking and other needs; the striking horses are the raiding band.
[RAZI-FULL:v32p61#3/r7] author | reports | Other metaphorical readings are tongues kindling enmity and men's thoughts igniting deceit; Ibn ʿAbbas is cited for the latter, including fires lit to exaggerate an army's size.
[RAZI-FULL:v32p61#3/r8] عكرمة | reports | ʿIkrima says the sparks are made by spearheads.
[RAZI-FULL:v32p61#3/r9] author | reports | A final view reads the phrase as people succeeding in a raid or pilgrimage; it could mean the successful group or its mounts, with a line by Jarir using the idiom of kindling a flint.
[RAZI-FULL:v32p62/r1] author | holds | The idiom قدح أورى means a strike that makes sparks, while the same root can metaphorically mean success; literal fire is preferred here.
