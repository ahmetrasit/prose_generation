## ELMALILI: Elmalılı Muhammed Hamdi Yazır, d. 1361 AH
[ELMALILI:16:66-67/r1] author | holds | This is the first verse revealed about intoxicants; although it does not yet prohibit wine, contrasting intoxicant with good provision shows it is not good.
[ELMALILI:16:66-67/r2] author | holds | The contrast between intoxicant and good provision parallels the preceding contrast between milk and filth and blood, hinting that intoxicants are impure. | mentions 16:66
[ELMALILI:16:66-67/r3] author | holds | Good provision here means molasses and other sweet products.

## IBNASHUR-FULL: Ibn ʿĀshūr, d. 1393 AH
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v14p202/r1] author | holds | Verse 67 is coordinated with the preceding lesson about livestock.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v14p202/r2] author | holds | The opening من implies a verb from the previous نسقيكم: God gives drink from the fruits; it does not modify تتخذون, preserving the framing as a divine blessing.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v14p202/r3] author | holds | This adds one blessing to another; both concern drink and pressing by hand, and Arabs could call pressing grape juice “milking,” as Ḥassān's poem witnesses.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v14p202/r4] author | holds | The two kinds of drink are related signs; the concluding sign specifically follows the fruit's suitability for pressing and fermentation and its benefits and pleasures, as in Q 16:11.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v14p202/r5] author | holds | تتخذون منه سكرا is circumstantial.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v14p202/r6] author | holds | Both instances of من mark source: the first belongs to the implied “We give you drink,” the second to “you make”; the latter is not partitive because intoxicant is not a piece of fruit.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v14p203/r1] author | holds | سَكَر means an intoxicating drink.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v14p203/r2] author | holds | Intoxicating drink is mentioned as a pleasure then widespread and permissible; the verse is Meccan, before wine was prohibited in Medina.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v14p203/r3] author | holds | رزق حسن means beneficial food, especially dates and grapes, sweet and tasty fresh or dried and suitable for storage; grape juice can also become vinegar or syrup.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v14p203/r4] author | holds | The sign clause is repeated because this is an independent sign; it points to milk, drink, and fruit together, like the sign in Q 16:65.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v14p203/r5] author | holds | “For people who reason” fits because recognizing the wisdom in milk's formation requires reflection, unlike the obvious sign of rain.

## TABATABAI: Muḥammad Ḥusayn al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, d. 1402 AH
[TABATABAI:16:65-67/r6] المفردات | reports | Al-Mufradāt distinguishes sukr as a state that clouds reason from sukr as the intoxicating substance that causes that state.
[TABATABAI:16:65-77/r1] ابن عباس | reports | One no-abrogation interpretation attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās says sukr means vinegar in the Abyssinian language.
[TABATABAI:16:65-77/r2] أبو عبيدة | reports | Abū ʿUbayda is reported to interpret sukr as a flavorful food or snack, citing a poetic line about making the noble people’s faults one’s food.
[TABATABAI:16:65-77/r3] الحنفيون | reports | The Hanafis reject abrogation by taking sukr as a non-intoxicating fermented drink; they infer that drinking it below the point of intoxication is permitted because God mentions it among blessings.
[TABATABAI:16:65-77/r4] author | rejects | The author rejects Ibn ʿAbbās’s foreign-language gloss: where clear Arabic sukr means wine and the proposed Abyssinian meaning means the opposite, using the foreign word would obscure rather than clarify the eloquent text.
[TABATABAI:16:65-77/r5] author | rejects | He rejects Abū ʿUbayda’s food gloss by referring back to his earlier linguistic argument.
[TABATABAI:16:65-77/r6] author | rejects | He rejects the Hanafi inference because the verse only says people make such products from fruit; it does not express divine favor toward every lawful or unlawful product, and the contrast with good provision carries reproach.
[TABATABAI:16:67-68/r1] المجمع | reports | Al-Majmaʿ lists four lexical senses of sukr: intoxicant, a food or taste, stillness, and the verbal noun for bewilderment or clouded perception, illustrating them with poetry and Qurʾānic usage.
[TABATABAI:16:67-68/r2] author | prefers | The basic sense of sukr is loss of reason through an intoxicating cause; the other meanings derive from it through metaphor or extended usage.
[TABATABAI:16:67-68/r3] author | reports | The author cites al-Majmaʿ’s anonymous poems as evidence for sukr meaning taste, stillness, and a wind becoming still; no poets are named.
[TABATABAI:16:67-68/r4] author | reports | Grammatically, the phrase from the fruits may be a nominal clause coordinated with the earlier livestock clause, with an omitted what or thing, or a verbal clause coordinated with God sent down, understood as created or granted. | mentions 16:65, 16:66
[TABATABAI:16:67-68/r5] author | holds | You take from it may stand in apposition or begin an answer to an implied question; the singular pronoun means the mentioned produce, as with the singular pronoun for livestock bellies. | mentions 16:66
[TABATABAI:16:67-68/r6] author | holds | Sukr means intoxicating products such as wine made from dates and grapes, while good provision includes dates, raisins, syrup, and other nourishing foods.
[TABATABAI:16:67-68/r7] author | holds | The verse merely enumerates uses of fruit for Meccan polytheists and does not declare alcohol lawful or commend its use; its contrast with good provision may even censure intoxicants, so it states no ruling for later abrogation. | mentions 5:90
