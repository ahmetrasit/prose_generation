## IBNASHUR-FULL: Ibn ʿĀshūr, d. 1393 AH
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v28p250/r5] author | holds | The discourse turns from exposing hypocrites to urging believers not to be distracted and to spend for good and communal needs, countering the order not to support the Prophet’s followers. | mentions 63:7, 63:10
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v28p251#2/r1] author | holds | Working for wealth and children is not blameworthy when it does not distract from God’s remembrance; such distraction has degrees.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v28p251#2/r2] علماء أصول الفقه | reports | The warning that whoever does this is a loser supports the legal principle that a prohibition requires refraining from the act.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v28p251#2/r3] author | holds | “That” means being distracted by wealth and children: abandoning required remembrance such as prayer on time and failing to observe divine commands and prohibitions.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v28p251#2/r4] author | holds | Distraction by anything other than wealth or children is even more deserving of the prohibition and warning.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v28p251#2/r5] author | holds | The separating pronoun rhetorically restricts “the losers” to those who disobey, exaggerating their loss; the demonstrative says they deserve that outcome.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v28p251/r1] author | holds | Addressing believers through the relative clause signals concern that they obey the prohibition.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v28p251/r2] author | holds | Wealth and children are singled out because wealth absorbs time in acquisition and growth, and its accumulation can displace remembrance of the duty to give.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v28p251/r3] author | holds | Care for children, managing their needs, and enjoying their company can also distract from remembering divine commands and prohibitions.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v28p251/r4] author | holds | The wording addresses wealth and children as the distractors but means their owners; the following “whoever does that” confirms this causal rhetorical construction.

[SAMARRAI-LAMASAT:p178/r1] author | holds | The warning concerns managing and growing wealth or attending to children until one neglects God's remembrance and prefers them over Him; such a person suffers immense loss.
[SAMARRAI-LAMASAT:p179/r1] author | prefers | Ilha means distracting, unlike ordinary occupation, which can be praiseworthy, as prayer is described as an occupation and Paradise's people are joyfully occupied; the stronger blameworthy word best suits the warning. | mentions 36:55
[SAMARRAI-LAMASAT:p179/r2] author | holds | The grammar makes wealth and children the agents forbidden to distract, while believers are the intended people being warned against becoming preoccupied with them.
[SAMARRAI-LAMASAT:p179/r3] author | holds | The rhetorical shift parallels the warning that worldly life and the Deceiver not delude people: the apparent target is the cause, while believers are told not to be deceived by it. | mentions 31:33
[SAMARRAI-LAMASAT:p179/r4] author | holds | Arabic prohibition grammatically targets the agent, as in the examples of telling Mahmoud not to strike Khalid or Ibrahim not to travel.

[SAMARRAI-LAMASAT:p179/r6] author | holds | He asks why the verse does not use the more natural direct wording, do not be distracted by wealth and children, before explaining the advantages of its shift.

[SAMARRAI-LAMASAT:p180/r2] author | holds | The wording heightens the warning by depicting money and children as pursuing the believer and seeking to tempt him, so that prohibiting them cuts off the cause of distraction.
[SAMARRAI-LAMASAT:p180/r3] author | holds | Attributing distraction to wealth and children warns believers to stay alert before they are ensnared without noticing, like the warning not to let someone deceive you.
[SAMARRAI-LAMASAT:p180/r4] author | holds | The metaphor gives wealth deliberate, rational agency, as though it lays a trap to catch the believer.
