## IBNJAWZI-ZAD: Ibn al-Jawzī, d. 597 AH
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p409#5/r1] ابن عباس والأكثرون | reports | One occasion involved Arabs professing faith: prosperity in livelihood, horses, and sons reassured them and seemed to confirm the religion, while adversity led them to call it bad and abandon it.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p409#5/r2] عطية عن أبي سعيد الخدري | reports | Another account concerns a Jewish convert who lost sight, wealth, and children, blamed Islam, and asked to leave; the Prophet replied that Islam smelts people like fire removes impurities from iron, silver, and gold.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p410/r1] مجاهد وقتادة | reports | على حرف means doubt.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p410/r2] أبو عبيدة | reports | A doubter is on an edge: not firmly established, like someone precariously standing at the edge of something.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p410/r3] author | holds | Good fortune means ease and health; a trial means drought or poverty. The person turns back to the direction from which he came, namely unbelief, losing his worldly aim and the afterlife.
[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v5p410/r4] أبو رزين العقيلي وأبو مجلز ومجاهد وطلحة بن مصرف وابن أبي عبلة وزيد عن يعقوب | reports | They read خاسر with an alif before sīn, with rāʾ accusative and الآخرة genitive.

## RAZI-FULL: Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, d. 606 AH
[RAZI-FULL:v23p13/r1] author | holds | Although blessings too are trials, Arabic often calls a hardship a fitna because the term usually applies to what feels burdensome; the hypocrite recognizes only worldly good and harm. | mentions 89:15
[RAZI-FULL:v23p13/r2] author | reports | An objection asks how someone can turn back if he never truly accepted Islam in the first place.
[RAZI-FULL:v23p13/r3] author | holds | Turning back means changing what one says: he praised the religion before hardship, then disparages it when trouble comes, which is a real reversal.
[RAZI-FULL:v23p13/r4] مقاتل | reports | Muqatil asks why the verse contrasts good with fitna rather than saying that evil befalls the person.
[RAZI-FULL:v23p13/r5] author | holds | God does not call the hardship evil because hardship is not inherently morally ugly; the verse uses a description that does not imply ugliness.
[RAZI-FULL:v23p13/r6] author | holds | He loses worldly honor, dignity, spoils, and eligibility for testimony, leadership, and judgment, and loses protection for his property and blood; in the next life he loses lasting reward and gains lasting punishment.

## QURTUBI-FULL: al-Qurṭubī, d. 671 AH
[QURTUBI-FULL:22:11/r1] author | holds | Min is a nominative subject, and the clause ends at inqalaba ʿalā wajhihi on the common reading; the passage describes hypocrites.
[QURTUBI-FULL:22:11/r10] الحسن | reports | Hasan says this is a hypocrite who worships with his tongue but not his heart; in general such a person is not wholly committed.
[QURTUBI-FULL:22:11/r11] author | holds | When health and prosperity come, he is pleased and stays in the faith; when tried by hardship he turns back to his former disbelief, losing both worlds.
[QURTUBI-FULL:22:11/r12] مجاهد وحميد بن قيس والأعرج والزهري وابن أبي إسحاق ويعقوب | reports | They read khāsir al-dunyā with an alif as an accusative circumstantial form, so there is no pause at wajhihi; his worldly loss is no share in spoils or praise, and his loss in the next life is no reward.
[QURTUBI-FULL:22:11/r2] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas says the verse concerns Shayba ibn Rabiʿa, who had become Muslim before the Prophet’s mission and apostatized when revelation came.
[QURTUBI-FULL:22:11/r3] أبو سعيد الخدري | reports | Abu Saʿid reports that a Jewish convert who lost his sight, wealth, and children asked the Prophet to release him from Islam; the Prophet said Islam smelts away impurity as fire purifies metals, and the verse was revealed.
[QURTUBI-FULL:22:11/r4] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas says a man judged the religion good when his wife bore a son and his horses foaled, but bad when neither happened.
[QURTUBI-FULL:22:11/r5] المفسرون | reports | Commentators say Bedouins came to the Prophet, stayed Muslim when they prospered, and apostatized when hardship struck.
[QURTUBI-FULL:22:11/r6] author | reports | Other identifications are al-Nadr ibn al-Harith and, according to Ibn Zayd and others, the hypocrites.
[QURTUBI-FULL:22:11/r7] مجاهد | reports | Mujahid and others gloss worshipping on an edge as worship based on doubt; literally it describes someone unstable at an edge.
[QURTUBI-FULL:22:11/r8] author | reports | Another interpretation makes it one-sided worship: they worship in prosperity but not adversity; gratitude in good times and patience in hardship would mean serving God on both sides.
[QURTUBI-FULL:22:11/r9] author | reports | It may also mean worship on condition: Shayba asked the Prophet for wealth, animals, and children before believing; after receiving and then losing them as a test, he apostatized.

## BAYDAWI-FULL: al-Bayḍāwī, d. 685 AH
[BAYDAWI-FULL:22:11/r1] author | holds | A worshipper on the edge of religion resembles a soldier at the edge of an army: he stays if victory seems near, but flees if not.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:22:11/r2] author | reports | One reported occasion is Bedouins who converted in Medina and remained content when health, livestock, wealth, or children went well, but abandoned faith when misfortune struck.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:22:11/r3] أبو سعيد | reports | Abu Saʿid reports a Jewish convert hit by misfortunes who blamed Islam and asked the Prophet to release him; the Prophet said Islam does not release people, and the verse was revealed.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:22:11/r4] النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | The Prophet's reported reply to the convert was that Islam does not release someone from it.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:22:11/r5] author | holds | Apostasy costs him worldly protection and nullifies his deeds in the next life; the variant forms of خسارة emphasize his loss.

## NASAFI-FULL: al-Nasafī, d. 710 AH
[NASAFI-FULL:v3p97#4/r1] author | holds | Worshipping God “on an edge” pictures unstable commitment rather than settled conviction.
[NASAFI-FULL:v3p97#4/r2] author | holds | Good means bodily health and prosperity, while trial means illness or hardship; receiving good makes the person settle into the faith.
[NASAFI-FULL:v3p97#4/r3] author | holds | When affliction comes, such a person turns back to disbelief, like someone at a military camp's edge who flees if victory and spoils do not come.
[NASAFI-FULL:v3p97#4/r4] author | reports | The verse is said to concern Bedouins who migrated to Medina, then judged Islam by their health, children, wealth, and livestock.
[NASAFI-FULL:v3p97#4/r5] author | holds | The circumstantial reading “losing this world and the next” has a variant nominative reading by Rūḥ and Zayd; worldly loss is death and afterlife loss is eternal fire.
[NASAFI-FULL:v3p97#4/r6] author | holds | Their loss is unmistakable: in this life it is death, and in the next it is perpetual punishment.

## ABUHAYYAN-FULL: Abū Ḥayyān al-Gharnāṭī, d. 745 AH
[ABUHAYYAN-FULL:v7p488/r10] ابن عباس ومجاهد وقتادة وغيرهم | reports | The verse is said to concern converts among Bedouins who feared Muhammad would not prevail and that their Jewish allies would then stop hosting and protecting them.
[ABUHAYYAN-FULL:v7p488/r11] ابن عباس ومجاهد وقتادة وغيرهم | reports | Another report concerns uncertain converts who prospered in wealth or sons and called Islam good, but reverted in adversity, as the Uraniyyin did.
[ABUHAYYAN-FULL:v7p488/r12] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas also identifies the person as Shayba ibn Rabi'a, who embraced Islam before the Prophet's mission and apostatized after revelation began.
[ABUHAYYAN-FULL:v7p489/r1] author | reports | A report says a Jewish convert, stricken by misfortune, became pessimistic about Islam and asked the Prophet to undo his conversion; the verse was then revealed.
[ABUHAYYAN-FULL:v7p489/r10] الجمهور | reports | The majority read khasira as a past-tense verb, either a fresh report or a circumstantial clause without qad; Abu al-Fadl also permits it as a substitute for 'turned back.'
[ABUHAYYAN-FULL:v7p489/r2] الحسن | reports | Al-Hasan says the verse describes a hypocrite who worships with his tongue but not his heart.
[ABUHAYYAN-FULL:v7p489/r3] ابن عيسى | reports | Ibn Isa explains the precarious worship as arising from weak conviction.
[ABUHAYYAN-FULL:v7p489/r4] أبو عبيد | reports | Abu Ubayd glosses 'upon an edge' as being in doubt.
[ABUHAYYAN-FULL:v7p489/r5] ابن عطية | reports | Ibn Atiyya explains 'upon an edge' as deviating from clear doctrine or standing on its brink, ready to fall away.
[ABUHAYYAN-FULL:v7p489/r6] الزمخشري | reports | Al-Zamakhshari pictures the worshipper as on the edge rather than the center of religion: secure if victory and spoils come, but ready to flee if not.
[ABUHAYYAN-FULL:v7p489/r7] author | holds | Worldly loss is suffering loss of property or loved ones without accepting God's decree; afterlife loss is forfeiting the reward of patience by apostatizing.
[ABUHAYYAN-FULL:v7p489/r8] مجاهد وحميد والأعرج وابن محيصن وقنب والجحدري وابن مقسم | reports | These readers pronounce khāsir al-dunyā as an accusative active participle functioning as a circumstantial expression.
