## ALUSI-FULL: al-Ālūsī, d. 1270 AH
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p62#3/r1] author | reports | Here ḥattā begins a sentence and marks the endpoint of an omitted prior clause; the ‘even Kalīb insults me’ example leaves the statement that everyone insults the speaker implicit.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p62#3/r10] المازني | reports | Al-Māzinī says the plural pronoun signals repeated pleading, so ‘return me’ means it three times; he compares the dual in qifā nabki.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p62#3/r2] author | reports | Alternatively it attaches to the second ‘they describe,’ making slander last until death; ‘say, Lord’ interrupts to urge protection from Satan and obedience to repel evil graciously.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p62#3/r3] author | rejects | Attaching ḥattā to the first ‘they describe’ or to ‘they associate partners’ is judged untenable.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p62#3/r4] الزمخشري | reports | Al-Zamakhsharī links the passage to ‘they are liars’: denying God a son through this point interrupts the discourse to establish their lies and deserved punishment; al-Ālūsī rejects it.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p62#3/r5] ابن عطية | reports | Ibn ʿAṭiyya allows ḥattā to begin a sentence without endpoint force; Abū Ḥayyān objects that it necessarily retains endpoint meaning.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p62#3/r6] author | holds | The pronoun ‘one of them’ refers to unbelievers; death’s arrival means signs of death and conditions of the next life appear.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p62#3/r7] author | reports | The plural imperative ‘return me’ may honor God, as poetry shows one person addressed in plural; the author rejects denial of this usage.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p62#3/r8] author | reports | Alternatively, the plural addresses angels, with ‘my Lord’s angels’ understood, or ‘Lord’ invokes God while ‘return me’ addresses angels.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p62#3/r9] ابن جرير عن ابن جريج | reports | A report relayed by Ibn Jurayj says believers offered return reject it as return to grief, whereas unbelievers ask God to send them back.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63/r1] الخفاجي | reports | Al-Khafājī objects that the singular imperative’s underlying form contains no plural pronoun, leaving al-Māzinī’s metaphor unexplained.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63/r2] الخفاجي | reports | Al-Khafājī proposes substituting one word-form for another without regard to meaning, letting the plural pronoun signal repetition for emphasis.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63/r3] author | rejects | Al-Ālūsī says al-Khafājī went too far: plural is an honorific address to one treated as a group, making the action resemble repeated group action.
[ALUSI-FULL:v18p63/r4] author | reports | This explanation extends to the dual in qifā nabki only if dual address can honor one person; al-Ālūsī does not recall seeing this and leaves it open.

## KURANYOLU-TEFSIR: Hayreddin Karaman, Mustafa Çağrıcı, İbrahim Kâfi Dönmez, Sadrettin Gümüş
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:23:99/r1] author | holds | ‘They’ especially means unbelievers who deny resurrection; only after death, when worldly ties and desires end, do they recognize their mistake and express despair and regret.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:23:99/r2] Fahreddin er-Râzî | reports | Razi says they cry out at death—or, in a weak view, on seeing their place in Hell—knowing return is impossible but grieving that they died unbelievers.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:23:99/r3] author | holds | The verses explicitly reject reincarnation, an idea traced to ancient Indian religions and Plato: if souls could migrate between bodies until purified, God would not refuse the sinners’ request to return and correct their lives.
