## BURSEVI: İsmāʿīl Ḥaqqī Bursevī, d. 1127 AH
[BURSEVI:v10p340#3/r3] author | holds | The moon follows the sun after its setting, drawing on the sun's light during the month's first half; al-Raghib says following can mean bodily succession, imitation, or following in judgment, recitation, and reflection.
[BURSEVI:v10p340#3/r4] author | holds | The moon receives its light from the sun and is like its deputy; radiance ranks above light, since every radiance is light but not every light is radiance. | mentions 10:5
[BURSEVI:v10p340#3/r5] شيخي وسندي | reports | The cited spiritual master says the sun is a sign of the all-comprehensive and most perfect divine reality.
[BURSEVI:v10p340#3/r6] author | holds | In the inward reading, the moon is the heart following the spirit, turning toward it and receiving its light rather than following the ego into darkness.
[BURSEVI:v10p341#2/r1] author | holds | Although the conditional sense of idha seems to require an answer and an operator, the author reads it causally in these oaths: swear because the moon follows, the day reveals, and the night covers; al-Qamus also allows a present-time meaning, “when.”
[BURSEVI:v10p341#2/r7] author | prefers | The oath wa's are conjunctive and share the implied verb, avoiding the problem of coordinating words governed by different agents or multiplying oaths with one answer.

## ALUSI-FULL: al-Ālūsī, d. 1270 AH
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p140#2/r1] author | reports | “Following” can mean the moon rises after the sun early in the month; the moon is then like the young morning, and the crescent becomes visible after sunset.
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p140#2/r2] قتادة | reports | Qatada's view is that the moon follows the sun's setting at full moon on the fourteenth night, rising at the eastern horizon opposite the setting sun; the moon is then at its full strength.
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p140#2/r3] ابن زيد | reports | Ibn Zayd says the moon follows the sun throughout the lunar month: by rising during the first half and by setting during the second.
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p140#2/r4] ابن سلام | reports | Ibn Sallam says the moon follows the sun by receiving and displaying light from it during the waxing half; al-Zamakhshari prefers this explanation.
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p140#2/r5] الحسن والفراء | reports | Al-Hasan and al-Farra say the moon follows the sun at all times because its illumination comes from the sun; Alusi says the changing phases and loss of light when Earth blocks sunlight make this clear.
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p140#2/r6] author | rejects | Alusi disputes the claim that early authorities never held lunar light to derive from sunlight; alternative explanations for the phases or disappearance during Earth's obstruction are weaker.
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p140#2/r7] الزجاج | reports | Al-Zajjaj and others say tala can mean to fill out and become round, so the moon follows the sun in rounding and reaching full light.

## ELMALILI: Elmalılı Muhammed Hamdi Yazır, d. 1361 AH
[ELMALILI:91:2/r1] author | holds | The moon follows the sun most fully when it rises after sunset as a bright full moon, especially on nights fourteen to sixteen.
[ELMALILI:91:2/r10] author | rejects | The crescent-night interpretation draws attention to the new moon, but restricting following to sunset alone lacks a decisive reason.
[ELMALILI:91:2/r11] author | prefers | The first view illuminates the moon's day-by-day waxing toward fullness more broadly than the fourth view.
[ELMALILI:91:2/r12] author | prefers | Ultimately, the fourth view is clearest: the full moon resembles a secondary sun and its reflected light prepares for the soul's perfection.
[ELMALILI:91:2/r13] author | holds | The moon's reflected, nonfiery light leads from sensory evidence to intelligible truth, the seen to the unseen, and effect to its cause.
[ELMALILI:91:2/r14] author | holds | Reading talā figuratively likens the moon's spreading the unseen sun's reflected light to a reciter explaining a Qur'anic verse; this evokes the purified soul and the Qur'an as light.
[ELMALILI:91:2/r2] author | holds | The moon can seem to follow the sun at other times, but before fullness it is incomplete and after the sixteenth night it increasingly lags behind sunset.
[ELMALILI:91:2/r3] author | holds | The verb talā derives from tulūw, meaning to follow behind; recitation is related because a reader follows and conveys words.
[ELMALILI:91:2/r4] Atâ | reports | Atā, transmitting from Ibn ʿAbbās, says the moon remains risen when the sun sets, which occurs in the first half of the month.
[ELMALILI:91:2/r5] Katâde ile Kelbî | reports | Qatada and Kalbi say the moon follows the sun by setting after it, as on the crescent night.
[ELMALILI:91:2/r6] Ferrâ | reports | Farra interprets following as the moon receiving its light from the sun.
[ELMALILI:91:2/r7] Zeccâc | reports | Zajjaj takes the phrase as the moon's turning and completion, when its light reaches fullness and it resembles the sun in illumination.
[ELMALILI:91:2/r8] author | reports | A fifth possibility relates the moon's perceptible size and the world's benefits to its movement.
[ELMALILI:91:2/r9] author | holds | The reported senses share the idea of following or resembling the sun in rising, setting, light, size, or benefit; the moon also derives its light from the sun.

## IBNASHUR: Ibn ʿĀshūr, d. 1393 AH
[IBNASHUR:91:2/r1] author | holds | Tala means following: moonlight follows sunlight after sunset, figuratively, and the moon follows the sun through its phases; at the full moon its following is literal around sunset.
[IBNASHUR:91:2/r2] author | holds | The crescent appears after sunset for three nights; near its first visibility and at full moon it follows the sun, and it illuminates many nights as a substitute for sunlight.
[IBNASHUR:91:2/r3] author | holds | The restriction to the moon's following highlights its visible manifestation, making the oath one by the moon and by the time it follows the sun, like the duha oath.
[IBNASHUR:91:2/r4] author | holds | The verse signals that moonlight is borrowed from sunlight striking the moon's earth-facing surface, rather than being intrinsic; Ibn Ashur calls this a scientific miracle noted in his tenth introduction.
[IBNASHUR:91:2/r5] author | holds | The order symbolizes Islam: the sun's guidance leaves no path for error and promises worldwide spread, while the moon shining in darkness resembles Islam's first appearance amid polytheism.
[IBNASHUR:91:2/r6] author | holds | Day and night then represent Islam's clarity after polytheist error, opposite the order in Surat al-Layl; sky, earth, and finally the human soul follow as signs of guidance and misguidance.

## IBNASHUR-FULL: Ibn ʿĀshūr, d. 1393 AH
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p366/r1] author | holds | The oath confirms the threat of destruction implicitly conveyed by the news; each waw following a pause is an oath particle, and the sun, moon, sky, earth, and human soul display God's wise and powerful creation.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p366/r2] author | holds | The dawn brightness, moon following the sun, daylight, and night are all examples of the fine order God established.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p366/r5] author | holds | Following means the moon's light appearing after sunset, figuratively following the sun; its phases also follow the sun, and at full moon it literally appears around sunset. Its illumination replaces the sun for several nights, so the oath highlights its visible following state as well as the moon itself.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p368/r2] author | holds | إذا is a circumstantial time adverb attached to the state of each moon, day, and night; each is sworn by in the condition that best displays God's artistry.
