## IBNASHUR: Ibn ʿĀshūr, d. 1393 AH
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r1] author | holds | The surah is called Surat al-Asr in many Qurans, most tafsirs, and a fifth-century Kufic Qayrawani codex; some tafsirs and Sahih al-Bukhari title it Surat wa-l-Asr, following its first word.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r10] author | holds | Whichever sense of al-asr is intended, it must denote a state that points to a divine act, established through an implied adjunct, contextual clue, or prior reference; as time it recalls God's power in creation and momentous blessings.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r11] author | reports | The most familiar sense is the late-afternoon period, from when an object's shadow equals its noon shadow plus its length until it reaches twice its length beyond the noon shadow, around yellowing and the start of evening.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r12] الحارث بن حلزة | reports | Al-Harith ibn Hilliza's verse uses asran for the late-afternoon period approaching evening.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r13] author | holds | Late afternoon recalls the nearing end of day, the sun and earth, Earth's orbit producing night and day, and changing atmospheric conditions, like the oaths by duha, night, day, and dawn.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r14] author | holds | It is when people prepare to stop work in fields, gardens, and markets, recalling social order and God's inspiration of work rhythms; they return home to family, and the time is a blessing.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r15] author | holds | The day's decline also hints at human life nearing its end after youth, maturity, and old age; on the general-time reading, the article means every age.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r16] author | reports | Al-asr can mean the highly honored afternoon prayer, possibly the middle prayer in the cited verse; two hadith reports warn of missing it and of swearing a false sales oath after it. | mentions 2:238
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r17] author | holds | On the prayer reading, the article is definite by reference and the word has become a conventional name, like al-aqaba.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r18] author | reports | Al-asr can mean an era identified by a generation, ruler, prophet, or religion, such as the eras of Ibrahim, Alexander, or Jahiliyya; it could here mean the Prophet's own era.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r19] author | holds | On the Prophet's-era reading, the article refers to the present era, like saying today; the oath resembles God's oath by the Prophet's life and by the sacred city where he is present, as Fakhr explains. | mentions 15:72, 90:2
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r2] author | holds | Most authorities consider it Meccan; Qatada, Mujahid, and Muqatil are reported to consider it Medinan.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r20] author | reports | Another possibility is the whole Islamic era, the final religious age of this world; a Prophetic parable compares Muslims, Jews, and Christians with workers hired through a day, with Muslims completing the remaining time after afternoon and receiving both groups' full wages.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r21] author | holds | That parable may be connected to the surah's symbolic reference to the Islamic era.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r22] author | reports | Al-asr may also mean all time; Ibn Atiyya reports Ubayy ibn Ka'b asking the Prophet about it and receiving the answer that the Lord swears by the latter part of the day.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r23] author | prefers | Reading al-asr as the Islamic era especially suits the surah, which distinguishes people in that era by disbelief or faith and by how fully they practice Islam; pre-Islamic peoples differed in access to messengers and held superseded or idolatrous religions. | mentions 3:85
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r3] author | holds | It was the thirteenth surah revealed, after al-Inshirah and before al-Adiyat; it has three verses and is among the shortest surahs, alongside al-Kawthar and al-Nasr.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r4] author | holds | Its aims include declaring the grave loss of disbelievers and those persisting in false practices after Islam's call reaches them, and the salvation of believers who do good and call others to truth.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r5] author | holds | It also establishes the virtue of patiently purifying oneself and inviting others to truth.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r6] عبيد الله بن عبد الله بن الحصين الأنصاري | reports | The Tabi'i reports that two Companions would not part after meeting without one reciting Surat al-Asr to the other, then exchanging the customary farewell greeting; the departure greeting is Sunnah like the greeting on arrival.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r7] الشافعي | reports | Al-Shafi'i says that if people reflected on this surah, it would suffice them; another reported wording says that if no other revelation had come, it would have sufficed.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r8] author | reports | Others are said to hold that the surah encompasses all the Quran's sciences, which the commentary will explain later.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r9] author | holds | God swears by al-asr to confirm the report, as Quranic oaths generally do; the sworn time manifests God's creative artistry, power, and knowledge.

[TABATABAI:103:1-3/r1] author | holds | The surah condenses Quranic teachings and gathers its aims in the briefest statement; although it could be Meccan or Medinan, it seems more likely Meccan.
