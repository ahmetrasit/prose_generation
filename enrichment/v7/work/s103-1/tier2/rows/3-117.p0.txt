## MUQATIL: Muqātil b. Sulaymān, d. 150 AH
[MUQATIL:3:116-117/r1] author | holds | Lower-ranking Jews spent food and fruit for their leaders Ka'b b. al-Ashraf and his companions, hoping for the afterlife despite their disbelief.
[MUQATIL:3:116-117/r2] author | holds | Their spending, including that of Meccan unbelievers, was destroyed like a freezing wind that ruins the crop of people who wronged themselves.
[MUQATIL:3:116-117/r3] author | holds | God did not wrong them by refusing their expenditure; they wronged themselves through disbelief.

## TAB-FULL: al-Ṭabarī, d. 310 AH
[TAB-FULL:v5p703/r1] author | holds | The spent wealth of disbelievers, even when given as devotion, is annulled because they deny God's oneness and the Messenger; it is like a freezing wind destroying a sinful people's hoped-for harvest.
[TAB-FULL:v5p703/r2] author | holds | The comparison concerns God's cancellation of their reward, as the context makes clear, like the parable of the person who kindled a fire. | mentions 2:17
[TAB-FULL:v5p703/r3] مجاهد | reports | Mujāhid takes the spending to mean a disbeliever's expenditure in this world.
[TAB-FULL:v5p703/r4] السدي | reports | Al-Suddī instead interprets it as words spoken without inward belief, rendered fruitless by associating partners with God.
[TAB-FULL:v5p703/r5] author | holds | Ṣirr means intense cold, caused by a fierce north wind.
[TAB-FULL:v5p707/r1] عكرمة | reports | ʿIkrima glosses ṣirr as severe cold; Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatāda, al-Rabīʿ, al-Suddī, and al-Ḍaḥḥāk likewise identify it as cold or severe cold.
[TAB-FULL:v5p707/r2] ابن زيد | reports | Ibn Zayd says the cold wind destroys crops and is called ḍarīb when it leaves plants burned by freezing cold.
[TAB-FULL:v5p707/r3] author | holds | God does not cancel their reward unjustly: their deeds lacked monotheistic sincerity and belief in the messengers despite prior warning; their unbelief makes them responsible for punishment.

## ZAJJAJ: al-Zajjāj, d. 311 AH

[ZAJJAJ:v1p460/r6] قال بعضهم | reports | Some say the example refers to Meccans who pooled money to oppose the Prophet; others take the spending as their deeds in idolatry.
[ZAJJAJ:v1p460/r7] قال بعضهم | reports | Another linguistic possibility takes ṣirr as a sound, the noise of fire consuming the crop, which the author says is not impossible.
[ZAJJAJ:v1p460/r8] author | holds | Spending to support hostility against religion harms and destroys its proponents in this life and the next.

[THALABI:v3p133/r6] author | holds | Unbelievers' spending is like a cold wind or fire destroying a crop: its hoped-for benefit disappears when needed, and God has not wronged them; they wrong themselves by disbelief, sin, and withholding God's due.

## WAHIDI-BASIT: al-Wāḥidī, d. 468 AH
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p523/r1] يمان | reports | Yaman says the verse concerns Abu Sufyan and the polytheists funding opposition to the Prophet at Badr and Uhud.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p523/r10] ابن كيسان | reports | Ibn Kaysan and Ibn al-Anbari take ṣirr to mean fire.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p523/r11] مجاهد | reports | Mujahid has the fire interpretation in Ibn Abi Najih's report.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p523/r12] ابن الأنباري | reports | Ibn al-Anbari links the name of fire as ṣirr to its sound as it blazes.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p523/r13] الزجاج | reports | Al-Zajjaj allows the fire interpretation and says a flame's sound is called ṣarr, with 'door creak' and a bird's cry as lexical parallels.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p523/r2] مقاتل | reports | Muqatil identifies the spending as that of lower-ranking Jews on their religious scholars.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p523/r3] مجاهد | reports | Mujahid takes it broadly as all spending and charitable giving by disbelievers in worldly life.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p523/r4] الزجاج | reports | Al-Zajjaj says it covers every expenditure meant to display hostility toward religion.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p523/r5] author | holds | Mathal denotes a likeness made familiar like an established sign through repeated use; an omitted phrase, the destruction of their spending, parallels the wind's destruction of crops.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p523/r6] author | reports | Most exegetes and linguists, including Ibn Abbas in Ataa's report, Qatada, al-Rabi, al-Suddi, and Ibn Zayd, define ṣirr as intense cold.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p523/r7] author | holds | Spending against religion ruins the disbelievers' deeds in the afterlife just as the cold wind ruins the crop it strikes.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p523/r8] الزجاج | reports | Al-Zajjaj explains that their spending harms them just as the wind harms the crop.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p523/r9] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas also explains ṣirr as the lethal, hot simoom winds.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p528/r1] الراعي | reports | Al-Rai's line describes a harsh summer wind, supporting the hot-wind interpretation and its audible quality.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p528/r2] author | holds | On this reading, the wind is a scorching wind like fire that burns the crop, or a noisy fire; the sound sense also suits hot simoom winds.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p528/r3] ابن عباس | reports | A report through al-Suddi and Abu Malik from Ibn Abbas says the wind contains fire.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p528/r4] ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas says the people wronged themselves by denying God's favor; others explain their wrong as unbelief and disobedience.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p528/r5] author | holds | God does not wrong them: whatever He does with His creation is just, since He acts within His own dominion.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v5p528/r6] author | holds | People wrong themselves through unbelief and disobedience; the image is especially grievous because hoped-for benefits of spending turn to harm, like a ruined harvest.
