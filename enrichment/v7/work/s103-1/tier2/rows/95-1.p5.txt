## TABATABAI: Muḥammad Ḥusayn al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, d. 1402 AH
[TABATABAI:95:3-6/r2] author | reports | Views on the fig and olive include the familiar fruits with their many benefits, the two trees, or the mountains near Damascus and Jerusalem; the mountains may take their names from those crops and be oaths for the regions where many prophets appeared. Other views also exist.

## ISLAHI-TADABBUR: Amin Ahsan Islahi (d. 1997)
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p370/r1] author | holds | The oath particle wa introduces an oath; Qur’anic oaths by objects and places bear witness to the premise stated after them.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p370/r2] author | holds | Al-Tīn means the Mount of Figs, a place known for producing figs, rather than the dried fruit; Farāhī explains the Arab place-naming custom.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p371/r1] Nābighah Dhubyānī | reports | Farāhī cites a couplet in which al-Tīn is used as a place name, supporting the reading of the oath as a mountain.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p371/r2] some people | reports | Some identify the mountain meant by al-Tīn as a mountain between Ḥalwān and Ḥamdān.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p371/r3] Imām Farāhī | prefers | Farāhī says al-Tīn is either Mount Jūdī or a nearby mountain, linking it with the post-Flood dispersal of humankind described in the Torah and placed near Jūdī by the Qur’an.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p371/r4] author | holds | Al-Zaytūn likewise names the Mount of Olives, the site associated with Jesus’ preaching, worship, and supplication, rather than the olive or its tree.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p371/r5] Imām Farāhī | reports | Farāhī says the Mount of Olives was named for its abundant olives and identifies it as the Gospel site where Jesus worshipped and prayed. | mentions 42:37
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p371/r6] Ibn ‘Abbās and Ka‘b | reports | Ibn ‘Abbās and Ka‘b interpret al-Zaytūn as Bayt al-Maqdis.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p371/r7] Qatādah | reports | Qatādah interprets al-Zaytūn as the mountain on which Bayt al-Maqdis is situated.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p374/r3] Imām Farāhī | holds | Farāhī identifies the Mount of Figs with Jūdī and presents Adam’s and Noah’s episodes there as examples of divine retribution.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p374/r4] Imām Farāhī | reports | Farāhī recounts that Adam and his wife forgot their covenant, ate the forbidden fruit after Satan’s temptation, and lost their success and heavenly clothing; the Qur’an recalls the episode. | mentions 7:27
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p374/r5] author | reports | The account notes that the Torah identifies the leaves Adam and Eve used to cover themselves as fig leaves.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p374/r6] Imām Farāhī | reports | After Adam and Eve repented and God accepted them, He promised guidance and reward to its followers, making this a second covenant with Adam.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p375/r1] Imām Farāhī | holds | The Adam episode has two sides: God removed one favor because of the broken promise, then gave another after Adam and Eve recognized their folly and repented.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p375/r2] Imām Farāhī | reports | At Noah’s time God destroyed wrongdoers near the mountain and saved the righteous from the Flood, as shown in the Jūdī landing and Noah’s later promise of peace and blessings. | mentions 11:44, 11:48
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p375/r3] Imām Farāhī | holds | Farāhī concludes that the Mount of Figs is a major site where God’s law of retribution became manifest.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p375/r4] Imām Farāhī | holds | Farāhī says that in Jesus’ final years God transferred the sharī‘ah and trust from the Jews to Abraham’s other line; Jesus grieved for his people as they pursued his life.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p376/r1] Imām Farāhī | reports | Farāhī cites Jesus’ rejected-stone saying and his warning that God’s kingdom would be taken from the Jews and given to a fruitful people as evidence of their lost trust. | mentions 19:22, 40:21, 40:22
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p378/r1] author | holds | The oath sites testify to retribution and announce who merits God’s blessings and who faces His wrath.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p378/r2] Imām Farāhī | holds | Farāhī orders the sites by historical and thematic resemblance: Adam precedes Jesus because of their likeness, then Sinai and Makkah evoke Moses and Muḥammad. | mentions 3:59, 73:15

## KURANYOLU-TEFSIR: Hayreddin Karaman, Mustafa Çağrıcı, İbrahim Kâfi Dönmez, Sadrettin Gümüş
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:95:1/r1] author | holds | God swears by four signs of His knowledge, artistry, and power—fig and olive as bodily food, Sinai as revelation's spiritual food, and secure Mecca—to affirm that He made humans best and endowed them to bear physical and spiritual duties (cf. 17:70). | mentions 17:70
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:95:1/r2] Müfessirler | reports | One symbolic view takes fig and olive as lands rich in those trees, Palestine and Syria, symbols of the blessing of prophetic teachings conveyed there.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:95:1/r3] Müfessirler | reports | Other symbolic interpretations identify the fig with Mecca's Sacred Mosque and the olive with Jerusalem's al-Aqsa Mosque.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:95:1/r4] Şevkânî | rejects | Shawkani argues that these symbolic interpretations lack rational or transmitted evidence.

## SAMARRAI-ASRAR: فاضل صالح السامرائي
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17#3/r11] author | holds | The ending returns to the opening: the one who sent the great revelations and their exalted laws is the wisest judge.
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r1] author | reports | The opening oath may refer to the familiar fig and olive trees.
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r10] مفسرون | reports | Another interpretation takes fig and olive as two Holy Land mountains, called Tour Tina and Tour Zaita in Syriac, where those trees grow.
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r11] author | rejects | On the mountain interpretation, the connection between the two names and what follows is not clear without strained reasoning.
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r12] مفسرون | reports | A reported interpretation identifies three places of major prophetic missions: Jerusalem for Jesus, Sinai for Moses, and Mecca for Muhammad.
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r13] author | reports | Al-Tibyān reads the oath as the three prophetic places and explains them as successive manifestations of Jesus, Moses, and Muhammad and their major religious communities.
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r14] author | reports | Al-Tibyān says the oath ascends from the place of Jesus to Moses and then Muhammad, paralleling a Torah passage about Sinai, Seir, and Paran.
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r15] author | prefers | The author prefers the three prophetic locations because their relation to one another is clear on that reading.
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r16] author | holds | The sequence begins with fig then olive, with olive ranked higher because God calls it blessed; it serves as fruit, food, and lamp oil. | mentions 24:35
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r2] مفسرون | reports | Exegetes explain the paired trees as two kinds, one whose fruit has no seed and one whose fruit has a seed.
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r20] author | holds | The oath progresses from fig to olive to Sinai to the secure city, moving from the noble to the noblest and ending at the home of the final, most honored mission.
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r3] رسول الله | reports | A report says the Prophet received a plate of figs, ate them, and told his companions that if any fruit came from Paradise, it would be this one because Paradise's fruit has no seeds.
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r4] author | reports | A report says Adam used fig leaves to cover his nakedness after it was exposed in Paradise.
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r6] author | reports | The author speculates that the eight verses might correspond to Paradise's eight gates, but leans toward this being pure conjecture.
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r7] author | reports | He notes numerical patterns elsewhere: eight repetitions of the blessing question in descriptions of Paradise and seven in warnings, corresponding to Paradise's and Hell's gates. | mentions 55:13, 55:31
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r8] author | reports | Other proposed numerical patterns say Sura al-Qadr has thirty words for Ramadan's days and places huwa at word 27 to signal the 27th night. | mentions 97:5
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r17/r9] author | holds | The author cautions that many such numerical correspondences may simply be coincidences.
