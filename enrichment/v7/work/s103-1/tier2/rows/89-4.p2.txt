## SAMIN-DURR: al-Samīn al-Ḥalabī, d. 756 AH
[SAMIN-DURR:89:4#2/r1] author | holds | ‘Idha yasri’ is governed by an omitted verb of swearing: ‘I swear by it at the time it travels by night’. 
[SAMIN-DURR:89:4#2/r2] نافع، أبو عمرو | reports | Nafi‘ and Abu ‘Amr omit the yā’ of ‘yasri’ at pause but retain it in connected recitation.
[SAMIN-DURR:89:4#2/r3] ابن كثير | reports | Ibn Kathir retains the yā’ in both pausal and connected forms; the other readers omit it in both.
[SAMIN-DURR:89:4#2/r4] author | holds | Retention follows the original imperfect ending; omission follows the written muṣḥaf and verse cadence, like rhyme, while readers who distinguish pause treat it as a resting point.
[SAMIN-DURR:89:4#2/r5] الأخفش | reports | Al-Akhfash says night is figuratively described as traveling, though what is meant is that something travels through it.
[SAMIN-DURR:89:4#2/r6] author | reports | Another interpretation is that the night wanes, as in ‘idha adbara’ and ‘idha ‘as‘asa’.  | mentions 74:33, 81:17
[SAMIN-DURR:89:4/r3] أبو الدينار الأعرابي | reports | Most readers do not nunate ‘al-fajr’, ‘al-watr’, and ‘yasr’; Abu al-Dinar al-A‘rabi nunates all three, treating verse endings like poetic rhymes.
[SAMIN-DURR:89:4/r4] ابن خالويه | reports | Ibn Khalawayh calls the poetic nunation ‘tanwin al-tarannum’, including nunation at verse endings even for verbs and definite nouns; his quoted couplet illustrates it.
[SAMIN-DURR:89:4/r5] author | holds | Some grammarians object to the label ‘tanwin al-tarannum’ and prefer ‘tanwin tark al-tarannum’; another type, ‘tanwin al-ghali’, appears on a closed rhyme and is disputed, with different rules from ordinary nunation.
[SAMIN-DURR:89:4/r6] author | holds | The reader has treated the Qur’anic endings like rhyme endings; the author cites similar forms in al-Ahzab and al-Ra‘d as parallels. | mentions 33:66, 33:67, 33:10, 13:9

## BIQAI-FULL: al-Biqāʿī, d. 885 AH
[BIQAI-FULL:v8p413#2/r6] author | holds | The night of departure passes as worldly night passes and dawn replaces its darkness; pilgrims return to their homes after laying down their burdens.
[BIQAI-FULL:v8p413#2/r7] author | holds | Ibn Kathir and Yaʿqub retain the final yāʾ in yasri, while the other readers omit it.
[BIQAI-FULL:v8p413#2/r8] الأخفش | reports | Asked why the final yāʾ was omitted, al-Akhfash answered that night is traveled in rather than itself traveling, so the altered sense requires an altered form; the author says this entails rejecting transmitted affirmation readings. | mentions 19:28
[BIQAI-FULL:v8p414/r1] الأخفش | reports | Al-Akhfash analogizes the omitted yāʾ in yasri to baghiy, ‘unchaste,’ rather than baghiya, ‘unchaste woman’; the author says this linguistic rationale rejects transmitted readings that affirm the yāʾ. | mentions 19:28
[BIQAI-FULL:v8p414/r2] author | holds | Omission may signal pilgrims who remain at the sacred sites, savoring the night, while affirmation may signal those whose full night has passed as they return home at dawn.
[BIQAI-FULL:v8p414/r3] author | holds | Alternatively, omission points to a short night and affirmation to a long one, with worshippers standing through the long night before the King.
[BIQAI-FULL:v8p414/r4] تقي الدين بن دقيق العيد | reports | Ibn Daqiq al-ʿId’s verse evokes nights of traveling through the night without sleep or rest, supporting the long-night interpretation.
[BIQAI-FULL:v8p414/r5] أبو طالب | reports | Abu Talib’s poem invokes the night at Muzdalifah and the stations of Mina as sacred gathering places.
[BIQAI-FULL:v8p414/r6] author | holds | Differences in night length and in the travelers’ situations show that one sovereign Creator freely controls all these signs and can destroy tyrants.

## BURSEVI: İsmāʿīl Ḥaqqī Bursevī, d. 1127 AH
[BURSEVI:v10p326#2/r1] author | holds | The phrase about night passing can mean travelers move through it; night protects them from heat and often from highwaymen, and the Prophet recommended night travel because the earth is folded then.
[BURSEVI:v10p326#2/r2] الأخفش | reports | When asked about the missing yā in “yasr,” al-Akhfash first had no answer, then explained that night is traveled through rather than traveling, so the form was altered to mark the shift in meaning.
