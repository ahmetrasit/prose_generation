## BURSEVI: İsmāʿīl Ḥaqqī Bursevī, d. 1127 AH
[BURSEVI:v10p336/r10] author | holds | The preposition fi suggests the person is surrounded by hardship as a vessel is by its contents; humanity undergoes successive pains from birth, circumcision, schooling, marriage, and children.
[BURSEVI:v10p336/r9] author | holds | Kabid originally describes an aching, swollen liver, then broadens to all hardship; humans are enveloped in toil from the womb through death and what follows.

## ALUSI-FULL: al-Ālūsī, d. 1270 AH
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p135/r1] author | prefers | Human beings continually endure hardship from the infusion of the spirit through death and beyond; the word spread from pain in the liver to every kind of toil, and the verse's likely sense is hardship.
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p135/r2] لبيد | reports | Labid's elegy for his brother uses fi kabad for the severity and difficulty of a situation, supporting the sense of hardship.
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p135/r3] ابن عمر | reports | Ibn ʿUmar says people struggle to show gratitude in prosperity and patience in adversity.
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p135/r4] ابن عباس وعبد الله بن شداد وأبو صالح والضحاك ومجاهد | reports | These authorities explain that God made humans stand upright rather than crawl face-down; Alusi calls this and the related fetal-position account weak.
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p135/r5] ابن كيسان | reports | Ibn Kaysan says the fetus is upright in its mother's womb until birth, when it turns its head toward her feet; Alusi groups this with weak interpretations.
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p135/r6] author | reports | A possible third interpretation makes kabad a severe illness, namely sickness of the heart and inward corruption; the human then means those God knew would never believe, while other readings take all humanity. | mentions 90:1, 90:2
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p135/r7] ابن زيد | reports | Ibn Zayd says the human is Adam and kabad is the sky or its middle; Alusi rejects this outright.

## ELMALILI: Elmalılı Muhammed Hamdi Yazır, d. 1361 AH
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#2/r1] Sâhib-Keşşâf | reports | Al-Kashshaf derives kabad from liver pain or swelling, then its sense broadens to toil and hardship; mukabada, struggling against hardship, comes from it.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#2/r10] Râzî | reports | Afterlife hardship proceeds through death, questioning by angels, the grave, resurrection, and standing before God until one reaches Paradise or Hell.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#2/r11] Râzî | reports | Razi adds that worldly pleasures are only relief from pain: food relieves hunger and clothes relieve heat or cold, so life alternates between pain and respite.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#2/r12] Râzî | holds | Because a wise Creator would not make pain the ultimate purpose, and nonexistence would equally avoid pleasure and pain, human hardship implies a necessary afterlife of happiness and honor.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#2/r2] author | reports | Others make severity the basic meaning, citing the thickening or solidifying of milk; this reverses the first account’s derivation from the organ to hardship.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#2/r3] some exegetes | reports | Another view takes kabad as uprightness or steadiness, perhaps from the straight liver or the expression “kabad of heaven”; it sees humans standing upright and spiritually capable of ascent. | mentions 87:2
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#2/r4] author | rejects | Elmalılı considers deriving the uprightness sense from the vowelled form kabad a very remote interpretation.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#2/r5] some exegetes | reports | A third view reads kabad as the strength of human constitution, with God giving the body strength; this can be included within the hardship sense.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#2/r6] Keşfü’l-Esrâr | reports | Kashf al-Asrar offers a physiological analogy of bodily membranes, vessels, and circulation to illustrate how life and formation emerge through stages of condensation.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#2/r7] Râzî | reports | On the hardship reading, Razi says the verse can encompass worldly, religious, and afterlife trials, or all of them together; the comprehensive reading is correct. | mentions 76:1
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#2/r8] Râzî | reports | Worldly hardship covers each stage from passage through the loins and womb, nursing and puberty, earning a livelihood, and finally death.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#2/r9] Hasan | reports | Religious hardship means giving thanks in joy, remaining patient in distress, and laboring to fulfill acts of worship.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#3/r1] İbn Abbas | reports | A second interpretation takes kabad as uprightness: unlike animals that move head-down, humans stand erect, which is a blessing in their creation.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#3/r16] author | holds | God’s gifts guide humanity toward its purpose and establish divine knowledge and power to reckon and punish; the contrast between tranquil and unbelieving souls further explains hardship.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#3/r2] Kelbî | reports | Al-Kalbi says the verse was revealed about Abu al-Ashadd of Banu Jumah, whose strength was said to keep him immovable even when leather was pulled from beneath his feet.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#3/r3] author | holds | Although a particular occasion of revelation is possible, “human” means humanity in general, not a single named person; the hardship interpretation is the intended one.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#3/r4] Âlûsî | reports | Al-Alusi regards all interpretations besides hardship and toil as weak; he reports that al-Hakim authenticated the hardship report from Ibn Abbas and others transmitted it from early authorities.
[ELMALILI:90:3-7#3/r5] author | holds | The verse first consoles and encourages the Prophet by stating that hardship is built into human life, then rebukes arrogant opponents who disrespect him; the earlier promise of success is recalled. | mentions 90:2
[ELMALILI:90:3-7/r12] author | holds | The majority’s sense of kabad is that humanity was created amid hardship: life develops through severe trials, by divine care, so people should endure, give thanks, and show mercy to sufferers.

## QUTB-ZILAL: Sayyid Quṭb, d. 1386 AH
[QUTB-ZILAL:90:4/r1] author | holds | Human life is continual struggle, hardship, effort, and toil, beginning in the womb and continuing through birth, breathing, bodily functions, movement, learning, thought, and every new experience. | mentions 84:6
[QUTB-ZILAL:90:4/r2] author | holds | People’s toil takes many forms and ends in different directions: some strive for God, faith, and good, while others pursue desire, wealth, power, or Hell.
[QUTB-ZILAL:90:4/r3] author | holds | Worldly struggle is life’s nature, but the worst loser bears it only to reach greater hardship in the next life; the successful person strives toward God and ultimate rest.
[QUTB-ZILAL:90:4/r4] author | holds | Even on earth, noble striving brings inner calm, relief in sacrifice, and a different experience of hardship than toil spent on trivial or base aims.
