## ASAD-NOTES: Muhammad Asad, d. 1412 AH
[ASAD-NOTES:102:1#n1/r1] author | holds | Takathur means greedily striving to increase benefits, whether tangible or intangible, real or illusory.
[ASAD-NOTES:102:1#n1/r2] author | holds | Here it means obsessively seeking more comforts, material goods, and power over other people.
[ASAD-NOTES:102:1/r1] author | holds | An exclusive pursuit of these gains blocks spiritual insight and acceptance of limits grounded in moral values.
[ASAD-NOTES:102:1/r2] author | holds | The pursuit gradually destabilizes individuals and whole societies, eliminating their chance of happiness.
[ASAD-NOTES:102:1/r3] author | holds | Unrestrained economic growth destroys the natural environment and brings frustration, unhappiness, and confusion to people losing their remaining spiritual orientation.
[ASAD-NOTES:102:1/r4] author | holds | The unchecked pursuit of economic growth has brought frustration and confusion and threatens the remnants of spiritual and religious orientation.
[ASAD-NOTES:102:intro/r1] author | holds | This early Meccan surah is a powerful prophetic passage about humanity's unbounded greed.
[ASAD-NOTES:102:intro/r2] author | holds | It especially addresses greed-driven tendencies that have come to dominate technological societies.

## BINTSHATI: ʿĀʾisha ʿAbd al-Raḥmān (Bint al-Shāṭiʾ), d. 1419 AH
[BINTSHATI:v1p195/r1] author | holds | The Meccan surah is commonly dated as the sixteenth in revelation, after al-Kawthar; its concise, emphatic warning dominates its tone.
[BINTSHATI:v1p195/r2] النيسابوري | reports | Al-Nīsābūrī links this surah to al-Qāriʿah; the author rejects a chronological link because Takāthur precedes it by thirteen surahs, while allowing a shared warning tone in the muṣḥaf arrangement. | mentions 101:1
[BINTSHATI:v1p198/r3] author | holds | Ḥattā marks the endpoint: accumulation ends at the cemeteries, and the abrupt movement from rivalry to graves completes the warning.
[BINTSHATI:v1p198/r4] author | holds | The Qur’an uses ziyārah only here and the related form tazāwar in al-Kahf; the root also denotes falsehood or deviation from truth. | mentions 18:17, 25:4, 25:72, 22:30
[BINTSHATI:v1p199/r1] author | holds | The sensory root of z-w-r is inclination or curvature; its derivatives describe turning toward someone, a dream-vision, or deviation from truth. | mentions 58:2
[BINTSHATI:v1p199/r2] بعض المفسرين | reports | One interpretation takes the visit literally: people went to the graves to count their dead.
[BINTSHATI:v1p199/r3] أبو حيان | rejects | A second interpretation treats visiting as metaphorical mention of dead ancestors while boasting; Abū Ḥayyān rejects it because the wording visit is awkward for that sense.
[BINTSHATI:v1p199/r4] بنو سهم وبنو عبد مناف | reports | A reported occasion says these clans competed over numbers, then counted their dead as well as the living after one claimed losses in pre-Islamic feuding.
[BINTSHATI:v1p199/r5] الطبري | prefers | A third interpretation takes visiting the graves as dying; al-Ṭabarī adopts it, citing familiar Arabic usage and poetry by Jarīr.
[BINTSHATI:v1p200/r1] author | prefers | Reading grave-visiting as death implies a temporary stay: visitors leave, so the wording itself hints at resurrection, reckoning, and recompense.
[BINTSHATI:v1p200/r2] أعرابي | reports | A Bedouin who heard the verse inferred resurrection, saying visitors depart rather than remain.
[BINTSHATI:v1p200/r3] عمر بن عبد العزيز | reports | ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz is also reported to have expressed a view like the Bedouin’s insight.
[BINTSHATI:v1p200/r4] أبو حيان | reports | Abū Ḥayyān passes quickly over the Bedouin’s insight and instead cites a view that the verse rebukes boasting through repeated cemetery visits, distinguishing later permitted visits for admonition.
[BINTSHATI:v1p200/r5] author | holds | Al-Maqābir appears only in this verse, while qubūr appears five times and the singular qabr is used in al-Tawbah. | mentions 9:84
[BINTSHATI:v1p201/r1] author | prefers | Although maqābir suits takāthur in sound, its stronger reason is semantic: it evokes the vast collective cemetery across ages and classes, broader than individual graves. | mentions 9:84
[BINTSHATI:v1p201/r2] author | rejects | Reducing maqābir to a lexical synonym for graves misses its broad setting and the verse’s compressed journey from worldly rivalry to death.
