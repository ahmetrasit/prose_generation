## BURSEVI: İsmāʿīl Ḥaqqī Bursevī, d. 1127 AH
[BURSEVI:v10p325#2/r1] author | holds | The false dawn resembles a wolf’s tail and carries no legal ruling; the true dawn triggers fasting and prayer. Its emergence spreads light and sends people and animals out for provision, resembling resurrection.
[BURSEVI:v10p325#2/r2] الكاشفي | reports | Kashifi interprets dawn as the time when beloveds commune with God.
[BURSEVI:v10p325#2/r3] author | reports | Other interpretations identify dawn as the morning of Arafah, when pilgrims go to the plain and perform supplications, citing the report “Hajj is Arafah.”
[BURSEVI:v10p325#2/r4] author | reports | Dawn may instead mean the Day of Sacrifice, a great day for the required circumambulation, shaving, and stoning; it is reported as the greater Hajj day.
[BURSEVI:v10p325#2/r5] التبيان | reports | Tabyan offers further dawn images: the New Year, Friday as the poor person’s pilgrimage, miraculous water from the Prophet’s fingers or Moses’ stone, Salih’s she-camel from rock, springs, rain, or sinners’ tears.
[BURSEVI:v10p325#2/r6] author | holds | The dawn imagery also urges repentance: if you carry a stain, wash it away with streams of tears.

## ALUSI-FULL: al-Ālūsī, d. 1270 AH
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p119/r1] علي وابن عباس وابن الزبير | reports | The dawn is the familiar dawn, as transmitted from Ali, Ibn Abbas, Ibn al-Zubayr, and others; Alusi considers the true dawn especially fitting for the oath because it governs fasting and prayer.
[ALUSI-FULL:v30p119/r2] author | holds | Dawn is named for splitting the night broadly; false dawn does not determine fasting or prayer, whereas true dawn does, and the likely referent is the true dawn in general.

## ELMALILI: Elmalılı Muhammed Hamdi Yazır, d. 1361 AH
[ELMALILI:89:1-3#2/r1] author | holds | Fajr is the first whiteness as night darkness breaks: the false dawn is vertical and the true dawn horizontal; fasting and prayer rulings attach only to the true dawn.
[ELMALILI:89:1-3#2/r10] author | reports | Jabir reports from the Prophet that the oath’s dawn and ten nights refer to the Adha ten; al-Tabari says this is correct because the authorities of interpretation agree.
[ELMALILI:89:1-3#2/r2] author | holds | Dawn is the joyful instant when the world begins moving from darkness into light, so the generic dawn is a meaningful oath, as in 81:18. | mentions 81:18
[ELMALILI:89:1-3#2/r3] author | reports | Some take the article as specific and understand the dawn of a blessed Friday or feast; Mujahid’s reported view most immediately points to the tenth of Dhul Hijjah, the Adha feast day.
[ELMALILI:89:1-3#2/r4] author | prefers | Although generic dawn includes the feast dawn, the particular dawn more strongly conveys the collective joy of the occasion.
[ELMALILI:89:1-3#3/r11] author | prefers | The reports identifying Dhul Hijjah’s nights, Arafah, and the sacrifice day best fit the sequence and surah’s purpose, include the possible senses, and urge worship during life’s limited span to attain afterlife joy.
[ELMALILI:89:1-3/r1] author | holds | The opening oaths direct attention to temporal events that show worldly change, move from darkness to light and grief to joy, and indicate the Creator’s lordship.
[ELMALILI:89:1-3/r2] author | reports | Commentators offer many views on the definite or indefinite wording, the scope of the articles, and the identification and interpretation of the oath objects.

## IBNASHUR: Ibn ʿĀshūr, d. 1393 AH
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r1] author | holds | The surah is consistently titled Surat al-Fajr without an initial waw in Qurans, tafsirs, and hadith books.
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r10] author | holds | The extended oath builds anticipation for the assertion that follows.
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r11] author | holds | Fajr is the first dawn light at the eastern horizon, the initial solar ray that pushes back darkness before illuminating the horizon and preceding sunrise; it is a striking sign of divine power and artistry.
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r12] author | holds | Dawn is blessed because it ends sleep, likened to death, and people regain awareness and turn to their familiar useful work.
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r13] author | prefers | The definite article in al-fajr is generic, the reading that best suits its coordination with the night in verse 4. | mentions 89:4
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r14] قتادة | reports | One reported specific referent for al-fajr is the daily dawn prayer time.
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r15] ابن عباس وعطاء وعكرمة | reports | Another reported referent is the dawn of the Sacrifice day, when pilgrims are at Muzdalifa; on this reading the article is definite by prior reference.
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r2] author | holds | It is Meccan by agreement, except for a report by Ibn Atiyya from Abu Amr al-Dani that some scholars considered it Medinan.
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r3] author | holds | It was the tenth surah revealed, after al-Layl and before al-Duha.
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r4] author | holds | Verse counts differ by regional tradition: Medina and Mecca count 32, Kufa and Syria 30, and Basra 29, with different verse endings assigned to نعمه, رزقه, بجهنم, and في عبادي.
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r5] author | holds | The surah warns Meccan polytheists through the examples of Ad, Thamud, and Pharaoh's people, promises the Prophet his enemies' decline, and warns of the afterlife.
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r6] author | holds | It refutes the prosperous polytheists' belief that wealth means divine honor and believers' poverty means humiliation: they failed to share, grew greedier, and will regret not preparing through faith and good works.
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r7] author | holds | The oaths invoke times that display God's craftsmanship and power, especially dawn's transition from night to day and fully dark night, as well as times of worship such as the ten, even, and odd nights. | mentions 89:2, 89:3, 89:4
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r8] author | holds | The oath confirms the assertion: an oath calls God to witness the statement, and God's oaths serve confirmation alone.
[IBNASHUR:89:1/r9] author | holds | The address is directed to the Prophet, as the later references to God's acts toward Ad and God's watchfulness indicate; the oath indirectly assures deniers that its assertion will occur. | mentions 89:6, 89:14
