## QURTUBI-FULL: al-Qurṭubī, d. 671 AH
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9#2/r1] ابن عمر | reports | The Prophet says God draws a believer close, makes him acknowledge his sins, then forgives him and gives him his record of good deeds; disbelievers and hypocrites are publicly exposed.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9#2/r2] author | holds | The believer’s delivery of a record of good deeds proves deeds are written in records and weighed.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9#2/r3] عبد الله بن عمرو | reports | A hadith describes a man’s ninety-nine vast records outweighed by a small card affirming God’s oneness and Muhammad’s messengership; al-Tirmidhi adds that nothing outweighs God’s name and grades it hasan gharib.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9#2/r4] author | holds | The plural ‘balances’ may mean multiple scales, one for each class of deed, or a single balance expressed in the plural, like travel by ships or mules.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9#2/r5] author | reports | Another grammatical interpretation takes ‘balances’ as the plural of weighed things, meaning the deeds being weighed rather than the scales themselves.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9#3/r1] القشيري | reports | Al-Qushayri reports that the Prophet places a small card in the balance when a believer’s good deeds are light; it bears the reward of the believer’s prayers for the Prophet, repaid when most needed.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9#3/r2] القشيري | reports | Al-Qushayri explains that bitāqa, with kasra on its first letter, means a small slip recording goods in Egyptian usage.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9#3/r3] محمد بن يحيى | reports | Ibn Majah’s Muhammad ibn Yahya likewise says bitāqa means a slip of paper, as Egyptians call a slip.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9/r1] author | holds | ‘The weighing that day is true’ can be parsed as subject and predicate, with ‘true’ as an adjective, or with ‘true’ as an accusative verbal noun.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9/r10] author | prefers | Qurtubi’s preferred account is that deeds written in records are placed in the balance’s pans, as the report of the shahada card demonstrates.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9/r11] صفوان بن محرز | reports | A Sahih Muslim narration from Safwan preserves Ibn Umar’s report of the Prophet’s conversation with God about the believer’s record.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9/r2] author | holds | The verse means deeds are weighed on the balance; Ibn Umar says the records of people’s deeds are weighed, which Qurtubi calls correct based on transmitted reports.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9/r3] author | reports | Another view says the balance is the book in which people’s deeds are written.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9/r4] مجاهد | reports | Mujahid says the good and evil deeds themselves are weighed.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9/r5] مجاهد والضحاك والأعمش | reports | Mujahid, al-Dahhak, and al-A‘mash take weighing and balance as metaphors for justice and judgment, as ordinary speech uses weight to mean equivalence.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9/r6] الزجاج | reports | Al-Zajjaj accepts the metaphor as linguistically sound, but Qurtubi prefers the sound reports affirming a real balance.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9/r7] القشيري | reports | Al-Qushayri argues that metaphorizing the balance would also invite metaphorizing the Path, Paradise and Fire, jinn, and angels; the early community accepted these descriptions literally.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9/r8] المعتزلة | reports | The Mu‘tazilites deny the balance because accidents cannot be weighed and do not subsist independently.
[QURTUBI-FULL:7:8-9/r9] بعض المتكلمين | reports | Some theologians say God turns accidents into bodies for weighing; Qurtubi rejects this and holds that written records make the scales heavier or lighter.

## BAYDAWI-FULL: al-Bayḍāwī, d. 685 AH
[BAYDAWI-FULL:7:8/r1] author | reports | Al-wazn may mean judgment or the weighing of deeds by comparison with their recompense.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:7:8/r2] author | reports | Most scholars hold that records of deeds are weighed on a balance with a tongue and two pans, publicly demonstrating justice and removing excuses, as people's deeds are also elicited and acknowledged by speech and limbs.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:7:8/r3] author | reports | A report describes a man given ninety-nine scrolls, each as far as the eye can reach, and a card bearing the two testimonies; the card outweighs the scrolls.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:7:8/r4] رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Another view weighs people themselves, supported by the prophetic report that a huge, heavy man will weigh no more than a gnat's wing with God.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:7:8/r5] author | holds | Al-haqq is either an adjective describing the weighing or the predicate of an omitted subject; it means just and sound.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:7:8/r6] author | holds | Heavy scales mean good deeds or what weighs them; the plural reflects differing weighed things or multiple weighings, and the successful are those saved and rewarded.

[NASAFI-FULL:v2p4#3/r1] author | holds | The weighing is the weighing and distinguishing of deeds by relative heaviness; يومئذ means the day God questions nations and messengers, with the clause omitted and replaced by nunation.
[NASAFI-FULL:v2p4#3/r2] author | reports | One view says records of deeds will be weighed on a scale with a tongue and two pans, manifesting the attribute and ending excuses; another takes the phrase as just judgment, while God knows its manner.

## QASHANI: ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Qāshānī, d. 736 AH
[QASHANI:7:8-9/r1] author | holds | Weighing is assessment of deeds at the lesser resurrection and is just or real; lasting good deeds weigh heavily and yield the heart's paradise of divine attributes.
[QASHANI:7:8-9/r2] author | holds | The scale's tongue is justice, with sense and intellect as its pans; enduring rational acquisitions, virtues, good works, and sincere intentions have weight, unlike transient sensations, desires, and vices.
