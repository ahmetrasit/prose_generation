# 6:76: 28 questions

## 6:76/q01 How does the nightfall scene connect to the preceding account?  [فَلَمَّا · links]
turns on: Whether the fāʾ resumes the vision of the kingdom in 6:75 or Abraham's speech to his father in 6:74.
- 6:76/q01/p1 It elaborates God's showing Abraham the heavenly kingdom and the ensuing reasoning. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya takes the fāʾ as linking a summary to its detail; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the star belongs to that kingdom and rejects a remote link across a parenthesis. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī places it after rejection of idols and God's showing of creation's dependence; Islāḥī says celestial obedience to divine law expands the preceding account. [10 notes: THALABI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(ابن عطية); JISHUMI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNASHUR-FULL+]
- 6:76/q01/p2 It resumes Abraham's earlier speech to his father; 'Thus We showed' is parenthetical. — Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, al-Samīn and some investigators cited by al-Ālūsī take 6:75 as an intervening parenthesis; al-Samīn prefers this. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL(صاحب الكشاف); BAYDAWI-FULL(قيل); NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(بعض المحققين)+; SAMIN-DURR+; IBNASHUR-FULL-]
- 6:76/q01/p3 This episode is distinct from the vision of the kingdom. — Al-Qurṭubī says the nightfall is a different story. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]

## 6:76/q02 What does جَنَّ عَلَيْهِ ٱلَّيْلُ mean, and what does its root suggest?  [جَنَّ ٱلَّيْلُ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the phrase stresses mere covering, the onset of darkness, or a figurative inner night.
- 6:76/q02/p1 Night covered or concealed Abraham and darkened over him. — Abū ʿUbayda, Mujāz, al-Ṭabarī, al-Zajjāj, al-Māwardī, al-Wāḥidī and later authors give this core sense. Poems by Durayd, a Hudhalī, ʿUbayd, and an anonymous poet illustrate covering darkness. The root explains jinn hidden from sight, and the garden, fetus, madness, shield, grave or heart as concealed or concealing; al-Rāghib says the root means concealment from the senses. Ibn ʿĀshūr pictures darkness like a garment and infers Abraham was outdoors; al-Biqāʿī says the brief verb intensifies darkness and ʿalā pictures it falling over him. [44 notes: MAJAZ; AKHFASH(الشاعر); TAB-FULL(الهذلي,عبيد); ZAJJAJ; THALABI(أبو عبيدة); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; DAMGHANI; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(الهذلي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); RAZI-FULL(الواحدي,بعض النحويين); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الراغب); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(الراغب)]
- 6:76/q02/p2 It is the darkness of bodily nature in childhood and early youth. — Al-Qāshānī's inward reading treats night as the body-bound state before spiritual ascent. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 6:76/q03 What verbal forms and syntactic construction underlie جَنَّ عَلَيْهِ?  [جَنَّ عَلَيْهِ · grammar]
turns on: How the simple and derived verbs differ in transitivity, darkness, and eloquence.
- 6:76/q03/p1 Janna ʿalayhi describes darkness falling; jannahu describes covering without the extra sense of darkening. — Al-Zajjāj prefers janna ʿalayhi and ajannahu; al-Jishumī distinguishes janna ʿalayhi from jannahu. Al-Samīn says the best usage makes the simple form intransitive and derived form transitive, while allowing both as transitive; al-Rāghib distinguishes providing a cover from covering itself. Al-Ṭabarī notes dialects: Asad uses jannah al-layl, Tamīm ajannah and jannah; his preferred alif forms and al-Jishumī's preferred non-alif form differ. Akhfash cites poems and junūn/جن as verbal nouns; Qays/Tamīm forms for concealment and 2:235, 37:49 are parallels. [20 notes: MAJAZ(بعضهم); AKHFASH(الشاعر); TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الراغب)]
- 6:76/q03/p2 The verb جَنَّ can be transitive with an omitted object, the visible things covered by night. — Al-Samīn cites a poem about night covering water as evidence for the less usual transitive form. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 6:76/q04 Where and under what circumstances did Abraham first see the star?  [فَلَمَّا جَنَّ عَلَيْهِ ٱلَّيْلُ رَءَا كَوْكَبًا · reports]
turns on: The sources offer different cave, emergence, travel, and observational circumstances.
- 6:76/q04/p1 He emerged from a cave at night or sunset and then saw the star. — Muqātil puts it at month's end; al-Suddī places the emergence after sunset. Ibn Isḥāq and historians say he recognized a creator from creatures after leaving the cave. Some accounts say parents brought him out and he reasoned from animals; a variant has him see the star through the cave's opening or split rock. Ibn ʿAṭiyya's tale says he first became aware of the world then. These versions differ in details. [16 notes: MUQATIL; IBNABIHATIM(السدي); WAHIDI-BASIT(السدي ومحمد بن إسحاق والعلماء بأخبار الماضين); BAGHAWI-FULL(إبراهيم); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); IBNKATHIR-FULL(محمد بن إسحاق); RAZI-FULL(أكثر المفسرين); BURSEVI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR-]
- 6:76/q04/p2 He was with his people observing the sky and arguing against their star worship. — Ibn ʿĀshūr infers a group from the later address yā qawm; he identifies Chaldean Sabians. Islāḥī says Abraham's words were audible self-talk before listeners; an al-Ālūsī report places him amid a debate with people urging planet worship. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī suggests events across about two days and one night. [5 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); TABATABAI]
- 6:76/q04/p3 The star had just risen or become visible in the night. — Al-Māwardī calls it Venus rising at dusk; al-Biqāʿī says it appeared or rose. The star could have been a bright object noticed incidentally near mid-sky, Ibn ʿĀshūr says, or deliberate looking may be elided as in 26:63. [3 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(مجاهد); BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 6:76/q04/p4 The star was already near setting, revealed only after the sun's light ceased obscuring it. — Bursevī and the Shaykh al-Islām cited by al-Ālūsī deny this was its first rising; the star became visible as daylight faded. [3 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(شيخ الاسلام)]
- 6:76/q04/p5 Abraham watched the star through the cave roof or opening. — The cave accounts differ over an opening versus a crack in the stone over its entrance. [2 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(إبراهيم); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]

## 6:76/q05 What reports describe Abraham's birth, concealment, and age at this episode?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports differ on cave or riverbed, care, duration of concealment, and age.
- 6:76/q05/p1 Nimrod's astrologers forecast a boy who would change religion and topple or kill him, prompting an order to kill newborn boys. — Ibn Qutayba says astrology and divination dominated; al-Thaʿlabī adds Nimrod ibn Canaan as first crown-wearer and a forecast said to be in prophetic books. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Ibn al-Jawzī recount watching pregnant women and killing boys; Ibn Isḥāq relates the warning. [6 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; THALABI(الكهان والمنجمون,المفسرون); IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو صالح عن ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR-FULL(محمد بن إسحاق)]
- 6:76/q05/p2 His mother concealed his birth in a cave and left him hidden there. — Versions have a cave birth while his father was away; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says he sucked fingers that yielded honey and butter, while another says an angel fed him, or his mother brought milk from bereaved women. Al-Rāzī says Gabriel's miraculous care is possible, disputing the Qāḍī's objection to pre-mission marvels; his school allows irhāṣ. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ālūsī preserve the cave tradition. [10 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL(أصحابنا,أكثر المفسرين,القاضي); TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); IBNKATHIR-FULL(محمد بن إسحاق)]
- 6:76/q05/p3 As a child he questioned his parents and Nimrod about their makers. — One account has the adults silence him; another has his mother call herself his lord, then his father and Nimrod, and rebuke dangerous questions. Ibn al-Jawzī's report places the parents' conversation inside the hiding place. [6 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(إبراهيم); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو صالح عن ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL(أكثر المفسرين); BURSEVI]
- 6:76/q05/p4 He remained hidden or argued at seventeen years. — Al-Baghawī lists seventeen cave years; al-Qurṭubī says he was seventeen when he argued with Nimrod; Bursevī repeats a seventeen-year cave account. [3 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BURSEVI]
- 6:76/q05/p5 He remained hidden for seven years. — Al-Baghawī lists seven among three cave durations; al-Qurṭubī separately gives his age as seven. [2 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]
- 6:76/q05/p6 He remained hidden or reached the event at thirteen years. — Al-Baghawī and Bursevī record thirteen cave years, with Bursevī's Persian account saying he looked eighteen at eighteen months. [2 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); BURSEVI]
- 6:76/q05/p7 He remained hidden for fifteen years, or was fifteen years old. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya gives ten or fifteen hidden years and Ibn Isḥāq, cited by al-Qurṭubī, gives fifteen as his age; the former also notes unusually fast growth. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(محمد بن إسحاق)]
- 6:76/q05/p8 His mother hid him in a dry riverbed among reeds; his father made a sealed tunnel there. — The Ibn ʿAbbās report through Abū Ṣāliḥ says his mother nursed him in that tunnel. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو صالح عن ابن عباس)]
- 6:76/q05/p9 He remained hidden for ten years. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya gives ten as an alternative to fifteen, with unusually fast growth. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 6:76/q05/p10 He appeared mature at eighteen months, resembling an eighteen-year-old. — Bursevī transmits this Persian account, and reports how a relative revealed him to Āzar, who admired his beauty and was told to bring him to Nimrod. [3 notes: BURSEVI]

## 6:76/q06 What is the star identified as?  [كَوْكَبًا · referent]
turns on: The transmitted identifications are Venus and Jupiter; some keep the star indefinite.
- 6:76/q06/p1 Venus. — Attributed variously to Zayd b. ʿAlī, Qatādah, Ibn ʿAbbās or Mujāhid; Muqātil places it near month's end. Several authors transmit Venus as one of two alternatives, and Islāḥī allows Venus or another star. [17 notes: MUQATIL; IBNABIHATIM(زيد بن علي); THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL(مجاهد); WAHIDI-BASIT(السدي ومحمد بن إسحاق والعلماء بأخبار الماضين); BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس وقتادة); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(زيد بن علي); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(قتادة); IBNASHUR-FULL(زيد بن علي); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 6:76/q06/p2 Jupiter. — Attributed variously to al-Suddī, Mujāhid or Ibn ʿAbbās; reports say it appeared at sunset toward the direction of prayer, and the moon rose late in the month. [16 notes: IBNABIHATIM(السدي); THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(السدي ومحمد بن إسحاق والعلماء بأخبار الماضين); BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(مجاهد والسدي); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(مجاهد); DURR-FULL(السدي); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL(السدي)]
- 6:76/q06/p3 No specific star is intended; the argument works for any setting star. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the indefinite noun deliberately avoids a particular identity and does not mean an arbitrary pointing at one of countless stars. [1 notes: TABATABAI]

## 6:76/q07 What does كَوْكَبًا mean and how is it formed?  [كَوْكَبًا · meaning]
turns on: The lexicographers differ over whether the initial kāf or wāw belongs to the root.
- 6:76/q07/p1 It is a star or shining celestial light. — Abū Ḥayyān notes a broader sense for something shining with fire; Ibn Kathīr gives the ordinary gloss. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 6:76/q07/p2 It is quadriliteral, with original wāw. — Al-Jawharī, perhaps following al-Layth, lists it as kawkab. Al-Samīn also reports an analysis as a rare repeated-radical quadriliteral on faʿfal, like fawfal. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجوهري والليث); SAMIN-DURR]
- 6:76/q07/p3 The initial kāf is augmentative, so the root is wāw-kāf-bāʾ. — Al-Ṣāghānī proposes this. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn dispute the alleged precedent Hindakī: the plural hanādik may be a longer paired form; Abū Ḥayyān instead takes Hindakī as an Ethiopian-derived nisba and denies that precise grammarians admit initial added kāf. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الصاغاني); ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ)-]
- 6:76/q07/p4 The word derives from k-b/b-k-b, with an extra wāw and substitution. — Al-Rāghib proposes derivation from kabba and kabkaba; al-Samīn calls it very strange. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR(الراغب)]

## 6:76/q08 How is رَءَا pronounced in the variant readings, and why?  [رَءَا · readings]
turns on: Full opening, full or partial imāla, and mixed vowel treatments depend on transmitted route and whether the following alif remains pronounced.
- 6:76/q08/p1 Both rāʾ and hamza are inclined toward kasra by imāla. — Abū Bakr from ʿĀṣim, Ibn ʿĀmir, Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī and Khalaf appear in transmissions, with contextual differences. Imāla starts with original yāʾ and spreads to hamza and neighboring rāʾ; Abū ʿAmr also has a transmitted both-kasra route. Al-Fārisī adduces the imperfect pattern and guttural hamza for rāʾ kasra. Al-Wāḥidī says a dropped alif can remain conceptually present; al-Samīn details additional routes. [17 notes: IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; FARISI-HUJJA(أبو عمرو,القطعي عن عبيد بن عقيل عن أبي عمرو,عاصم في رواية أبي بكر وابن عامر وحمزة والكسائي); WAHIDI-BASIT(يحيى بن آدم عن أبي بكر); JISHUMI(ابن عامر وحمزة والكسائي,يحيى عن أبي بكر عن عاصم); BAGHAWI-FULL(أبو عمرو وابن عامر وحمزة والكسائي وأبو بكر); TABRISI(ابن عامر وحمزة والكسائي وخلف ويحيى عن أبي بكر); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن كثير وحفص عن عاصم); ALUSI-FULL(أبو عمرو وورش من طريق البخاري؛ ابن عامر وحمزة والكسائي وخلف ويحيى عن أبي بكر); SAMIN-DURR]
- 6:76/q08/p2 The rāʾ is open and hamza is inclined or kasra-marked. — Abū ʿAmr and Warsh from Nāfiʿ are named. Al-Fārisī says Nāfiʿ's intermediate form may incline only hamza or both vowels; Ibn Khālawayh's bayna-bayna lies between dialectal pronunciations. [10 notes: IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; FARISI-HUJJA(أبو عمرو,نافع); JISHUMI(أبو عمرو وورش عن نافع); BAGHAWI-FULL(أبو عمرو وابن عامر وحمزة والكسائي وأبو بكر); TABRISI(أبو عمرو وورش); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن كثير وحفص عن عاصم); ALUSI-FULL(أبو عمرو وورش من طريق البخاري؛ ابن عامر وحمزة والكسائي وخلف ويحيى عن أبي بكر)]
- 6:76/q08/p3 Both rāʾ and hamza are open (fatḥa), without imāla. — Ibn Kathīr and Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim are named; other readers use this in the stated contexts. Al-Fārisī says the original form preserves alif from yāʾ after fatḥa, as with raʿā and ramā; al-Wāḥidī says the loss of the following alif removes the cause of imāla. Ibn Khālawayh prefers tafkhīm before alif-lām because the yāʾ disappears in pronunciation. [10 notes: IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; FARISI-HUJJA(ابن كثير وعاصم في رواية حفص); WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن كثير وحفص عن عاصم)]
- 6:76/q08/p4 The rāʾ is kasra-marked while the hamza stays open. — Al-Wāḥidī reports this for Ḥamza and Abū Bakr in a route, explaining rāʾ independently from hamza imāla; Ibn Khālawayh calls one report weak. Jishumī records this before a connecting alif, and Ibn al-Jawzī notes contexts before vowelless letters or attached pronouns. [5 notes: IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; WAHIDI-BASIT(حمزة وعاصم في رواية أبي بكر); JISHUMI(حمزة ويحيى عن أبي بكر ونصيرا عن الكسائي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو بكر عن عاصم وحمزة)]

## 6:76/q09 How does the first فَلَمَّا clause work syntactically?  [فَلَمَّا رَءَا كَوْكَبًا قَالَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether its answer is 'he saw' or 'he said,' and whether the speech is an independent sentence.
- 6:76/q09/p1 'He saw a star' answers 'when night covered him'; 'he said' begins a new explanatory sentence. — Abū Ḥayyān calls this the apparent parse; al-Samīn prefers independent speech, rejecting an adjective of kawkaban for lack of a linking pronoun and because the star would wrongly be made to speak. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the speech answers the reader's question raised by the sighting. [5 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 6:76/q09/p2 'He said' answers 'when night covered him'; 'he saw a star' is circumstantial. — Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn report this alternative. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]
- 6:76/q09/p3 'He said: this is my lord' qualifies the star as an adjective. — Abū Ḥayyān calls this awkward and al-Samīn rejects it because the necessary pronoun is absent and the sense makes the star speak. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR-]

## 6:76/q10 What does هَٰذَا رَبِّى designate as a claim about lordship?  [هَٰذَا رَبِّى · referent]
turns on: Whether 'lord' means creator and object of worship or a delegated governor; how the demonstrative works.
- 6:76/q10/p1 It identifies a supposed delegated governor of affairs, not the supreme Creator. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the Creator is not a place-bound visible object; Sabians and polytheists assigned domains to subordinate beings. Al-Rāzī records astrologers' claim that God creates planets which generate lower life, while al-Zajjāj says they meant 'this governs me.' Ibn ʿĀshūr describes celestial spirits and possible idol images. [6 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); RAZI-FULL(المنجمون وأصحاب الوسايط); TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 6:76/q10/p2 It identifies a proposed creator, manager and worship-worthy lord. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the definite phrase provisionally singles out one of the people's gods to draw them toward monotheism; its demonstrative can point to an investigated find as well as the visible star, as in 30:56, 12:32, 12:65. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 6:76/q10/p3 The demonstratives portray Abraham as unfamiliar with these luminaries. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says he knew they were worshipped and sacrificed to, but not yet their individual phenomena; he later entertained the star hypothesis until it set. [2 notes: TABATABAI]

## 6:76/q11 How should Abraham's words 'This is my Lord' be understood?  [قَالَ هَٰذَا رَبِّى · interpretation]
turns on: Whether it is genuine inquiry or worship, a provisional hypothesis, a concession to opponents, an omitted quotation or question, or sarcasm; prophetic protection and the passage's sequence are decisive grounds in the notes.
- 6:76/q11/p1 He stages or concedes his opponents' claim to refute it when the star sets. — Ibn Qutayba likens the method to a convert who feigned idol devotion; the tactic gains trust without provoking inherited beliefs. Al-Rāzī prefers it as a debater's fair rehearsal of a premise and cites 6:83; al-Nasafī and al-Ālūsī prefer its gentle persuasion. Al-Ālūsī compares permitted speech under coercion and 37:88–89; Ibn ʿĀshūr compares faithful-hearted forced denial, says literal speech to a group and possible revelation made this strategy apt. Islāḥī calls it gradual argument, audible self-talk, double meaning and humor; ʿAbd Allāh b. Muslim and al-Farrāʾ are named as supporters. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī allows a hypothetical premise without asserting belief. The people's star worship, astrology, and assigned idols supply the audience. [46 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; TUSTARI; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو بكر بن الأنباري); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(محمد بن حمران); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ALUSI-FULL+; NASAFI-FULL+; RAZI-FULL+]
- 6:76/q11/p2 It is a rebuking question, 'Is this my Lord?', with the interrogative hamza omitted. — Poems cited by Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Ṭabrisī and 90:11 or 21:34 cited by later authors illustrate the omission. Ibn al-Anbārī calls it anomalous unless a cue such as am distinguishes the question; Ibn ʿAṭiyya disputes one poetic proof. The tone could signal the question, says al-Nasafī; Burhān states it directly. [25 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الشاعر); TABRISI(أوس بن حجر,ابن عباس,الأخطل,شاعر,عمر بن أبي ربيعة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن الأنباري); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الهذلي,قيل); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(ابن الأنباري); BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); ELMALILI; IBNATIYYA-FULL-]
- 6:76/q11/p3 He actually believed or worshipped the star until it set. — Ibn ʿAbbās reports worship of each celestial body and subsequent disavowal; some take the plea for guidance as uncertainty. Opponents reject this for prophetic protection, 37:84's sound heart, prior guidance in 21:51, his dispute with his father in 6:74, 6:75's kingdom, and 6:83's public proof. Ibn Kathīr cites the fitra report; al-Rāzī argues even simple reason sees signs of creation. Abū Ḥayyān questions the Ibn ʿAbbās report's authenticity. The literal account is sometimes confined to childhood before accountability. [10 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); THALABI(ابن عباس,بعضهم); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL(بعضهم); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(علي بن أبي طلحة عن ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL(من الناس)-; THALABI-; WAHIDI-BASIT(أهل التحقيق من العلماء)-; IBNJAWZI-ZAD-; RAZI-FULL-; QURTUBI-FULL(الزجاج,قوم)-; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; IBNKATHIR-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم)-; ALUSI-FULL(المحققون)-; ISLAHI-TADABBUR-]
- 6:76/q11/p4 He speaks according to his people's claim: 'this is my lord, as you say.' — Al-Zajjāj compares God's ironic 'Where are My partners?' in 28:62; al-Wāḥidī adds 44:49. This treats the statement as attribution rather than Abraham's own belief. [10 notes: ZAJJAJ; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو بكر بن الأنباري); QURTUBI-FULL(الزجاج,قيل); NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 6:76/q11/p5 It is provisional inquiry or supposition, abandoned after testing. — Al-Māwardī and al-Bayḍāwī allow reasoning from a hypothesis; al-Ṭabrisī says supposing eternity to expose absurdity need not be a false assertion, or it may report a thought provisionally entertained. Al-Ṭabarī, as cited by Ibn Kathīr, favors contemplation from the plea for guidance. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites Imams saying a seeker before conclusive proof did not commit shirk, and distinguishes later speakers. Al-Naḥḥās calls Abraham's progress 'light upon light': heart and signs preceded greater revealed knowledge. Al-Qurṭubī reports a mistaken identification of the star's light with the Lord's light. [17 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI; BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن جرير); NAHHAS(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(النحاس,قيل); TABATABAI(أبو عبد الله,حجر,محمد بن حمران,محمد بن مسلم); ISLAHI-TADABBUR-]
- 6:76/q11/p6 He spoke as a child before proof or legal responsibility, so the words were neither faith nor unbelief. — Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Māwardī, al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī report this; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says many place it before puberty. Al-Rāzī reports rigorous scholars' rejection of a post-puberty searching account; al-Ālūsī rejects the childhood-ignorance defense outright and says the context supports later mature debate. [8 notes: THALABI(بعضهم); MAWARDI-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL(بعضهم); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); RAZI-FULL(أكثر المحققين); ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); THALABI-; QURTUBI-FULL(الزجاج)-; ALUSI-FULL(المحققون)-]
- 6:76/q11/p7 An omitted 'they say' or 'you say' makes the words a quotation. — Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī and al-Rāzī cite elided speech elsewhere, especially the prayer after building the House in 2:127 and 39:3. [7 notes: THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو بكر بن الأنباري); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]
- 6:76/q11/p8 He said it ironically or sarcastically to taunt the claim. — Al-Rāzī compares calling a lowly leader 'your master'; al-Ālūsī records the same alternative. Elmalılı says the words taunt onlookers about whether a star can nurture a human. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); ELMALILI]

## 6:76/q12 What did Abraham's people worship, and why address stars?  [* · reports]
turns on: The sources differ in how idols, planets, and a supreme creator related in his people's worship.
- 6:76/q12/p1 They worshipped idols together with celestial bodies and sometimes Nimrod. — Ibn Qutayba describes an astrological culture claiming stars forecast hidden events; al-Zajjāj, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī and Bursevī name sun, moon, stars and idols. Bursevī says some also worshipped Nimrod; Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies Chaldean Sabians and suggests planetary spirits, images or lesser idols. Kur'an Yolu dates Abraham around 2100 BCE and reports Torah/Ur tablets with about five thousand divine names. [11 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; ZAJJAJ; THALABI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 6:76/q12/p2 They revered planets as delegated subordinate lords or governors. — Al-Rāzī says astrologers allowed the supreme deity to create planets which create lower plants and animals. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says Sabians accepted the ultimate Creator but attributed earthly and human governance to celestial agents, explaining why arguments merely proving a first cause miss them; Ibn ʿĀshūr allows planets represented by images. [8 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); RAZI-FULL(المنجمون وأصحاب الوسايط); ALUSI-FULL(قيل); TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 6:76/q12/p3 Celestial images were linked to planets, but the planets may have been thought independently effective. — An al-Ālūsī report assigns gold idols to the sun and silver to the moon, and says Abraham first denied images then their celestial sources. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]

## 6:76/q13 What does أَفَلَ mean?  [أَفَلَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether disappearance specifically means horizon setting, a broader going away, or motion from the zenith.
- 6:76/q13/p1 It set or disappeared from view. — Abū ʿUbayda, Ibn Isḥāq, Saʿīd b. Jubayr and many authors give this gloss. Dhū al-Rumma's āfilāt and a Pleiades line attest disappearing stars; al-Ṭabarī cites the idiom 'where did you disappear from us?' Ibn ʿAṭiyya says 'went away' and 'disappeared' differ only verbally. The imperfect and verbal noun afūl are supplied by Mujāz, Akhfash, al-Jishumī and others; al-Samīn distinguishes unrelated ifāl (young sheep) and afīl (small foal). Bint al-Shāṭi says Qur'anic use is confined to the three luminaries. [35 notes: MAJAZ; AKHFASH; TAB-FULL(ابن إسحاق,ذو الرمة); ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(سعيد بن جبير); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(ذو الرمة); TABRISI(ذو الرمة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ذو الرمة); SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL(محمد بن إسحاق بن يسار); QURTUBI-FULL; BURHAN; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(أبو عبيدة,الهروي)]
- 6:76/q13/p2 It denotes a celestial body's disappearance below the horizon, not concealment by cloud. — Ibn ʿĀshūr explains it through Earth's daily rotation; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī notes that apparent disappearance reflects the viewer's changed position. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 6:76/q13/p3 It means moving away from the zenith. — Ibn ʿAbbās is cited with a line by Kaʿb b. Mālik; Bint al-Shāṭi calls this approximate and says the word still evokes disappearance at sunset. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن عباس)]
- 6:76/q13/p4 It may be etymologically linked to a wet nurse whose milk has dried up. — Bint al-Shāṭi offers this as a possible derivation from al-afīl. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ]

## 6:76/q14 What is meant by 'I do not love those that set'?  [لَآ أُحِبُّ ٱلْءَافِلِينَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether love is ordinary affection, refusal of worship, or the necessary bond between dependent creature and lord.
- 6:76/q14/p1 He refuses to take a disappearing being as a lord or object of worship. — Al-Māwardī allows ordinary affection for stars; the rejected love is devotion to a worshipped lord. Al-Ālūsī notes either denying their divinity or an ellipsis 'I do not love worship of them.' Ibn ʿĀshūr glosses aḥibbu as approve or want, with the worshipped object supplied by context. Al-Suddī and Qatādah gloss the clause as rejection of a lord that disappears or passes away. Al-Mujāz says the plural generalizes beyond these three bodies. [14 notes: MAJAZ; IBNABIHATIM(السدي,قتادة); MAWARDI-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; DURR-FULL(السدي,قتادة); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(قتادة)]
- 6:76/q14/p2 Loving the Lord is necessary to lordship; transient beauty cannot command enduring natural love. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī makes this the direct demonstrative proof and prefers it over a popular insinuation; the relation of dependent creature to lasting manager entails love. He cites a report from al-Ṣādiq, 'Is religion anything but love?' Elmalılı and Islāḥī likewise make love central to worship, with Islāḥī distinguishing it from mere fear. [10 notes: TABATABAI; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; TABATABAI+]
- 6:76/q14/p3 He grieves at the loss of spiritual witnessing. — Al-Wāsiṭī reads dislike of setting as pain at losing the state of immediate presence to God. [1 notes: SULAMI(الواسطي)]

## 6:76/q15 Why does the verse use the masculine plural ٱلْءَافِلِينَ?  [ٱلْءَافِلِينَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether it generalizes to all supposed lords or marks the worshippers' attribution of reason to celestial agents.
- 6:76/q15/p1 It treats the celestial bodies as putative rational lords, and extends the verdict to everything that sets. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the referents are 'lords,' so the sound masculine plural fits; Ibn ʿĀshūr says Abraham's people viewed stars as intelligent universal agents. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says rational-language usage hints that an unreasoning thing cannot merit lordship, citing 19:42 and 26:70–74. Abū Ḥayyān says the plural points to many stars sharing the trait; al-Mujāz says all things that set are covered. [5 notes: MAJAZ; IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 6:76/q16 Why is setting, rather than rising, chosen as Abraham's test?  [فَلَمَّآ أَفَلَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether its force is clearer visible diminishment, the absence of oversight or love, or narrative economy.
- 6:76/q16/p1 Setting visibly removes light, apparent power and authority, making the proof clearer to ordinary hearers. — Al-Rāzī says motion at rising also shows origination, but its proof is subtle; al-Biqāʿī says rising retains honor and apparent authority, while setting displays insignificance. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Kashshāf emphasize concealment; al-Rāzī and al-Biqāʿī cite astrologers' view that planets weaken in the west. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports these readings but says they miss the verse's argument from love. [13 notes: RAZI-FULL(العوام); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI(الرازي,الكشاف); TABATABAI-]
- 6:76/q16/p2 Setting makes the lord absent from dependents or unable to command enduring love. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says a continuously governing deity must attend servants' needs; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says only setting frustrates the love essential to lordship and rising is irrelevant to that direct proof. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(الكشاف)]
- 6:76/q16/p3 The first setting of that day was not witnessed, so citing the observed setting is briefer. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says using rising would require explaining that the star had set earlier. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 6:76/q17 What exactly does the star's setting prove against lordship?  [فَلَمَّآ أَفَلَ لَآ أُحِبُّ ٱلْءَافِلِينَ · theology]
turns on: The sources distinguish change and origination, contingency, loss of presence, and love; some dispute whether first-cause arguments answer subordinate star worship.
- 6:76/q17/p1 A setting body changes, moves, is originated and governed, so cannot be a god. — Al-Zajjāj compares its states to acknowledged non-gods; al-Māwardī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Rāzī, al-Nasafī, Ibn Kathīr and others infer a creator or governor from movement and change. Qatādah contrasts it with an enduring Lord. Al-Rāzī says origination entails an eternal powerful agent or infinite regress, and the Creator of greater heavens can create humans without planet intermediaries (40:57, 36:81). Al-Biqāʿī says visible celestial bodies outrank earthly idols, so their failure also refutes idols; Elmalılı applies this to their fashioned images. Islāḥī says regulated celestial paths show subjection. [39 notes: TAB-FULL(قتادة); ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); SULAMI(قيل); THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); RAZI-FULL(الأوساط); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; TABATABAI(آخرون)-]
- 6:76/q17/p2 It reveals contingency and dependence on a Necessary Existent. — Al-Rāzī assigns this to elite hearers and cites 53:42; Ibn Sīnā and al-Ghazālī treat the luminaries' possibility as requiring an efficient cause and necessary terminus. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says it is an inference from setting, not the word's definition, yet argues star worshippers already accept dependent planets, so it misses their claim; al-Rāzī finds Ghazālī's reading possible but unlikely from the wording. [10 notes: RAZI-FULL(أبو علي بن سيناء,الغزالي,بعض المحققين); BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI(بعض المحققين,بعضهم); RAZI-FULL-; TABATABAI-]
- 6:76/q17/p3 The same phrase teaches different audiences through contingency, motion, or visible loss of power. — Al-Rāzī and al-Biqāʿī distinguish elite, intermediate, and ordinary hearers; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī quotes each tier and rejects the layered proof as unsupported by the verse and ineffective against subordinate star worship. [12 notes: RAZI-FULL(الأوساط,العوام,بعض المحققين); BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI(الرازي,بعض المحققين); TABATABAI-]
- 6:76/q17/p4 It disproves a planet's continuous delegated governance because absence breaks its relation to those it governs. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the star's disappearance prevents attending dependents' needs and insists a change-based argument would not convince people who accept periodic celestial motion. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the dispute is over earthly governance, not a first creator; lack of durable beauty means lack of the love lordship requires, while mere invisibility alone would not prove it, since God is unseen. [13 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; TABATABAI+]
- 6:76/q17/p5 The conclusion also forms a syllogism: the body sets; the true Lord does not. — Al-Zarkashī calls it a second-figure syllogism and, in this discussion, glosses the celestial body as the moon; al-Rāzī notes the Qur'an states foundational premises while dialectic expands them. [2 notes: BURHAN; RAZI-FULL]
- 6:76/q17/p6 Setting is merely a popular insinuation that hidden stars do not know worshippers' affairs. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports this view but rejects it because the certainty and kingdom passages demand a demonstrative argument based on love rather than disappearance alone. [1 notes: TABATABAI(بعضهم); TABATABAI-]

## 6:76/q18 What did Abraham know of God before and during this argument?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether he was already certain, gained increasing knowledge, or was still seeking before conclusive proof.
- 6:76/q18/p1 He already knew God and had been protected from shirk; the scene argues to others. — Ibn Qutayba invokes the sound heart in 37:84, prior kingdom vision in 6:75, and earlier confrontation with his father. Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī and others cite prophetic protection, 21:51, 6:83 and 14:35. Al-Rāzī says preaching his father gently preceded strong public polemic. Ibn Kathīr cites the newborn fitra report. Islāḥī argues this happened after appointment because prophets invite kin first and 6:79–80 speaks with authority; he calls the cave tale an invention to explain the order of luminaries. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī has Abraham know the supreme God even while examining proposed delegated governors. [32 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL(من الناس); TUSTARI; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أهل التحقيق من العلماء); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الزجاج,قوم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); ALUSI-FULL(المحققون); TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 6:76/q18/p2 He knew God by heart and signs, then received an increase of certain knowledge. — Ibn ʿAbbās's light-upon-light explanation, favored by al-Naḥḥās and quoted by al-Qurṭubī, relates 24:35 to Abraham; al-Naḥḥās cites 6:80. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī's reports distinguish a seeker before clear proof from an unbeliever. Islāḥī also calls prophetic revelation light upon light. [8 notes: NAHHAS(ابن عباس); TABATABAI(أبو عبد الله,حجر,محمد بن مسلم); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; QURTUBI-FULL(النحاس)+]
- 6:76/q18/p3 He was seeking his Lord without having reached disbelief. — Al-Ṭabarī, as cited by Ibn Kathīr, bases contemplation on Abraham's request for guidance; Muqātil describes the same sequence. Reports from two Imams and al-Ṣādiq say his words while seeking were not shirk, though the same words from another after clear proof would be. [6 notes: MUQATIL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن جرير); TABATABAI(أبو عبد الله,حجر,محمد بن مسلم); ISLAHI-TADABBUR-]
- 6:76/q18/p4 Some place the investigation after puberty and legal accountability. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports this from a group and says al-Ṭabarī transmits it; al-Rāzī says rigorous scholars reject a post-puberty search for God. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(فرقة); RAZI-FULL(أكثر المحققين)-]

## 6:76/q19 What does this episode teach about reasoning and religious knowledge?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the focus is proof against imitation, inference from creation, or how to persuade an audience.
- 6:76/q19/p1 Faith and worship should rest on examination of created signs and proof, not imitation. — Al-Rāzī says prophetic knowledge is inferential, drawn from creatures, and that proof is necessary or Abraham's debate would be purposeless; al-Biqāʿī agrees. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls the argument demonstrative and says the visible phenomena offered evidence suited to the conversation. Islāḥī says using innate intellect lifts heavenly bodies above man-made idols, while Kur'an Yolu says the sequence demonstrates correct belief and opposes associationism. [9 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 6:76/q19/p2 A preacher may use gradual, fair and indirect reasoning adapted to hostile listeners. — Ibn Qutayba's ḥawārī example, al-Nasafī's fair-minded rehearsal and al-Ālūsī's concern about obstinacy illustrate this. Islāḥī contrasts prophetic styles and says Abraham's adversarial audience required staged reasoning, possible turiya, humor and sarcasm; his idol-smashing argument elicited their own admission that idols cannot speak. [11 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 6:76/q19/p3 The higher Creator can dispense with all celestial intermediaries. — Al-Rāzī says if stars created humans no one could identify which to thank, while the one creator of everything is knowable and deserves worship. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 6:76/q20 How does Abraham's example function in this sūra and for Muhammad's audience?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes relate his universal reputation, prophetic continuity, and the sūra's argument against associationism.
- 6:76/q20/p1 Its anti-idolatry reasoning turns from earthly idols to more impressive celestial bodies. — Al-Māwardī, al-Biqāʿī and Elmalılı say if visible, brighter heavenly objects fail the test, handmade idols fail more clearly. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī narrates the move from debating his father to the star, moon and sun, then pure monotheism. [5 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI]
- 6:76/q20/p2 His acknowledged standing makes his monotheistic debate a proof against Arab polytheists. — Al-Rāzī says all communities honored Abraham and Arabs claimed descent from him; he fulfilled a covenant through servanthood (2:40, 2:124, 2:131), knowledge, proof, endurance, sacrifice and hospitality (26:84), and debated father, people and ruler (19:42, 2:258, 21:58–68). Islāḥī says Israelites and Ishmaelites both claimed him though later Jewish, Christian and Ishmaelite practices had no basis in his religion; Muhammad renews it. [7 notes: RAZI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 6:76/q20/p3 Muhammad follows the same prophetic message, preaching without forcing belief. — Islāḥī says Abraham and later prophets share the mission and Muhammad must follow their guidance and leave rejecters to themselves. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 6:76/q21 What inward meanings are given to the star, its setting, and Abraham's vision?  [* · inward]
turns on: The inward readings map the lights variously to spiritual stages, faculties, or witnessing.
- 6:76/q21/p1 The inner cosmos has lights of unity that overcome doubts aroused by physical luminaries. — Al-Sulamī transmits this unnamed account. [1 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم)]
- 6:76/q21/p2 Abraham saw the Real in his innermost secret, rather than the star itself. — Al-Wāsiṭī reads the subsequent dislike of setting as loss of absorbed witnessing. [2 notes: SULAMI(الواسطي)]
- 6:76/q21/p3 Setting created branches reveal their nullity and awaken longing for the never-setting One. — Al-Naṣrābādhī contrasts the setting outward sun with the inward sun of the heart that never disappears. [1 notes: SULAMI(النصرآباذي)]
- 6:76/q21/p4 The sequence moves from darkness and compulsion through lordly lights and awe to effacement and abiding in God. — An unnamed al-Sulamī report ends in disavowal of association; Ibn ʿAṭāʾ connects longing with losing sight of created traces and seeing only their Maker, the station of union. [2 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء,بعضهم)]
- 6:76/q21/p5 Star, moon and sun mark intellect and proof, knowledge and evidence, then gnosis. — Al-Qushayrī says the final sun-like stage removes doubt and concealment. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]
- 6:76/q21/p6 The signs are first seen through God, then as His possessions, finally effaced in Him. — Al-Qushayrī reports this alternative sequence. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]
- 6:76/q21/p7 Star, moon and sun signify sense or animal soul, imagination or sphere-soul, and intellect. — Al-Rāzī reports both an anonymous mapping of finite faculties and al-Ghazālī's mapping to animal rational soul, rational soul of its sphere and separate intellect; he finds Ghazālī's account possible in itself but unlikely to fit the wording. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL(الغزالي,بعض الناس); RAZI-FULL-]
- 6:76/q21/p8 Night is bodily nature; the star is the soul's spiritual life and its setting gives way to the heart's light. — Al-Qāshānī says the name al-Muḥyī manifests in soul-life; recognition of the soul's contingency and bodily dependence leads Abraham beyond it. [3 notes: QASHANI]
- 6:76/q21/p9 The first statement distinguishes Abraham from his people, then asks increased guidance. — Ibn ʿAṭāʾ says established proof ends in disavowal of their associations. [1 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء)]

## 6:76/q22 How do cited poems use moon and sun imagery?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether definiteness and number imply literal luminaries or figurative human counterparts.
- 6:76/q22/p1 Definite 'the moon' tends toward the actual moon; plural moons or 'I am a sun' admit a human metaphor. — Al-Jurjānī says al-Mutanabbī's 'two moons at once' pairs the real moon with a woman's sun-like face; two compared poems choose day or night but have the same poetic substance, and Abū al-Fatḥ captured only the general image of a gazelle and sunlight, not its special craft. [4 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR]
- 6:76/q22/p2 The word qamar names the familiar moon and evokes whiteness. — Ibn Qutayba, as cited by Abū Ḥayyān, relates it to a bright qamrāʾ night. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن قتيبة)]

## 6:76/q23 What further theological implications are drawn from the denial of setting lords?  [* · theology]
turns on: The sources extend the anti-setting principle to corporeality, changing attributes, and cosmic causation.
- 6:76/q23/p1 God is not a body and cannot acquire changing states. — Al-Rāzī says a spatial body would be absent or setting relative to us; descent and ascent also imply movement. He rejects the Karrāmiyya's claim of originated attributes in God because change would imply setting. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 6:76/q23/p2 Celestial mediation is unnecessary because the supreme Creator directly governs the lower world. — Al-Rāzī says divine power over the heavens entails power over human creation, so worship of the creator takes priority. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 6:76/q24 What other reports or objections are attached to this story?  [* · reports]
turns on: These are separate transmitted applications or doubts about the narrative's setting.
- 6:76/q24/p1 A report about seeing sins in the kingdom says God restrained Abraham's imprecations. — Ibn Qutayba reports that a sinner may repent, bear good offspring, or face the Fire. [1 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL]
- 6:76/q24/p2 The Prophet's east-and-west report is connected to Abraham's rejection of setting stars. — Al-Zarkashī says the source of fitna and disbelief is toward the east, while repentance's door opens from the west. [1 notes: BURHAN(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 6:76/q24/p3 Harran poses a historical objection to the proposed timing of the debate. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī admits that Abraham could only have debated celestial worshippers after reaching Harran, so his earlier interpretation of the passage faces a problem. [1 notes: TABATABAI]
- 6:76/q24/p4 The daytime absence of the sun from the opening scene has several possible explanations. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī suggests the family debate left no time before night, clouds obscured the sun, or the people were present only at sunrise. [1 notes: TABATABAI]

## 6:76/q25 What is the 'kingdom' shown to Abraham in the preceding verse?  [* · links]
- 6:76/q25/p1 It means the signs. — Mujāhid gives this gloss of malakūt, supplying the context for the sight of the star. [1 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد)]

## 6:76/q26 What is the grammatical structure of 'this is my Lord'?  [هَٰذَا رَبِّى · grammar]
- 6:76/q26/p1 It is a subject and predicate clause. — Al-Naḥḥās parses hādhā as subject and rabbī as predicate. [1 notes: NAHHAS]

## 6:76/q27 Can the star itself point to the true Lord?  [كَوْكَبًا · interpretation]
- 6:76/q27/p1 The luminary can function as a sign indicating Abraham's Lord. — Al-Qurṭubī reports this further interpretation. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]

## 6:76/q28 What further evidence and objections bear on Abraham's wording and argument?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: How contextual sequence and analogy are used to assess the narrative.
- 6:76/q28/p1 A quoted ironic address can describe how claimants see themselves. — Al-Qurṭubī cites the address 'You are indeed the mighty and noble' in 44:49 as an analogy for attributed speech. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]
- 6:76/q28/p2 A supposedly first observed setting raises an objection because the luminaries had set before. — Al-Rāzī reports an objector's question about what this particular night adds; his reply puts the emphasis on demonstrating the loss of power before star worshippers. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(سائل)]
- 6:76/q28/p3 Accounts of a debate after maturity are challenged on contextual grounds. — Al-Ālūsī rejects the proposed settings of that mature public debate, especially the Imam's, because they rely on the very mature-mission sequence they invoke. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 6:76/q28/p4 The sequence may have taken about two days and one night. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī estimates this duration for the family and celestial debates. [1 notes: TABATABAI]
