# 6:77: 20 questions

## 6:77/q01 How is رَءَا read here?  [رَءَا · readings]
turns on: The vowel treatment of rāʾ, hamza and the underlying alif, with differences by reader, transmission and following sound.
- 6:77/q01/p1 Both rāʾ and hamza are opened. — Al-Fārisī lists Ibn Kathīr, Nāfiʿ, Abū ʿAmr, al-Kisāʾī, Ibn ʿĀmir before a consonant and Ḥafṣ throughout; Jishumī calls this the reading of the remaining readers. Al-Fārisī compares 18:53 and 16:86 and says opening can follow ramā and raʿā or preserve a second accepted language form. [4 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(ابن كثير ونافع وأبو عمرو والكسائي وابن عامر,حفص عن عاصم); JISHUMI]
- 6:77/q01/p2 The rāʾ is inclined or broken while the hamza is opened. — Al-Fārisī attributes it to Abū Bakr from ʿĀṣim and Ḥamza, and permits independent kasra of rāʾ without hamza imāla. Jishumī specifies Ḥamza, Yaḥyā from Abū Bakr, and a route from al-Kisāʾī before a connecting alif; Samīn adds Abū Bakr and one route of al-Sūsī and distinguishes temporary alif deletion. [5 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(عاصم في رواية أبي بكر وحمزة); JISHUMI(حمزة ويحيى عن أبي بكر ونصيرا عن الكسائي); SAMIN-DURR(حمزة)]
- 6:77/q01/p3 Both rāʾ and hamza are inclined or broken. — Al-Fārisī reports Khalaf via Yaḥyā and Abū Bakr from ʿĀṣim; Jishumī describes the general practice of Ibn ʿĀmir, Ḥamza and al-Kisāʾī, with contextual exceptions, and Yaḥyā's matching transmission. Samīn gives one route of Abū Bakr and al-Sūsī, and explains the alif from yāʾ as imāla's actual target, restored at pause. Al-Fārisī rejects calling the hamza a literal kasra: its fatḥa is inclined. [5 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(خلف عن يحيى بن آدم عن أبي بكر عن عاصم); JISHUMI(ابن عامر وحمزة والكسائي,يحيى عن أبي بكر عن عاصم); SAMIN-DURR(حمزة); FARISI-HUJJA-]
- 6:77/q01/p4 The rāʾ is opened and the hamza inclined or broken. — Jishumī attributes this throughout to Abū ʿAmr and Warsh from Nāfiʿ. Al-Fārisī says the inclination of hamza can affect rāʾ, while also recognizing open rāʾ as a separate accepted form. [2 notes: JISHUMI(أبو عمرو وورش عن نافع); FARISI-HUJJA]
- 6:77/q01/p5 Attached-pronoun forms follow additional routes, while permanently alifless forms have no imāla. — Samīn reports Ibn Dhakwān's two routes for pronoun-attached examples in 37:55, 27:10 and 21:36; attachment places alif farther from the ending. He excludes permanent loss of alif in raʾathum and raʾū, or alif changed to yāʾ, citing 25:12, 10:54 and 76:20. Jishumī says all agree on fatḥa in raʾūka and raʾathum. [4 notes: SAMIN-DURR(ابن ذكوان); JISHUMI]

## 6:77/q02 Why is the alif absent in connected pronunciation of رَءَا ٱلْقَمَرَ?  [رَءَا ٱلْقَمَرَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the question concerns phonetic loss in liaison or abbreviation in writing; al-Nashr expressly disputes the two-sukūn account of the written omission.
- 6:77/q02/p1 It is temporarily dropped to prevent two adjacent vowelless sounds and returns at pause. — Samīn distinguishes this from forms with permanently missing alif and says the temporary deletion permits different imāla treatments here and in 6:78, 18:53 and 16:85. Jishumī likewise connects imāla to the underlying yāʾ and deletion before connecting alif. Al-Fārisī says removal of alif also removes its inclination and the preceding fatḥa's dependent inclination. [4 notes: SAMIN-DURR; JISHUMI; FARISI-HUJJA]
- 6:77/q02/p2 The following alif is omitted in writing as an abbreviation because two identical letters meet. — Ibn al-Jazarī says the fixed alif is the written shape of hamza and expressly denies that this written omission is caused by two sukūns. [1 notes: NASHR]

## 6:77/q03 What does ٱلْقَمَرَ mean and why is it so named?  [ٱلْقَمَرَ · meaning]
turns on: Competing reported explanations of the noun's derivation.
- 6:77/q03/p1 Qamar is the moon after its first three nights, named for its whiteness or spreading light. — Jishumī and Ṭabrisī call the first three nights hilāl and the remainder qamar; Ibn al-Jawzī transmits Ibn Qutayba's aqmar 'white' and layla qamrāʾ 'bright night.' Ṭabrisī compares a white camel; Samīn reports the whiteness/spreading-light derivation, bright nights lasting until morning, and a narrower use of badr after the tenth through fifteenth night, citing 2:189. [6 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة); SAMIN-DURR]
- 6:77/q03/p2 Qamar is named for overcoming or appropriating the stars' light. — Samīn reports this as another etymology and also records qamrāʾ for moonlight, aqmar for the color of a moonlit donkey, and qamartu fulānan for tricking someone out of something, compared with a waterskin sealed by moonlight. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 6:77/q04 What does بَازِغًۭا mean?  [بَازِغًۭا · meaning]
- 6:77/q04/p1 Rising or first appearing, with light breaking through darkness. — Majāz, Ṭabarī, Māwardī, Wāḥidī, Jishumī, Baghawī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī, Rāzī, Qurṭubī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, Samīn, Ibn Kathīr, Biqāʿī and Ālūsī give rising, first appearance or spreading light; some apply it also to sun and stars. Al-Azharī derives it from splitting darkness; Wāḥidī cites al-Ṭirimmāḥ's veterinary incision, while Jishumī, Qurṭubī, Samīn and Ālūsī add bloodletting or tooth emergence as cognate uses. [21 notes: MAJAZ; TAB-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الأزهري,الطرماح); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(الأزهري); ALUSI-FULL(الأزهري؛ الراغب)]

## 6:77/q05 What is the grammatical role of بَازِغًۭا?  [بَازِغًۭا · grammar]
- 6:77/q05/p1 It is a circumstantial accusative describing the moon. — Samīn says the root verb can be transitive of a veterinarian releasing blood or intransitive of blood flowing, before its generalized use for rising. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 6:77/q06 When and in what setting did Abraham see the rising moon?  [فَلَمَّا رَءَا ٱلْقَمَرَ بَازِغًۭا · reports]
turns on: The chronology of the visible bodies and whether cave concealment or a blocked western horizon explains it.
- 6:77/q06/p1 He saw it late at night after the star, having emerged from concealment around the month's end. — Muqātil places him in a cave at the month's end and names the star Venus. Ibn ʿAbbās in Ibn Abī Ḥātim says he emerged at month's end, had not previously seen the moon, and saw it late that night. Ibn Isḥāq in Ibn Kathīr says his mother hid him in a cave after Nimrod ordered male infants killed; Ibn Qutayba says astrologers predicted a child who would threaten Nimrod. Islahi objects that the cave story was invented to repair the sequence's difficulty. [5 notes: MUQATIL; IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR-FULL(محمد بن إسحاق); IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR-]
- 6:77/q06/p2 A high western mountain hid the star and moon near midday while the east remained open. — Bursevī proposes this to account for the moon rising after the star's apparent setting and disappearing before sunrise; he says the moon had passed west of the meridian when Abraham prayed. Ālūsī transmits Shaykh al-Islam's similar reconstruction, with the star initially close to Abraham. [4 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(شيخ الاسلام)]
- 6:77/q06/p3 The star, moon and sun observations likely spanned about two days and one night. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī places the observations after Abraham lived apart from, then joined, his father's society and debated idols before testing the celestial objects in turn. Abū Ḥayyān says he did not await the first star's rise because stars appear as night darkens, but did await the brighter moon; Ālūsī notes the apparent order is after the star's setting. [4 notes: TABATABAI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 6:77/q06/p4 The scene belongs to an astral-worshipping milieu. — Ibn Qutayba says people revered stars, practiced astrology and divination, and claimed stars revealed hidden events. Kuryolu dates Abraham around 2100 BCE, identifies him with the Hanif tradition, and describes celestial worship and idol symbols; its cited Torah and Ur tablets allegedly record about five thousand divine names. Bursevī says moon worshippers bow to the rising moon, likened to a silver tray on a green celestial table. [4 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; BURSEVI]

## 6:77/q07 What does Abraham mean by هَٰذَا رَبِّى?  [هَٰذَا رَبِّى · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the demonstrative is Abraham's belief, a provisional argumentative concession, or elliptical quotation of his opponents.
- 6:77/q07/p1 He adopts the worshippers' premise provisionally as a tactful argument, without sincerely affirming the moon as Lord. — Ibn Qutayba compares a disciple who feigned idol devotion, then exposed its impotence, and invokes 37:84; Thaʿlabī reports feigned agreement and arguing from opponents' assumptions, including the larger moon. Biqāʿī calls it the earlier rhetorical tactic; Abū Ḥayyān a concession to the opponent. Elmalılı calls it an ironic taunt; Ṭabāṭabāʾī hypothetical accommodation and says the demonstratives sound like someone unfamiliar with the heavenly bodies, though aware of their worship and sacrifices; Islahi calls it gradual reasoning, turiya, humor or sarcasm, citing the idol-smashing episode and the audience's polemics. Kuryolu judges him probably already monotheist. Ālūsī says the subsequent wording favors debate with the people. [19 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; THALABI; BIQAI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ALUSI-FULL+; ISLAHI-TADABBUR+; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR+]
- 6:77/q07/p2 The declaration is sincere during Abraham's childhood or period of inquiry before full guidance. — Ibn ʿAbbās reports he worshipped the star, moon and sun until each set, then renounced shirk; another report says he worshipped the moon until disappearance. Thaʿlabī reports the child was then neither unbeliever nor believer. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the wording yahdinī supports a childhood setting; Abū Ḥayyān says some use the description of the astray to support it. Ibn Jarīr, reported by Ibn Kathīr, favors contemplation because of the conditional request. Qurṭubī allows a period of inquiry. Ibn Qutayba, Thaʿlabī, Ibn Kathīr and Islahi reject the implied earlier error or sequence, citing prophetic protection, Abraham's sound heart (37:84), the newborn's fitra, and the odd star-first order. [8 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); THALABI(ابن عباس,بعضهم); IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن جرير)+; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL(من الناس)-; THALABI-; IBNKATHIR-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم)-; ISLAHI-TADABBUR-]
- 6:77/q07/p3 The implied speech is 'they say: this is my Lord.' — Thaʿlabī treats the words as ellipsis and compares the implicit speech in the prayer after Abraham and Ishmael raised the House's foundations in 2:127. [1 notes: THALABI]

## 6:77/q08 Why does Abraham move from the star to the moon and then the sun?  [ٱلْقَمَرَ هَٰذَا رَبِّى · rhetoric]
- 6:77/q08/p1 He repeats one test on successively larger and brighter objects to make their shared incapacity plain. — Muqātil and Qatāda compare the bodies' brightness; Thaʿlabī says the brighter sun's disappearance makes the point clearer; Wāḥidī, Rāzī and Ṭabrisī call it the same consideration. Abū Ḥayyān says the moon was awaited at first rising and sun is larger, brighter, more useful; Ibn Kathīr describes the sun as greater in size and brightness. Ṭabāṭabāʾī notes the sun is called greater. Elmalılı says moonlight can captivate observers; Bursevī locates moon worshippers' bowing at moonrise and says it begins rising after the star sets. [16 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(قتادة); THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; RAZI-FULL; TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; TABATABAI; ELMALILI; BURSEVI]

## 6:77/q09 What does the moon's أَفَلَ describe?  [أَفَلَ · meaning]
- 6:77/q09/p1 Its setting or disappearance from sight. — Al-Suddī explicitly glosses it as disappearance. Ibn ʿAbbās reports the moon was worshipped until it disappeared; Bursevī locates the plea after the moon went west of the meridian in his reconstruction. [3 notes: IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس,السدي); BURSEVI]

## 6:77/q10 Why does the moon's setting rule out its divinity?  [فَلَمَّآ أَفَلَ · theology]
- 6:77/q10/p1 Setting exposes change, dependence and createdness; a governed, disappearing thing cannot be the enduring Lord. — Qatāda contrasts the everlasting Lord with a disappearing star. Māwardī argues change entails governance and origination; Bayḍāwī and Nasafī apply this to moon worship. Ibn Kathīr points to fixed God-governed planetary paths and no self-direction; Islahi and Kuryolu say all celestial bodies submit to divine laws and only their Creator merits worship or love. Ṭabrisī cites the moon's size and light before its setting. Ibn Qutayba, Thaʿlabī, Muqātil and Abū Ḥayyān likewise extend the test from star to moon and sun. [16 notes: MUQATIL; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; TAB-FULL(قتادة); THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 6:77/q11 Why does Abraham point to setting rather than rising?  [فَلَمَّآ أَفَلَ · rhetoric]
- 6:77/q11/p1 Setting makes the change especially clear through concealment and disappearance. — Nasafī says rising also entails change, but disappearance is clearer. Biqāʿī says the prior star's setting had already unsettled hearers. Islahi says Abraham turns their claim that shining proves divinity against them by showing that the same lights fade; his appeal to love emphasizes that fading objects cannot merit divine love. [4 notes: NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 6:77/q12 What is Abraham asking for in لَئِن لَّمْ يَهْدِنِى رَبِّى?  [لَئِن لَّمْ يَهْدِنِى رَبِّى · interpretation]
turns on: Whether Abraham is seeking initial truth, preservation of guidance already possessed, or addressing the worshippers by conditional example; Islahi disputes making the condition past.
- 6:77/q12/p1 Continued, increased or firm guidance already possessed. — Tustarī and Sahl seek uninterrupted establishment; Sahl says without God's help Abraham could fall into his people's error. Ibn ʿAṭāʾ calls it increased guidance after distinguishing Abraham from his people. Wāḥidī, Baghawī and Ibn al-Jawzī compare Abraham's prayer in 14:35 that he and his children avoid idols; Qurṭubī compares 1:6. Sulamī's unnamed transmitters ask increase, firmness and protection against worship like the people; al-Wāsiṭī fears return to his own attributes. Qurṭubī cites Shuʿayb's conditional return in 7:89 as a rational possibility. Islahi says the conditional is a general truth and rejects shifting it into the past. [14 notes: TUSTARI; SULAMI(ابن عطاء,الواسطي,بعضهم,سهل); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD+; ISLAHI-TADABBUR+; QURTUBI-FULL+; WAHIDI-BASIT+; WAHIDI-WAJIZ+]
- 6:77/q12/p2 Divine aid to attain or recognize the true Lord and affirm His unity. — Ṭabarī says guidance enables the truth of tawḥīd; Bayḍāwī calls it aid to attain truth; Ālūsī glosses the inescapable path since none of the observed bodies can be God. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says yahdinī plainly means 'guide me' and supports a childhood episode; Ibn Jarīr, as reported by Ibn Kathīr, takes the request as evidence for contemplation. Ālūsī reports a request for help finding truth but finds this reading remote from the apparent sense; he nevertheless says certainty acquired through reasoning can coexist with debate. [7 notes: TAB-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن جرير)+; TAB-FULL+; ALUSI-FULL(قيل)-]
- 6:77/q12/p3 A fair-minded conditional warning to moon worshippers about their own straying. — Al-Zamakhsharī, transmitted by Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī, says anyone deifying the setting moon is astray. Bayḍāwī and Nasafī say Abraham guides his people through the example; Bursevī says the plea indirectly names their error; Ibn al-Munīr calls it stronger than the earlier 'I do not love those that set.' Biqāʿī and Ālūsī see a tactful, escalating repudiation, the first proof having softened resistance before explicit disavowal in 6:78. Elmalılı and Ṭabāṭabāʾī treat the conditional as a hypothetical of what persistence in moon worship would mean. Islahi calls it a mirror held to the people. [14 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن المنير,الزمخشري); ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 6:77/q12/p4 The condition refers to a past absence of guidance. — Islahi reports this interpretation only to reject it as an unnecessary strain on the wording. [0 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR(some exegetes)-]

## 6:77/q13 What does the conditional plea teach about the source of guidance?  [لَّمْ يَهْدِنِى رَبِّى · theology]
- 6:77/q13/p1 Only God grants effective guidance; displayed proofs and human reasoning alone do not supply it. — Rāzī says enabling, obstacle removal and presentation of proofs were already present, so the requested guidance goes beyond them; he adduces 26:78 and 14:35. Biqāʿī likewise excludes other agents, mere displayed proof and already possessed inferential knowledge. Bayḍāwī says truth requires divine enabling; al-Wāsiṭī says a servant supplies the invocation but God supplies guidance. Tustarī, Elmalılı, Islahi and Study Quran stress reliance on divine guidance; Islahi describes revelation as 'light upon light' with 24:35. Al-Zamakhsharī's reported warning also depends on God's enabling grace. [13 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; SULAMI(الواسطي); TUSTARI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN(R); RAZI-FULL+]

## 6:77/q14 How is لَئِن ... لَأَكُونَنَّ constructed?  [لَئِن لَأَكُونَنَّ · grammar]
- 6:77/q14/p1 The first lām marks an oath; the second introduces its answer. — Jishumī identifies these respective functions in Abraham's conditional statement. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 6:77/q15 Who are ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلضَّآلِّينَ?  [ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلضَّآلِّينَ · referent]
- 6:77/q15/p1 People who miss true guidance by worshipping created beings, especially the moon, stars or idols. — Ṭabarī names worshippers of anyone besides God; Baghawī glosses distance from guidance; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says idolaters and worshippers of created things are intended, although 'astray' is broader. Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Bursevī and al-Zamakhsharī apply the warning to the moon's worshippers. Al-Suddī reports Abraham's plea after the moon's disappearance; Study Quran says it may expose his people's religious status. [13 notes: TAB-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(السدي); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري); TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(R)]

## 6:77/q16 How do inward readings understand the moon and Abraham's guidance?  [ٱلْقَمَرَ يَهْدِنِى رَبِّى · inward]
turns on: Which inner faculty or spiritual passage the celestial sequence signifies.
- 6:77/q16/p1 The moon is the illuminated heart; its setting discloses dependence on the spirit, and guidance passes beyond luminous inner veils to divine light. — Qāshānī places the heart above the soul, illuminated by disclosures through the names al-ʿAlīm and al-Ḥakīm; its light is borrowed from the spirit and may be obscured by the soul. He likens stopping at such luminous veils to Christians' error. [3 notes: QASHANI]
- 6:77/q16/p2 The sequence depicts passage from human faculties and darkness toward subsistence and direct realization in God. — Sulamī reports movement from compulsion through lordship's and awe's lights, effacement of human traits and subsistence in the Everlasting. Qushayrī says gnosis removes seeking and doubt; another account moves from seeing signs through God, to seeing them as God's, to seeing them effaced in God. Rāzī reports mapping star, moon and sun to sense, imagination/estimation and intellect, all finite and governed. Tustarī says Abraham's path is generosity and disavowal of all but God; the Gabriel episode illustrates reliance on God alone. Al-Wāsiṭī says looking at one's own attributes risks misguidance. [7 notes: SULAMI(الواسطي,بعضهم); QUSHAYRI; RAZI-FULL(بعض الناس); TUSTARI(إبراهيم)]

## 6:77/q17 How does the moon scene fit Abraham's argument and the surrounding passage?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the focus is the immediate progression of Abraham's argument or its place in the sūra's prophetic message.
- 6:77/q17/p1 It continues the signs shown to Abraham and advances his gradual refutation of worshipped celestial bodies. — Jishumī links the narrative to 6:75's signs and Abraham's reasoning. Wāḥidī says the moon and sun repeat the star's argument. Biqāʿī says the earlier setting prepared the audience; Ālūsī says resistance softened before the stronger insinuation here and disavowal at 6:78. Ibn ʿAṭāʾ says guidance increases once proof is established. Islahi says this expands the prior brief statement that heavenly bodies obey divine law; Abraham had first addressed his father, then his nation, and 6:79–80 speaks with prophetic authority. Ṭabāṭabāʾī likewise places the astral test after debate with father and idol worshippers. [11 notes: JISHUMI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; SULAMI(ابن عطاء); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; TABATABAI]
- 6:77/q17/p2 The account grounds the sūra's call to Muhammad in the shared monotheistic mission of Abraham and the prophets. — Islahi says Muhammad follows earlier prophets' guidance, preaches without compelling belief, and revives Abraham's religion for Israelites and Ishmaelites who claimed him while inheriting later fabrications. He says prophets share a mission but adapt their speech: John and Jesus use parables, while Abraham's combative audience required gradual reasoning, double meaning and even gentle humor. Kuryolu says the Qur'an chiefly confronts corrupted innate divinity through shirk; Abraham's hanif confession culminates in worship of the Creator alone. [9 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 6:77/q18 What further lesson is drawn from Abraham's observation of the heavenly bodies?  [* · interpretation]
- 6:77/q18/p1 Observing their dependence directs worship and love to their Creator and warns against idolatry. — Islahi says fading objects cannot merit the Creator's love, the moon scene makes such worship a grave and sorrowful error, and reason and innate capacity can move people beyond man-made idols. Kuryolu says Abraham's observation establishes the proper faith in God and the Qur'an confronts polytheism as corruption of an innate sense of divinity. Elmalılı notes the moon's beauty can seduce without divine guidance. [7 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ELMALILI]
- 6:77/q18/p2 The eastward location of rising signs and westward setting connect this scene to reported directions of trial and repentance. — Burhān cites the Prophet's report that fitna and disbelief come from the east and the door of repentance opens from the west, and attaches it to Abraham's rejection of setting stars. [1 notes: BURHAN(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 6:77/q19 What exegetical sciences are catalogued alongside this passage?  [* · sciences]
- 6:77/q19/p1 The reported catalog covers revelation contexts, transmission and readings, and recitation practice within a broader scheme of Qurʾanic studies. — Suyūṭī says earlier scholars lacked a comparable compilation; al-Kāfījī's short work covered definitions of tafsīr, taʾwīl, Qurʾān, sūra and verse, opinion exegesis conditions, and etiquette. Al-Balqīnī introduced Jalāl al-Dīn's organized Mawāqiʿ al-ʿUlūm. Its divisions include Meccan/Medinan, travel/residence, time and occasion of revelation; mutawātir, solitary and anomalous readings plus transmitters and memorisers; and pauses, beginnings, imāla, lengthening, hamza easing and assimilation. [6 notes: ITQAN(الكافيجي)]

## 6:77/q20 What is the meaning of the preceding star term carried into this comparison?  [* · meaning]
- 6:77/q20/p1 Kawkab denotes a star, particularly one as it appears. — Samīn gives the variant form kawkaba and reports al-Rāghib's restriction to an appearing star. Al-Ṣaghānī derives it from wakaba with an extra initial kāf and places it under that root, against al-Jawharī's and al-Layth's quadriliteral analysis. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR(الصغاني)]
