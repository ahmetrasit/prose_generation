# 100:1: 11 questions

## 100:1/q01 Who or what are meant by al-ʿādiyāt?  [ٱلْعَٰدِيَٰتِ · referent]
turns on: Whether the oath names war horses, pilgrimage camels, both, or another class of swift runners; the readings weigh the following descriptions, Arabic usage, the Badr report, and the interpretation of ḍabḥ.
- 100:1/q01/p1 War horses charging in God’s cause or in battle, either generally or as the horses of a particular raid. — Most authorities identify them as horses. Their speed, hooves, panting, sparks, dawn attack, and dust suit the following descriptions; Ibn ʿAbbās and others connect them with raiding for God. Al-Ṭabarī prefers horses because camels do not pant. Al-Rāzī adds that sparks better fit iron horseshoes and dawn attacks are easier on horseback; Al-Biqāʿī notes their worldly and religious benefits and their reward or sin according to use. Ibn ʿAbbās’s account may concern a separate detachment; some take the definite article generically, others for a particular expedition. Poetic evidence includes verses by ʿAntarah and others. Some say horsemen rather than horses, or restrict the honored horses to Muslim horses at Badr. Reports also describe horses’ value for warfare, defense, family protection, and horsemanship. [109 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); FARRA(ابن عباس); MAJAZ; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,الضحاك,سالم,عطاء,عكرمة,قتادة,مجاهد); NAHHAS(مجاهد وعكرمة عن ابن عباس); THALABI(ابن عباس,مقاتل); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس وأنس والحسن); QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-ASBAB(ابن عباس,مقاتل); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس,الكلبي,مقاتل); WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن عباس وعطاء ومجاهد وعكرمة والحسن والكلبي وقتادة والمقاتلان وأبو العالية); KASHSHAF-FULL(ابن عباس,عنترة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس); TABRISI(ابن عباس); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس,ابن قتيبة,الزجاج,الفراء); RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس ومجاهد وقتادة والضحاك وعطاء,الكلبي); QURTUBI-FULL(أهل اللغة,ابن عباس,جمهور المفسرين وأهل اللغة,عطاء,عنترة,مسلم); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,عطية,قتادة,مجاهد); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس,الجمهور,عنترة); ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; BINTSHATI(ابن عباس والحسن,علي); TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL+; ELMALILI(İbn Abbas)+; ISLAHI-TADABBUR+; RAZI-FULL+; TAB-FULL+; TAB-FULL(ابن عباس وعلي)-; KASHSHAF-FULL(علي)-; IBNATIYYA-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب)-; TABRISI(علي)-; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(علي)-; RAZI-FULL(علي وابن مسعود وإبراهيم والقرظي)-; QURTUBI-FULL(علي)-; ALUSI-FULL-; TABATABAI-; ISLAHI-TADABBUR-]
- 100:1/q01/p2 Pilgrims’ camels running between ʿArafāt, Muzdalifa, and Minā, or camels in a raid. — ʿAlī’s interpretation identifies pilgrims’ mounts and cites Badr, where Muslims reportedly had only one or two horses, as an objection to the horse view. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to retract and adopt ʿAlī’s answer; Ibn Masʿūd, Ibrāhīm, Ubayd ibn Umayr, Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb, al-Suddī, and others are also named. Safiyya’s poem depicts runners at Jamʿ raising dust. Variants put the route from ʿArafāt through Muzdalifa to Minā, or include camels at Badr or in a raid; some reports say dust comes from hooves and feet. Some authorities prefer ʿAlī’s transmission as more authoritative. The interpretation is disputed because lexicographers may not use ḍabḥ for camel sounds, and some treat it as metaphor or as ḍabʿ. - 100:1/q01/p3 Both war horses and pilgrims’ camels, or more generally any swift runners. — Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī presents the oath as admitting horses, camels, or both. Other explanations use al-ʿādiyāt for swift runners generally, including horses, camels, and renowned human runners; the root is glossed as running quickly, crossing or exceeding a limit, or troops rushing to attack. Some distinguish attacking infantry from cavalry. Some sources leave the oath animals open to either the warhorse or pilgrimage reading. [26 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو عبيدة,المبرد); SAMIN-DURR(أبو عبيدة والمبرد); ELMALILI(Bazıları); IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو عبيدة,ابن عباس,الكشاف); BINTSHATI; STUDYQURAN; WAHIDI-ASBAB; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 100:1/q01/p4 Modern fire-sparking weapons or fast motorized assault vehicles. — Elmalılı extends the scene literally to modern weapons and by extension to motorized assault vehicles; he argues that a Meccan dating, when Muslims had neither horses nor weapons, would make a future reference especially clear. The distinctive plural forms and successive fāʾs suggest development through the ages, including horses and motorized raiders. [2 notes: ELMALILI; ELMALILI+]
- 100:1/q01/p5 In inward readings, striving or longing souls, or souls engaged in spiritual struggle. — Al-Qāshānī reads the chargers as striving souls traveling God’s path through ascetic discipline, strenuous effort, and longing. Al-Ālūsī reports longing souls whose prayer sounds from yearning, thought kindles knowledge, and they charge desire and habit toward sacred light; another reading maps dhikr, guidance, the soul crossing bodily darkness, and the heart’s forces joining remembrance. Al-Bayḍāwī’s allegory has souls transmitting holiness, kindling knowledge, attacking caprice, and seeking the highest Garden. [4 notes: QASHANI; ALUSI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(al-Bayḍāwī)]

## 100:1/q02 What does ḍabḥan describe?  [ضَبْحًۭا · meaning]
turns on: Whether ḍabḥ denotes actual breathing, a related movement or gait, or a broader metaphorical sound; the camel reading also turns on whether it can be used for camels or equated with ḍabʿ.
- 100:1/q02/p1 Strenuous breathing or panting, especially the breath of horses as they run. — Lexicographers and exegetes call it breath from the nostrils, throat, chest, or insides; a loud or rattling exertion sound distinct from neighing, whinnying, roaring, barking, a camel’s bellow, or ordinary calls. Ibn ʿAbbās imitates it as “aḥ aḥ,” “ah ah,” or “aj aj.” Some say a horse makes a whinny-like ḥamḥama while a camel breathes; others describe camel breathing as ḍabḥ. Reports also call it snorting or groaning. Poetry by ʿAntarah and others illustrates horses panting, while fox poems and a bow verse support broader usage. Horses, dogs, foxes, and other animals are named; fatigue, fear, or appetite may cause the sound. Some say only dogs and horses (or horses, dogs, and foxes) make it; other authors reject that restriction and cite camels, snakes, owls, rabbits, echoes, and bows. Some explain its origin through fire changing or slightly darkening color. [97 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); FARRA(ابن عباس); MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB(آخرون); ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,سالم,عطاء,عكرمة,علي,قتادة,مجاهد); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); NAHHAS(ابن عباس); THALABI(أهل اللغة,ابن عباس,الحكماء,شاعر); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس); QUSHAYRI; QASHANI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,ابن عباس,الليث,مقاتل); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن عباس,ابن عباس وعطاء ومجاهد وعكرمة والحسن والكلبي وقتادة والمقاتلان وأبو العالية); KASHSHAF-FULL(عنترة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبو حنيفة,ابن عباس); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة,الزجاج,الفراء); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو صالح,أهل اللغة,ابن عباس,توبة,عطاء,عنترة); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو حنيفة,الجمهور من أهل التفسير واللغة); SAMIN-DURR(أبو حنيفة,ابن عباس,عنترة); IBNKATHIR; BIQAI-FULL(ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,عطية,علي بن أبي طالب,مجاهد); ALUSI-FULL(أبو حنيفة,أبو عبيدة,ابن عباس,عنترة); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو عبيدة,ابن عباس); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BINTSHATI; TABATABAI]
- 100:1/q02/p2 An extended foreleg movement or hard, fast running, expressed through the related word ḍabʿ. — Some equate ḍabḥ with ḍabʿ, extending the forelegs while running; explanations include dialectal interchange of ḥāʾ and ʿayn, a stretched neck, a horse’s quick gait, or running very fast. Al-Kashshāf rejects the proposed letter interchange as unsupported, while al-Qāmūs adopts it; another account calls the camel explanation insecure. Some apply the related foreleg sense to horses as well. [16 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB(آخرون); THALABI(الحكماء); WAHIDI-BASIT(الأزهري); JISHUMI; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو عبيدة,المبرد); SAMIN-DURR(أبو عبيدة والمبرد); IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو عبيدة); KASHSHAF-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); IBNASHUR-FULL(الكشاف)-]
- 100:1/q02/p3 A broader loud sound or exertion noise, extended metaphorically beyond animals; one gloss also gives “ash.” — Abū Ḥayyān calls ḍabḥ a loud sound during strenuous running, distinct from ordinary animal calls. Some say it began with foxes and transferred to horses; others extend it to many animals, echoes, or bows. Al-Qurṭubī also records “ash” as a sense. [13 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبو حنيفة,ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو حنيفة,ابن عباس); SAMIN-DURR(أبو حنيفة); QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حنيفة); BIQAI-FULL]

## 100:1/q03 How does ḍabḥan function grammatically?  [ضَبْحًۭا · grammar]
turns on: Whether the accusative is governed by an implied cognate verb, is circumstantial, modifies al-ʿādiyāt directly, or marks time.
- 100:1/q03/p1 A cognate accusative or verbal noun governed by an implied verb: the runners pant (or run) a panting (or running). — Al-Naḥḥās calls it a temporal adverb, while al-Thaʿlabī, al-Zajjāj, and others parse it as a cognate accusative. Other analyses supply an omitted verb such as taḍbaḥu; some take it as an emphatic verbal noun for the active participle or, under the ḍabʿ/fast-running gloss, as a cognate accusative of running. [16 notes: NAHHAS; THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL(الزجاج,الفراء); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ELMALILI; ALUSI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); BINTSHATI+]
- 100:1/q03/p2 A circumstantial accusative, or verbal noun used circumstantially to describe the runners as panting. — The Basrans analyze it as a circumstantial accusative; al-Zamakhsharī and others allow a circumstantial expression or an omitted verb whose clause describes the runners. Al-Bayḍāwī lists omitted-verb, cognate-accusative, and circumstantial possibilities. Ibn ʿĀshūr allows a circumstantial expression of sound, or manner of running if derived from ḍabʿ. [9 notes: RAZI-FULL(البصريون); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI+]
- 100:1/q03/p3 A modifier governed by al-ʿādiyāt, as if the phrase meant “the panting runners.” — Al-Zamakhsharī allows ḍabḥan to modify al-ʿādiyāt because panting accompanies running. Abū Ḥayyān objects that running and panting are not identical; Sāmīn says this objection misreads the explanation, which concerns the inseparable relation and governance, not synonymy. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR+; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ)-]

## 100:1/q04 How is al-ʿādiyāt formed and what does its root convey?  [ٱلْعَٰدِيَٰتِ · meaning]
- 100:1/q04/p1 A feminine sound plural active participle of ʿadw, “running or racing quickly,” with yāʾ replacing wāw after a kasra. — The word is described as the plural of ʿādiyah, a swift-running creature, or the feminine plural active participle. The root ʿ-d-w conveys crossing or exceeding; as movement it gives running, and in other uses hostility, oppression, or contagion. The feminine form agrees with a non-rational referent. Al-ʿādiyāt occurs only here in the Qurʾān. Some military usage applies ʿādiyah to troops that rush to attack; some distinguish infantry from cavalry. [14 notes: JISHUMI; SAMIN-DURR; ELMALILI(Bazıları); IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الجمهور)]

## 100:1/q05 What force does the initial wāw have?  [وَٱلْعَٰدِيَٰتِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the wāw has its conventional oath function or works rhetorically beyond a literal oath.
- 100:1/q05/p1 It is an oath particle introducing an oath by the chargers. — Al-Naḥḥās says wāw governs the genitive; the usual reading treats it as magnifying and emphasizing its object. Other explanations say God swears by the cavalry or by valued horses and pilgrimage mounts. Ibn al-ʿArabī compares this oath with oaths by Yā Sīn and Muḥammad’s life. [9 notes: NAHHAS; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن العربي); BINTSHATI(ابن عباس والحسن); IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN; TAB-FULL(زيد بن أسلم); BINTSHATI-]
- 100:1/q05/p2 It may work rhetorically beyond a literal oath; Asad translates it “Oh” for an imagined parable. — Asad prefers “Oh” because he takes the following clauses as an imaginary parable. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ argues that wāw may move beyond its literal oath function for a rhetorical purpose and that assuming an oath object must be magnified drives far-fetched readings. [3 notes: BINTSHATI; ASAD-NOTES(Asad)+; BINTSHATI+; BINTSHATI-]

## 100:1/q06 What event or scene do reports associate with the oath?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports differ over the expedition, its commander and route, or whether the oath evokes ʿAlī’s campaign or a pilgrimage procession.
- 100:1/q06/p1 A delayed expedition or cavalry detachment, most often the campaign against Banū Kināna under al-Mundhir ibn ʿAmr. — Reports say the Prophet sent horses or a force away; after a month or other delay without news, hypocrites or Jews said the men had been killed, causing anxiety. God or Gabriel then disclosed the expedition’s progress, route, or safe return through the oath. Versions place the force against one or two Kināna clans, or in Tihāmah, and describe horses snorting through their nostrils, striking sparks, attacking at dawn, raising dust, and reaching the enemy. One version says it was sent to Hunayn. Reports vary over the commander and whether the report came from Ibn ʿAbbās, Muqātil, al-Kalbī, or others; one account identifies Mirthad ibn Abī Mirthad’s horse. Ibn Kathīr calls a Bazzār version very strange; Ibn ʿĀshūr notes the weak Ḥafṣ ibn Jamīʿ in its chain. [28 notes: MUQATIL(مقاتل); THALABI(مقاتل); WAHIDI-ASBAB(ابن عباس,مقاتل); WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي,مقاتل); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس,مقاتل); RAZI-FULL(الكلبي); QURTUBI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); SUYUTI-LUBAB(ابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن كثير,مقاتل); TABRISI(مقاتل); TABATABAI(مقاتل); STUDYQURAN]
- 100:1/q06/p2 ʿAlī’s campaign at Dhāt al-Salāsil, where he defeated the enemy after earlier commanders returned unsuccessful. — A report from Abū ʿAbd Allāh connects the sūra with ʿAlī’s campaign. Another report says Gabriel announced ʿAlī’s victory at dawn and that he returned with captives and spoils; Dhāt al-Salāsil is said to be named for captives bound like chains. [5 notes: TABRISI(أبو عبد الله); TABATABAI(the Prophet,أئمة أهل البيت,أبو عبد الله)]
- 100:1/q06/p3 A pilgrimage procession of camels between the rites, described by ʿAlī as the oath’s referent. — In the reported debate, ʿAlī identifies the procession from ʿArafāt through Muzdalifa to Minā; Ibn ʿAbbās first answers horses and is said to retract. The report uses the small number of horses at Badr to support the camel interpretation. [15 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس وعلي); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); TABRISI(علي); THALABI(ابن عباس,علي بن أبي طالب); QURTUBI-FULL(الشعبي,علي); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,علي بن أبي طالب); ALUSI-FULL(علي); BINTSHATI(ابن عباس,الزمخشري,علي); STUDYQURAN(others,ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib)]

## 100:1/q07 When and where was the sūra revealed, and how is it counted?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Meccan versus Medinan attribution, including whether the raid report is reliable and whether the horse interpretation implies a post-Hijra setting.
- 100:1/q07/p1 Meccan, with eleven verses; one Meccan chronology places it fourteenth in revelation order. — Al-Ḥasan, ʿIkrima, ʿAṭāʾ, Ibn Masʿūd, Jābir, and others report or classify it as Meccan. Ibn Kathīr and al-Durr say it has eleven verses. Jābir ibn Zayd places it fourteenth, after al-ʿAṣr and before al-Kawthar. Some reports from Ibn ʿAbbās say it was revealed in Mecca; the Makkan dating is associated by some with style. [11 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(ابن مسعود وجابر والحسن وعكرمة وعطاء); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن مسعود,جابر بن زيد); STUDYQURAN(most commentators); RAZI-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس وأنس بن مالك وقتادة)-; JISHUMI(الحسن وعكرمة وقتادة)-; RAZI-FULL(الكلبي)-; IBNASHUR-FULL(أنس بن مالك)-; TABATABAI-]
- 100:1/q07/p2 Medinan, possibly revealed around a raid or after permission to fight. — Ibn ʿAbbās, Anas, and Qatāda are cited for Medinan classification. Al-Kalbī reasons that if the horse interpretation is accepted, fighting was permitted in Medina. Ibn ʿĀshūr considers Medinan the stronger view despite the strange expedition report; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the oaths appear to describe warrior horses and jihad began after the Hijra. Others tie the date to the expedition report. A report says al-Zalzalah and al-ʿĀdiyāt each equal half the Qurʾān. [9 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس وأنس بن مالك وقتادة); JISHUMI(الحسن وعكرمة وقتادة); RAZI-FULL(الكلبي); IBNASHUR-FULL(أنس بن مالك); TABATABAI; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,الحسن); IBNASHUR-FULL+; MAWARDI-FULL(ابن مسعود وجابر والحسن وعكرمة وعطاء)-; IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن مسعود)-; STUDYQURAN(most commentators)-]

## 100:1/q08 What does the oath contribute to the sūra’s message?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the oath models service, recalls the benefits of pilgrimage mounts, or functions as testimony for the sūra’s claim about human ingratitude.
- 100:1/q08/p1 The horses’ exertion models dutiful service and makes the oath evidence for human ingratitude and failure to prepare for judgment. — Islāḥī says exhausted horses spend their last energy on their master’s task, which testifies against human ingratitude to God. Al-Biqāʿī connects the oath to people preferring transient wealth and status over what endures, and says horses earn reward or sin according to whether they serve God or pride and harm. The oath recognizes worldly and religious benefits and warns against keeping horses only for display; Q 16:8’s purpose marker applies to riding rather than ornament. Other accounts say a horse used for God and cared for in fulfillment of God’s right becomes protection and reward. [17 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BINTSHATI(ابن القيم,محمد عبده); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL; RAZI-FULL]
- 100:1/q08/p2 The camel oath recalls the benefits of camels, encourages pilgrimage, and makes the oath persuasive to people who esteem pilgrimage mounts. — Al-Rāzī links the camel reading to the benefits of camels in Q 88:17, God’s service to people, and pilgrimage; he also says neglecting required ḥajj is characterized as disbelief. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the reading honors mounts esteemed by pagans and Muslims, making the sworn claim persuasive to both. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 100:1/q08/p3 The oath’s subject is evidence or testimony for the claim that follows, rather than an object that must itself be sacred. — Islāḥī rejects the assumption that an oath object must be sacred and says the horses’ testimony supports the later premise about human ingratitude. The first five verses swear by chargers, while verses 6–11 discuss ingratitude and judgment. [3 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN; ISLAHI-TADABBUR+]

## 100:1/q09 How does the opening scene connect to the rest of the sūra and other verses?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the link is the raid-to-judgment analogy, the sequence of the oath’s actions, or wording that accommodates competing referents.
- 100:1/q09/p1 The opening raid’s sudden violence mirrors the disclosure of graves and hearts and the rapid events of judgment later in the sūra. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ links the familiar dawn raid, dust, and scattered gathering to people leaving graves in groups and facing linked events before the All-Aware; she notes Q 99:6. Qutb says the rough, rumbling rhythm suits both the noisy dust and disclosure of graves and hearts, framing the moral and resurrection scenes. Al-Biqāʿī reads the sūra after al-Zalzalah as tracing the disposition behind evil deeds and imagined worldly gain. Other sources say the oath and its answer disclose the sūra’s purpose and explain its title. [15 notes: BIQAI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 100:1/q09/p2 The successive fāʾs link the descriptions as ordered events, and the opening’s forms and rhythm enact a swift charge. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the apparent reading of the first five verses favors cavalry on a particular raid whose actions are successively linked by fāʾ. Ibn ʿĀshūr describes the usual sequential use of fāʾ, with examples where it may mark order of mention. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says fāʾ marks sparks as a result of hard running. Qutb describes rapid, forceful jumps, like a runner reaching the finish. [5 notes: TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI; QUTB-ZILAL; ELMALILI]
- 100:1/q09/p3 The wording can accommodate either war horses or pilgrimage camels as a rhetorical arrangement. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the chosen words accommodate both readings. Elmalılı’s camel reading also explains the next oath image as pilgrims traveling by night and lighting a fire at the Sacred Monument. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 100:1/q10 What reward or virtue do reports associate with reciting the sūra?  [* · sciences]
- 100:1/q10/p1 A reciter receives ten good deeds for each person who spent the night at Muzdalifah and witnessed Jamʿ; another report assigns the sūra half the Qurʾān together with al-Zalzalah. — Jishumī and al-Kashshāf report ten good deeds for every person who spent the night at Muzdalifah and witnessed Jamʿ. Al-Durr reports al-Ḥasan’s Prophetic saying that al-Zalzalah and al-ʿĀdiyāt each equal half the Qurʾān, and another version through ʿAṭāʾ from Ibn ʿAbbās that also gives one-third to al-Ikhlāṣ and one-quarter to al-Kāfirūn. Al-Rāzī reports a reward measured by the number who spend the night at Muzdalifah and witness Jamʿ. [5 notes: JISHUMI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); KASHSHAF-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وآله وسلم); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,الحسن); RAZI-FULL(أبي عن النبي)]

## 100:1/q11 What does the basmala signify in the opening commentary?  [* · inward]
- 100:1/q11/p1 It is a guarded, jealous word suited to a protected tongue, heart, or spirit; it also presents God as sovereign, generous, and specially merciful through guidance. — Al-Qushayrī says only a tongue protected from idle talk and backbiting may utter the basmala, only a guarded heart may know it, and only a spirit protected from attachments and veils is fit to love it. Al-Biqāʿī describes God’s complete command, universal gifts of creation and clarification, and special blessing through guidance to the elect. [4 notes: QUSHAYRI; BIQAI-FULL]
