# 100:1: 19 questions

## 100:1/q01 What does the opening wāw do?  [وَٱلْعَٰدِيَٰتِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the opening is a literal honorific oath, testimony, or a rhetorical evocation of an imagined scene.
- 100:1/q01/p1 It introduces God's oath by the chargers. — Al-Naḥḥās says the oath wāw governs the genitive. Zayd b. Aslam declined to interpret the opening letters beyond calling them God's oath. Elmalılı calls it an oath. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ records the usual view that an oath magnifies its object, but criticizes the restrictive interpretations it produced. Islahi treats the oath as the animals' testimony to the later claim about ingratitude. [6 notes: NAHHAS; TAB-FULL(زيد بن أسلم); ELMALILI; BINTSHATI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 100:1/q01/p2 The wāw evokes the scene rhetorically rather than serving only as a literal oath. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ appeals to her treatment of 93:1 and says the honorific-oath assumption leaves the horse and camel dispute unresolved. Asad prefers an exclamatory rendering because the following clauses form an imagined parable. [3 notes: BINTSHATI; ASAD-NOTES(Asad)+; BINTSHATI+]
- 100:1/q01/p3 It is an exclamation: 'Oh, the chargers!' — Asad prefers 'Oh' to his usual 'Consider' and most translators' 'By,' on the basis of the imaginary parable that follows. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES(Asad)+]

## 100:1/q02 What does al-ʿādiyāt mean and how is it formed?  [ٱلْعَٰدِيَٰتِ · meaning]
- 100:1/q02/p1 Swift runners or chargers, the feminine plural active participle of running. — Jishumī calls it the plural of ʿādiyah. Al-Qurṭubī defines running as rapid motion with limbs spread; Abū Ḥayyān says swift runners; al-Samīn and Bursevī explain the yāʾ replacing wāw after kasra, as does al-Ālūsī. Elmalılı traces ʿadw to crossing a limit: running, hostility, oppression, and spatial contagion are related senses; in battle ʿadī/ʿādiya name attacking troops, though some assign the former to infantry and the latter to cavalry. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ notes this is the word's only Qur'anic occurrence and sees distance or exceeding in running, enmity and transgression. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the term especially describes horses and camels and can analogically describe human runners; he names al-Sulayk, al-Shanfarā, Taʾabbaṭa Sharran and ʿAmr b. Umayya. Its feminine form agrees with a non-rational referent. [20 notes: JISHUMI; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الجمهور); ELMALILI(Bazıları); BINTSHATI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 100:1/q03 Who or what are the chargers?  [ٱلْعَٰدِيَٰتِ · referent]
turns on: Whether ḍabḥ and the later sparks, dawn attack and dust fit horses or camels; the reported ʿAlī–Ibn ʿAbbās dispute and the number of horses at Badr; whether the oath may accommodate both or a further figurative referent.
- 100:1/q03/p1 Horses, usually cavalry charging or raiding in God's cause. — Mujāhid, Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatāda and many others identify horses; some say the Prophet's cavalry, some a particular detachment, and some horses generally. Al-Ṭabarī chooses horses because camels do not pant; al-Rāzī adds that iron shoes spark and horses suit dawn attacks. Al-Ṭabrisī and others cite Ibn ʿAbbās's point that hooves raise dust. Al-Wāḥidī attributes preference to al-Farrāʾ and al-Zajjāj. ʿAntarah and other poets depict panting chargers. Al-Ālūsī says Badr's few horses cannot exclude other horses or the delayed expedition, doubts the report and cites conflicting transmissions. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the five sequential descriptions favor a particular cavalry raid; Islahi says they fit warhorses but disputes confining them to Muslim raiders as the oath's moral proof. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says ḍabḥ points to the animals rather than horsemen. Ibn ʿAbbās, as reported by Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, initially specified horses raiding for God's cause; al-Ṭabarī prefers horses because, in his judgment, camels do not make ḍabḥ. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī lists camels in a raid as an alternative distinct from pilgrims' camels. [4 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB(علي); IBNATIYYA-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); TABATABAI]
- 100:1/q03/p4 Both warhorses and pilgrimage camels are encompassed. — Ibn ʿAṭiyyah explicitly allows both; al-Qurṭubī reports Ibn ʿAbbās applying the verses to battle and pilgrimage. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the selected wording accommodates both, and Study Quran says each fulfills a function worthy of the oath. - 100:1/q03/p5 Striving or longing souls are the inward chargers. — Al-Qāshānī reads ascetic striving, discipline and intense longing; al-Ālūsī reports souls advancing against desire and habit toward sacred light. - 100:1/q03/p6 The image can extend to modern weapons and swift motorized assault vehicles. — Elmalılı relates fire-sparking arms and motor vehicles to the distinctive plurals and successive fāʾs as a historical development; on Meccan dating this would allude to equipment Muslims did not yet possess. [2 notes: ELMALILI; ELMALILI+]
- 100:1/q03/p7 Camels, with no particular journey specified. — ʿAbd Allāh and ʿUbayd ibn ʿUmayr identify the runners simply as camels; Ibrāhīm reports a camel interpretation and distinguishes camel breathing from horse panting. [3 notes: TAB(إبراهيم,عبد الله,عبيد بن عمير)]

## 100:1/q04 Do the horses denote a particular force or horses generally?  [ٱلْعَٰدِيَٰتِ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the definite article identifies the delayed expedition or names a general class, and whether the following actions describe one raid.
- 100:1/q04/p1 A particular expedition's horses are in view. — Al-Rāzī says the definite article may refer to the expedition. Reports variously identify the delayed force with Kinānah, give a month's missing news, or connect ʿAlī's Dhāt al-Salāsil campaign. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites the five successive actions. Al-Ālūsī says a particular expedition remains possible even if Badr had few horses. Al-Suyūṭī cites Ibn ʿAbbās's report of a cavalry detachment absent for a month without news as an argument for Medinan revelation. [12 notes: MUQATIL(مقاتل); WAHIDI-ASBAB(ابن عباس,مقاتل); WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي,مقاتل); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL(الكلبي); ALUSI-FULL; ITQAN(ابن عباس); TABATABAI+]
- 100:1/q04/p2 Horses in general, especially those that run in God's cause, are in view. — Al-Wāḥidī explicitly generalizes the oath to horses running for God. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah notes their long-standing power to conquer and the good in their forelocks; Ibn al-Qayyim says their nobility, strength, victory and vengeance need no restriction to raiders. Al-Rāzī allows a generic definite article; al-Ālūsī says Badr cannot rule this out. [5 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI(ابن القيم); ISLAHI-TADABBUR-]

## 100:1/q05 What is ḍabḥ here?  [ضَبْحًۭا · meaning]
turns on: Whether ḍabḥ is a respiratory sound, a form of ḍabʿ describing gait, or a transferred term in the camel interpretation.
- 100:1/q05/p1 The hard runner's audible breath or panting, usually attributed to horses. — Authorities place the sound in the throat, chest, insides, mouth, nose or lips; al-Layth distinguishes it from neighing and Biqāʿī from snorting or bellowing. Ibn ʿAbbās imitates 'aḥ aḥ' or 'aj aj'; ʿAlī contrasts horse whinny-like panting with camel breath. Some call it snorting; al-Durr's version even has feet, whereas Ibn Mardawayh has nostrils. Exhaustion, fear and appetite may prompt it, and al-Qurṭubī suggests muzzles suppressed neighing lest an enemy be alerted. Al-Wāḥidī, al-Ṭabrisī and al-Rāzī distinguish ordinary calls; Islahi says the breath reveals obedience and spent energy. ʿAntarah, an unnamed Iraqi poet and other poems illustrate charging horses. Ibrāhīm distinguishes camel breathing from a horse's panting; ʿAlī also distinguishes these sounds. ʿIkrimah describes the pant of a running horse. [90 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); FARRA(ابن عباس); MAJAZ; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,سالم,عطاء,عكرمة,علي,قتادة,مجاهد); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); NAHHAS(ابن عباس); THALABI(أهل اللغة,ابن عباس,الحكماء,شاعر); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس); QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,ابن عباس,الليث); WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن عباس,ابن عباس وعطاء ومجاهد وعكرمة والحسن والكلبي وقتادة والمقاتلان وأبو العالية); KASHSHAF-FULL(عنترة); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس,ابن قتيبة,الزجاج,الفراء); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو صالح,ابن عباس,جمهور المفسرين وأهل اللغة,عنترة); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور من أهل التفسير واللغة); SAMIN-DURR(ابن عباس,عنترة); IBNKATHIR; BIQAI-FULL(ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,عطية,علي بن أبي طالب,قتادة,مجاهد); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس,عنترة); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن عباس); TABATABAI; BINTSHATI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; TAB(إبراهيم,ابن عباس,سالم,عكرمة,علي,قتادة,مجاهد)]
- 100:1/q05/p2 It denotes vigorous running with stretched forelegs or necks, linked to ḍabʿ. — Abū ʿUbaydah equates ḍabḥ and ḍabʿ; al-Azharī, al-Mubarrad and others describe extended forelegs, while some camel interpreters say stretched necks. Ṣafiyyah's poem is cited. Al-Kashshāf calls the letter substitution insecure; Ibn ʿĀshūr says linguists do not normally apply breath-sound ḍabḥ to camels and reports both dialect-substitution and metaphor alternatives; al-Qāmūs accepts interchange. Abū ʿUbaydah also allows the related verb for horses. Biqāʿī reports a nuance of running short of a full gallop. Al-Zamakhsharī reports an explanation in terms of camels' shoulders extending during travel, while saying its derivation is not established. [22 notes: MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB(آخرون); THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الأزهري); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو عبيدة,المبرد); SAMIN-DURR(أبو عبيدة والمبرد); BIQAI-FULL(ابن عباس); ALUSI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو عبيدة,الكشاف); KASHSHAF]
- 100:1/q05/p3 For camels it is a metaphorical application of the panting sound. — Al-Zamakhsharī conditions this on the authenticity of ʿAlī's report and likens it to transferred body terms; al-Samīn and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ repeat that analysis. Ibn ʿĀshūr also allows metaphor for a camel's intensified running sound. Al-Zamakhsharī compares metaphorical application to camels with bodily terms such as muzzle and hoof being transferred to humans, conditional on the report's soundness. [5 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI(الزمخشري); KASHSHAF]
- 100:1/q05/p4 It is a rattling sound in the saddlebag or movement of the horses' lips. — Al-Thaʿlabī reports these two explanations from unnamed authorities; al-Naḥḥās has Ibn ʿAbbās describe blowing through the lips. [2 notes: THALABI(الحكماء); NAHHAS(ابن عباس)]
- 100:1/q05/p5 The word can also mean ash. — Al-Qurṭubī records this lexical sense without applying it to the oath. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]

## 100:1/q06 Which creatures or things can make a ḍabḥ?  [ضَبْحًۭا · meaning]
turns on: Whether the reported restriction is sound and whether the wider Arabic examples are literal uses or metaphors.
- 100:1/q06/p1 Only horses and dogs, sometimes with foxes added, properly make the sound. — Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿAṭāʾ and others give versions with dog and horse or with fox too; lexicographers are cited for the restricted list. Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn and Biqāʿī say fox usage preceded transfer to horses; Tawbah's fox poem and the fire-darkening analogy support the usage. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī challenge the restriction as inconsistent with broader Arabic use. Al-Zamakhsharī reports a restriction to horses, dogs and foxes, alongside the horse and dog restriction attributed to ʿAṭāʾ and Ibn ʿAbbās. [24 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,عطاء); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); THALABI(أهل اللغة,ابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن عباس); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس,توبة,عطاء); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); SAMIN-DURR(ابن عباس); BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن عباس); TAB(ابن عباس,عطاء); KASHSHAF]
- 100:1/q06/p2 Arabic usage extends ḍabḥ to many animals and even a bow or echo. — Ibn ʿAṭiyyah lists camels, snakes, owls, rabbits, foxes, echoes and bows; Abū Ḥanīfah's poem gives a bow a fox-like ḍabḥ. Abū Ḥayyān doubts the restrictive Ibn ʿAbbās attribution. Al-Samīn regards wider uses as metaphors from a fox origin, whereas Ibn ʿAṭiyyah cites them against a strict animal limit. Al-Ālūsī also invokes the bow poem. [8 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبو حنيفة,ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو حنيفة,ابن عباس); SAMIN-DURR(أبو حنيفة); ALUSI-FULL(أبو حنيفة)]

## 100:1/q07 How is ḍabḥan parsed?  [ضَبْحًۭا · grammar]
turns on: Which verb or participle governs the accusative and whether the word names panting, a circumstance, or a kind of running.
- 100:1/q07/p1 A cognate accusative or verbal noun governed by an omitted 'they pant.' — Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī, al-Zajjāj through al-Rāzī, al-Kashshāf, al-Nasafī, al-Ālūsī, Elmalılı and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ give the implied verb. Al-Samīn says the supplied verbal clause is circumstantial. Al-Bayḍāwī allows an omitted verb's object. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ prefers the unrestricted verbal-noun force. [12 notes: THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; BINTSHATI+]
- 100:1/q07/p2 A circumstantial accusative: the runners are panting or have panting. — The Basrans, al-Kashshāf, al-Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn, Ibn ʿĀshūr and others allow an adjective or verbal noun used as a state; Ibn ʿĀshūr also links manner of running to the ḍabʿ etymology. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ acknowledges this but prefers the absolute noun. [10 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL(البصريون); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI]
- 100:1/q07/p3 Al-ʿādiyāt itself governs the noun, as running implies panting. — Al-Farrāʾ and al-Zamakhsharī paraphrase the runners as the panting ones. Abū Ḥayyān objects that running and panting are not identical; al-Samīn replies that Zamakhsharī meant inseparable accompaniment and grammatical governance, not synonymy. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL(الفراء); KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ELMALILI; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ)-]
- 100:1/q07/p4 A cognate accusative to running if ḍabḥ is a kind of fast gait. — Abū ʿUbaydah's and al-Mubarrad's ḍabʿ-based gloss supplies a verbal noun of motion; al-Samīn makes it emphatic of the participle and al-Ālūsī permits this parse. [3 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(أبو عبيدة)]
- 100:1/q07/p5 An adverb of time. — Al-Naḥḥās gives this parse. [1 notes: NAHHAS]

## 100:1/q08 What event is reported as the occasion of the opening?  [* · reports]
turns on: Which campaign or journey the narrations identify, and whether the debated Badr horse count bears on the verse's occasion.
- 100:1/q08/p1 A delayed expedition against Kinānah under al-Mundhir ibn ʿAmr was announced by the verses. — Muqātil says it went to Ḥunayn; others say against one or two Kinānah clans. Reports differ on whether Jews, hypocrites or both spread rumors that the men had died. Gabriel disclosed their route or success, reassuring the Prophet and worried relatives. Al-Qurṭubī identifies al-Mundhir as an al-ʿAqabah leader. Al-Wāḥidī glosses ashabat as ranging across broad plains. Ibn ʿĀshūr notes Ibn Kathīr found al-Bazzār's version very strange and the chain has weak Ḥafṣ b. Jamīʿ, though he thinks the story possible. [18 notes: MUQATIL(مقاتل); THALABI(مقاتل); WAHIDI-ASBAB(مقاتل); WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي,مقاتل); TABRISI(مقاتل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(مقاتل); RAZI-FULL(الكلبي); QURTUBI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن كثير,مقاتل); TABATABAI(مقاتل); STUDYQURAN]
- 100:1/q08/p2 A delayed expedition to Tihāmah caused anxiety before Gabriel described its panting horses. — Muqātil's alternative says Jewish and hypocritical onlookers frightened those with missing relatives, and Gabriel came on Friday morning; the horses panted like foxes. [1 notes: MUQATIL(مقاتل)]
- 100:1/q08/p3 An unnamed cavalry detachment was away for a month without news. — Ibn ʿAbbās says the Prophet sent cavalry; the missing news troubled him and the sūra described the horses' nostril-breathing, sparks, dawn assault, dust and entry into enemy ranks. Ibn Abī Ḥātim, al-Wāḥidī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Durr and al-Suyūṭī preserve versions. This account need not identify Badr. Al-Suyūṭī cites the month without news from Ibn ʿAbbās as an argument for Medinan revelation. [8 notes: IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-ASBAB(ابن عباس); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); SUYUTI-LUBAB(ابن عباس); ITQAN(ابن عباس)]
- 100:1/q08/p4 It followed ʿAlī's victory at Dhāt al-Salāsil. — An Abū ʿAbd Allāh report says earlier commanders returned, then ʿAlī prevailed. Another report says the Prophet recited the sūra at dawn after Gabriel announced victory; ʿAlī returned with prisoners and spoils. Dhāt al-Salāsil is explained by the captives being tied with rope-like chains; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites Imams and some Sunni reports. [5 notes: TABRISI(أبو عبد الله); TABATABAI(the Prophet,أئمة أهل البيت,أبو عبد الله)]
- 100:1/q08/p5 The horses or camels are linked to Badr. — Some commentators restrict Muslim horses to Badr, but ʿAlī's counterreport says it had only one or two horses, named variously as al-Miqdād's and al-Zubayr's. Other accounts use Badr as evidence for pilgrimage camels rather than an occasion of revelation. Al-Kashshāf reports ʿAlī challenging Ibn ʿAbbās's initial horse answer because Badr had only two horses; al-Ṭabarī's version says Ibn ʿAbbās retracted it. [6 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس وعلي); BINTSHATI(ابن عباس والحسن,علي); STUDYQURAN(others); TAB(علي); KASHSHAF(ابن عباس)]

## 100:1/q09 Was the sūra revealed in Mecca or Medina?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether style favors early Mecca or the warhorse and expedition interpretation requires a Medinan setting.
- 100:1/q09/p1 Meccan. — Ibn Masʿūd, Jābir, al-Ḥasan, ʿIkrimah, ʿAṭāʾ and others are cited; Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, al-Durr and Quṭb classify it so. Study Quran cites style and early Meccan placement. Elmalılı says this dating makes the later martial imagery a future allusion. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī contests it because the images appear to concern jihad after the Hijra. [11 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(ابن مسعود وجابر والحسن وعكرمة وعطاء); JISHUMI(الحسن وعكرمة وقتادة); RAZI-FULL; IBNKATHIR; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن مسعود); STUDYQURAN(most commentators); ELMALILI]
- 100:1/q09/p2 Medinan. — Ibn ʿAbbās, Anas and Qatāda are cited. Al-Kalbī infers it if the oath is about warhorses, because permission to fight was in Medina. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī adopts that inference; Ibn ʿĀshūr prefers this dating despite the weakness in the expedition report's chain. Ibn ʿĀshūr cautions that ʿAlī's camel statement itself does not specify either period. Al-Suyūṭī cites the month-long missing cavalry report as a Medinan argument, but says al-ʿĀdiyāt resembles Meccan revelation despite its Medinan classification. [11 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس وأنس بن مالك وقتادة); JISHUMI(الحسن وعكرمة وقتادة); RAZI-FULL(الكلبي); IBNASHUR-FULL(أنس بن مالك); TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(most commentators); ITQAN(ابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL+; ITQAN+]

## 100:1/q10 What is the sūra's verse count and revelation order?  [* · sciences]
- 100:1/q10/p1 It has eleven verses; one Meccan ordering puts it fourteenth, after al-ʿAṣr and before al-Kawthar. — Al-Rāzī, al-Durr, Quṭb and Ibn ʿĀshūr give eleven; Ibn ʿĀshūr cites Jābir b. Zayd's Meccan revelation order. Al-Māwardī separately notes that the following al-Qāriʿah is called Meccan by all authorities he cites. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL; DURR-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR-FULL(جابر بن زيد); MAWARDI-FULL]

## 100:1/q11 How is the sūra titled?  [ٱلْعَٰدِيَٰتِ · sciences]
turns on: Whether the sūra name includes the opening conjunction.
- 100:1/q11/p1 Al-ʿĀdiyāt, without the opening wāw. — Ibn ʿĀshūr cites early Qayrawan, Tunisian and eastern codices and some commentaries. Biqāʿī says the title points to the purpose through the oath and its answer. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 100:1/q11/p2 Wa-l-ʿĀdiyāt, retaining the initial wāw. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says other tafsīrs preserve the opening wāw in the title. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 100:1/q12 What recitation merit is reported for the sūra?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports describe different reward measures.
- 100:1/q12/p1 It equals half the Qur'an in recitation reward. — Al-Ḥasan transmits a Prophetic saying assigning half to both al-Zalzalah and al-ʿĀdiyāt. A report through ʿAṭāʾ from Ibn ʿAbbās likewise assigns half to each, a third to al-Ikhlāṣ and a quarter to al-Kāfirūn. [2 notes: DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,الحسن)]
- 100:1/q12/p2 Recitation brings ten good deeds for each person who spent the night at Muzdalifah and witnessed Jamʿ. — Jishumī and al-Kashshāf transmit the ten-deeds wording; al-Rāzī reports via Ubayy a reward measured by those who overnighted there and witnessed Jamʿ. [3 notes: JISHUMI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); KASHSHAF-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وآله وسلم); RAZI-FULL(أبي عن النبي)]

## 100:1/q13 What is said of the basmala before this sūra?  [* · inward]
turns on: The notes offer distinct spiritual and theological explanations of the basmala.
- 100:1/q13/p1 Its utterance, knowledge and love call for a guarded tongue, heart and spirit. — Al-Qushayrī calls it a jealous word: the tongue must avoid idle talk and backbiting, the heart be guarded, and the spirit be free of attachments and veils. [2 notes: QUSHAYRI]
- 100:1/q13/p2 It presents God's sovereignty and His general and special mercy. — Biqāʿī says God commands all affairs, universally gives creation and clarification, and specially grants guidance and completed blessing to His devoted servants; the two notes repeat this account. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 100:1/q14 Why swear by these chargers?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the oath chiefly honors valuable mounts, presents their obedience as evidence against people, honors the Prophet, or frames judgment.
- 100:1/q14/p1 To honor useful, noble mounts and call attention to God's gift. — Al-Rāzī cites camels' many benefits in 88:17, God's service to people, and pilgrimage, invoking 3:97 against neglecting obligatory ḥajj; for horses he cites pursuit, retreat and religious uses and warns against display alone. His reading of 16:8 treats riding, not adornment, as the stated purpose. Biqāʿī says horses confer honor or wealth and receive reward or sin by use; good care in God's cause protects the owner, and their distinctive qualities ultimately point to the one sovereign giver. Ibn ʿĀshūr says pilgrims' service is esteemed by pagans and Muslims. Ibn al-Qayyim praises horses' strength and victory; Muḥammad ʿAbduh urges acquiring, training and riding them for defense. Islahi stresses clan protection, pedigree and their esteem in poetry, citing a Ḥamāsī poem. Kuran Yolu says horses were central to old warfare and beloved possessions through which God stresses the message. [14 notes: RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI(ابن القيم,محمد عبده); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ISLAHI-TADABBUR-]
- 100:1/q14/p2 The animals' dutiful exertion testifies against human ingratitude. — Islahi relates their exhausted obedience to 100:6 and rejects readings whose sanctity premise leaves the oath disconnected. Biqāʿī says the oath rebukes preference for transient wealth and failure to prepare for Judgment. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says human love of wealth and ingratitude are known to people and become evidence at judgment; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the sūra condemns these traits, warning even Muslims. [9 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 100:1/q14/p3 It is an oath by the Prophet's cavalry that honors him. — Ibn al-ʿArabī compares God's oath by Muḥammad in Yā Sīn and by his life in al-Ḥijr with the oath here by his cavalry, its neighing, dust and sparks. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(ابن العربي)]
- 100:1/q14/p4 It directs attention to coming judgment and the disclosure of deeds. — Biqāʿī reads the oath and answer as exposing the pursuit of status and wealth before the upheaval; Ibn ʿĀshūr says it promises reassurance to believers and threat to deniers. Study Quran divides the five charging verses, the ingratitude of 6–8 and the judgment of 9–11. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says the familiar surprise of a dusty dawn raid prefigures the unseen Resurrection catching the ungrateful unprepared. [7 notes: BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN; BINTSHATI-IJAZ]

## 100:1/q15 How does the opening connect with the rest of the sūra and its neighbors?  [* · links]
turns on: Different notes relate the opening to the moral claim, the closing resurrection, the preceding sūra, or a wider Qur'anic rhetorical pattern.
- 100:1/q15/p1 The raid's motion and sound anticipate ingratitude, graves opened and hearts exposed. — Quṭb likens the sūra's quick jumps and rough rumbling rhythm to the charging horse and to violent disclosure of graves and hearts; he says image and frame mirror one another. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ compares the familiar sudden dawn raid, dust and scattered targets with the unseen resurrection, people emerging in groups as in 99:6 and rapidly facing the All-Aware. Biqāʿī links the oath and answer to the sūra's purpose. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ compares the scattered targets of a dawn raid with people scattered from graves, citing 101:4 and 54:7 alongside 100:6 and 100:9–11. [11 notes: QUTB-ZILAL; BINTSHATI; BIQAI-FULL; BINTSHATI-IJAZ]
- 100:1/q15/p2 After al-Zalzalah's judgment scene, this sūra shows the disposition behind evil deeds. — Biqāʿī says the prior sūra describes judgment, while this one traces the desire for worldly gain producing bad deeds. Study Quran reports an early Meccan ordering after al-Qāriʿah rather than this thematic link. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(most commentators)]
- 100:1/q15/p3 Its oath sequence creates a riddling rhetorical tension. — Corpus Coranicum compares the sequence with teaching questions at 90:12–16, 97:2–4, 101:1–11 and 104:5–9. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 100:1/q16 What do the successive fāʾs imply?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the linkage simply orders the actions or also marks a causal relation at the sparks.
- 100:1/q16/p1 They mark the successive actions in one charge. — Ibn ʿĀshūr calls this the normal pattern for successive adjectives or places, comparing Ibn Ziyābah's verse, while noting that 37:1 can use fāʾ for mention order. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the sequence supports a particular raid; Elmalılı sees successive stages and even an age-by-age development of attack vehicles. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ELMALILI]
- 100:1/q16/p2 The first fāʾ also signals that the sparks result from hard running. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says the hooves strike stones as a consequence of the horses' pace. [1 notes: BINTSHATI]

## 100:1/q17 How is the verse read inwardly?  [* · inward]
turns on: The inward readings choose different spiritual processes for the charging image.
- 100:1/q17/p1 It depicts souls striving toward God and breathing out their longing. — Al-Qāshānī makes ascetic discipline and strenuous effort the runners, with panting the exhalation of longing. Al-Ālūsī describes yearning prayer, knowledge kindled by thought, and souls charging desire and habit toward sacred light. The allegory attributed to al-Bayḍāwī has souls convey holiness, kindle knowledge, attack caprice, raise desire and enter the highest Garden, with reference to 83:18–19. [4 notes: QASHANI; ALUSI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(al-Bayḍāwī)]
- 100:1/q17/p2 It maps spiritual struggle through dhikr and the soul's passage from bodily darkness. — Al-Ālūsī's second account has remembrance kindle guidance in the body, the soul cross to dawn, stir passing thoughts and marshal the heart's forces in an army of remembrance. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 100:1/q18 Are the opening oath passages classified as poetry or assigned a meter?  [* · rhetoric]
- 100:1/q18/p1 Some classify the oath passages as poetry, and assign the opening of al-ʿĀdiyāt to al-basīṭ meter. — Al-Bāqillānī reports unnamed people calling passages from al-ʿĀdiyāt and al-Dhāriyāt poetry, with al-basīṭ assigned to the latter. Al-Zarkashī reports classification of both opening oath sequences as al-basīṭ. [2 notes: BAQILLANI(زعموا); BURHAN(قالوا)]

## 100:1/q19 Is the tāʾ at the end of al-ʿādiyāt assimilated into the following ḍād?  [ٱلْعَٰدِيَٰتِ ضَبْحًۭا · readings]
- 100:1/q19/p1 Ibn Khayrūn uniquely transmits Khallād's assimilation of the tāʾ into the following ḍād. — Ibn al-Jazarī reports this unique transmission from Ibn Khayrūn concerning Khallād's reading of wa-l-ʿādiyāti ḍabḥan. [1 notes: NASHR(ابن خيرون عن خلاد)]
