6:52/q07 What does al-ʿashiyy mean and what form is it? [ٱلْعَشِىِّ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the form names one evening or is collective/plural, and how wide a time it covers.)
  6:52/q07/p1 It is the late part of the day, usually a singular noun like ʿashiyya. — reasons: Abu Hayyan cites 38:31 for late afternoon; Samin favors synonymy with ʿashiyya because that verse means one evening; Alusi places it at day's end and derives it from a waw root. Ibn Ashur gives a wider noon-to-dawn span.
  6:52/q07/p2 It may be a collective or plural of ʿashiyya. — reasons: Abu Hayyan raises the collective analysis; Samin compares qamh/qamha; Alusi records but finds plural unlikely.
6:52/q08 What is meant by seeking God's face? [يُرِيدُونَ وَجْهَهُۥ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether face stands for God Himself, His pleasure/reward, His commanded path, or the inward goal of direct nearness.)
  6:52/q08/p1 They sincerely seek God Himself and His approval, without worldly aims. — reasons: Tustari, Zajjaj, Kashshaf and many later sources equate face with the divine self/essence as an honorific Arabic usage and infer sincerity. Ibn Abbas and others say reward, pardon, pleasure or favor through righteous deeds; Razi adds a lover's desire for the beloved's face, while StudyQuran records literal hope to see it. Ibn Ashur invokes 76:9 and 2:115; Bursawi cites a Persian couplet comparing worship without sincerity to grain without a kernel. Ibn Qutayba calls 'His face' an added expression for seeking God through supplication. Damghani identifies seeking God and His pleasure and says other cited uses of face likewise denote God. Ibn al-Jawzi reports the exegetes' sense as God's own self, grouped with 18:28, 28:88, 30:38 and 76:9.
  6:52/q08/p2 They seek obedience to God, His commanded direction and the means of nearness. — reasons: Mawardi and the people of meanings use face for the aim or direction commanded; Wāhidi links 2:115, Alusi reports Zajjaj. Tabataba'i includes God's names, religion, good works and nearness as aspects through which creatures encounter God.
  6:52/q08/p3 In an inward reading they seek only the divine Essence beyond changing attributes, reward, and intermediaries. — reasons: Qashani describes annihilation of means and self; Qushayri says nothing worldly or of the afterlife occupies them; Tustari distinguishes the striving seeker from one enabled and sought by God.
6:52/q09 Does 'His face' imply a bodily part? [وَجْهَهُۥ · theology] (turns on: Whether face is read figuratively, as a divine attribute, or as a bodily part.)
  6:52/q09/p1 No; face is an Arabic metonym or metaphor for essence, aspect, direction, favor, or what brings one near God. — reasons: Jishumi notes bodily and figurative senses in Arabic. Razi answers the anthropomorphists with God's oneness in 112:1; Abu Hayyan rejects their inference. Tabataba'i denies body and material direction, takes 55:27's grammatical modifier with face, and uses 28:88 and other verses for enduring nearness of divine attributes and righteous realities. Qushayri and Alusi discuss the difficulty of willing God's eternal essence when will ordinarily attaches to originated or possible things. Ibn Qutayba treats 'His face' as an expression for seeking God in supplication; Damghani and the exegetes reported by Ibn al-Jawzi understand it as God Himself or His pleasure.
  6:52/q09/p2 It may be understood as God's possessed Face or literal sight of it. — reasons: Qurtubi records that God is described as possessing the Face, citing 55:27 and 13:22; StudyQuran reports Razi's lover analogy as literal hope to see it. Razi reports anthropomorphists' bodily argument only to refute it.
6:52/q10 How does 'seeking His face' attach grammatically? [يُرِيدُونَ وَجْهَهُۥ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the circumstantial clause qualifies the worshippers or the object of their calling.)
  6:52/q10/p1 It is a circumstantial clause qualifying the callers' supplication and sincerity. — reasons: Qushayri, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi and Ibn Ashur identify the ḥāl; Bursevi calls sincerity central. Samin prefers attachment to the subject of يدعون, with an implicit 'through their supplication at those times.'
  6:52/q10/p2 It could be a circumstance of the object of يدعون. — reasons: Samin records this possible grammatical attachment but prefers the subject.
6:52/q11 Whose account is meant by the reciprocal pronouns? [حِسَابِهِم حِسَابِكَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the nearest antecedent governs both clauses or the clauses parenthetically address concern for the chiefs' conversion.)
  6:52/q11/p1 The poor worshippers' account and the Prophet's account. — reasons: The nearest explicit group and the later object 'expel them' favor this for Wahidi, Razi, Abu Hayyan, Samin and Kuranyolu. Al-Mahdawi says all pronouns refer to believers; Alusi cites Ata and most commentators.
  6:52/q11/p2 The first reciprocal pronouns refer to the polytheists; the later 'expel them' refers to believers. — reasons: Ibn Abbas's version assigns reward and punishment to God; Zamakhshari, Ibn Atiyya, Razi, Baydawi, Alusi and Islahi say the Prophet need not court pagan conversion. StudyQuran reports this as more plausible, while other sources prefer the believers by proximity. Samin notes parenthesis need not depend on this referent.
6:52/q12 What is the 'account' for which neither side is responsible? [مَا عَلَيْكَ مِنْ حِسَابِهِم مِّن شَىْءٍۢ وَمَا مِنْ حِسَابِكَ عَلَيْهِم مِّن شَىْءٍۢ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether حساب is moral reckoning, provision/sufficiency, or the disbelievers' responsibility for faith.)
  6:52/q12/p1 The reckoning of deeds, faith and hidden intentions belongs to each person and ultimately God. — reasons: Hasan and the majority take deeds; Noah's reply at 26:112–113 and the rule that no bearer bears another's burden support this. Zamakhshari, Razi, Biqai and Bursawi say the Prophet judges outward piety, even against a claim that the poor seek food and clothes. Abu Hayyan rejects positing actual inward blame after God's testimony; Ibn Ashur likewise finds no sound report of hypocrisy and rejects treating both clauses as merely divine accounting. Asad extends personal accountability to differing belief and conduct. Elmalılı says the Prophet should not expel them on a pretext of having to account for them.
  6:52/q12/p2 It means worldly provision or sufficiency: neither the Prophet nor the poor believers must provide for the other. — reasons: Tabari, Ibn Bahr and others say fear of maintaining the poor cannot justify expulsion; God provides for both. Ibn Bahr and Biqai cite 78:36 for ample gift; Biqai adds 2:212, Abu Ubayd and Ibn Faris for aḥsabtuhu 'gave enough,' and a report on hospitality. Kuranyolu phrases the practical result as not sending worshippers away to earn a living.
  6:52/q12/p3 It is the chiefs' own response to faith, for which the Prophet is not liable. — reasons: Ibn Abbas's alternative and Ibn Ashur/Islahi say eagerness for conversion cannot cost existing believers their rights. Ibn Ashur also allows 'their account' to mean the chiefs' reckoning of the believers' poverty.
6:52/q13 Why are both reciprocal account clauses stated? [مَا عَلَيْكَ مِنْ حِسَابِهِم مِّن شَىْءٍۢ وَمَا مِنْ حِسَابِكَ عَلَيْهِم مِّن شَىْءٍۢ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether repetition supplies reciprocal completeness alone or also exposes the requesters' false rationale.)
  6:52/q13/p1 Together they state complete reciprocity: neither party bears the other's account. — reasons: Zamakhshari, Razi and others say both halves complete one point, invoking the no-burden rule. Bursawi reports Abu al-Suud that the uncontroversial reverse reinforces the first; Zajjaj says the second could be omitted structurally but confirms parallel phrasing. Ibn Ashur says five emphases, including two min, fronting, restriction and reciprocal completion, repel pressure from Quraysh; he also gives the first clause priority. Abu Hayyan and Samin record a wording objection to Zamakhshari's singular ṣāḥibihi and Samin's repair by supplying 'each one.' Burhan names the reciprocal account clauses an inversion, comparing wives as each other's garment, mutual unlawfulness and night entering day; Itqan calls the form aks, a reversal of the first statement's parts in the second, and compares 57:6, 30:19, 2:187 and 60:10. Burhan also describes interwoven premise–consequence pairs.
  6:52/q13/p2 The second clause chiefly exposes the chiefs' pretense that poor believers obstructed their access to the Prophet. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says no believer should lose rights to satisfy another; if chiefs truly sought faith they could attend without judging others, as 6:55 clarifies. He rejects reducing the two clauses to one statement that God judges all, because that makes the second redundant in context.
