  6:52/q14/p2 The fronting of عليك and عليهم may honor the Prophet, emphasize the lack of obligation, or mark restriction. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and Samin say putting the Prophet first twice is honor and reciprocal framing, رد الأعجاز على الصدور, supported by proverb and poem. Ibn Ashur says negated indefinite subjects permit fronting; Kashshaf favors restriction, implying only God accounts for believers, comparing 37:47, while Ibn Ashur also permits simple emphasis because restriction fits imperfectly. He explains على as burden and discusses whether the genitive means the Prophet's reckoning or chiefs' assessment. As general word-order background rather than a direct parsing of 6:52, Jurjani argues that a meaningful placement consistently carries its benefit and illustrates with questions: verb-first asks whether an act occurred, while noun-first asks who did it.
  6:52/q14/p3 The two clauses may interrupt the prohibition as parenthetical explanation. — reasons: Alusi says the clause supports the ban; Ibn Ashur says it can give the reason or invalidate the rationale for expulsion. Samin rejects making parenthesis depend on reference to polytheists; Abu Hayyan identifies the interruption before the ban's result.
6:52/q15 What governs the two subjunctive verbs after the account clauses? [فَتَطْرُدَهُمْ فَتَكُونَ · grammar] (turns on: Which preceding negative or prohibition supplies the result, and whether the second verb answers that ban or coordinates with the first.)
  6:52/q15/p1 فتطردهم answers the preceding negation and فتكون answers the prohibition. — reasons: Akhfash, Tabari, Zajjaj, BaghaWi and others make the first consequence follow 'no account' and the second the ban. Abu Hayyan says the first denies an expulsion arising from that account; Samin rejects a reading where the visit occurs without conversation. Ibn Ashur allows the repeated expulsion clause to bridge the long interruption. Alusi rejects making the second denial govern if it would imply expulsion proper when accountability exists. Burhan describes two interwoven premise–consequence pairs: the ban on expulsion leads to avoiding injustice, and the reciprocal account clauses lead to the question of expelling them.
  6:52/q15/p2 Both verbs follow the prohibition, warning that expulsion would make the Prophet unjust. — reasons: Akhfash reports this alternative; Abu Muslim parses both as consequences. Samin and Ibn Ashur note that repeated expulsion reconnects the warning after intervening clauses; Alusi objects to a literal 'do not expel and then expel' reading.
  6:52/q15/p3 فتكون is coordinated with فتطردهم, so both expulsion and wrongdoing are negated as linked consequences. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and Samin prefer coordination; Zamakhshari and Razi allow the injustice to result causally from expulsion. Alusi answers that when expulsion is denied, its injustice ceases, though an objection worries this would imply expulsion could be just under a different accounting relation.
6:52/q16 What wrong would expelling the worshippers constitute? [فَتَكُونَ مِنَ ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the injustice falls on the poor, the Prophet, or is only the hypothetical consequence of a contemplated act.)
  6:52/q16/p1 It would wrong the believers who deserved welcome and honor. — reasons: Ibn Zayd calls their exclusion undeserved; Wahidi calls this sound. Qurtubi and Kuranyolu say humbling people God values for faith is injustice; Baydawi and Alusi make it a consequence of dismissal; Biqai says putting things out of place would not win pagan belief. Abu Hayyan defines injustice as putting a thing in the wrong place.
  6:52/q16/p2 It could mean the Prophet would harm himself through disobedience or loss of reward. — reasons: Ibn Abbas uses Arabic and Quranic ظلم for diminution; Razi reports both self-harm and wrong to believers; Alusi adds becoming characterized by injustice.
  6:52/q16/p3 It is a conditional warning, not evidence that the Prophet actually committed injustice. — reasons: Qurtubi compares the impossible conditional warning against shirk in 39:65; Ibn Ashur also makes it hypothetical. Razi calls any mistaken preference forgivable rather than violation of duty, since the Prophet pursued conversion, while critics argue the incident shows sin.
6:52/q17 Did the Prophet sin or expel the poor before the warning? [وَلَا تَطْرُدِ فَتَكُونَ مِنَ ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · theology] (turns on: Whether reported consideration became actual expulsion or culpable disobedience.)
  6:52/q17/p1 He did not expel them; he considered a separate audience to attract the chiefs, and the warning is conditional. — reasons: Qurtubi, Alusi, Razi, Ibn al-Anbari and StudyQuran stress his intent to win converts; Razi says any mistaken judgment was forgoing the better course, not violating a duty. Qurtubi cites 39:65 to show a conditional warning proves no actual act. Ibn Ashur treats the warning likewise; reports vary between outright refusal and temporary willingness.
  6:52/q17/p2 Critics of prophetic impeccability treat the incident as a sin and rebuke. — reasons: They allege he dismissed poor believers, invoking Noah's refusal to expel them (11:29), the command to follow prophets (6:90), worldly adornment (20:131), and 18:28. Razi rejects the inference by explaining the Prophet's intention and conditional wording.
6:52/q18 How does this verse connect to its surrounding passage and other Quranic episodes? [* · links] (turns on: Whether adjacent passages explain the command through context, analogous prophetic history, or later applications.)
  6:52/q18/p1 It follows the warning in 6:51 by commanding care for the devout, and continues through the trial and mercy statements in 6:53–54. — reasons: Kashshaf, Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan and Ibn Ashur mark the turn from warning the heedless to honoring worshippers. Alusi allows, but does not require, simultaneous revelation of warning and prohibition. Ibn Ashur says renewed relative wording makes the entitlement to company explicit. Later verses test proud chiefs through poor believers and assure the latter of mercy; the question 'does God not know the grateful?' confirms His knowledge, TabRisi comparing Jarir's poetry. Wording of 6:53's outcome can mark consequence rather than purpose. Qatada, reported by Abd al-Razzaq, glosses God's testing some through others simply as mutual trial. Ibn Ishaq reports 6:52–54 together as the response to Quraysh's mockery of the poor companions. Khabbab's report in Ibn Majah joins the prohibition with 6:53–54 as God's favor to those believers.
  6:52/q18/p2 It parallels Noah's refusal to dismiss low-status followers and God's command to remain with these believers in 18:28. — reasons: Zajjaj, Wahidi, Razi and Ibn Kathir compare 11:27 and 26:111–113, including laborers and craftsmen whose account belongs to God. Several reports make 18:28 the later command that the Prophet remain seated with the poor; StudyQuran also links the rebuke at 80:1–10 about a blind poor man. Khabbab's report in Ibn Majah also connects 18:28 to the Prophet's continued companionship with the poor believers.
  6:52/q18/p3 Reports about adjacent verses or other figures may be applications rather than original occasions. — reasons: Tabataba'i treats claims about enemies of the Prophet's family as applications or inward readings due to the Meccan, single-period descent. Islahi notes Farahi's related discussion in his commentary on Abasa.
6:52/q19 What ethical rule does the prohibition teach? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the enduring addressees are faithful Muslims in the Prophet's company or all sincere seekers of God.)
  6:52/q19/p1 Sincere faith and devotion give the poor a claim to company and honor regardless of rank, dress or wealth. — reasons: Zajjaj says religion takes priority over status and leaders' pretext is exposed. Qushayri says God champions vulnerable believers and tells the Prophet to see inward longing beyond ragged clothes. Qurtubi and Kuranyolu forbid elevating people for finery or lineage. Islahi says prophetic concern for wayward people has limits when loyal companions would be neglected; Jesus' lost-sheep parable illustrates concern for returnees but cannot override this duty. Biqai says the mission seeks the afterlife, with no worldly gain in replacing poor with wealthy.
  6:52/q19/p2 The enduring command also applies to people with incomplete beliefs: explain patiently rather than repel them. — reasons: Asad reads the wording beyond its occasion, applying it to anyone who believes in God and seeks grace; Kuranyolu says even a disputed occasion leaves a universal ethical lesson.
6:52/q20 What inward instruction is drawn from the verse? [* · inward] (turns on: Different inward emphases on constant remembrance, self-effacement, and the disciplines of devotion.)
  6:52/q20/p1 Remain with sincere seekers, recognize inward rank, and cultivate uninterrupted remembrance. — reasons: Sulami transmits counsel to treat seekers gently and overlook slips, to keep the secret pure, and not distance those adorned with servanthood. Qushayri says God publicly attests to sincerity, while the seeker is driven by need and longing; a poem depicts crossing the wild without fear.
