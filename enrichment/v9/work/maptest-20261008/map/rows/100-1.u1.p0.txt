## THE CURRENT MAP
100:1/q01 What does the opening wāw do? [وَٱلْعَٰدِيَٰتِ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the opening is a literal honorific oath, testimony, or a rhetorical evocation of an imagined scene.)
  100:1/q01/p1 It introduces God's oath by the chargers. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās says the oath wāw governs the genitive. Zayd b. Aslam declined to interpret the opening letters beyond calling them God's oath. Elmalılı calls it an oath. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ records the usual view that an oath magnifies its object, but criticizes the restrictive interpretations it produced. Islahi treats the oath as the animals' testimony to the later claim about ingratitude.
  100:1/q01/p2 The wāw evokes the scene rhetorically rather than serving only as a literal oath. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ appeals to her treatment of 93:1 and says the honorific-oath assumption leaves the horse and camel dispute unresolved. Asad prefers an exclamatory rendering because the following clauses form an imagined parable.
  100:1/q01/p3 It is an exclamation: 'Oh, the chargers!' — reasons: Asad prefers 'Oh' to his usual 'Consider' and most translators' 'By,' on the basis of the imaginary parable that follows.
100:1/q02 What does al-ʿādiyāt mean and how is it formed? [ٱلْعَٰدِيَٰتِ · meaning]
  100:1/q02/p1 Swift runners or chargers, the feminine plural active participle of running. — reasons: Jishumī calls it the plural of ʿādiyah. Al-Qurṭubī defines running as rapid motion with limbs spread; Abū Ḥayyān says swift runners; al-Samīn and Bursevī explain the yāʾ replacing wāw after kasra, as does al-Ālūsī. Elmalılı traces ʿadw to crossing a limit: running, hostility, oppression, and spatial contagion are related senses; in battle ʿadī/ʿādiya name attacking troops, though some assign the former to infantry and the latter to cavalry. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ notes this is the word's only Qur'anic occurrence and sees distance or exceeding in running, enmity and transgression. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the term especially describes horses and camels and can analogically describe human runners; he names al-Sulayk, al-Shanfarā, Taʾabbaṭa Sharran and ʿAmr b. Umayya. Its feminine form agrees with a non-rational referent.
100:1/q03 Who or what are the chargers? [ٱلْعَٰدِيَٰتِ · referent] (turns on: Whether ḍabḥ and the later sparks, dawn attack and dust fit horses or camels; the reported ʿAlī–Ibn ʿAbbās dispute and the number of horses at Badr; whether the oath may accommodate both or a further figurative referent.)
  100:1/q03/p1 Horses, usually cavalry charging or raiding in God's cause. — reasons: Mujāhid, Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatāda and many others identify horses; some say the Prophet's cavalry, some a particular detachment, and some horses generally. Al-Ṭabarī chooses horses because camels do not pant; al-Rāzī adds that iron shoes spark and horses suit dawn attacks. Al-Ṭabrisī and others cite Ibn ʿAbbās's point that hooves raise dust. Al-Wāḥidī attributes preference to al-Farrāʾ and al-Zajjāj. ʿAntarah and other poets depict panting chargers. Al-Ālūsī says Badr's few horses cannot exclude other horses or the delayed expedition, doubts the report and cites conflicting transmissions. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the five sequential descriptions favor a particular cavalry raid; Islahi says they fit warhorses but disputes confining them to Muslim raiders as the oath's moral proof. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says ḍabḥ points to the animals rather than horsemen.
  100:1/q03/p2 Pilgrims' camels running from ʿArafāt through Muzdalifah to Minā. — reasons: ʿAlī invokes the one or two horses reported at Badr against identifying the oath with a cavalry raid; some versions name al-Miqdād alone, others him and al-Zubayr, and Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to recant. Ibn ʿAbbās's rival answer says the horses were a separate detachment. Ṣafiyyah's poem pictures animals at Jamʿ raising dust; Abū Ṣāliḥ and Muslim prefer ʿAlī's authority. Interpretations vary on whether the camels' ḍabḥ is breath, stretched necks or forelegs, or a metaphor; pilgrims kindle fires at Muzdalifah and raise dust en route, including Wādī Muḥassir. Al-Ṭabarī objects that camels do not pant; al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī find later descriptions ill-suited; al-Ālūsī questions the debate report.
  100:1/q03/p3 Camels associated with Badr or with a raid. — reasons: Ibn Qutaybah's report from ʿAlī calls them Badr's camels, citing only al-Miqdād's horse; Ibn ʿAṭiyyah reports ʿAlī including Badr's camels alongside pilgrims' camels. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī lists camels in a raid as an alternative distinct from pilgrims' camels.
  100:1/q03/p4 Both warhorses and pilgrimage camels are encompassed. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyyah explicitly allows both; al-Qurṭubī reports Ibn ʿAbbās applying the verses to battle and pilgrimage. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the selected wording accommodates both, and Study Quran says each fulfills a function worthy of the oath.
  100:1/q03/p5 Striving or longing souls are the inward chargers. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī reads ascetic striving, discipline and intense longing; al-Ālūsī reports souls advancing against desire and habit toward sacred light. Study Quran attributes to al-Bayḍāwī an allegory of souls conveying holiness, attaining knowledge and entering the highest Garden.
  100:1/q03/p6 The image can extend to modern weapons and swift motorized assault vehicles. — reasons: Elmalılı relates fire-sparking arms and motor vehicles to the distinctive plurals and successive fāʾs as a historical development; on Meccan dating this would allude to equipment Muslims did not yet possess.
100:1/q04 Do the horses denote a particular force or horses generally? [ٱلْعَٰدِيَٰتِ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the definite article identifies the delayed expedition or names a general class, and whether the following actions describe one raid.)
  100:1/q04/p1 A particular expedition's horses are in view. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says the definite article may refer to the expedition. Reports variously identify the delayed force with Kinānah, give a month's missing news, or connect ʿAlī's Dhāt al-Salāsil campaign. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites the five successive actions. Al-Ālūsī says a particular expedition remains possible even if Badr had few horses.
  100:1/q04/p2 Horses in general, especially those that run in God's cause, are in view. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī explicitly generalizes the oath to horses running for God. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah notes their long-standing power to conquer and the good in their forelocks; Ibn al-Qayyim says their nobility, strength, victory and vengeance need no restriction to raiders. Al-Rāzī allows a generic definite article; al-Ālūsī says Badr cannot rule this out.
100:1/q05 What is ḍabḥ here? [ضَبْحًۭا · meaning] (turns on: Whether ḍabḥ is a respiratory sound, a form of ḍabʿ describing gait, or a transferred term in the camel interpretation.)
  100:1/q05/p1 The hard runner's audible breath or panting, usually attributed to horses. — reasons: Authorities place the sound in the throat, chest, insides, mouth, nose or lips; al-Layth distinguishes it from neighing and Biqāʿī from snorting or bellowing. Ibn ʿAbbās imitates 'aḥ aḥ' or 'aj aj'; ʿAlī contrasts horse whinny-like panting with camel breath. Some call it snorting; al-Durr's version even has feet, whereas Ibn Mardawayh has nostrils. Exhaustion, fear and appetite may prompt it, and al-Qurṭubī suggests muzzles suppressed neighing lest an enemy be alerted. Al-Wāḥidī, al-Ṭabrisī and al-Rāzī distinguish ordinary calls; Islahi says the breath reveals obedience and spent energy. ʿAntarah, an unnamed Iraqi poet and other poems illustrate charging horses.
  100:1/q05/p2 It denotes vigorous running with stretched forelegs or necks, linked to ḍabʿ. — reasons: Abū ʿUbaydah equates ḍabḥ and ḍabʿ; al-Azharī, al-Mubarrad and others describe extended forelegs, while some camel interpreters say stretched necks. Ṣafiyyah's poem is cited. Al-Kashshāf calls the letter substitution insecure; Ibn ʿĀshūr says linguists do not normally apply breath-sound ḍabḥ to camels and reports both dialect-substitution and metaphor alternatives; al-Qāmūs accepts interchange. Abū ʿUbaydah also allows the related verb for horses. Biqāʿī reports a nuance of running short of a full gallop.
  100:1/q05/p3 For camels it is a metaphorical application of the panting sound. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī conditions this on the authenticity of ʿAlī's report and likens it to transferred body terms; al-Samīn and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ repeat that analysis. Ibn ʿĀshūr also allows metaphor for a camel's intensified running sound.
  100:1/q05/p4 It is a rattling sound in the saddlebag or movement of the horses' lips. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī reports these two explanations from unnamed authorities; al-Naḥḥās has Ibn ʿAbbās describe blowing through the lips.
  100:1/q05/p5 The word can also mean ash. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī records this lexical sense without applying it to the oath.
100:1/q06 Which creatures or things can make a ḍabḥ? [ضَبْحًۭا · meaning] (turns on: Whether the reported restriction is sound and whether the wider Arabic examples are literal uses or metaphors.)
  100:1/q06/p1 Only horses and dogs, sometimes with foxes added, properly make the sound. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿAṭāʾ and others give versions with dog and horse or with fox too; lexicographers are cited for the restricted list. Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn and Biqāʿī say fox usage preceded transfer to horses; Tawbah's fox poem and the fire-darkening analogy support the usage. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī challenge the restriction as inconsistent with broader Arabic use.
  100:1/q06/p2 Arabic usage extends ḍabḥ to many animals and even a bow or echo. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyyah lists camels, snakes, owls, rabbits, foxes, echoes and bows; Abū Ḥanīfah's poem gives a bow a fox-like ḍabḥ. Abū Ḥayyān doubts the restrictive Ibn ʿAbbās attribution. Al-Samīn regards wider uses as metaphors from a fox origin, whereas Ibn ʿAṭiyyah cites them against a strict animal limit. Al-Ālūsī also invokes the bow poem.
