  6:52/q20/p3 Purity of sustenance, command keeping, sincere treatment of creatures and gratitude describe the servant's path. — reasons: Tustari says the ascetic has pure food, the devout works hardest at commands and prohibitions, and the beloved is most sincere to creatures; he calls a squandered life wasted. 6:52/q21 What reports and ancillary teachings are attached to keeping the poor near? [* · reports] (turns on: Whether the ancillary material supplies a virtue report, a creed discussion, or merely reproduces the passage.)
  6:52/q21/p1 The Prophet honored the poor companions and warned against upsetting them. — reasons: Thalabi reports a promise that destitute migrants enter Paradise half a day, five hundred years, before wealthy believers; another report says the gathering resembles earlier prophets and righteous people. In a report about Abu Bakr and Abu Sufyan, upsetting Salman, Suhayb and Bilal upsets God, and they forgive Abu Bakr. Ibn Kathir notes al-Hakim's grading of Sa'd's six-companion report by Bukhari and Muslim criteria and Ibn Hibban's route, and says Ibn Jarir transmitted a related report through Asbat.
  6:52/q21/p2 A report condemns denial of divine decree as resembling Magians. — reasons: Tabataba'i reads it as condemning independent human creation of acts; Mu'tazilites invert its target to those affirming decree. He rejects contradictory manuscript combinations of 'no decree' and denial of human will as corruption.
  6:52/q21/p3 One segment merely reproduces Quran 6:36–52 without commentary. — reasons: The Abu Hayyan note has no further interpretive claim.
  6:52/q21/p4 Reports prefacing an inward reading describe several layers of Quranic sense and distinct audiences for them. — reasons: Sulami reports Ibn Masud's seven modes, outward and inward senses, and each letter's limit and opening; reports from Jafar ibn Muhammad and Ali distinguish worship, allusion, subtleties and realities, their audiences, and their relation to hearing, reason, witnessing and surrender.
6:52/q22 Why is the Prophet addressed directly rather than the chiefs? [وَلَا تَطْرُدِ · rhetoric]
  6:52/q22/p1 The indirect address exposes the chiefs' arrogance and their unworthiness of direct address. — reasons: Islahi says their social claim was a pretext; Ibn Ashur says the address implicitly rebukes their assertion that poor believers prevent access to the Prophet. The renewed relative description makes sincerity the reason to retain them.
6:52/q23 What does Ibn Ashur's cited poetic example illustrate? [* · meaning]
  6:52/q23/p1 The verb aḥrazū describes polytheists seizing Muslims' collected spoils. — reasons: Ibn Ashur cites the poem for this usage.
6:52/q24 What is the lexical range of wajh used in 'His face'? [وَجْهَهُۥ · meaning] (turns on: The word's ordinary physical sense, its extensions in Arabic, and its referent in this verse.)
  6:52/q24/p1 Its basic sense is an animal's face, through which facing ordinarily occurs. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi gives the basic sense and its explanation from the act of facing.
  6:52/q24/p2 By extension it can mean an aspect, a leader or front, or the beginning of a day. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi lists these extensions; he reports al-Zajjaj's poetic evidence for wajh al-nahar as the day's beginning.
  6:52/q24/p3 In this verse it denotes God's own self. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi reports the exegetes' explanation, also grouping 18:28, 28:88, 30:38 and 76:9 with that sense.
6:52/q25 What dialectal substitutions for final weak sounds are recorded alongside al-ʿashiyy? [ٱلْعَشِىِّ · grammar] (turns on: Dialects and pause versus connected speech affect the realization of weak final sounds; only the Banū Saʿd example directly names al-ʿashiyy.)
  6:52/q25/p1 Banū Saʿd replace a weak final yāʾ with jīm at pause; a cited line says bi-l-ʿashj for bi-l-ʿashiyy. — reasons: Sibawayh gives Tamimj, ʿalj and ʿurbanj as further examples, with an unattributed poetic line also using al-baranj for al-barani.
  6:52/q25/p2 Some dialects replace final alif with yāʾ or wāw at pause, while others retain alif; the familiar pronunciation retains it. — reasons: Sibawayh reports al-Khalil and Abu al-Khattab on rare Fuzara and Qays pausal yāʾ, prefers alif in pause and connection, reports Tayyiʾ's retained alif, and notes some Tayyiʾ speakers use a clearer wāw such as afʿaw.
  6:52/q25/p3 Final yāʾ can alternate with hāʾ by dialect and by pause versus connection. — reasons: Sibawayh reports Banū Tamim's hādhihi in pause and hādhi in connection, while Hijaz and other Qays speakers retain hāʾ in both environments in contrast to Tayyiʾ's yāʾ; he says the latter is exceptional.
6:52/q26 What general weak-letter changes do the accompanying language notes describe? [* · grammar] (turns on: These are general morphological examples in the accompanying material; the notes do not apply them to a particular word of 6:52.)
  6:52/q26/p1 Medial wāw may be lengthened, hamzaed, changed to yāʾ, or preserved according to pattern and proximity to the word's end. — reasons: Sibawayh gives ghuwuran and al-quwwul, sawm/saym and qawl/qil, contrasts retained wāw in zuwwar and siwwam, describes analogical alternations in mashub/mashib and hawr/hayr, and gives retained wāw in tawil/tuwal, augmented patterns and fiʿlaʾ. He calls extension of the fiʿl change to fiʿlan and fiʿla irregular and contrasts lighter yāʾ.
  6:52/q26/p2 Root-initial yāʾ is usually retained but changes in specified environments and analogical forms. — reasons: Sibawayh contrasts root yāʾ in yaʾisa and yabisa with wāw, cites retained yāʾ in yayiʾasu and yaybasu, its change to wāw after ḍamma, isolated analogies in vocatives, its change to tāʾ in iftiʿal, and preservation in afʿal; he limits nonproductive cases and reports Abu ʿAmr's hamza-to-yāʾ reading.
6:52/q27 How can Arabic noun forms denote an act or a concrete thing in the accompanying lexical notes? [* · meaning] (turns on: Sibawayh says the same outward pattern has several lexical functions that must be learned from usage.)
  6:52/q27/p1 A form's lexical meaning can be an action, an amount, its result, an agent or patient, or a collective member; the surface pattern alone does not decide. — reasons: Sibawayh discusses faʿul verbal nouns, waqud as burning or fuel, shibʿ and milʾ as amounts or acts, nouns with the same sense as their sources, unpredictable khars, kayla, qut and halab, mirya as milk quantity, laʿna and khalq as affected people or things, karaʿ and dhu tadruʾ as concrete referents, passive-result and agent senses, changed milk and pleasing usage, and collective members marked with feminine taʾ. These examples are ancillary and do not explicitly gloss a word in 6:52.
