100:1/q07 How is ḍabḥan parsed? [ضَبْحًۭا · grammar] (turns on: Which verb or participle governs the accusative and whether the word names panting, a circumstance, or a kind of running.)
  100:1/q07/p1 A cognate accusative or verbal noun governed by an omitted 'they pant.' — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī, al-Zajjāj through al-Rāzī, al-Kashshāf, al-Nasafī, al-Ālūsī, Elmalılı and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ give the implied verb. Al-Samīn says the supplied verbal clause is circumstantial. Al-Bayḍāwī allows an omitted verb's object. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ prefers the unrestricted verbal-noun force.
  100:1/q07/p2 A circumstantial accusative: the runners are panting or have panting. — reasons: The Basrans, al-Kashshāf, al-Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn, Ibn ʿĀshūr and others allow an adjective or verbal noun used as a state; Ibn ʿĀshūr also links manner of running to the ḍabʿ etymology. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ acknowledges this but prefers the absolute noun.
  100:1/q07/p3 Al-ʿādiyāt itself governs the noun, as running implies panting. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ and al-Zamakhsharī paraphrase the runners as the panting ones. Abū Ḥayyān objects that running and panting are not identical; al-Samīn replies that Zamakhsharī meant inseparable accompaniment and grammatical governance, not synonymy.
  100:1/q07/p4 A cognate accusative to running if ḍabḥ is a kind of fast gait. — reasons: Abū ʿUbaydah's and al-Mubarrad's ḍabʿ-based gloss supplies a verbal noun of motion; al-Samīn makes it emphatic of the participle and al-Ālūsī permits this parse.
  100:1/q07/p5 An adverb of time. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās gives this parse.
100:1/q08 What event is reported as the occasion of the opening? [* · reports] (turns on: Which campaign or journey the narrations identify, and whether the debated Badr horse count bears on the verse's occasion.)
  100:1/q08/p1 A delayed expedition against Kinānah under al-Mundhir ibn ʿAmr was announced by the verses. — reasons: Muqātil says it went to Ḥunayn; others say against one or two Kinānah clans. Reports differ on whether Jews, hypocrites or both spread rumors that the men had died. Gabriel disclosed their route or success, reassuring the Prophet and worried relatives. Al-Qurṭubī identifies al-Mundhir as an al-ʿAqabah leader. Al-Wāḥidī glosses ashabat as ranging across broad plains. Ibn ʿĀshūr notes Ibn Kathīr found al-Bazzār's version very strange and the chain has weak Ḥafṣ b. Jamīʿ, though he thinks the story possible.
  100:1/q08/p2 A delayed expedition to Tihāmah caused anxiety before Gabriel described its panting horses. — reasons: Muqātil's alternative says Jewish and hypocritical onlookers frightened those with missing relatives, and Gabriel came on Friday morning; the horses panted like foxes.
  100:1/q08/p3 An unnamed cavalry detachment was away for a month without news. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās says the Prophet sent cavalry; the missing news troubled him and the sūra described the horses' nostril-breathing, sparks, dawn assault, dust and entry into enemy ranks. Ibn Abī Ḥātim, al-Wāḥidī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Durr and al-Suyūṭī preserve versions. This account need not identify Badr.
  100:1/q08/p4 It followed ʿAlī's victory at Dhāt al-Salāsil. — reasons: An Abū ʿAbd Allāh report says earlier commanders returned, then ʿAlī prevailed. Another report says the Prophet recited the sūra at dawn after Gabriel announced victory; ʿAlī returned with prisoners and spoils. Dhāt al-Salāsil is explained by the captives being tied with rope-like chains; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites Imams and some Sunni reports.
  100:1/q08/p5 The horses or camels are linked to Badr. — reasons: Some commentators restrict Muslim horses to Badr, but ʿAlī's counterreport says it had only one or two horses, named variously as al-Miqdād's and al-Zubayr's. Other accounts use Badr as evidence for pilgrimage camels rather than an occasion of revelation.
100:1/q09 Was the sūra revealed in Mecca or Medina? [* · sciences] (turns on: Whether style favors early Mecca or the warhorse and expedition interpretation requires a Medinan setting.)
  100:1/q09/p1 Meccan. — reasons: Ibn Masʿūd, Jābir, al-Ḥasan, ʿIkrimah, ʿAṭāʾ and others are cited; Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, al-Durr and Quṭb classify it so. Study Quran cites style and early Meccan placement. Elmalılı says this dating makes the later martial imagery a future allusion. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī contests it because the images appear to concern jihad after the Hijra.
  100:1/q09/p2 Medinan. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās, Anas and Qatāda are cited. Al-Kalbī infers it if the oath is about warhorses, because permission to fight was in Medina. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī adopts that inference; Ibn ʿĀshūr prefers this dating despite the weakness in the expedition report's chain. Ibn ʿĀshūr cautions that ʿAlī's camel statement itself does not specify either period.
100:1/q10 What is the sūra's verse count and revelation order? [* · sciences]
  100:1/q10/p1 It has eleven verses; one Meccan ordering puts it fourteenth, after al-ʿAṣr and before al-Kawthar. — reasons: Al-Rāzī, al-Durr, Quṭb and Ibn ʿĀshūr give eleven; Ibn ʿĀshūr cites Jābir b. Zayd's Meccan revelation order. Al-Māwardī separately notes that the following al-Qāriʿah is called Meccan by all authorities he cites.
100:1/q11 How is the sūra titled? [ٱلْعَٰدِيَٰتِ · sciences] (turns on: Whether the sūra name includes the opening conjunction.)
  100:1/q11/p1 Al-ʿĀdiyāt, without the opening wāw. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr cites early Qayrawan, Tunisian and eastern codices and some commentaries. Biqāʿī says the title points to the purpose through the oath and its answer.
  100:1/q11/p2 Wa-l-ʿĀdiyāt, retaining the initial wāw. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says other tafsīrs preserve the opening wāw in the title.
100:1/q12 What recitation merit is reported for the sūra? [* · reports] (turns on: The reports describe different reward measures.)
  100:1/q12/p1 It equals half the Qur'an in recitation reward. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan transmits a Prophetic saying assigning half to both al-Zalzalah and al-ʿĀdiyāt. A report through ʿAṭāʾ from Ibn ʿAbbās likewise assigns half to each, a third to al-Ikhlāṣ and a quarter to al-Kāfirūn.
  100:1/q12/p2 Recitation brings ten good deeds for each person who spent the night at Muzdalifah and witnessed Jamʿ. — reasons: Jishumī and al-Kashshāf transmit the ten-deeds wording; al-Rāzī reports via Ubayy a reward measured by those who overnighted there and witnessed Jamʿ.
100:1/q13 What is said of the basmala before this sūra? [* · inward] (turns on: The notes offer distinct spiritual and theological explanations of the basmala.)
  100:1/q13/p1 Its utterance, knowledge and love call for a guarded tongue, heart and spirit. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī calls it a jealous word: the tongue must avoid idle talk and backbiting, the heart be guarded, and the spirit be free of attachments and veils.
  100:1/q13/p2 It presents God's sovereignty and His general and special mercy. — reasons: Biqāʿī says God commands all affairs, universally gives creation and clarification, and specially grants guidance and completed blessing to His devoted servants; the two notes repeat this account.
100:1/q14 Why swear by these chargers? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the oath chiefly honors valuable mounts, presents their obedience as evidence against people, honors the Prophet, or frames judgment.)
  100:1/q14/p1 To honor useful, noble mounts and call attention to God's gift. — reasons: Al-Rāzī cites camels' many benefits in 88:17, God's service to people, and pilgrimage, invoking 3:97 against neglecting obligatory ḥajj; for horses he cites pursuit, retreat and religious uses and warns against display alone. His reading of 16:8 treats riding, not adornment, as the stated purpose. Biqāʿī says horses confer honor or wealth and receive reward or sin by use; good care in God's cause protects the owner, and their distinctive qualities ultimately point to the one sovereign giver. Ibn ʿĀshūr says pilgrims' service is esteemed by pagans and Muslims. Ibn al-Qayyim praises horses' strength and victory; Muḥammad ʿAbduh urges acquiring, training and riding them for defense. Islahi stresses clan protection, pedigree and their esteem in poetry, citing a Ḥamāsī poem. Kuran Yolu says horses were central to old warfare and beloved possessions through which God stresses the message.
  100:1/q14/p2 The animals' dutiful exertion testifies against human ingratitude. — reasons: Islahi relates their exhausted obedience to 100:6 and rejects readings whose sanctity premise leaves the oath disconnected. Biqāʿī says the oath rebukes preference for transient wealth and failure to prepare for Judgment. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says human love of wealth and ingratitude are known to people and become evidence at judgment; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the sūra condemns these traits, warning even Muslims.
  100:1/q14/p3 It is an oath by the Prophet's cavalry that honors him. — reasons: Ibn al-ʿArabī compares God's oath by Muḥammad in Yā Sīn and by his life in al-Ḥijr with the oath here by his cavalry, its neighing, dust and sparks.
  100:1/q14/p4 It directs attention to coming judgment and the disclosure of deeds. — reasons: Biqāʿī reads the oath and answer as exposing the pursuit of status and wealth before the upheaval; Ibn ʿĀshūr says it promises reassurance to believers and threat to deniers. Study Quran divides the five charging verses, the ingratitude of 6–8 and the judgment of 9–11.
