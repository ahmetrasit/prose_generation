# 96:2/q01 What does the creation verb convey here? [خَلَقَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the noteworthy feature is the perfect's aspect or the shortened verbal form.
- 96:2/q01/p1 Its perfect form states a repeatedly occurring, generic act of divine creation.
  reasons: Asad reads the past tense as continual repetition. Corpus Coranicum calls the perfect aspectual rather than simply past, comparing divine-action perfects in 87:4-5, 53:43-44 and 85:13.
  holders (3 notes): ASAD-NOTES; CORPUSCORANICUM
  notes: ASAD-NOTES:96:1#n2/r1 CORPUSCORANICUM:96:1-2:anmerkung/r12 CORPUSCORANICUM:96:1-2:anmerkung/r13
- 96:2/q01/p2 The verb's shortened form signals the small, humble beginning from which creation grows.
  reasons: Al-Burhan relates the omitted final nun to the despised drop and to development that God alone fully encompasses.
  holders (1 notes): BURHAN
  notes: BURHAN:v1p407#2/r1

# 96:2/q02 How does this clause relate grammatically to the preceding 'who created'? [خَلَقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · grammar]
turns on: How the first verb's unexpressed object is construed, and whether repetition supplies specification, explanation, or emphasis.
- 96:2/q02/p1 The first 'created' has no expressed object and asserts God's creating as such.
  reasons: Razi allows an absolute or intransitive first verb, emphasizing that God alone originates. Ibn Ashur also allows omission for generality, with the object anticipated by this clause.
  holders (3 notes): RAZI; RAZI-FULL; IBNASHUR
  notes: RAZI:96:2/r1 RAZI-FULL:v32p16/r1 IBNASHUR:96:2/r3
- 96:2/q02/p2 The first verb refers to all creation, and this clause specifies human creation within it.
  reasons: Razi treats the unrestricted object as everything; Ibn Ashur calls the second clause a detailed or partitive apposition, first establishing dependence on God and then reinforcing proof with detail. Al-Burhan, Bint al-Shati and Islahi also describe general creation followed by specification.
  holders (8 notes): RAZI; RAZI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BURHAN; BINTSHATI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR

- 96:2/q02/p3 The second clause explains the first verb's initially unspecified object, drawing attention to human creation.
  reasons: Razi, Zamakhshari as reported by Abu Hayyan, Baydawi, Nasafi, Bursevi, Alusi and Ibn Ashur use clarification after initial vagueness to magnify the marvelous human constitution; Baydawi allows an independent new statement or completion of the relative description. # 96:2/q03 Who is 'the human' in this verse? [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · referent]
turns on: Whether al-alaq here is the blood stage of Adam's descendants or hand-clinging clay associated with Adam.
- 96:2/q03/p1 Humanity generically, especially Adam's descendants born through gestation.
  reasons: Mawardi, Wahidi, Baghawi, Qurtubi and others say all people or Adam's children. Ibn Atiyya excludes Adam himself, who was made from clay, and says the mind extends the generic reference to animals. Biqai notes familiarity with one's own species. Corpus Coranicum relates early Meccan creation language to embryos, citing 95:4, 90:4, 70:19 and 55:14. Itqan contrasts the first 'human' as species with later referents.

# 96:2/q04 What does 'ʿalaq' mean here? [عَلَقٍ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the expression names congealed blood, attachment broadly, a fertilized ovum, or sticky earth.
- 96:2/q04/p1 A moist, clinging piece of congealed blood or an embryonic clot.
  reasons: Tabari calls it blood. Mawardi, Jishumi and Qurtubi derive the name from sticking while wet and say dryness ends that designation; Qushayri distinguishes congealed from flowing blood. Mawardi and Qurtubi cite poetry about vein or aorta blood. Tabarsi and Ibn Ashur also note a leech sense; Ibn Ashur says the small dark-red clot resembles the aquatic leech. Qutb places the fixed blood point at the womb. Islahi compares 22:5, 23:14, 32, 75 and 76; Study Quran calls it gestation's third stage in 22:5, 23:14, 40:67 and 75:38.
