# 71:17: 13 questions

## 71:17/q01 What does أَنۢبَتَكُم mean here?  [أَنۢبَتَكُم · meaning]
turns on: Whether the plant verb is read as an expression for creation or for the process of growth.
- 71:17/q01/p1 It means God created or originated humans, expressed as causing growth. — Muqatil calls nabāt creation; al-Damghani gives 'create' as a sense of anbata. Ibn ʿAshur compares the likeness to human formation from earth's elements, 3:37, and the idiom 'God planted you for good.' Al-Alusi says growth makes origination from earth visible. [5 notes: MUQATIL; DAMGHANI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 71:17/q01/p2 It means to cause growth or bring forth progressively. — Al-Jishumi describes a plant emerging from earth stage by stage. Elmalılı explains anbata as making grow or bringing forth; growth in vegetation occurs through nourishment and reproduction. Asad describes gradual human growth. [4 notes: JISHUMI; ELMALILI; ASAD-NOTES]

## 71:17/q02 Is the comparison of human creation to plant growth figurative or literal?  [أَنۢبَتَكُم · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether shared earthly material and growth make the plant comparison literal or a metaphor for origination.
- 71:17/q02/p1 It is a metaphor for humans being originated from earth. — Ibn ʿAtiyya, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan and al-Samin call the growth language metaphorical, connecting Adam's earthly origin to his descendants. Al-Baydawi says the image conveys coming into being and formation especially well. Al-Alusi calls it an explicit transferred verbal metaphor, treating resurrection deniers as if they denied origination too. Ibn ʿAshur stresses the close likeness to plants through common earthly elements. [8 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 71:17/q02/p2 It speaks literally of bodies growing from specially combined earthly elements. — Tabataba'i says those elements nourish bodies, support growth and reproduction, and constitute the reality of plant growth. Asad also identifies organic and inorganic earthly substances in each developing body. [2 notes: TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES]

## 71:17/q03 In what sense did God grow humans from the earth?  [أَنۢبَتَكُم مِّنَ ٱلْأَرْضِ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the earthly origin is traced through Adam, each person's nourishment and elements, personal maturation, species development, or an inward account of the body.
- 71:17/q03/p1 Adam was made from earth, and the address includes his descendants through him. — Ibn Jurayj says Adam was made from soil of the whole earth; Qurtubi points to discussions in al-Anʿam and al-Baqara. Khalid ibn Maʿdan says humanity was made from clay and links this to hearts softening in winter. Al-Kalbi, al-Wahidi and others explain descent from Adam. Al-Tabrisi cites 4:1; al-Razi, Elmalılı, Ibn ʿAshur and Study Quran cite 3:59, the dust origin of Adam, with Study Quran noting the Adam–Jesus likeness. [23 notes: MUQATIL; NAHHAS(قيل); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن جريج,خالد بن معدان); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس,الكلبي,مقاتل); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن جريج,خالد بن معدان); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن جريج); BURSEVI; ELMALILI(الرازي); IBNASHUR-FULL(قيل); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 71:17/q03/p2 All people arise from earth through food, plants, nutrients, semen, or their bodily elements. — Al-Nahhas traces the sperm drop to earth; al-Razi specifies nutrients to plants to food to semen. Al-Mawardi and al-Tabrisi refer to nourishment from earth's growth and substances; al-Biqaʿi says earth supplies food. Bursawi includes the chain of sperm, plants and food. Elmalılı reports this alongside Adam's origin. Kur'an Yolu notes direct or indirect nourishment. Tabataba'i and Asad stress earthly elements; Asad connects this to stages in 71:14. Ibn ʿAshur says human bodies are formed from earth's elements like plants. [11 notes: NAHHAS(قيل); MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI(قيل); RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI(الرازي); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 71:17/q03/p3 The growth is each person's development from infancy or smallness to maturity and height. — Ibn Baḥr and al-Tabrisi describe movement from smallness to largeness or old age and from shortness to height. Ibn Jurayj, as reported by Qurtubi, describes humans growing on earth from childhood to adulthood and increasing in height. [4 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(ابن بحر); TABRISI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن جريج); ASAD-NOTES]
- 71:17/q03/p4 It also refers to the human species evolving from primitive organisms through increasingly complex development. — Asad extends the successive stages to bodily, mental and spiritual development of the species. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES]
- 71:17/q03/p5 In an inward reading, it is the growth of the body from its earthly basis. — Al-Qashani speaks of emergence from the 'earth of the body.' Bursawi reports people of spiritual knowledge who liken bodily nourishment and growth from earth to plants drawing on water and soil. [2 notes: QASHANI; BURSEVI(أهل المعرفة)]

## 71:17/q04 What does مِّنَ mark in مِّنَ ٱلْأَرْضِ?  [مِّنَ ٱلْأَرْضِ · grammar]
- 71:17/q04/p1 It marks the earth as the remote origin of creation. — Al-Alusi explicitly calls min originative and the origin remote. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 71:17/q05 What can نَبَاتًۭا mean as a noun?  [نَبَاتًۭا · meaning]
turns on: Whether nabāt is taken as an action noun or a noun for vegetation or the result.
- 71:17/q05/p1 It is a verbal noun meaning sprouting or growing. — Elmalılı gives this sense alongside the collective noun. Study Quran glosses the phrase 'like plants' as 'as growing things.' Ibn al-Jawzi also records creation or origination from earth as the contextual sense. [3 notes: ELMALILI; STUDYQURAN; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA]
- 71:17/q05/p2 It names vegetation or the result of growing, especially grass or fodder in ordinary usage. — Elmalılı gives nabāt or nabt as a collective noun. Al-Zarkashi contrasts the result noun nabāt with the proper action noun inbāt, comparing kalām/taklīm and salām/taslīm. [2 notes: ELMALILI; BURHAN]

## 71:17/q06 How is نَبَاتًۭا governed and parsed?  [نَبَاتًۭا · grammar]
turns on: Whether nabāt can be governed directly by anbata through shared meaning, requires an omitted growth verb or paired ellipsis, serves as a circumstance, or belongs to an implicit planting of vegetation.
- 71:17/q06/p1 It is an accusative verbal noun attached to أَنۢبَتَكُم by shared meaning, standing for the regular إنباتًا. — Sibawayh licenses a nonmatching verbal noun when meanings coincide, comparing اجتوروا/تجاوروا, انكسر/كسر, al-Qatami's تتبعه اتباعا, Ruʾba's تطويت انطواء, and يدعه تركا. Al-Akhfash, al-Nahhas, al-Thaʿlabi, al-Baghawi, al-Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, al-Suyuti, Bursawi, Elmalılı and Ibn ʿAshur distinguish regular inbāt from nabāt. Al-Zajjaj and Abu Isḥāq say 'made you grow' licenses the sense and adds emphasis. Ibn Qutayba compares tabtīlan at 73:8, two poets' ittibāʿan and ʿiwādan, and shared meanings of different verbs. Abu Hayyan and al-Samin call deletion of added letters an ism maṣdar. Al-Zarkashi allows direct government when meanings match; Elmalılı says a verbal noun is the prior, stronger analysis, since people are not simply plants. Ibn Kathir calls this noun particularly apt; Islahi says the verbal nouns emphasize their verbs in different ways. [36 notes: SIBAWAYH; AKHFASH; ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,الأخفش); BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة,الزجاج,الشاعر,الشاعر الآخر); QURTUBI-FULL(الخليل، الزجاج); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURHAN; ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان وجماعة,صاحب الكشف); ELMALILI(المفسرون,بعض المفسرين); IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ELMALILI+]
- 71:17/q06/p2 An omitted 'you grew' governs نَبَاتًۭا: God made you grow, so you grew. — Al-Khalil supplies fa-nabattum nabātan; al-Mubarrad gives the same parse, which Ibn Khuruf chose and Ibn Yaʿish accepted. Ibn al-Jawzi compares the growth in 3:37. Al-Zamakhshari alternatively treats anbata as containing the sense of nabattum; al-Samin identifies that with this omitted-growth parse. Al-Razi calls the resulting growth remarkable. Al-Kashf says agent-side inbāt entails recipient-side nabāt and may need no extra verb; al-Alusi notes that if anbata means preliminary seed planting, implied growth expresses swift divine power, comparing 7:160. Elmalılı calls it a resultative verbal noun. [18 notes: THALABI(الخليل); JISHUMI(الخليل); BAGHAWI-FULL(الخليل); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الخليل); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الخليل، الزجاج); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; BURHAN(المبرد); ALUSI-FULL(صاحب الكشف); ELMALILI(بعض المفسرين); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]
- 71:17/q06/p3 The wording compresses two clauses by omitting inbātan after أَنۢبَتَكُم and fa-nabattum before نَبَاتًۭا. — Al-Biqaʿi calls this iḥtibāk and reconstructs anbatakum inbātan fa-nabattum nabātan; al-Alusi reports the same paired ellipsis. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 71:17/q06/p4 نَبَاتًۭا is a circumstantial accusative: humans are plantlike in earthly origin and growth. — Bursawi reports this analysis while adding that humans possess a further quality. Elmalılı concedes it preserves the nuance but prefers the basic verbal-noun reading as prior and stronger. [1 notes: BURSEVI(بعضهم); ELMALILI-]
- 71:17/q06/p5 The implicit object of 'He grew' is plants for humans, making نَبَاتًۭا an explicit cognate accusative. — Qurtubi reports 'He grew plants for you from the earth' but judges the earlier analysis, the verbal noun standing for inbāt, clearer. [0 notes: QURTUBI-FULL-]

## 71:17/q07 Was the omitted-verb analysis rightly attributed to Sibawayh?  [نَبَاتًۭا · grammar]
turns on: The historical attribution of the parsing to Sibawayh.
- 71:17/q07/p1 It was attributed to Sibawayh. — Al-Zarkashi reports this attribution while citing al-Mubarrad's omitted-verb analysis and the support of Ibn Khuruf and Ibn Yaʿish. [1 notes: BURHAN(المبرد); BURHAN(ابن عصفور)-]
- 71:17/q07/p2 Ibn ʿAsfur disputes that attribution. — His dispute concerns the attribution, rather than necessarily the validity of the omitted-verb parse. [1 notes: BURHAN(ابن عصفور)]

## 71:17/q08 Why use the growth wording and نَبَاتًۭا rather than a plain statement of creation?  [أَنۢبَتَكُم نَبَاتًۭا · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the noteworthy force is visible proof, the emphatic and lighter noun, or compressed expression by entailment.
- 71:17/q08/p1 It makes divine origination observable and the argument perceptible. — Al-Razi says God's act itself is unseen and would otherwise need revelation, whereas marvelous complete growth is seen. Elmalılı endorses this. Al-Baydawi says the metaphor vividly conveys emergence and formation; al-Alusi likewise stresses visible origination. Ibn Kathir calls the noun especially apt. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL; ELMALILI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 71:17/q08/p2 The lighter noun or altered form gives emphasis and eloquence, and can suggest ease or elaborate, powerful creation. — Al-Zajjaj and Abu Isḥāq find nabāt more emphatic than the regular inbāt. Bursawi compares the following ikhrājan. Al-Biqaʿi says stripping the augmented form signals the ease of God's wondrous creation. Ibn ʿAshur calls the lighter established nabāt more eloquent. Islahi prefers elaborately wrought creation joined to divine might and wisdom over senses of mere power or ease, citing parallel verses and 71:14. [6 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR+]
- 71:17/q08/p3 The phrase is abbreviated because causing growth entails the subsequent growth. — Al-Baydawi supplies 'He caused you to grow from earth, then you grew.' Al-Qadi explains the shortening by entailment. Al-Khafaji objects that al-Qadi's account misses the rhetorical indication already described by al-Alusi. [2 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(القاضي); ALUSI-FULL(الخفاجي)-]

## 71:17/q09 What parallel readings are mentioned while explaining the wording?  [نَبَاتًۭا · readings]
turns on: These are different cited passages and readers, not rival readings of 71:17.
- 71:17/q09/p1 A reading attributed to Ibn Masʿud has 'wa-anzala l-malāʾikata tanzīlā' at 25:25. — Sibawayh uses the shared meanings of anzala and nazzala to illustrate a nonmatching verbal noun. [1 notes: SIBAWAYH(ابن مسعود)]
- 71:17/q09/p2 Abu ʿAmr reads 'mimmā khaṭāyāhum' with yāʾ, while the other readers use 'khaṭīʾātihim' with tāʾ, lengthening and hamza. — Al-Tabrisi reports these readings in the surrounding discussion; he also says the variant in waladuhu was treated under Surat Maryam 19:77. [2 notes: TABRISI(أبو عمرو)]

## 71:17/q10 How does this verse connect to the nearby signs and the resurrection?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the connection is the sequence of signs and resurrection or the continued syntax of the earlier rhetorical question.
- 71:17/q10/p1 After heavenly signs, the passage turns to humans and the earth as a proof of creation and resurrection. — Al-Razi says the verse returns to proofs in human selves and stages of creation, relating 71:14 to earth, return, and emergence; he calls it the third proof of oneness. Al-Biqaʿi calls humans the greatest created sign and says plant growth explicitly establishes resurrection. Ibn ʿAshur sees a turn from heavens to earth, death and burial. Islahi calls humans earth's noblest creatures and likens human death and return to vegetation dying and returning; renewed plants show God's power to raise people. Asad relates bodily stages to 71:14. [9 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ASAD-NOTES]
- 71:17/q10/p2 The verse continues a rhetorical question begun in verse 15. — Study Quran says verse 16 may begin 'and that He' and verses 17 and 19 continue the same question. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 71:17/q11 Who is speaking in verses 15–20?  [* · referent]
turns on: How the renewed speech introduction at 71:21 marks the boundary of Noah's speech.
- 71:17/q11/p1 They may continue Noah's speech. — Islahi acknowledges this reading of the six verses. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 71:17/q11/p2 They are divine insertions before Noah's prayer resumes. — Islahi prefers this because verse 21 restarts with 'Noah said, My Lord.' [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR+]

## 71:17/q12 What does this sign ask people to recognize about God?  [* · theology]
turns on: Which inference from earthly growth the commentator draws: sole worship, resurrection, or the one source of beings.
- 71:17/q12/p1 God alone creates and provides, so He alone deserves worship. — Al-Tabrisi says the favors prove God's exclusive claim to worship, His knowledge of human interests and wise governance, and condemn disbelief. Ibn Kathir lists sky, earth and provision, then denies any peer, partner, spouse, child, minister or adviser. Al-Razi calls the life cycle a proof of oneness. [4 notes: TABRISI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; RAZI-FULL]
- 71:17/q12/p2 The creation and return to earth attest God's power to resurrect the dead. — Al-Biqaʿi places the sign in the sūra's aim to establish resurrection and God's power to destroy rejecters and replace them. Ibn ʿAshur calls it an embedded promise of life after death. Islahi argues from renewed vegetation to God's ability to revive people at will. [4 notes: BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 71:17/q12/p3 The diversity of growing beings points to one ontological source in God. — Study Quran says all things emerge from God as one Source, whose substance is within them. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 71:17/q13 How is this sign framed by the sūra's overall purpose?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the broader framing emphasizes Noah's preaching, the threat and resurrection, or the symbolic spiritual struggle.
- 71:17/q13/p1 Noah's preaching uses creation and provision as signs in his call to his people. — Ibn Kathir says Noah invokes divine power, greatness and heavenly and earthly benefits. Asad calls the Meccan sūra entirely devoted to Noah's preaching to his erring people, comparing 11:25. [2 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES]
- 71:17/q13/p2 Noah's story demonstrates God's power to destroy rejecters, replace them, and raise the dead. — Al-Biqaʿi says the people's destruction for rejecting Noah establishes the threat. He describes the basmala as divine perfection in majesty and generosity, outward blessing to all as al-Raḥmān and lasting blessing for obedient allies as al-Raḥīm. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 71:17/q13/p3 The story symbolizes a believer's struggle against blind materialism and lost spiritual values. — Asad links this symbolic account with his description of Noah's story, citing 11:25. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES]
