# 43:11: 19 questions

## 43:11/q01 How does the relative clause beginning وَٱلَّذِى attach to the preceding words?  [وَٱلَّذِى · grammar]
- 43:11/q01/p1 It continues the description without a pause; alternatively an implied 'He is' permits a pause. — Abū Ḥātim, reported by Nasafī, disallows pausing at al-ʿAlīm because alladhī qualifies it, but permits a pause if huwa is supplied before alladhī. [1 notes: NASAFI-FULL(أبو حاتم)]

## 43:11/q02 Why is the relative pronoun repeated here?  [وَٱلَّذِى · rhetoric]
- 43:11/q02/p1 It magnifies the divine agent and marks resurrection through rain and revival as a greater sign than the earth's stability and roads. — Biqāʿī contrasts this sign with the preceding signs of stable earth and roads. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 43:11/q03 Whose speech are the descriptions of divine acts, and why does the voice change?  [وَٱلَّذِى فَأَنشَرْنَا · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the first-person wording is explained by a change of speaker after the pagans' words or as a meaningful shift within God's address; the notes also give different effects of the shift.
- 43:11/q03/p1 They are God's speech following the polytheists' admission, and the change to 'We revived' is a divine change of speaker. — Kashshāf says the descriptions are not the pagans' words. Nasafī says they cannot be the polytheists' speech because those people denied resurrection. Ālūsī follows Ibn al-Munīr: the first-person clause follows a speaker change naturally, comparing Moses' speech followed by God's 'We brought forth' at 20:52–53. Elmali says the descriptions clarify who God is and the first-person turn serves that clarification. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 43:11/q03/p2 The move from third-person description to first-person plural itself draws attention to God's revival of the earth. — Nasafī says the listener recognizes who the speaker is; Ālūsī says the shift highlights divine care and the act's importance; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says it signals special attention; Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies the grammatical shift. Ibn ʿĀshūr separately says the eleven second-person pronouns in these verses are ordinary direct address, not iltifāt. [5 notes: NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 43:11/q04 What does the form نَزَّلَ suggest about the sending of rain?  [نَزَّلَ · grammar]
- 43:11/q04/p1 Its form suggests recurring descent. — Asad renders it 'again and again'. Biqāʿī describes fresh water descending gradually from above. [2 notes: ASAD-NOTES; BIQAI-FULL]

## 43:11/q05 What does ٱلسَّمَآءِ mean as rain's source?  [ٱلسَّمَآءِ · referent]
turns on: Whether 'heaven' denotes the sky literally or the elevated clouds.
- 43:11/q05/p1 Heaven literally. — Rāzī raises this possibility and refers the issue to his earlier discussion. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 43:11/q05/p2 Clouds, called heaven because what is elevated is 'heaven'. — Rāzī raises this alternative without settling it here. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 43:11/q06 What is the مَآءً sent down?  [مَآءً · referent]
- 43:11/q06/p1 Rain. — Muqātil identifies the water as rain; Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls this identification consensual. [2 notes: MUQATIL; IBNATIYYA-FULL]

## 43:11/q07 What does بِقَدَرٍ say about the rain's amount?  [بِقَدَرٍ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether measure describes beneficial sufficiency, an eternal decree or specified quantity, and whether the total annual amount stays constant or varies. Ālūsī prefers wisdom and disputes human claims of exact measurement.
- 43:11/q07/p1 It is sufficient for benefit and livelihood, without destructive excess or useless shortage. — Ṭabarī, Thaʿlabī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya's 'others', Kashshāf, Ṭabrisī, Rāzī, Qurṭubī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ibn Kathīr, Bursevī, Biqāʿī and Study Quran describe useful sufficiency; Ṭabarī names crops, Ibn Kathīr crops, fruits and drink, and Biqāʿī fresh water for crops, fruit, people and livestock. Ibn ʿAbbās, as reported by Wāḥidī, Ibn al-Jawzī and Qurṭubī, contrasts measured beneficial rain with Noah's unmeasured, drowning flood; Baghawī and Rāzī repeat that contrast. Bursevī says harmful floods may occur every twenty or thirty years as trial and requital. Study Quran cites 23:18. [17 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(آخرون); TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس); STUDYQURAN(some commentators)]
- 43:11/q07/p2 It is a measure set by divine will, wisdom and interest, known precisely to God. — Wāḥidī al-Wajīz says the measure is known to Him. Ālūsī prefers divine wisdom and says only God knows exactly how much falls yearly; he doubts claims that a contemporary 'audometer' can precisely measure annual rain even in a small locality. [3 notes: WAHIDI-WAJIZ; ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 43:11/q07/p3 It is a decree fixed in pre-eternity. — A group reported by Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Baḥr reported by Ālūsī offer this gloss; Abū Ḥayyān includes it among two possible senses. Ālūsī instead prefers a measure governed by wisdom. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(فرقة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(البحر)]
- 43:11/q07/p4 It is a specified, carefully determined quantity. — A group reported by Ibn ʿAṭiyya gives this interpretation. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(فرقة)]
- 43:11/q07/p5 The same total amount of rain descends each year, though its location changes. — Some proponents of a specified quantity, reported by Ibn ʿAṭiyya, add this annual distribution claim. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 43:11/q07/p6 The amount varies from year to year under God's prior decree. — Another group reported by Ibn ʿAṭiyya gives this account. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(فرقة)]

## 43:11/q08 What does the measured rain show about God?  [بِقَدَرٍ · theology]
- 43:11/q08/p1 Its measured benefit shows deliberate, wise divine governance and power. — Ṭabrisī infers a powerful choosing agent who measures with wisdom and knowledge. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says intention and governance rule out chance; Study Quran links God's measuring all things at 25:2 and discussion of 54:49. Rāzī connects the land's revival with divine power and wisdom; Ṭabāṭabāʾī infers resurrection from God's creation and governance. [5 notes: TABRISI; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN; RAZI-FULL]

## 43:11/q09 What does فَأَنشَرْنَا mean here?  [فَأَنشَرْنَا · meaning]
- 43:11/q09/p1 God revived the dead land by the water, causing vegetation to appear. — Majāz and Ṭabarī cite Arab usage nasharat/nushirat and al-Aʿshā's line calling a dead person revived; Ṭabrisī and Ibn ʿĀshūr also cite that line. Ibn ʿĀshūr explains the causative hamza and cites 80:22; his report of Ibn ʿAbbās objects to a reading at 2:259 using 80:22. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the verb can describe either a dead person's revival or God's reviving him. Biqāʿī traces motion, extension and spreading; Ibn ʿĀshūr derives it from spreading what was folded, then revival and dispersal. Rummānī calls the appearance of trees, plants and fruit a figurative resurrection, stronger than mere showing; Ibn ʿĀshūr says reviving earth is metaphorical and anticipates resurrection. Nasafī, Bursevī and others give the plain gloss. [18 notes: MAJAZ; TAB-FULL; RUMMANI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن عباس,الأعشى); TABATABAI; DAMGHANI; QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 43:11/q10 What is the بَلْدَةً مَّيْتًۭا that the rain revives?  [بَلْدَةً مَّيْتًۭا · referent]
- 43:11/q10/p1 A drought-stricken, barren land without living vegetation. — Muqātil says water makes plants grow; Ṭabarī describes drought, devastation and no crops; Rāzī, Qurṭubī, Bursevī and Ālūsī also stress absence of growth. Biqāʿī depicts an inhabited working place whose vegetation dried up and people lacked irrigation, with 'dead' heightening barrenness, weak soil and inhabitants, and disappearance of former growth. Ibn ʿĀshūr says 'dead' opposes living and is conventional figurative usage, citing 36:33. Ālūsī calls the description an implicit or explicit metaphor; Bursevī likens its barrenness to bodily death. [13 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; TABRISI; DAMGHANI]

## 43:11/q11 Why does feminine بَلْدَةً take masculine مَّيْتًۭا?  [بَلْدَةً مَّيْتًۭا · grammar]
turns on: Whether masculine agreement follows the town's implied masculine referent, a referent in the rain, an adjective pattern, or a rhetorical gender contrast.
- 43:11/q11/p1 The town is understood as a masculine place, region or land, with nonliteral feminine gender. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls balda generic and its feminine gender unreal; Bayḍāwī equates it with balad or makān; Bursevī adds open space; Ṭabāṭabāʾī also calls it a place. Ālūsī reports the same treatment alongside the doubled reading; Nasafī reports Yazīd's masculine adjective agreeing with 'place'. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL(يزيد); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(أبو جعفر وعيسى); TABATABAI]
- 43:11/q11/p2 The adjective refers to the rain, while balda is a collective noun. — Abū Ḥayyān gives this grammatical account. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 43:11/q11/p3 The adjective follows forms originally used as verbal nouns, which lack feminine marking. — Ibn ʿĀshūr cites ʿadl and zūr and says the town's feminine gender is figurative. Ibn ʿAṭiyya also compares the preferred light form to zūr and ʿadl in discussing the reading. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 43:11/q11/p4 The gender contrast emphasizes the land's extreme weakness or lifelessness. — Saʿdī al-Muftī, reported by Bursevī, also sees God's care in the plural revival verb. Al-Jalabī, reported by Ālūsī, says the contrast may mark the extreme limit of lifelessness; Ālūsī calls it possible. [2 notes: BURSEVI(سعدي المفتي); ALUSI-FULL(الجلبي)]

## 43:11/q12 How is مَّيْتًۭا read?  [مَّيْتًۭا · readings]
turns on: Light versus doubled yāʾ; the notes give the same sense for both, while Ibn ʿAṭiyya prefers the lighter pattern.
- 43:11/q12/p1 The common or majority reading lightens the yāʾ: maytan. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya prefers this reading, comparing zūr and ʿadl. Abū Ḥayyān and Samīn call it the majority/common form. Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls mayt the lighter form of mayyit; Qirāʾāt ER says the forms share the sense dead or lifeless. [5 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو جعفر وعيسى); SAMIN-DURR(العامة); TABATABAI; QIRAAT-ER(Gm); IBNATIYYA-FULL+]
- 43:11/q12/p2 Abū Jaʿfar and ʿĪsā read mayyitan with doubled yāʾ. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Abū Ḥayyān, Samīn and Ālūsī name both readers. Nashr says Abū Jaʿfar doubles yāʾ across the listed forms of mayyita/maytan, balad mayyit and al-ḥayy min al-mayyit; Qirāʾāt ER calls the form fuller and phonetically weightier without changing 'dead/lifeless'. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو جعفر وعيسى); SAMIN-DURR(العامة); NASHR(أبو جعفر); ALUSI-FULL(أبو جعفر وعيسى); QIRAAT-ER(Gm)]

## 43:11/q13 Why is plant growth called revival while resurrection is called bringing out?  [فَأَنشَرْنَا تُخْرَجُونَ · rhetoric]
- 43:11/q13/p1 The asymmetry magnifies vegetation's emergence and makes human resurrection seem easy, strengthening the argument against denial. — Bursevī, Ālūsī and the explanation reported by Ṭabāṭabāʾī all make this point; Ālūsī explicitly says it rebuts deniers. [3 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI(قيل)]

## 43:11/q14 How does كَذَٰلِكَ work grammatically?  [كَذَٰلِكَ · grammar]
- 43:11/q14/p1 It modifies an understood verbal noun: you will be brought out in an emergence or resurrection like this one. — Naḥḥās puts the kāf in the accusative, supplying an emergence like this; Ālūsī supplies an omitted verbal noun meaning resurrection like this. [2 notes: NAHHAS; ALUSI-FULL]

## 43:11/q15 What emergence does كَذَٰلِكَ تُخْرَجُونَ signify?  [كَذَٰلِكَ تُخْرَجُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the emergence is bodily resurrection alone or also spiritual revival; the notes present the latter as an additional sense.
- 43:11/q15/p1 People will be raised alive from earth and graves after bodily death. — Muqātil compares people emerging from earth to plants. Ṭabarī specifies restoration after decay to the former bodily form; Qatāda compares Judgment Day resurrection with dead earth's revival. Thaʿlabī, Wāḥidī, Baghawī, Ṭabrisī, Qurṭubī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Bursevī, Ālūsī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī mention emergence alive from graves; Biqāʿī says God can restore disintegrated bodies, spirits, matter and attributes, and that the water alone has no independent power. Ibn Kathīr describes the earth swelling, rising and growing beautiful kinds as the sign of raised bodies. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the likeness is life after death and illustrates analogy with Abū al-Ṭayyib's musk and deer-blood comparison. Damghānī mentions 35:9; Study Quran lists parallels at 7:57, 16:65, 29:63, 30:19, 30:24, 30:50, 35:9, 36:33, 41:39, 45:5, 50:9–11 and 57:17. [23 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(قتادة); THALABI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; DAMGHANI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]
- 43:11/q15/p2 The image can also signify emergence from ignorance and the soul's death into faith or new life through the Qur'an. — Biqāʿī connects the spread of humans after death at 30:20 with emergence from ignorance into faith, wisdom and knowledge. Elmali gives life through the Qur'an as a spirit alongside raising from graves. Study Quran reports a symbolism of the soul's death and life and mentions 7:58, 22:63 and 30:50. [4 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; STUDYQURAN(some commentators)]

## 43:11/q16 Does the comparison describe a physical mechanism for resurrection?  [كَذَٰلِكَ تُخْرَجُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the likeness states only the possibility of resurrection or also how bodies will physically regrow; Rāzī rejects deriving the mechanism from this wording.
- 43:11/q16/p1 It establishes that God can raise the dead, as He raises vegetation from dead land, without specifying a mechanism. — Rāzī says the verse's wording only asserts resurrection, calling the proposed water-and-semen mechanism weak. Qurṭubī and Bursevī say the power to revive land establishes the power to raise people; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the simile shows resurrection possible. Biqāʿī says water itself has no power apart from God's measure. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL(بعضهم); QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 43:11/q16/p2 God will use water like semen to regrow bodily remains from earth, as rain grows plants. — Ibn Masʿūd, reported by Naḥḥās, describes rain like men's semen, remnants sprouting and flesh growing from soil and rain; Naḥḥās says this is known only through transmission. Rāzī reports a similar explanation from some but calls it weak because the verse says only that resurrection occurs. [1 notes: NAHHAS(عبد الله بن مسعود); RAZI-FULL(بعضهم)-]

## 43:11/q17 How is تُخْرَجُونَ read?  [تُخْرَجُونَ · readings]
turns on: Passive versus active voice and the transmitted reader or route, especially Ibn Dhakwān's exceptional Zayd–al-Ṣūrī route.
- 43:11/q17/p1 The majority read passive tukhrajūn, 'you are brought forth'. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya names most of the Seven, al-Aʿraj and Abū Jaʿfar; Ibn al-Jawzī, Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān and Ibn ʿĀshūr contrast the other readers with this form. Nashr identifies its ḍamma on tāʾ and fatḥa on rāʾ and says Zayd through al-Ṣūrī is the sole Ibn Dhakwān route reporting this common form here. Qirāʾāt ER says the passive and active retain the root and basic emergence; Ibn ʿĀshūr says they have the same meaning. Samīn refers back to a discussion of the readings in al-Aʿrāf. [8 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(حمزة والكسائي وابن عامر); QURTUBI-FULL(يحيى بن وثاب والأعمش وحمزة والكسائي وابن ذكوان عن ابن عامر); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور وابن وثاب وعبد الله بن جبير المصبح وعيسى وابن عامر والأخوان); SAMIN-DURR; NASHR(زيد من طريق الصوري); IBNASHUR-FULL(الجمهور); QIRAAT-ER(Pss)]
- 43:11/q17/p2 Named readers use active takhrujūn, 'you come forth', with fatḥa on tāʾ and ḍamma on rāʾ. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya names Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī, Ibn Waththāb and ʿAbd Allāh ibn Jubayr; Ibn al-Jawzī and Bayḍāwī include Ibn ʿĀmir; Qurṭubī names Yaḥyā ibn Waththāb, al-Aʿmash and Ibn Dhakwān from Ibn ʿĀmir; Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī additionally name ʿĪsā. Nasafī names Ḥamza and ʿAlī. Nashr names Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī and Khalaf in four places, Yaʿqūb and Ibn Dhakwān at 7:25, and Ibn Dhakwān here, with one contrary Ibn Dhakwān route. Qirāʾāt ER describes the change in grammatical agency while retaining kh-r-j. [12 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(حمزة والكسائي وابن عامر); QURTUBI-FULL(يحيى بن وثاب والأعمش وحمزة والكسائي وابن ذكوان عن ابن عامر); BAYDAWI-FULL(ابن عامر وحمزة والكسائي); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور وابن وثاب وعبد الله بن جبير المصبح وعيسى وابن عامر والأخوان); SAMIN-DURR; NASHR(حمزة، والكسائي، وخلف,زيد من طريق الصوري); ALUSI-FULL(ابن وثاب وعبد الله بن جبير وعيسى وابن عامر والأخوان); IBNASHUR-FULL(الجمهور); QIRAAT-ER(Pss)]

## 43:11/q18 What inward revival can the rain and emergence portray?  [مَآءً فَأَنشَرْنَا تُخْرَجُونَ · inward]
turns on: Whether the image is applied to guidance and faith or to lawful sustenance as a means of reviving the heart.
- 43:11/q18/p1 Guidance revives the dead heart and brings a person from darkness into faith. — Bursevī likens guidance from the spirit's sky to rain and says a servant cannot emerge from existential darkness until the heart is revived, just as a seed first receives rain underground. Biqāʿī describes emergence from ignorance into faith, wisdom and knowledge; Elmali mentions the Qur'an as a spirit; Study Quran reports the soul's death and life. [6 notes: BURSEVI; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; STUDYQURAN(some commentators)]
- 43:11/q18/p2 Lawful nourishment is another means by which the heart comes alive. — Bursevī says this directly. He recounts Uways al-Qaranī refusing a found dinar for fear it was stolen, then receiving food from a sheep as God's provision; he also reports that Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's wisdom and eloquence were attributed to the blessing of Umm Salama nursing him during his mother's absence. [3 notes: BURSEVI]

## 43:11/q19 How does this verse serve the surrounding argument and the sūrah?  [* · links]
- 43:11/q19/p1 The blessings of measured rain and revived land identify God's power and governance and answer polytheist denial of resurrection. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls these acts blessings and proof against every disbelieving polytheist. Nasafī says the divine descriptions cannot be polytheists' words since they denied resurrection. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the resurrection sentence a parenthesis within proofs of exclusive divinity, rebutting those who find resurrection impossible. Biqāʿī places the rain sign above the preceding signs of stable earth and roads. Study Quran says the sūrah challenges Makkan idolatrous belief and objections; Iṣlāḥī says all its parts relate to its central theme and form a harmonious unit. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; STUDYQURAN; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
