# 14:1: 42 questions

## 14:1/q01 What do the opening letters signify?  [الٓر · meaning]
turns on: Whether the letters name the sura or alphabet, encode divine declarations, or point to the Quran's composition and inimitability.
- 14:1/q01/p1 They are disjoined letter names serving as an opening or name of the sura or Quran. — Majaz calls them unvoweled letter names; al-Hasan reports companions using such openings as sura names and keys; al-Kashifi reports them as names of the Quran. Ibn Atiyya, Tabarsi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Ibn Kathir, Qurtubi, Asad, Islahi and Study Quran refer to earlier discussions; Kuran Yolu names them huruf al-muqatta'a. [12 notes: MAJAZ; JISHUMI(الحسن والأصم وأبو علي); BURSEVI(الكاشفي); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 14:1/q01/p2 They encode divine names or declarations. — Abu Salih and Ata give 'I am God, the Merciful'; Wahidi Wajiz and Ibn Abbas give 'I am God who sees'; Jishumi also reports derivation from God's names and attributes. [4 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو صالح وعطاء); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(ابن عباس,قيل)]
- 14:1/q01/p3 They demonstrate the Quran's inimitability using the Arabs' familiar letters. — Abu Muslim argues that people know the component letters yet cannot match the Quran. Bint al-Shati cites many opening sequences followed by declarations of revelation, Ibn Kathir and other earlier authorities; Rashid Rida repeats the argument. The pattern recurs here after five suras without letter openings, answering renewed objections. [5 notes: JISHUMI(أبو مسلم); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن كثير,محمد رشيد رضا)]
- 14:1/q01/p4 They are an oath affirming the revealed Book. — Qushayri gives this inward reading. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]
- 14:1/q01/p5 Their ultimate intended meaning is known to God. — Elmalili reads their letter shapes and recitation as suggesting unity and straightness, with alif assisting the other letters and lam and ra moving into multiplicity of address. [2 notes: ELMALILI]
- 14:1/q01/p6 They are the alphabetic material from which the Book is composed. — The author of al-Nazm treats the letters as a name for the alphabet's set; thus 'these letters are a Book sent down.' [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(صاحب النظم)]

## 14:1/q02 How are the opening letters and 'Book' construed grammatically?  [الٓر كِتَٰبٌ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the initials form a syntactic subject and whether an omitted demonstrative, qualifier, or verb is supplied.
- 14:1/q02/p1 'Book' predicates an omitted 'this is'; the revealed clause describes it. — Zajjaj, Wahidi, Ibn Atiyya and others supply hādhā. Majaz allows a new sentence or omitted pronoun and compares 2:1; Nasafi calls anzalnahu a nominative adjectival clause. Tabatabai describes the sentence as predicate of an omitted subject. [12 notes: MAJAZ; ZAJJAJ; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; TABATABAI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]
- 14:1/q02/p2 The letters are subject and 'Book' their predicate. — Ibn Atiyya says this follows treating the letters as alphabet names; Razi takes the letters as the sura's name; Jishumi offers this alongside the omitted-this construction. Zajjaj rejects it because the initials are only part of the whole Book. Abu Hayyan, Samin and Alusi list it. [6 notes: JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; ZAJJAJ(بعضهم)-]
- 14:1/q02/p3 'Book' is the subject, licensed by an implicit qualification, and the revelation clause its predicate. — Samin supplies 'a great book distinguished among heavenly books'; Abu Hayyan and Alusi similarly posit an understood adjective. Jishumi reports the subject-predicate parse. [5 notes: JISHUMI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]
- 14:1/q02/p4 Other parses make the letters predicate of an omitted subject or object of an implied command, or make 'Book' an omitted verb's object, second predicate, or enumeration title. — Abu Hayyan specifies an implied command to recite or hold to the letters; Alusi lists the Book alternatives. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 14:1/q03 What is the revealed Book here?  [كِتَٰبٌ · referent]
turns on: Whether 'Book' denotes the entire Quran or the sura at its opening.
- 14:1/q03/p1 It is the Quran. — Tabrisi and Jishumi say Gabriel brought it from God; Tabrisi excludes sorcery and poetry. Ibn Kathir calls it the noblest heavenly book sent to the noblest earthly messenger; Bursevi calls it proof of prophethood and compares Moses' signs in 14:5. Tabatabai calls it the sign of the Prophet's mission. [11 notes: TAB-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN; TABATABAI]
- 14:1/q03/p2 It is this sura. — Zamakhshari, Nasafi and Razi identify the specific sura as the Book denoted here. [3 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; RAZI-FULL]

## 14:1/q04 Why is 'Book' indefinite?  [كِتَٰبٌ · rhetoric]
- 14:1/q04/p1 Its indefiniteness magnifies the Book's greatness. — Biqai contrasts earlier descriptions as wise, firm, detailed, clear and true in 10:1, 11:1, 12:1 and 13:1; its greatness exceeds description. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 14:1/q05 How does 'We sent it down to you' attach and what does its wording convey?  [أَنزَلْنَٰهُ إِلَيْكَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the clause is descriptive, predicative, or explanatory.
- 14:1/q05/p1 The revelation clause qualifies 'Book'. — Nahas, Wahidi and Ibn Atiyya make it adjectival; Wahidi compares 'Zayd is a man I sent to you.' Razi and Nasafi agree; Alusi also reports predicate and explanatory parses. [6 notes: NAHHAS; WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 14:1/q05/p2 The clause may instead serve as a predicate or explain the preceding construction. — Alusi records both alternatives. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 14:1/q06 What does attributing the Book's descent to God imply about divine speech?  [أَنزَلْنَٰهُ · theology]
turns on: Whether descent is predicated of eternal divine speech itself or its conveyed meanings or letters.
- 14:1/q06/p1 What descends is conveyed meaning or originated letters, not God's eternal attribute itself. — Al-Qadi Abu al-Tayyib and Abu al-Ma'ali distinguish conveyed meanings from eternal speech; Razi answers the Mu'tazilites that Quranic letters are originated, which no one disputes. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(القاضي ابن الطيب وأبو المعالي وغيرهما); RAZI-FULL+]
- 14:1/q06/p2 The described descent shows the thing sent down cannot be eternal. — Razi reports this Mu'tazilite argument and replies that the originated letters are the thing described. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(المعتزلة); RAZI-FULL-]

## 14:1/q07 How does the wording honor the Prophet?  [أَنزَلْنَٰهُ إِلَيْكَ لِتُخْرِجَ · rhetoric]
- 14:1/q07/p1 The majestic plural, direct address, and attribution of bringing people out honor his role. — Abu Hayyan and Alusi highlight the plural and direct address; Ibn Atiyya says the honor lies in naming his invitation and warning although God creates guidance. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 14:1/q07/p2 Muhammad's favor over angels includes a promise of forgiveness. — Ibn Abbas compares the threat against an angel claiming divinity in 21:29 with the Prophet's forgiveness in 48:2. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]

## 14:1/q08 What does the lām of 'so that you bring out' express?  [لِتُخْرِجَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the clause states the intended end of revelation or a consequence that actually occurs to all.
- 14:1/q08/p1 It marks the purpose of sending the Book. — Nahas paraphrases 'so that people may come out'; Wahidi, Samin and Alusi attach it to anzalna. Tabrisi and Tabatabai say consequence would falsely imply that all became believers; Alusi rejects an attributed consequence reading, distinguishing universal purpose from each person's acceptance. [8 notes: NAHHAS; WAHIDI-BASIT; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL(الطبرسي)+; TABATABAI+; TABRISI+]
- 14:1/q08/p2 It is a lām of resulting consequence. — Alusi attributes this to al-Tabrisi but rejects it; Tabrisi's own note explicitly argues for purpose, and Tabatabai rejects consequence because not everyone believes. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الطبرسي); TABRISI-; TABATABAI-]

## 14:1/q09 Does the purpose clause imply that divine acts have purposes?  [لِتُخْرِجَ · theology]
turns on: Whether benefit governs or compels divine action, or divine purposes are affirmed as benefits for creatures.
- 14:1/q09/p1 God's acts and commands have beneficial purposes for creatures. — Razi reports Mu'tazilite inference from the lām; Ibn al-Qayyim and others cite over ten thousand apparent scriptural examples and call it the Salaf view, which Alusi finds persuasive. Ibn Ashur says commands serve graded wisdom and interests. Tabatabai says creaturely benefit is inferred from God's action but does not complete or compel Him. [10 notes: RAZI-FULL(المعتزلة); ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن القيم وغيره)+; TABATABAI+]
- 14:1/q09/p2 Apparent causal purposes must be reinterpreted because God cannot need an intermediary to achieve an end. — Razi presents his school's argument that acting for a further purpose implies inability without a means. Alusi reports the argument and questions its force. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL(أصحابنا); ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); ALUSI-FULL(ابن القيم وغيره)-]
- 14:1/q09/p3 Both compulsory welfare and purposeless arbitrariness are false extremes. — Tabatabai says benefits neither rule God's essence nor force His action, while His acts are purposeful; he reports the familiar dispute without assigning its formulations to particular schools. [2 notes: TABATABAI(الأشاعرة والمعتزلة); TABATABAI+]

## 14:1/q10 What is the variant reading of the purpose verb?  [لِتُخْرِجَ ٱلنَّاسَ · readings]
- 14:1/q10/p1 A reported reading has third-person yāʾ, li-yukhrij, with al-nās nominative as subject. — Kashshaf reports li-yukhrija al-nas; Samin and Abu Hayyan specify the nominative subject. Alusi describes it as 'the people may be brought out.' [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]

## 14:1/q11 Who are 'the people'?  [ٱلنَّاسَ · referent]
turns on: Whether the noun's unrestricted wording and universal mission or the immediate audience and preparedness determine its scope.
- 14:1/q11/p1 All humanity. — Jishumi prefers the universal scope because the Messenger was sent to all; Ibn Atiyya cites Quran and mass-transmitted reports, the Companions' witnessed invitation, and red and black peoples. Tabatabai cites the lack of restrictive wording and 25:1, 6:19, 7:158, 14:52. Ibn Kathir includes Arabs and non-Arabs; Asad contrasts earlier scripture for particular peoples. [12 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; JISHUMI(ابن عباس)+]
- 14:1/q11/p2 The Meccans. — Jishumi reports this from Ibn Abbas, but prefers all people on grounds of the Prophet's universal mission. [1 notes: JISHUMI(ابن عباس)]
- 14:1/q11/p3 Only people prepared for guidance attain its outcome. — Tabatabai reports an objection that universal grace reaches perfection only where readiness exists; he rejects limiting the noun, calling the clause a legislative aim. [1 notes: TABATABAI(بعضهم); TABATABAI-]

## 14:1/q12 What does the Prophet do in bringing people out?  [لِتُخْرِجَ ٱلنَّاسَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the Messenger's guidance is an indispensable source of knowledge, an alert to proof, or a mediated call under God's guidance.
- 14:1/q12/p1 He guides instrumentally by reciting, calling, explaining, and warning; God grants or creates the guidance. — Tabari distinguishes divine granting of faith from prophetic explanation; Jishumi calls the attribution figurative. Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi name invitation and warning; Tabatabai reconciles 28:56 with 42:52 as original versus mediated guidance. Islahi restricts his responsibility to delivery, with God deciding acceptance. [19 notes: TAB-FULL; JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(bir anlayış); STUDYQURAN]
- 14:1/q12/p2 Prophetic or imamic instruction is indispensable to attaining knowledge of God. — Razi and Alusi report this inference from the Messenger's role in moving people from unbelief to faith. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL(القائلون); ALUSI-FULL; RAZI-FULL-]
- 14:1/q12/p3 The Messenger alerts people, while they gain knowledge through proof. — Razi answers the instruction argument; Alusi repeats both claim and response. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL; RAZI-FULL+]

## 14:1/q13 What does bringing people from darkness into light mean?  [ٱلظُّلُمَٰتِ ٱلنُّورِ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the pair denotes core unbelief and faith or more specific kinds of ignorance, doubt, innovation, and conduct.
- 14:1/q13/p1 It is a move from unbelief, idolatry, or error to faith and guidance. — Qatada, Mujahid and others use error/guidance; Ibn Abbas and Muqatil use shirk or unbelief/faith. Abu Ali calls unbelief extreme confusion and faith illumination. Ibn Kathir cites 2:257 and 57:9; Study Quran notes parallel imagery in 14:5 and 65:11, and God as subject elsewhere. Islahi extends it to faith and righteous deeds; Bursevi includes hypocrisy, insincerity, doubt and innovation. [43 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(قتادة); ZAJJAJ; SULAMI(أبو عثمان); THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; DAMGHANI; JISHUMI(أبو علي,قتادة); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(العوفي عن ابن عباس,مجاهد وقتادة); RAZI-FULL(أبو مسلم الأصفهاني); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; DURR-FULL(قتادة); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN; BURHAN]
- 14:1/q13/p2 It is a move from doubt or conjecture to certainty or reality. — Mawardi, Ibn al-Jawzi and Qushayri give doubt/certainty; Abu Bakr and Alusi give conjecture/reality. Qurtubi treats this as close to the main sense. [6 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; SULAMI(أبو بكر); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الماوردي); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(أبو بكر بن طاهر)]
- 14:1/q13/p3 It is a move from innovation to Sunna or following revelation. — Ja'far, Mawardi, Qushayri and Qurtubi offer this as a parallel movement; Bursevi includes it in a longer list. [6 notes: SULAMI(جعفر); MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(جعفر)]
- 14:1/q13/p4 It is a move from ignorance to knowledge. — Tabari says the Quran shows the ignorant right guidance; Qushayri and Qurtubi supply this transition. Kuran Yolu pairs ignorance and false belief with knowledge and faith. [4 notes: QUSHAYRI; THALABI; QURTUBI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 14:1/q13/p5 It is a move from evil beliefs and deeds to true faith and righteous action. — Islahi broadens darkness to faith and deeds; Bursevi includes Quranic true beliefs and beneficial rulings. [3 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BURSEVI]

## 14:1/q14 Why are darkness and light used as images?  [ٱلظُّلُمَٰتِ ٱلنُّورِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the figures are separate transfers for darkness, light and exit or one composed rescue scene.
- 14:1/q14/p1 Confusion and obscurity resemble darkness; manifest faith and guidance resemble light. — Zajjaj and Abu Ishaq give the comparison; Razi calls kufr the farthest confusion and faith the clearest guidance. Biqai compares light protecting reason to physical light protecting sight on a road, citing 6:153. Ibn Ashur imagines a bewildered person emerging into brightness. Burhan says Quranic uses of both images are metaphorical; Itqan classifies this as an original metaphor with a borrowed generic noun. [10 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURHAN; ITQAN; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 14:1/q14/p2 The whole scene is a composite metaphor of rescue by a royal writ. — Al-Tibi imagines a lost person in a dark wilderness rescued with a royal writ securing help; the Messenger and Book guide by divine favor. Alusi finds it eloquent but somewhat remote. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الطيبي); ALUSI-FULL-]
- 14:1/q14/p3 'Bringing out' itself pictures a move from one state as leaving one place. — Ibn Ashur analyzes the transition and causation metaphors. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 14:1/q15 Why are darknesses plural and light singular?  [ٱلظُّلُمَٰتِ ٱلنُّورِ · rhetoric]
- 14:1/q15/p1 Error and false worship have many paths, while truth and guidance have one. — Razi, Tabatabai and Islahi contrast divergent desires and many paths with one truth; Alusi specifies multiple alleged deities versus tawhid. Ibn Ashur refers to his explanation at 6:1. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 14:1/q16 What inward movements do darkness and light depict?  [ٱلظُّلُمَٰتِ ٱلنُّورِ · inward]
turns on: Which inward states the spatial imagery represents.
- 14:1/q16/p1 From multiplicity and created nature through veils to unity and the divine essence. — Qashani gives three paired readings; Alusi repeats them; Bursevi describes bodily and spirit worlds and seventy thousand veils of light and darkness, with humans uniquely capable of passing through them by God's guardianship and the Prophet and Quran as means, citing 2:257. Qashani relates the divine names to each stage. [9 notes: QASHANI; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 14:1/q16/p2 From the darkness of the self to the light of the heart. — Ja'far pairs self/heart with disbelief/faith and innovation/Sunna; Qushayri moves from ego claims to heart gnoses. [3 notes: SULAMI(جعفر); QUSHAYRI; ALUSI-FULL(جعفر)]
- 14:1/q16/p3 From seeing one's own acts to seeing divine grace. — Abu Hafs gives this inward contrast. [1 notes: SULAMI(أبو حفص)]
- 14:1/q16/p4 From separation to unitive gathering. — Qushayri gives this as another movement. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]
- 14:1/q16/p5 From worldly planning to witnessing divine decree. — Qushayri contrasts deliberate worldly management with spacious witnessing. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]

## 14:1/q17 What does 'by their Lord's permission' mean?  [بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِمْ · meaning]
turns on: Whether permission names legal authorization, foreknowledge, gracious facilitation, or God's effective decree and creation.
- 14:1/q17/p1 By God's command or authorization to teach and carry out the mission. — Nahas calls idhn figurative command; Dahhak, Muqatil and Abu Ali give command. Zajjaj and Abu Ishaq attach it to bringing out as authorization to teach. Ibn Ashur says it is a command with the commander's consent and compares 4:64; Kuran Yolu adds God's will. [14 notes: MUQATIL; ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS; MAWARDI-FULL(الضحاك); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); JISHUMI(أبو علي); BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج,مقاتل); ALUSI-FULL(محيي السنة); IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 14:1/q17/p2 By God's enabling, grace, facilitation, and granting success. — Tabari, Tha'labi and Qurtubi stress gracious care; Zamakhshari derives a metaphor from permission as removal of a barrier, followed by Baydawi, Nasafi and Alusi. Biqai cites al-Rummani's original sense of release through audible speech. Alusi says the ease includes a turn toward truth (13:27) and finds Razi's rejection of the other glosses difficult. [14 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI; JISHUMI(قيل); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو سليمان); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); BIQAI-FULL(الرماني); ALUSI-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 14:1/q17/p3 By God's will, decree, and effective creation of guidance. — Ibn Abbas says no one is guided except by His decree and will; Qushayri adds choice and prior decree. Razi argues command and knowledge do not cause transition, while effective motive must originate in God. Bursevi reports God's power because the Prophet cannot guide independently; Islahi says God gives the deserving an urge for guidance. [15 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; QUSHAYRI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام,محيي السنة); ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(bir anlayış); RAZI-FULL+]
- 14:1/q17/p4 By God's knowledge. — Mawardi, al-Asamm and Baghawi report it; al-Asamm says God sent the Book with knowledge no one else could produce. Ibn Atiyya combines knowledge with decree and enabling. [5 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI(الأصم); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(محيي السنة)]
- 14:1/q17/p5 By readiness God gives and causes He prepares for the light to become actual. — Qashani describes the most holy effusion; Alusi repeats this inward reading. [2 notes: QASHANI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 14:1/q18 How does 'by their Lord's permission' attach grammatically?  [بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِمْ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the phrase modifies the act or serves as a circumstantial qualification of agent or people.
- 14:1/q18/p1 The prepositional phrase attaches to 'you bring out'. — Zajjaj and Abu Ishaq explain that the Prophet teaches by permission; Qurtubi and Abu Hayyan call this the apparent syntax; Ibn Ashur says the bāʾ is causal. [8 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 14:1/q18/p2 It is circumstantial, describing the authorized agent or the people brought out. — Abu al-Baqa, via Abu Hayyan, supplies the Prophet as authorized; Baydawi, Samin and Alusi allow the subject, and Baydawi and Alusi also the object. [4 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]

## 14:1/q19 Why say 'their Lord' here?  [رَبِّهِمْ · rhetoric]
- 14:1/q19/p1 It emphasizes God's nurture and beneficence toward the people guided. — Abu Hayyan points to stewardship; al-Tibi explains the shift from expected 'Our Lord' as pure favor; Alusi says nurture brings people toward perfection; Ibn Ashur says the sending serves people's interests. Bursevi reports that the phrase assigns the nurturing act to God rather than the Prophet. [5 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الطيبي); IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 14:1/q20 How does 'to the path' relate to 'to the light'?  [إِلَى ٱلنُّورِ إِلَىٰ صِرَٰطِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the second destination is syntactic apposition or a new answer to an implied question.
- 14:1/q20/p1 It is an appositive or replacement clarifying the light, with ilā repeated. — Ibn al-Anbari says the repeated preposition links the two without conjunction and magnifies the destination, with a poet's repeated 'I called' and his Arabic example. Kashshaf and Razi compare 7:75; Ibn Ashur compares 7:88. Abu Hayyan says the intervening permission phrase poses no obstacle; Alusi says figurative apposition preserves the metaphor. [17 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن الأنباري); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن الأنباري,الشاعر); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الإرشاد); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 14:1/q20/p2 It begins a fresh explanation answering 'to what light?' — Abu Ishaq makes the path clarify light; Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi and later grammarians allow a new elliptical sentence. [8 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]

## 14:1/q21 What is the path?  [صِرَٰطِ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the path is identified as Islam generally, the light's explanatory image, or specific religious leadership.
- 14:1/q21/p1 It is Islam, God's religion or obedience and faith leading to His mercy. — Muqatil and Baghawi call it religion; Jishumi and Bursevi say Islam, the latter adding Paradise, nearness and union. Ibn Atiyya describes the way to obedience, faith and mercy. Study Quran also reports the path as Islam or as the previously named light. [10 notes: MUQATIL; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن الأنباري); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; BURSEVI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN(some)]
- 14:1/q21/p2 It is the light or guidance just mentioned. — Wahidi Wajiz identifies the path as the light; Abu Ishaq says it clarifies that light; Biqai calls it the light clarified as a secure path. [5 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,ابن الأنباري); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BIQAI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(some)]
- 14:1/q21/p3 It is leadership of the Twelve Imams. — Study Quran reports this interpretation from al-Qummi. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(al-Qummī)]

## 14:1/q22 Why describe guidance as God's path?  [صِرَٰطِ ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلْحَمِيدِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the genitive points to the path's divine destination, God's disclosure of it, or its inward content.
- 14:1/q22/p1 The path is attributed to God because He is its destination or makes it clear. — Baydawi and Alusi give these alternatives; Ibn Ashur says path is an apt image for true religion within the larger darkness-to-light scene. Razi calls it exalted and straight because of its divine owner. [5 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 14:1/q22/p2 It points to divine mercy, oneness, and signs of uniqueness. — Qushayri reads the path inwardly as mercy and tawhid; his knowers' hearts are illumined and lovers' hearts burn and long for the Mighty. [4 notes: QUSHAYRI(أصحاب الوصول)]

## 14:1/q23 What does 'the Mighty' mean?  [ٱلْعَزِيزِ · meaning]
- 14:1/q23/p1 God is unconquerable, invulnerable, and sovereign in power. — Muqatil, Jishumi, Tabrisi and Ibn Kathir stress dominion and inability to be overcome. Qurtubi adds no equal or likeness; Nasafi specifies prevailing in retribution; Tabatabai says He is free of need. [15 notes: MUQATIL; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); ELMALILI; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]

## 14:1/q24 What does 'the Praiseworthy' mean?  [ٱلْحَمِيدِ · meaning]
turns on: Whether hamid directly names universal praiseworthiness or also yields knowledge and independence as its gloss.
- 14:1/q24/p1 God deserves or receives praise in every state for His acts, blessings, commands, and perfection. — Muqatil cites creatures' praise for His command; Jishumi distinguishes hamadtu 'I approved' and ahmadtu 'I found praiseworthy'; Ibn Kathir includes acts, words, laws, commands, prohibitions and truthful reports. Alusi says God praises Himself eternally and servants praise Him forever; Biqai includes divine perfection and nurturing blessings. [16 notes: MUQATIL; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 14:1/q24/p2 It implies God is knowledgeable and independent, alongside being praised in every act. — Razi reports a Mu'tazilite argument that right action requires full power, knowledge and freedom from need; he says the names entail these. Alusi objects that hamid is not elsewhere glossed specifically as knowledgeable and self-sufficient. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL(المعتزلة); ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); ALUSI-FULL-]

## 14:1/q25 Why are 'the Mighty' and 'the Praiseworthy' chosen and ordered?  [ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلْحَمِيدِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the order follows the verse's two acts, the traveler's encouragement, or a conceptual order among attributes.
- 14:1/q25/p1 Might suits revelation and overcoming opponents; praise suits the gift of guidance. — Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and Alusi map the two names to the preceding acts in that order; Ibn Ashur says the unconquerable God's Book proves His purpose and overcomes opponents. Tabatabai says the names call people to accept His blessing and fear punishment; Islahi says only He merits fear and hope. [7 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 14:1/q25/p2 The paired names assure and encourage the path's traveler. — Baydawi says the traveler will not be humiliated or disappointed; Elmalili says refuge in God brings honor by might and bounty; Alusi notes the difficult path and praise of those who walk it. [3 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 14:1/q25/p3 Power is mentioned first because knowledge of power precedes knowledge of divine knowledge and independence. — Alusi reports Razi's explanation but questions his sense of hamid and his ordering argument. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); ALUSI-FULL-]
- 14:1/q25/p4 Priority may go to negative attributes, like purification before adornment. — Alusi reports this suggestion with 42:11. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]

## 14:1/q26 How is 'the Praiseworthy' inflected with 'the Mighty'?  [ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلْحَمِيدِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the second epithet continues the genitive adjective chain or opens an independent clause.
- 14:1/q26/p1 Al-Hamid is genitive as a second adjective of al-Aziz's referent. — Zajjaj and Jishumi state this; Qurtubi compares multiple adjectives without a conjunction, as in 'Zayd the wise, the noble.' [3 notes: ZAJJAJ; JISHUMI; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 14:1/q26/p2 It can be nominative as a new subject with Allah its predicate. — Zajjaj and Jishumi allow this alongside the genitive parse; Zajjaj notes further subject-predicate inflections. [2 notes: ZAJJAJ; JISHUMI]

## 14:1/q27 How is the following divine name 'Allah' read and parsed?  [ٱلْحَمِيدِ · readings]
turns on: Whether the divine name continues the genitive construction across the verse boundary or starts a nominative sentence.
- 14:1/q27/p1 Allah is nominative, opening a new clause or predicating an omitted 'he'. — Ibn Mujahid names Nafi and Ibn Amir; Jishumi and Ibn Ashur add Abu Jafar. Thalabi calls them Medinan and Syrian readers and says the following alladhi is predicate; Ibn Ashur says Allah predicates an omitted 'he' and cites al-Sakkaki and poetry by Ibrahim al-Suli for ellipsis. Kashshaf gives 'He is God'; Yaqub raises it on pause. [12 notes: IBNMUJAHID(ابن كثير، أبو عمرو، عاصم، حمزة، الكسائي); IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; FARISI-HUJJA(نافع وابن عامر); THALABI(أهل المدينة والشام,يعقوب بن إسحاق الحضرمي); JISHUMI(أبو جعفر ونافع وابن عامر); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL(نافع، وابن عامر، وأبو جعفر); QIRAAT-ER(some readers)]
- 14:1/q27/p2 Allah is genitive, appositional to the preceding epithet or linked as an adjective. — Ibn Mujahid assigns this to Ibn Kathir, Abu Amr, Asim, Hamza and Kisai; Farisi also notes Hafs agreeing with Abu Amr and a rare al-Asma'i report from Nafi. Ibn Khalawayh and Wahidi distinguish apposition from adjective because Allah became a proper name; Wahidi invokes al-Nabigha's name in poetry. Abu Amr explains fronting as 'path of God, the Mighty, Praiseworthy.' Yaqub uses this in continuous reading. [16 notes: IBNMUJAHID(ابن كثير، أبو عمرو، عاصم، حمزة، الكسائي,نافع عن الأصمعي); IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; FARISI-HUJJA(ابن كثير وأبو عمرو وعاصم وحمزة والكسائي); THALABI(أبو عمر,الآخرون,يعقوب بن إسحاق الحضرمي); JISHUMI(أبو عمرو,الباقون); WAHIDI-BASIT(الشاعر); QIRAAT-ER(some readers)]

## 14:1/q28 Where is the stop after 'the Praiseworthy'?  [ٱلْحَمِيدِ · readings]
- 14:1/q28/p1 It is complete with nominative Allah and merely good or adequate with genitive Allah. — Itqan says complete versus good; Nashr notes that a stop's status changes with the reading, comparing another case in wa-ttakhidhu. Ibn Khalawayh and Thalabi connect nominative with a new sentence or Yaqub's pause. Ibn Ashur says both readings identify Allah, with nominative more emphatic. [5 notes: ITQAN; NASHR; IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; THALABI(يعقوب بن إسحاق الحضرمي); IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 14:1/q29 How is ṣirāṭ pronounced?  [صِرَٰطِ · readings]
turns on: Which transmitted phonetic realization of the first consonant is followed.
- 14:1/q29/p1 Some pronounce its ṣād as sīn. — Qiraat-Er links this pronunciation to a possible Syriac strata etymology without changing the meaning. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER]
- 14:1/q29/p2 A canonical ishmām gives ṣād a slight zāy quality. — Qiraat-Er describes a midpoint between ṣād and sīn. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER]

## 14:1/q30 How are the opening letters paused or pronounced?  [الٓر · readings]
- 14:1/q30/p1 Each letter is pronounced separately and paused with sukūn. — Qiraat-Er describes the waqf reading's distinct units; Majaz says they are unvoweled names. [2 notes: QIRAAT-ER; MAJAZ]

## 14:1/q31 Where and when was the sura revealed, and how many verses has it?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether the Badr-related passage is a Medinan exception to an otherwise Meccan sura.
- 14:1/q31/p1 It is Meccan, with two verses, 14:28–29, excepted as Medinan. — Zajjaj and Ibn Abbas report the exception; Durr and Tabatabai connect those verses to slain Badr polytheists. Abu Hayyan reports Ibn Abbas and Qatada. Study Quran says this is a commonly reported exception. [5 notes: ZAJJAJ; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); TABATABAI(ابن عباس والحسن وقتادة); STUDYQURAN(My)]
- 14:1/q31/p2 The whole sura is Meccan. — Abu Hayyan calls this the majority view; Ibn Abbas and al-Zubayr report Meccan revelation. Asad places it among late Meccan revelations immediately after sura 71. Razi warns the Meccan/Medinan classification rests on solitary reports and matters chiefly for possible abrogation. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); ASAD-NOTES]
- 14:1/q31/p3 The sura has fifty-two verses. — Razi, Baydawi, Biqai and Durr give this count. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL]

## 14:1/q32 What occasion of revelation is reported for this verse?  [* · reports]
- 14:1/q32/p1 The verse concerns opposing responses to Jesus and then Muhammad. — Ibn Abbas via Miqsam says former rejecters of Jesus believed Muhammad while former believers in Jesus rejected him; Mawardi and Qurtubi preserve the report. [2 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(مقسم عن ابن عباس)]

## 14:1/q33 How does the opening link to nearby suras and verses?  [* · links]
turns on: Which neighbouring textual pattern is being used to explain the opening.
- 14:1/q33/p1 It continues al-Ra'd's account of the Book and guidance. — Abu Hayyan cites 13:31, 13:37 and 13:43 on Quran, Arabic judgment and a witness with knowledge of the Book; after 13:27's request for a sign, this Book suffices as a sign. Biqai links the closing witness of 13:43 to the Quran's eloquence and knowledge. Islahi says 13:41's indirect warning and promise become explicit here. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 14:1/q33/p2 The repeated language of bringing people from darkness to light connects prophetic missions. — Abu Muslim compares Muhammad and Moses at 14:5; Study Quran cites 14:5, 65:11 and passages where God is subject (2:257, 5:16, 33:43, 57:9). Asad says revelation comes in each people's language, with Quran addressed to all humanity. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL(أبو مسلم الأصفهاني); STUDYQURAN; ASAD-NOTES]
- 14:1/q33/p3 The opening and conclusion frame one argument about God's oneness and universal warning. — Tabatabai says 14:52 closes the opening's claim, and heaven and earth show all blessing is God's; Ibn Ashur says the path of the owner of all things rebukes idolatrous paths and leads into threatened punishment. [3 notes: TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 14:1/q34 What is the sura's governing purpose and argument?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the thematic focus is monotheism and gratitude, Quraysh's warning, or revelation's proof and reach.
- 14:1/q34/p1 It presents the Quran as the Prophet's enduring sign and universal guide. — Burhan stresses the continuing miracle and the study of inimitability; Tabatabai names it the sign of mission; Asad contrasts former scriptures' local addressees with this universal Quran. Elmalili anticipates an objection to a universal messenger receiving an Arabic Book; Biqai says Arabic clarity and proof make the path unmistakable. [6 notes: BURHAN; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; ELMALILI; BIQAI-FULL]
- 14:1/q34/p2 It affirms God's oneness and shows revelation guiding people to His path. — Biqai calls Abraham's story its clearest evidence and links the Book to Abraham's prayer for a descendant messenger in 2:129. Tabatabai adds divine lordship, might and praiseworthiness, urging fear, thanks, trust and reflection. Study Quran stresses gratitude versus ingratitude across 14:5, 7, 8, 28, 32–34, 37. [4 notes: BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]
- 14:1/q34/p3 It warns Quraysh and promises the believers victory. — Islahi compares the false message to a rootless foul tree, Islam to a lasting tree, and Moses' and Abraham's stories to Quraysh's history and future. Abraham's migration and prayer for Arabia reinforce the argument. [3 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 14:1/q35 What does the opening report about the revelation itself?  [كِتَٰبٌ أَنزَلْنَٰهُ · inward]
- 14:1/q35/p1 It is a special covenant recounting earlier peoples' destruction and Muhammad's community's salvation. — Sulami reports Ja'far's inward account. [1 notes: SULAMI(جعفر)]

## 14:1/q36 What does 'the path of the Mighty, the Praiseworthy' imply for idolatry?  [صِرَٰطِ ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلْحَمِيدِ · interpretation]
- 14:1/q36/p1 Only the all-owning God's path reaches its destination; idolaters' alternatives fail. — Ibn Ashur says the next relative clause magnifies the known owner of heaven and earth and rebukes deficient idols; the discourse then threatens associators. Kuran Yolu says God guides seekers and leaves those choosing created beings wandering under warning. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 14:1/q37 How are the disconnected letters linked to other Quranic openings?  [الٓر كِتَٰبٌ · links]
- 14:1/q37/p1 Such openings are regularly followed by mention of the Book, verses, or revelation. — Kuran Yolu notes the pattern; Study Quran says Ibrahim is the fifth of six alif-lam-ra suras speaking of the Book; Bint al-Shati cites several opening pairs and says this one renews an answer to polytheist objections. [4 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN; BINTSHATI-IJAZ]

## 14:1/q38 What dispute over human acts does the guidance clause raise?  [لِتُخْرِجَ ٱلنَّاسَ مِنَ ٱلظُّلُمَٰتِ إِلَى ٱلنُّورِ بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِمْ · theology]
turns on: Whether the guidance effect requires created divine causation or human choice and reasoning.
- 14:1/q38/p1 God creates and governs guidance, while people acquire acts and the Messenger calls. — Tabari presents this affirmationist account against the Qadariyya. Razi says a decisive motive must ultimately come from God; he rejects an appeal to divine kindness that either has no causal effect or necessitates the result. Abu Hayyan sees a Mu'tazilite implication in Zamakhshari's enabling gloss; Alusi reports both Sunni and Mu'tazilite use of the verse. [6 notes: TAB-FULL; RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; RAZI-FULL+; JISHUMI-; RAZI-FULL(القاضي)-]
- 14:1/q38/p2 The verse presupposes people's ability to reason and choose faith. — Jishumi argues that if God created belief and unbelief, scripture and the Messenger could not bring a person out of unbelief. Al-Qadi argues the same and says reading the Book leads people to infer God's knowledge, power and wisdom, recognize its miracle and the Messenger's truth, then freely accept its laws. Alusi reports the two schools' competing use of the verse. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL(القاضي); ALUSI-FULL; JISHUMI+]

## 14:1/q39 How many metaphors does the enabling interpretation create?  [ٱلظُّلُمَٰتِ ٱلنُّورِ بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِمْ · rhetoric]
- 14:1/q39/p1 Three: permission, darkness, and light. — Alusi counts the permission-as-enabling image alongside the paired darkness and light images. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 14:1/q40 How do the epithets identify God at the verse boundary?  [ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلْحَمِيدِ · referent]
- 14:1/q40/p1 They function almost like a proper name for the uniquely worship-worthy God. — Zamakhshari says the next description of those who fear God and obey His command clarifies whom the epithets denote. [1 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL]

## 14:1/q41 What does the basmala frame at the sura opening?  [* · links]
- 14:1/q41/p1 It frames the clear Book as mercy from the uniquely perfect God. — Biqai distinguishes God's mercy to all creation through the Book from special grace to chosen servants. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 14:1/q42 What adjacent lexical gloss accompanies these notes?  [* · meaning]
- 14:1/q42/p1 Istiḥbāb means seeking to gain love. — Tabrisi distinguishes love as willing benefit or as inclination, desire and appetite, and says bughya and ibtighāʾ mean seeking. [1 notes: TABRISI]
