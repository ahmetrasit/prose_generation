# 50:11: 23 questions

## 50:11/q01 What is the provision mentioned here?  [رِّزْقًۭا · referent]
- 50:11/q01/p1 The rain-grown gardens, grain, palm fruit, and other plants provide sustenance for God's servants. — Muqatil says all the growth is provision. Mawardi includes blessed rain, plants, harvested grain, and clustered palm fruit; Study Quran likewise includes water. Abu Hayyan distinguishes recurring produce, one- or two-year crops, and mixed kinds, noting crops are mostly food and fruits mostly treats. Ibn Kathir extends the beneficiaries to all creatures. Tabatabai defines sustenance as what maintains life. Qurtubi defines provision as what is made ready for use. Tabrisi says all provision comes from God's direct action or causation, though ordinary speech may attribute provision to people. [15 notes: MUQATIL; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN]

## 50:11/q02 How is rizqan construed grammatically?  [رِّزْقًۭا · grammar]
turns on: Whether the accusative states the purpose of growth, names the provision constituted by growth or an implied providing verb, or describes a circumstance.
- 50:11/q02/p1 It is an accusative of purpose: the plants were grown to provide sustenance. — Abu Ishaq, reported by Zajjaj, Nahhas, and Wahidi, gives this as an alternative. Kashshaf, Razi, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Nasafi, and Samin also give it. Alusi chooses it and says it modifies the preceding 'We caused to grow'; Ibn Ashur calls it an infinitive expressing God's purpose to sustain people, and Tabatabai applies it to gardens, grain, and palms. [12 notes: ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 50:11/q02/p2 It is a cognate or substitute verbal noun: growing these plants itself constitutes provision. — Abu Ishaq supplies an implied 'We provided them provision,' according to Zajjaj and Wahidi; Nahhas calls it a verbal noun. Kashshaf says growing entails providing. Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, and Samin tie the noun to 'We caused to grow,' though it is not formed from that verb; Alusi compares 'I sat a sitting' (qaʿadtu julūsan). Ibn Atiyya simply calls it an accusative verbal noun. Qurtubi allows either an implied 'We provided' or a complement of the planting. [12 notes: ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL]
- 50:11/q02/p3 It is a circumstantial accusative, meaning 'provided for' or 'possessing provision.' — Samin reports both formulations; Alusi reports the first as an alternative. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]

## 50:11/q03 How does li-l-ʿibād attach to the construction?  [لِّلْعِبَادِ · grammar]
turns on: The syntactic role of the prepositional phrase in relation to rizqan.
- 50:11/q03/p1 It qualifies rizqan as an adjective. — Samin lists this attachment. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]
- 50:11/q03/p2 It attaches to the verbal noun rizqan. — Samin lists this attachment. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]
- 50:11/q03/p3 It is the verbal noun's object, with an additional lām. — Samin lists this analysis. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 50:11/q04 Who are the servants?  [ٱلْعِبَادِ · referent]
- 50:11/q04/p1 They are people described as God's servants. — Ibn Ashur distinguishes ʿibād from ʿabīd, the plural used for a human slave, and says the wording is both an argument and a favor. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 50:11/q05 Why is provision mentioned at this point?  [رِّزْقًۭا لِّلْعِبَادِ · rhetoric]
turns on: The notes give distinct purposes for naming provision: benefit, universal reach, priority of reflection, rebuke of denial, provision after resurrection, or reassurance.
- 50:11/q05/p1 It names the benefit and favor of the plants God created. — Razi says fruit's benefit is obvious, while the sky's provision is less obvious though decreed by God and accompanied by other benefits. Ibn Ashur says the phrase argues for God's power and reminds people of favor; revived land also supplies pasture to livestock and wild animals. Tabatabai says the plants' elaborate provision displays planning, knowledge, and power. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 50:11/q05/p2 Provision reaches everyone, although the preceding sign explicitly benefits the penitent. — Razi contrasts the penitent servant of 50:8, who eats gratefully and remembers God, with others who eat like livestock. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 50:11/q05/p3 Provision after the signs for reflection teaches that contemplation takes priority over eating. — Bursevi cites a Persian couplet: eat to live and remember God rather than live to eat. He says the previous verse's great bodies chiefly displayed power, with growth secondary, fitting reflection and reminder; this verse then states their benefits. Alusi agrees that reflection precedes and outweighs bodily enjoyment. [4 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 50:11/q05/p4 It stresses God's beneficence, making the deniers' rejection of their benefactor worse. — Razi presents this as a third explanation. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 50:11/q05/p5 It points to continuing sustenance after resurrection, alongside the earlier proof of creation and resurrection. — Razi says the heavens and earth point to creation and resurrection, while fruits point to sustenance afterward; the water-and-growth discussion starts after those proofs (50:8–9). [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 50:11/q05/p6 It encourages the person turning to God who fears rejection. — Biqai says human nature can pull down what reason elevates, so the passage reassures such a person by describing provision and revival. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 50:11/q06 What does bihi refer to?  [بِهِۦ · referent]
- 50:11/q06/p1 It refers to the rain or water just mentioned. — Baghawi, Ibn Atiyya, Samin, and Alusi identify the antecedent as rain or water. [4 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]

## 50:11/q07 How is 'We revived' connected to what precedes?  [وَأَحْيَيْنَا · grammar]
turns on: Whether the coordination reaches back to the previous explicit verb or to the verbal sense of rizqan.
- 50:11/q07/p1 It is coordinated with 'We caused to grow by it' in the previous verse. — Razi identifies the preceding verbal clause in 50:9 as its syntactic partner. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 50:11/q07/p2 It is coordinated with the derived noun rizqan, whose verbal sense is 'We provided.' — Ibn Ashur construes the sense as 'God provided people and revived land.' [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 50:11/q08 What is the dead town and what does its revival mean?  [بَلْدَةًۭ مَّيْتًۭا · meaning]
- 50:11/q08/p1 It is a barren tract of land whose vegetation has died or ceased; rain makes it grow again. — Muqatil, Nahhas, Tabrisi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Ibn Kathir, Bursevi, Alusi, and Elmalılı describe absence of crops, drought, or dried vegetation; Ibn Kathir and Bursevi add swelling, stirring, flowers, and greenery. Ibn Ashur defines balda as a tract or section of land and explains that drought is likened to death because life signs disappear; cultivation and watering are idiomatically called reviving dead land. Study Quran cites 16:65, 2:164, 41:39, 45:5, and 57:17 for rain's revival of land. Ibn Jawzi and Baghawi identify rain as the means. [16 notes: MUQATIL; NAHHAS; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; BAGHAWI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN]

## 50:11/q09 Why describe the town as feminine and dead?  [بَلْدَةًۭ مَّيْتًۭا · rhetoric]
- 50:11/q09/p1 The feminine ending of balda suggests extreme weakness and need for support, and 'dead' stresses complete dependence on revival. — Biqai gives both nuances. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 50:11/q10 Why is maytan masculine with the feminine balda?  [بَلْدَةًۭ مَّيْتًۭا · grammar]
turns on: Whether agreement follows the masculine meaning of 'place,' the behavior and sense of the adjective, or an implicit noun.
- 50:11/q10/p1 Balda is construed as masculine balad or makān, a place. — Qurtubi allows feminine maytatan too. Samin, Burhan, Bursevi, Alusi, and Ibn Ashur invoke the intended masculine place or synonym. Itqan gives the general rule that a feminine noun can agree with a masculine equivalent, with other examples including admonition, sun/rising body, and raḥma; it cites al-Jawhari's explanation of qarīb through iḥsān. [7 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURHAN; ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 50:11/q10/p2 The lightened mayt and its faʿīl pattern allow feminine marking to be present or absent. — Razi says faʿīl can have an active sense and take a feminine marker, yet here it lacks a gender marker because distinguishing agent from patient matters more than distinguishing male from female patients; faʿīl can serve active and passive senses, unlike mafʿūl. He compares the feminine adjective for earth in 36:33, where earth is described actively, and 'a good town' in 34:15, while this town's underlying state is life. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 50:11/q10/p3 An ellipsis of 'rain' or 'land' is understood with the description of the town. — Ibn Atiyya gives these possible implicit terms. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL]

## 50:11/q11 How is maytan read?  [مَّيْتًۭا · readings]
turns on: Whether the yāʾ is lightened or doubled.
- 50:11/q11/p1 Most readers use the lightened or contracted form maytan. — Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, and Samin report this as the common reading. Qiraat-ER contrasts it with the doubled yāʾ form. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(العامة); QIRAAT-ER(vw)]
- 50:11/q11/p2 Abū Jaʿfar and Khālid read mayyitan with doubled yāʾ. — Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, Samin, and Alusi name both readers. Nashr says Abū Jaʿfar doubles the yāʾ in all listed forms of al-mayta/maytan, balad mayt, and al-ḥayy min al-mayt wa-l-mayt min al-ḥayy; Qiraat-ER describes the vw form as doubled. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(العامة); NASHR(أبو جعفر); ALUSI-FULL(أبو جعفر، خالد); QIRAAT-ER(vw)]

## 50:11/q12 Do the light and doubled forms of maytan differ in meaning?  [مَّيْتًۭا · meaning]
turns on: Whether the two pronunciations are synonymous or mark dying versus already dead.
- 50:11/q12/p1 The light form is synonymous with the doubled form. — Ibn Ashur says 36:33 likewise calls dead land a sign. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 50:11/q12/p2 The doubled form may mean one now dying, while the contracted form means one already dead. — Qiraat-ER reports this as a grammatical debate, rather than a settled distinction. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER(vw)]

## 50:11/q13 What is al-khurūj?  [ٱلْخُرُوجُ · referent]
- 50:11/q13/p1 It is the known resurrection, when the dead emerge from graves. — Majaz calls it the Day of Resurrection and cites al-ʿAjjāj's poetic name 'day of rising.' Nahhas, Ibn Atiyya, and Ibn Jawzi explain emergence from graves. Ibn Ashur says the article marks a known event and that khurūj became a conventional name for resurrection, citing 70:43 and 50:42. Study Quran includes all humans and jinn. [6 notes: MAJAZ; NAHHAS; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]

## 50:11/q14 How is 'thus is the emergence' parsed?  [كَذَٰلِكَ ٱلْخُرُوجُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the comparison phrase is subject or fronted predicate, and whether an additional 'like' construction is admissible.
- 50:11/q14/p1 The kāf in 'thus' is the subject of an equative clause. — Nahhas treats it as a subject-predicate clause; Kashshaf and Nasafi explicitly put the kāf in the nominative subject position. Alusi allows this parse. [4 notes: NAHHAS; KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري)]
- 50:11/q14/p2 The fronted 'thus' is the predicate, with 'the emergence' as subject. — Qurtubi puts the kāf in predicate position. Alusi reports this from Zamakhshari and calls it the apparent parse. Bursevi and Ibn Ashur discuss the fronted predicate's restrictive or anticipatory effect. [4 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري)+]
- 50:11/q14/p3 The construction treats kāf as 'like' in a compound subject-predicate analysis of 'that emergence.' — Alusi says the analogy of 'Abū Yūsuf [is] Abū Ḥanīfa' permits this formally, but calls it contrived. [0 notes: ALUSI-FULL-]

## 50:11/q15 How does the revival of land explain resurrection?  [وَأَحْيَيْنَا بِهِۦ بَلْدَةًۭ مَّيْتًۭا كَذَٰلِكَ ٱلْخُرُوجُ · interpretation]
turns on: The evidence can be the observed parallel of revived land, God's greater first creation, or the knowledge and power displayed in providence; the notes often combine them.
- 50:11/q15/p1 As rain visibly brings dead land and plants to life, God can bring dead people out of graves. — Muqatil compares emergence through water with plants; Wahidi cites 7:57. Mawardi says re-creating what already had a source is easier than first creation and points to restoration for recompense. Qushayri says gathering people is no harder than reviving land. Tabrisi says power over either entails power over the other, and explains that habitual sight of land's revival versus no customary sight of humans returning causes doubt. Razi says earth splits and people come out like plants. Ibn Kathir says the witnessed sign is clearer to deniers, citing 40:57, 46:33, and 41:39; Bursevi reports Kashifi's reflection on a buried seed. Ibn Ashur calls resurrection a remarkable restoration after decay. Tabatabai says an analogous observed event establishes possibility, distinguishing this from the prior knowledge-and-power proof. Islahi calls the land's revival the sura's central analogy and says it makes life after death imaginable. Study Quran cites 30:19, 16:65, 25:48–49, 30:50, 35:9, 36:33, 43:11, and 45:5. [33 notes: MUQATIL; ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق); MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(الكاشفي); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 50:11/q15/p2 The Creator of these things can create people again; restoring existing bodies is easier than creating from nothing. — Zajjaj and Nahhas state the inference from creation. Ibn Kathir cites the heavens and earth as greater than people, with 40:57, 46:33, and 41:39. Ibn Ashur says these beings' creation from nothing proves possible the restoration of already existing, weaker beings. Kuran Yolu adds that God knows what remains or decays in each body and can restore its like and return its soul; Islahi invokes 17:99 and 79:27–33. [8 notes: ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق); IBNKATHIR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 50:11/q15/p3 The detailed provision displays God's knowledge, planning, and power, so dispersed human particles remain within His ability to restore. — Tabatabai calls this the earlier proof, distinct from the subsequent proof by analogy. Kuran Yolu also cites God's exact knowledge of bodily decay and ease in returning souls. [3 notes: TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 50:11/q16 What reports describe rain in relation to people and resurrection?  [بِهِۦ ٱلْخُرُوجُ · reports]
turns on: The reported details differ: an unspecified seminal rain, a forty-night rain with bodily regrowth, or a general rain-and-plant comparison.
- 50:11/q16/p1 Rain falls like seminal fluid, and flesh and bones grow on the dead. — Wahidi reports this from Ibn Abbas; no number of nights is specified. [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس)]
- 50:11/q16/p2 God sends rain for forty nights; it enters the earth like male seed, grows flesh, veins, and bones, then God revives and brings people out. — Bursevi gives this as an unattributed report. [1 notes: BURSEVI]
- 50:11/q16/p3 People emerge from graves through rain as plants emerge through rain. — Wahidi transmits Muqatil's comparison; Muqatil's own note likewise describes emergence through water. [2 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل)]

## 50:11/q17 What report connects rain with the coming year's food?  [رِّزْقًۭا بِهِۦ · reports]
- 50:11/q17/p1 When rain made gutters flow, the Prophet said, 'No famine upon you this year.' — Bursevi reports this from Abū Hurayra without grading it. [1 notes: BURSEVI(أبو هريرة)]

## 50:11/q18 Why is resurrection worded as 'emergence' while the plants' growth is called 'revival'?  [وَأَحْيَيْنَا ٱلْخُرُوجُ · rhetoric]
- 50:11/q18/p1 The paired terms magnify vegetation's renewal, make resurrection seem easy, and clarify the likeness. — Bursevi states all three effects; Alusi agrees. Elmalılı attributes the explanation to Abū al-Suʿud. [3 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI(Ebussuud)]

## 50:11/q19 What effect does the fronted 'thus' and its demonstrative have?  [كَذَٰلِكَ ٱلْخُرُوجُ · rhetoric]
- 50:11/q19/p1 The fronted comparison focuses or restricts the claim: resurrection corresponds to the striking revival of land. — Bursevi says nothing in that resurrection is unlike the wondrous life brought by rain. Alusi calls the focus emphatic and restrictive, says 'that' points to the life just described, and its distance suggests extraordinary rank; he adds resurrection is not essentially different. Ibn Ashur says fronting draws attention to the rebuttal of impossibility and creates anticipation of the subject; his demonstrative points to reviving dead earth and magnifies resurrection. [5 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 50:11/q20 How does the word khurūj answer the denial in 50:3?  [ٱلْخُرُوجُ · links]
turns on: Whether rajʿ in 50:3 is intransitive return or implies God's act of extraction, whose effect is emergence.
- 50:11/q20/p1 Its wording supports taking the deniers' rajʿ as intransitive 'return.' — Razi prefers this: if they had denied God's extracting people, the reply would suitably have used ikhrāj, but it uses khurūj. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL+]
- 50:11/q20/p2 Khurūj names the effect of extraction; denying extraction entails denying emergence, so the verse emphatically affirms that effect. — Razi gives this alternative if 50:3 is understood as denial of extraction and its result. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 50:11/q21 How does this verse fit the passage's argument?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes explain the sequence as a move from observable signs to conclusion, or account more specifically for the order of provision and revival and the final clause's discourse role.
- 50:11/q21/p1 The passage moves from resurrection-denial to visible signs in heaven, earth, rain, and revived land, then concludes that emergence is likewise possible. — Ibn al-Zubayr, reported by Burhan, links the move from 50:1 to 50:6 and 50:9 with signs deniers cannot dispute; Burhan says their challenge is left unanswered until the concluding phrase. Biqai says heaven's majestic wonder precedes the familiar earth and fruit, whose evidence people overlook. Ibn Ashur says the shift from heaven and earth to repeated life-and-death cycles prepares the proof. Tabatabai says the passage recalls God's knowledge and power in adorned heaven, spread and planted earth, and providential rain. Islahi calls land's revival the sura's central theme; Kuran Yolu says the broader passage answers denial through creation, knowledge, power, and blessings. [7 notes: BURHAN(ابن الزبير); BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 50:11/q21/p2 The provision evidence precedes the land-revival proof because heaven and earth already established resurrection; crops and fruit are mentioned before the land's greenery as the greater blessing. — Razi reports the first ordering explanation, then an alternative: water first makes land green and flowery, a more widespread adornment, but crops and fruit appear first in the wording for their greater benefit. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 50:11/q21/p3 The final clause resumes and summarizes the preceding proof of resurrection, possibly as a parenthetical close. — Ibn Ashur prefers resumption, allowing a parenthesis for grammarians who recognize it. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL+]

## 50:11/q22 What further teaching do these signs carry?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes draw both recognition of divine attributes and the duty of accountability from the same signs.
- 50:11/q22/p1 They reveal God's oneness, power, wisdom, and providence and call for reflection and gratitude. — Muqatil says rain and growth disclose oneness and resurrection power. Islahi says heaven and earth are signs of monotheism, resurrection, reward and punishment for those turning humbly to God; their harmony argues for oneness, and his account of 79:27–33 displays greatness, wisdom, and provision. Study Quran calls nature's beauty, order, and bounty signs of mercy, wisdom, and resurrection and cites 16:79–81, 24:43–50, 26:7–8, 33:27, 35:27–28, 36:71–73, and 45:1–5. Tabatabai stresses knowledge and power. [7 notes: MUQATIL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 50:11/q22/p2 Divine benefits imply accountability, reward, and punishment. — Islahi says providential benefits entail judgment and that people will be questioned about favors; orderly clusters display lavish power, wisdom, and creativity while imposing an obligation. Mawardi says the restored dead face recompense. [5 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 50:11/q23 What further symbolic revival is read in this verse?  [* · inward]
turns on: Whether the further revived subject is a community through Islam's victory or spiritually dead hearts.
- 50:11/q23/p1 It alludes to Islam's emergence and victory through divine favor, giving new life to a dead community. — Elmalılı offers this additional allusion. [1 notes: ELMALILI]
- 50:11/q23/p2 The earth's revival can symbolize God's revival of spiritually dead hearts. — Study Quran offers this as an additional symbolic sense. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]
