# 87:4/q01 What does God's bringing out the pasture mean? [أَخْرَجَ · interpretation]
- 87:4/q01/p1 God causes vegetation to emerge and grow from the earth.
  reasons: Al-Kashshāf explicitly glosses akhraja as causing growth; Ibn ʿĀshūr says bringing it out of the earth is making it grow. Al-Wāḥidī and al-Jishumī locate its emergence in the earth. Al-Biqāʿī adds rain from clouds and says the growth occurs initially and again.

# 87:4/q02 What does al-marʿā denote? [ٱلْمَرْعَىٰ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the term covers all earth-grown plants, specifically grazed growth, a reported human referent, or an inward image.
- 87:4/q02/p1 Vegetation broadly, including plants beyond livestock fodder.
  reasons: Abū Razīn and Ibrāhīm gloss it simply as vegetation. Al-Thaʿlabī describes green, yellow, red, and white plants; Qatāda also lists yellow, red, and white sprouts. Al-Māwardī cites a verse of Zafar ibn al-Ḥārith about pasture growing over ruins; his fuller text gives the verse's contrast with lasting human grief. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says plants sustain livestock and benefit people directly. Al-Rāzī includes fruits, crops, and grasses; Ibn Kathīr says every kind of plant and crop. Elmalılı includes grasslands, trees, and fruits for human and animal use and cites 79:31–33. Quṭb expressly extends the term beyond livestock pasture to provisions for walking, hidden, and flying creatures. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the whole plant kingdom, including trees, is recalled through a useful example. Study Quran likewise says all vegetation.

- 87:4/q02/p2 The growth or fodder grazed by livestock, often fresh grass or green forage.
  reasons: Al-Ṭabarī specifies varied earth-grown plants and grasses serving as livestock feed. Al-Māwardī explains the name through grazing and cites Zafar's verse. Al-Baghawī gives green, yellow, red, and white grasses; Ibn ʿAbbās calls it green herbage, as reported by al-Rāzī and al-Būrsawī. Al-Būrsawī and al-Ālūsī stress tender, fresh growth; al-Ālūsī adds moist and verdant. Al-Biqāʿī says rain clouds produce grass and other fodder. Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies grazed vegetation. Al-Rāzī's fuller note juxtaposes Ibn ʿAbbās's green fodder with the author's broader definition.

# 87:4/q03 How is the noun al-marʿā formed and used? [ٱلْمَرْعَىٰ · grammar]
- 87:4/q03/p1 It may be a noun of grazing place or a verbal noun used for what is grazed.
  reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr gives both nominal possibilities while identifying the referent as vegetation grazed by livestock.

  reasons: Al-Jishumī notes these related root senses alongside the pasture gloss.

# 87:4/q05 Why does the verse name pasture specifically? [ٱلْمَرْعَىٰ · rhetoric]
- 87:4/q05/p1 It evokes the benefit livestock provide people and preserves the verse ending while recalling the wider plant kingdom.
  reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says forage is one useful kind of the whole plant kingdom, including trees; naming it rather than plants in general brings livestock's human benefit to mind and fits the verse ending.
  holders (2 notes): IBNASHUR
  notes: IBNASHUR:87:4/r1 IBNASHUR:87:4/r4
sources: IBNASHUR = Ibn ʿĀshūr, d. 1393

# 87:4/q06 How does the pasture's growth relate to its later dark state? [* · interpretation]
turns on: The force and attachment of aḥwā, the meaning of ghuthāʾ, and whether the sequence ends in decay or lush growth; Ibn Jarīr appeals to transmitted explanations, whereas Iṣlāḥī appeals to usage and poetry.
- 87:4/q06/p1 God brings forth living pasture and subsequently turns it into dark, dry stubble or refuse.
  reasons: Muqātil specifies winter herbage drying after fresh green growth. Al-Thaʿlabī says its varied colors become dry and brittle and reads aḥwā as black from age and drying. Al-Burhān notes intervening stages between emergence and dark stubble. Al-Būrsawī transmits al-Jawharī's definitions of ghuthāʾ as flood-borne scraps and qumāsh as things collected from here and there. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ calls the change an instance of divine governance. Corpus Coranicum treats the cycle as recurring; Kuran Yolu also offers living things receiving life and dying as a metaphor. Study Quran says blackened stubble illustrates creation and destruction.
  holders (8 notes): MUQATIL(مقاتل); THALABI; BURHAN; BURSEVI(الجوهري); TABATABAI; CORPUSCORANICUM; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN; ISLAHI-TADABBUR-
  notes: MUQATIL:87:1-7/r5 THALABI:v10p183#2/r16 BURHAN:v4p295/r3 BURSEVI:v10p314/r9 TABATABAI:87:1-19#2/r4 CORPUSCORANICUM:87:2-5:anmerkung/r1 KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:87:1/r5 STUDYQURAN:87:4/r1 | against: ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p275/r1
- 87:4/q06/p2 Aḥwā describes pasture as it emerges dark green; it is then made dry debris, with the apparent word order postponed.
  reasons: Al-Zajjāj construes aḥwā as a circumstantial accusative of pasture and likens the later brittle stubble to debris atop floodwater. Ibn Kathīr reports Arab-language scholars who move the dark-green description before the making of debris. Ibn Jarīr objects that this conflicts with transmitted exegetical explanations.

  notes: ZAJJAJ:v5p315/r5 IBNKATHIR:87:5/r3 | against: IBNKATHIR:87:5/r4
- 87:4/q06/p3 The pasture is made luxuriant and blackish green; ghuthāʾ and aḥwā describe fertile growth rather than decay.
  reasons: Iṣlāḥī, following Imām al-Farāhī, cites Qaṭṭāmī's valley couplet on dense, layered undergrowth to support ghuthāʾ as luxuriant vegetation. He says aḥwā is the reddish or greenish blackness of freshness and fertility and qualifies ghuthāʾ; he explicitly rejects decay-blackness and waste readings.
