# 20:72: 31 questions

## 20:72/q01 What does 'we will not prefer you' mean?  [لَن نُّؤْثِرَكَ · meaning]
- 20:72/q01/p1 They will not choose Pharaoh, his religion, obedience, protection, or worldly gains over the truth and God. — Jishumi, Qurtubi, Nasafi and Ibn Ashur gloss īthār as choice or preference; Ibn Ashur compares Joseph's brothers in 12:91 and supplies the implied comparison with Pharaoh's obedience or religion. Tabari says following Pharaoh would reject Moses. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan include safety from torture; Tabatabai says the object is Pharaoh's claimed wealth and resources, not merely his person. Biqai notes his punishment is temporary. Wahidi-Wajiz and Bursevi specify Pharaoh's religion and obedience. [26 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; THALABI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(السحرة); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 20:72/q02 What are the clear proofs that came to them?  [مَا جَآءَنَا مِنَ ٱلْبَيِّنَٰتِ · referent]
turns on: Whether bayyināt identifies the sensory miracles, the resulting certainty, an argument for unity, or the particular evidence that the implements vanished.
- 20:72/q02/p1 The visible miracles of Moses, especially the shining hand and the staff. — Muqatil, Mujahid as cited by Thalabi, and others name the hand and staff. Tabatabai details the staff becoming a serpent, swallowing the ropes and staffs, then returning to its former state. Tabarsi calls them miracles beyond human power proving Moses' prophethood; Baydawi, Nasafi, Bursevi and Alusi call them manifest or decisive signs. Bursevi says the disappearance of the magicians' implements rules out magic. [12 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI(مقاتل); WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(مقاتل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; TABRISI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 20:72/q02/p2 Certainty, knowledge, clarification, or guidance received through the signs. — Ibn Abbas calls the proofs certainty and knowledge, cited by Wahidi-Basit and Qurtubi; Baghawi records clarification and knowledge. Wahidi-Wajiz and Ibn Kathir include guidance and certainty. [5 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 20:72/q02/p3 Arguments demonstrating divine unity. — Jishumi transmits this alongside the hand and staff explanation. [1 notes: JISHUMI]
- 20:72/q02/p4 The disappearance of the magicians' ropes and staffs proves the event was no sorcery. — Baghawi records the argument that their implements would have remained if Moses' act were magic; Bursevi repeats it. [2 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); BURSEVI]

## 20:72/q03 Why do they say the proofs came 'to us'?  [جَآءَنَا · rhetoric]
- 20:72/q03/p1 Their expertise in magic let them recognize Moses' act as genuine, so the proof particularly reached and benefited them. — Ibn Jawzi says they knew magical tricks best and made the truth clearer to others; Abu Hayyan adds that others relied on their expertise. Alusi says they knew the signs directly and fully rather than by imitation. [3 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 20:72/q04 How is 'what came to us' constructed?  [مَا جَآءَنَا · grammar]
turns on: What unspoken referent or resumptive the relative clause requires.
- 20:72/q04/p1 Mā is a relative pronoun with an implied resumptive referring to the signs that came to them. — Alusi describes the relative clause and its concealed resumptive; Baydawi says the pronoun can refer back to what came to them. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL]
- 20:72/q04/p2 The suppressed element is Moses or a prepositional phrase: what Moses brought to us. — Alusi reports this proposal but calls it strained, including the proposed deletion of the prepositional phrase. [0 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)-]

## 20:72/q05 What force does 'of the clear proofs' have?  [مِنَ ٱلْبَيِّنَٰتِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether min denotes the several observed signs or partitions one sign from further unseen signs.
- 20:72/q05/p1 It points to the several signs they actually witnessed. — Tabatabai enumerates the serpent, its swallowing their implements, and its return to a staff. [1 notes: TABATABAI+]
- 20:72/q05/p2 Min is partitive: they saw one sign and inferred that God had many more. — Tabatabai records this possibility and judges it far-fetched. [0 notes: TABATABAI-]

## 20:72/q06 Who is 'the One who formed us,' and what does fataranā mean?  [ٱلَّذِى فَطَرَنَا · referent]
turns on: Whether the phrase is read as ordinary creation or as an inward allusion to primordial religious nature.
- 20:72/q06/p1 God, who created or originated the magicians and all creation. — Akhfash, Tabari, Ibn Atiyya, Thalabi and others gloss faṭara as creating or originating. Ibn Kathir says He formed them from nothing and clay and alone deserves worship. Biqai includes Pharaoh and everyone else under His lordship; Alusi includes the higher and lower realms. Tabatabai says creation makes God the source of every real good. [12 notes: AKHFASH; TAB-FULL; THALABI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; JISHUMI(أبو علي); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 20:72/q06/p2 Inwardly, God formed them upon Islam's primordial nature. — The inward commentary cited by Bursevi says this original nature makes preference for Pharaoh impossible. [1 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات)]

## 20:72/q07 How does 'and the One who formed us' attach to the refusal?  [وَٱلَّذِى فَطَرَنَا · grammar]
turns on: Whether wāw coordinates a second object of preference or starts an oath; under the oath parse, whether lan can answer it.
- 20:72/q07/p1 It is coordinated with the proofs: they will not prefer Pharaoh over the signs or over their Creator. — Farra supplies repeated ʿalā; Tabari, Zajjaj, Abu Ishaq through Nahhas and many later commentators give this genitive parse. Razi expands the understood object to obeying and worshipping the Creator. Ibn Ashur specifies refusal to prefer Pharaoh in divinity. Jishumi also describes the relative as coordinated with what came to them, once calling its case accusative. [25 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق); MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء والزجاج); JISHUMI(أبو علي); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(فرقة); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الفراء والزجاج); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+]
- 20:72/q07/p2 The wāw introduces an oath by their Creator: by God, we will not prefer Pharaoh. — Farra, Tabari, Zajjaj and later sources offer the genitive oath. Nasafi puts its response before the oath, but Abu Hayyan, Samin and Alusi say lan rarely serves as the answer to a negative oath and prefer an omitted answer understood from the refusal; Bursevi calls the lan construction exceptional. [26 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق); THALABI(قيل); MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء والزجاج); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(فرقة); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الفراء والزجاج); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI(التفسير الفارسي,بعضهم); ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]

## 20:72/q08 Why is the Creator mentioned after the signs?  [مَا جَآءَنَا مِنَ ٱلْبَيِّنَٰتِ وَٱلَّذِى فَطَرَنَا · rhetoric]
- 20:72/q08/p1 The speech progresses from witnessed signs to their Creator and the greater, rational ground for rejecting Pharaoh. — Samin calls it progression from lesser to greater; Abu Hayyan says the miracle leads to the One who can break nature and who exposes Pharaoh's inability to create even a fly. Bursevi and Alusi contrast sensory evidence with reflective proof. Ibn Ashur says the signs show God wants faith in Moses and that the order indirectly invites Pharaoh to believe. Alusi and Biqai say naming the Creator refutes Pharaoh's divinity and shows his helplessness; Tabatabai says the Creator is the source of real good. [8 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 20:72/q09 What does 'carry out what you are to carry out' mean?  [فَٱقْضِ مَآ أَنتَ قَاضٍ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the words address Pharaoh's intended action in the ordinary sense or allude inwardly to God's prior decree.
- 20:72/q09/p1 Pharaoh may do, execute, or decide whatever he intends, including the threatened cutting and crucifixion. — Muqatil, Thalabi, Wahidi-Wajiz, Qurtubi, Bursevi and Ibn Ashur specify the punishments in 20:71. Majaz, Zajjaj and Nasafi cite Abu Dhuʾayb's qaḍāhumā for making or finishing garments or armor; Damghani and Ibn Jawzi give qaḍā as carrying out, including God's decree or marriage. Ibn Jawzi says its root sense is firm execution; Alusi cites 41:12 for making the heavens, while allowing ordinary judging. Jishumi and Tabarsi stress thorough, decisive action. Biqai says Pharaoh acts only by God's permission; Ibn Kathir limits it to what lies within his power. [29 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; FARRA; MAJAZ; ZAJJAJ(أبو ذؤيب); THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس وجميع المفسرين); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; DAMGHANI; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أهل التفسير); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس,قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 20:72/q09/p2 Inwardly, they accept the martyrdom God eternally decreed for them. — The inward commentary transmitted by Bursevi treats their imperative as willing acceptance of God's decree. [1 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات)]

## 20:72/q10 What is the rhetorical force of the command to Pharaoh?  [فَٱقْضِ مَآ أَنتَ قَاضٍ · rhetoric]
- 20:72/q10/p1 It conveys defiance and indifference to his threat, not an instruction for him to act. — Jishumi, Tabarsi, Razi and Alusi say nothing he does will make them abandon faith. Ibn Ashur says all, part, or none of the threat is equally immaterial. Majaz says their decision was already made; Abu Hayyan says the words belittle the threat. [8 notes: MAJAZ; JISHUMI; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 20:72/q11 How does mā work in 'mā anta qāḍ'?  [مَآ أَنتَ قَاضٍ · grammar]
turns on: Whether mā refers to the thing Pharaoh will decide or forms a temporal verbal noun with the nominal clause.
- 20:72/q11/p1 Mā is relative, 'whatever you judge,' with the object pronoun omitted. — Qurtubi rejects taking it as a verbal noun joined to a verb; Samin calls the relative analysis clearer and permits omission of ـه because the relative occupies accusative position. Tabarsi also records mā as the object of qāḍ with its resumptive omitted; Abu Hayyan notes the same analysis. [4 notes: TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 20:72/q11/p2 Mā is a temporal verbal noun: carry out judgment for as long as you judge. — Tabarsi and Abu al-Baqa through Samin report this. Samin notes an objection that verbal-noun mā cannot introduce a nominal clause, then replies that many grammarians permit it; Ibn Malik calls nominal clauses after temporal mā common and adduces a poem, though non-temporal examples are rarer. Abu Hayyan also notes grammarians who allow nominal clauses. [4 notes: TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء,ابن مالك); SAMIN-DURR-]

## 20:72/q12 Why can the yāʾ of qāḍ be absent or retained?  [قَاضٍ · grammar]
turns on: Whether pausal form preserves the connected deletion or restores the yāʾ once its phonetic cause ends.
- 20:72/q12/p1 It drops in connected speech when its still letter meets the still tanwīn, and may drop in pause to reflect that form. — Nahhas explains the connected form and the analogous pause; Qurtubi repeats the connected-speech reason. [2 notes: NAHHAS; QURTUBI-FULL(سيبويه)]
- 20:72/q12/p2 The yāʾ is better retained in pause because the collision of still letters has ended. — Nahhas and Qurtubi report Sībawayh's preference. [2 notes: NAHHAS(سيبويه)+; QURTUBI-FULL(سيبويه)+]

## 20:72/q13 What does 'you judge only this worldly life' mean?  [إِنَّمَا تَقْضِى هَٰذِهِ ٱلْحَيَوٰةَ ٱلدُّنْيَآ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether qaḍā describes Pharaoh's limited jurisdiction, the passing of worldly life, or his ability to end their lives.
- 20:72/q13/p1 Pharaoh's power, sentence, or harm reaches only mortal worldly life, not the enduring afterlife. — Ibn Qutayba, Tabari and Wahb, Nahhas, Thalabi, Ibn Abbas through Wahidi, and many others stress the limit of his dominion. Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and Biqai contrast the believers' bliss with Pharaoh's punishment; Razi reasons that finite harm is bearable for eternal bliss. Biqai restricts Pharaoh to bodies, even were his rule to last to the world's end. Baydawi points to 87:17. Bursevi and Alusi add that they neither crave worldly pleasure nor fear punishment. Ibn Ashur says innamā makes this an absolute restriction. Tabatabai contrasts Pharaoh's belief that earthly life is all with the magicians' belief in eternal life. [32 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(وهب بن منبه); THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(المفسرون); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; QASHANI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 20:72/q13/p2 This worldly life itself passes away, while the afterlife remains. — Majaz glosses taqḍī as passing or ending, comparing completion of a journey; Mawardi and Tabarsi give this alternative. Qushayri says worldly suffering ends however long it lasts. Asad observes qaḍā can mean either decree or end. [5 notes: MAJAZ; MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; TABRISI; ASAD-NOTES]
- 20:72/q13/p3 Pharaoh can end only the magicians' worldly lives. — Asad takes qada in the sense 'end,' applying it to their mortal lives. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES]

## 20:72/q14 How is 'this worldly life' governed in the final clause?  [إِنَّمَا تَقْضِى هَٰذِهِ ٱلْحَيَوٰةَ ٱلدُّنْيَآ · grammar]
turns on: The function of mā, whether a preposition or object is understood, and whether life is accusative or nominative.
- 20:72/q14/p1 It is an accusative expression of time: Pharaoh judges only during worldly life. — Farra and Ibn Jawzi treat innamā as one particle. Tabari, Nahhas, Razi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Samin, Alusi and Ibn Ashur take life as an accusative time expression; Razi compares ṣumtu yawm al-jumʿa ṣīma, and Ibn Ashur identifies the intended duration. Qurtubi allows an understood object; Samin says mā enables inna to govern a verb; Abu Hayyan permits preparatory or masdariyya mā. Biqai supplies omitted fī. Kashshaf says the adverb may be construed like an object. [15 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL; NAHHAS; JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الفراء); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 20:72/q14/p2 It is an accusative direct object or extended object: Pharaoh judges worldly affairs or worldly life. — Jishumi and Qurtubi give the direct object construction; Tabarsi supplies omitted 'affairs of' worldly life. Samin calls it object by extension, with Abu Haywa's passive reading as support. Kashshaf allows the adverb to be construed as an object; Baydawi compares an active construction to passive ṣīma yawm al-jumʿa. Qurtubi says mā restricts inna. [6 notes: JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]
- 20:72/q14/p3 With mā as a relative, 'this worldly life' is nominative predicate: what he judges is worldly life. — Farra, Nahhas, Ibn Jawzi and Qurtubi require an omitted object pronoun in taqḍī. Zajjaj allows nominative but knows no reader of it; he glosses it as worldly enjoyment. Abu al-Baqa through Samin says it would be inna's predicate if transmitted; Jishumi describes it as predicate after hādhihi. [8 notes: FARRA; ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS(الفراء); JISHUMI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء)]
- 20:72/q14/p4 Mā is a verbal noun subject of inna, with the following phrase as predicate: his deciding is only in worldly life. — Samin and Alusi give this parse, preserving the afterlife contrast. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]
- 20:72/q14/p5 The verb can be intransitive, with no object suppressed. — Alusi records this further parsing. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 20:72/q15 Which variant readings of the final clause are reported?  [تَقْضِى هَٰذِهِ ٱلْحَيَوٰةَ ٱلدُّنْيَآ · readings]
turns on: Which verb voice or subject is read, and whether a proposed nominative active form is attested.
- 20:72/q15/p1 The received active taqḍī has al-ḥayāta in the accusative. — Zajjaj attests the accusative and says he knows no reader using nominative; Razi calls this the received reading. Baydawi likens the active construction to passive ṣīma yawm al-jumʿa. [4 notes: ZAJJAJ; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL]
- 20:72/q15/p2 Some read the verb passively, with al-ḥayātu nominative. — Ibn Jawzi names Ibn Abi ʿAbla and Abu al-Mutawakkil; Alusi names Abu Haywa and Ibn Abi ʿAbla; Samin uses Abu Haywa's passive reading to support the adverb construed as object. [3 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن أبي عبلة وأبو المتوكل); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حياة وابن أبي عبلة)]
- 20:72/q15/p3 An alternative has naqḍī, 'we spend this worldly life.' — Razi records this alternative. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 20:72/q15/p4 A nominative al-ḥayātu is grammatically possible with active taqḍī, though its transmission is uncertain. — Zajjaj knows no one who reads it; Ibn Jawzi says a reader may use it; Abu al-Baqa through Samin conditions his analysis on its transmission. [3 notes: ZAJJAJ; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء)]

## 20:72/q17 How many magicians were there, and who were they?  [قَالُوا۟ · reports]
turns on: The differing narrated headcounts.
- 20:72/q17/p1 Seventy-two: two Egyptian or Coptic leaders and seventy Israelites forced to learn sorcery. — Thalabi attributes the account to Mujahid under a Muqatil heading; Kashshaf reports two Copts and the remainder Israelites. [2 notes: THALABI(مقاتل); KASHSHAF-FULL]
- 20:72/q17/p2 Seventy thousand magicians assembled by Pharaoh. — Qasim's report in Thalabi says their ropes and staffs appeared to move before Moses' staff swallowed them. [1 notes: THALABI(القاسم بن أبي برزة)]

## 20:72/q18 What did the magicians learn from Moses' staff while he slept?  [مَا جَآءَنَا مِنَ ٱلْبَيِّنَٰتِ · reports]
- 20:72/q18/p1 Seeing the staff guard a sleeping Moses, they inferred it was not sorcery, which ceases when the magician sleeps; Pharaoh still compelled them to perform. — Abd al-Aziz's account in Thalabi and Kashshaf's report agree on this test. [2 notes: THALABI(عبد العزيز بن أبان); KASHSHAF-FULL]

## 20:72/q19 What did the magicians see during prostration?  [مَا جَآءَنَا مِنَ ٱلْبَيِّنَٰتِ · reports]
turns on: Whether the vision report names only their Paradise places or also Hell and its occupants' rewards.
- 20:72/q19/p1 They saw their places in Paradise before lifting their heads, strengthening their refusal. — Ikrima through Wahidi and Qurtubi reports God showing them their destined places; Baghawi's Qasim report also mentions their own places. [3 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(عكرمة); BAGHAWI-FULL(القاسم بن أبي بزة); QURTUBI-FULL(عكرمة وغيره)]
- 20:72/q19/p2 They saw Paradise, Hell, and their inhabitants' rewards before lifting their heads. — Qasim through Thalabi, Wahidi and Baghawi gives this fuller vision; Thalabi places it after the staff swallowed the magicians' implements. [3 notes: THALABI(القاسم بن أبي برزة); WAHIDI-BASIT(القاسم بن أبي بزة); BAGHAWI-FULL(القاسم بن أبي بزة)]

## 20:72/q20 What other test persuaded a chief magician?  [مَا جَآءَنَا مِنَ ٱلْبَيِّنَٰتِ · reports]
- 20:72/q20/p1 A chief magician learned that the serpent did not fear, recognized the Maker's work, and believed. — Qurtubi records this separate account. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]

## 20:72/q21 What happened to Pharaoh's wife after Moses prevailed?  [قَالُوا۟ · reports]
- 20:72/q21/p1 She believed after learning Moses and Aaron won, remained faithful despite Pharaoh's threat, and God took her soul before the stone struck her body. — Mawardi and Qurtubi report the episode. Thalabi's Qasim report adds that she was shown her home in Paradise before her soul was taken and the stone fell on her lifeless body. [3 notes: THALABI(القاسم بن أبي برزة); MAWARDI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]

## 20:72/q22 Did Pharaoh execute the magicians after their declaration?  [فَٱقْضِ مَآ أَنتَ قَاضٍ · reports]
turns on: Whether promised victory excludes their execution, or can coexist with their martyrdom.
- 20:72/q22/p1 He crucified them as threatened, and they became martyrs that day. — Ibn Atiyya reports this group's account and says martyrdom followed their morning as sorcerers. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(طائفة)]
- 20:72/q22/p2 He did not execute them, since God promised victory to Moses and his companions. — Ibn Atiyya reports this second group's inference from the promise. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(فرقة)]
- 20:72/q22/p3 Either account is possible; execution need not contradict the promised victory. — Ibn Atiyya says only an apparently general reading of the promise creates a conflict, which can be resolved. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL]

## 20:72/q23 What spiritual resolve does the magicians' refusal display?  [* · inward]
turns on: The first draws out the strength of certainty; the second turns that example into a rebuke of later readers.
- 20:72/q23/p1 Certainty and recognition of God make worldly pleasure, threats, and pain slight beside lasting bliss. — Dhu al-Nun through Sulami says preferring God makes sacrifice easy. Sulami and Bursevi say divine disclosure lightens burdens; Qushayri says knowing the ordeal comes from God makes it sweet, and Bursevi quotes a Sufi that knowing the tester eases affliction. Qashani and Alusi contrast bodily suffering with enduring intellectual or spiritual bliss. Razi sees certainty in religious fundamentals and reasons from finite harm to eternal benefit. Ibn Ashur compares the swift transformation of converts such as ʿUmar; Tabatabai describes their release from awe of Pharaoh, flattery and worldly glitter into hope and fear of God alone. Study Quran reports K's contrast of finite trials and eternal felicity. [18 notes: SULAMI(ذو النون); QUSHAYRI; RAZI-FULL; QASHANI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI(الكاشفي,بعض الكبار); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(K)]
- 20:72/q23/p2 Their instant faith rebukes people who study the Quran for decades yet sell religion cheaply. — Al-Hasan, cited by Jishumi, Razi and Bursevi, contrasts the magicians' immediate courage with some sixty-year companions of the Quran. ## 20:72/q24 How does the speech portray Pharaoh's claim to authority?  [* · theology]
- 20:72/q24/p1 His imagined divinity and ownership of worldly life are exposed as false by God's creative power and the believers' recognition of truth. — Abu Hayyan says he cannot create even a fly; Alusi says Pharaoh himself was created. Biqai calls him helpless before the Creator. Tabatabai says Pharaoh imagines himself Egypt's lord over subjects, land, and armies and expects truth to wait for his leave, whereas the believers see his wealth as a mirage. Ibn Ashur's comparison over the Creator similarly denies Pharaoh divinity. [7 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 20:72/q25 How does this reply connect to the following speech about forgiveness and the next life?  [* · links]
- 20:72/q25/p1 The limited worldly threat gives way to the believers' hope of forgiveness and the contrasting destinies of believers and criminals. — Thalabi says they seek forgiveness for sins and the sorcery Pharaoh compelled. Qushayri says knowing God makes forgiveness the chief concern and cites 28:16, 40:55 and 48:2; he adds the Prophet's report of seeking forgiveness seventy times daily. Razi identifies the transition to the criminal's Hell in 20:74; Baydawi says the worldly limit prepares the contrast with the hereafter, citing 87:17. Study Quran cross-references 7:123-26 and 26:49. [6 notes: THALABI; QUSHAYRI(النبي); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; STUDYQURAN]

## 20:72/q27 Is the shift from addressing Pharaoh to first-person speech an iltifāt?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether iltifāt requires continuity of the referent across the change in person.
- 20:72/q27/p1 It can count as iltifāt for scholars who do not require the same referent throughout. — Burhan presents the magicians' switch from address to first person, extending to 20:73, as an example under that definition. [1 notes: BURHAN]
- 20:72/q27/p2 It fails as an iltifāt because the intended speakers are not one referent. — Itqan rejects the proposed example on referent identity grounds. [0 notes: ITQAN-]

## 20:72/q28 Where is the answer to the oath understood?  [وَٱلَّذِى فَطَرَنَا · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether lan can serve as an oath answer and whether that answer may precede its oath.
- 20:72/q28/p1 The oath's answer is omitted and recovered from the refusal already spoken. — Burhan says an omitted element can be inferred from 'we will never prefer you.' Abu Hayyan, Samin, Bursevi and Alusi say an explicit answer with lan is rare or inadmissible outside exceptional poetry. [6 notes: BURHAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI(بعضهم); ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
- 20:72/q28/p2 The response to the oath is the preceding 'we will not prefer you.' — Nasafi allows the response to precede the oath; Abu Hayyan and Samin object that lan ordinarily cannot answer a negative oath, and Bursevi calls it exceptional. [1 notes: NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; SAMIN-DURR-; BURSEVI(بعضهم)-; ALUSI-FULL(قيل)-]

## 20:72/q29 What general principle of word arrangement is illustrated around this verse?  [* · rhetoric]
- 20:72/q29/p1 Excellent composition depends on grammatical meanings, their relations, and purposeful placement. — Jurjani says no form is inherently excellent in every context; he compares arrangement to selecting and placing dyes. He observes that effect can accumulate across lines or appear in a single masterful verse. [3 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL]

## 20:72/q30 What related general reading or rhetorical rules are cited?  [* · readings]
turns on: These are separate ancillary rules cited in the source notes; the last two differ on the force of confirmation.
- 20:72/q30/p1 Disputed possessive yāʾ readings in this chapter concern connected recitation; the other 566 are agreed to have sukūn. — Nashr states the count and the connected-recitation limit. [1 notes: NASHR]
- 20:72/q30/p2 Ḥattā is an endpoint preposition, used with an overt noun after something divisible or contiguous and with gradual progress. — Itqan compares it to ilā but says it cannot mark a starting point. [2 notes: ITQAN]
- 20:72/q30/p3 A rhetorical question can retain its interrogative sense while gaining another force. — Itqan prefers retention, supported by Tanukhi's account of laʿalla. [1 notes: ITQAN+]
- 20:72/q30/p4 A confirmatory question asks the addressee to acknowledge a proposition. — Itqan finds this more apparent and explicit in al-Īḍāḥ, while reporting an alternative that the question simply asserts the proposition. [1 notes: ITQAN+]
- 20:72/q30/p5 A confirmatory question simply asserts the proposition is true. — Itqan reports this alternative without choosing it. [1 notes: ITQAN]

## 20:72/q31 Why is God better and more enduring than Pharaoh?  [* · interpretation]
- 20:72/q31/p1 Pharaoh is mortal and will perish, while God remains. — Thalabi draws this contrast. [1 notes: THALABI]
