JISHUMI (author): Wine is unquestionably forbidden: it was once permitted, prohibition is necessarily known as part of the religion, its denier is an unbeliever, and its drinker a sinner. JISHUMI (النبي): The Prophet is cited as saying that wine is forbidden for its own essence, while intoxication is forbidden from every drink. KASHSHAF-FULL (author): The account begins with the Meccan verse permitting wine made from date palms and grapes; Muslims drank it as lawful. | mentions 16:67 KASHSHAF-FULL (author): Khamr is named for covering the intellect and discernment; sakr likewise names its blocking of them, and khamr may be an intensive verbal noun from covering. IBNATIYYA-FULL (النبي): A Prophetic report says every intoxicant is wine and forbidden; what intoxicates in a large amount is forbidden in a small amount. IBNATIYYA-FULL (author): “الخمر” derives from “خمر,” to cover; the Prophet's “خمّروا الإناء” and a woman's “خمار” illustrate the root. IBNATIYYA-FULL (author): The word applies to what hides or shades a person, as in a poet's reference to a concealed hollow by the road. IBNATIYYA-FULL (author): Al-Ajjaj uses “الخمر” for a hidden army; other Arabic usage calls a concealed place “غمار الناس وخمارهم.” IBNATIYYA-FULL (author): Wine is named for covering and clouding the intellect. IBNATIYYA-FULL (أبو حنيفة): Abu Hanifa says wine can also be made from grains. IBNATIYYA-FULL (النبي): A report from the Prophet says wine comes from grapevine and date palm. TABRISI (كثير): The poet Kathīr uses مخامر for an affliction that mingles or remains mixed in, illustrating the root sense of khamr. TABRISI (author): A small palm-fiber prayer mat is called khumra because it covers the face from the ground during prostration. TABRISI (author): The apparent sense in Imami reports, also adopted by al-Shafiʿi, is any intoxicating drink that covers the mind; even a little is forbidden if much intoxicates. TABRISI (author): Wine is called ithm in Arabic, as the cited poem shows; the verse’s description of it as a great sin also supports prohibition. IBNJAWZI-ZAD (الزجاج): Al-Zajjāj says khamr is what covers the intellect; khimār can mean a crowd in which one is hidden, and a woman’s head covering is named for covering. IBNJAWZI-ZAD (الزجاج): Al-Zajjāj says the named wine is the agreed-upon kind, and by analogy every drink that acts like it shares its prohibition. IBNJAWZI-ZAD (ابن جبير): Ibn Jubayr says wine had been permitted under the verse about drink made from date palms and grapes. | mentions 16:67 RAZI-FULL (الشافعي): Al-Shafiʿi defines wine broadly: every intoxicating drink is khamr. RAZI-FULL (ابن عمر): Ibn Umar reports that when wine was prohibited, it was made from five substances: grapes, dates, wheat, barley, and maize; Razi uses this to show these drinks were all called wine and covered by the prohibition. RAZI-FULL (رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم): Al-Nuʿman ibn Bashir transmits the Prophet's statement that wine can come from grapes, dates, honey, wheat, and barley; Razi says this explicitly brings those drinks under the verse. RAZI-FULL (رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم): Ibn Umar transmits the Prophet's statement that every intoxicant is wine and every intoxicant is forbidden. RAZI-FULL (author): Razi concludes that the hadiths collectively establish that every intoxicant is wine and forbidden. RAZI-FULL (أهل اللغة): Linguists derive khamr from covering: khimar covers the head, khamr can conceal something, and khamartu means covering a vessel; Razi takes the derivation to support concealment of the intellect. RAZI-FULL (author): Razi rejects the objection that this proves a language by analogy: derivation identifies a word's referent, just as Hanafis derive intercourse for nikah and restraint for fasting. RAZI-FULL (author): Razi says the community recognizes three wine-related verses: two use khamr, while the prayer verse refers to intoxication, confirming that khamr means an intoxicant. | mentions 4:43, 5:90 RAZI-FULL (رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم): Razi cites the Prophetic saying that wine is the mother of evils, since loss of reason opens the way to every vice. QURTUBI-FULL (عثمان): Al-Nasāʾī transmits from ʿUthmān the warning that wine is the mother of evils, illustrated by a devout man lured by a woman who offers sex, wine, or killing a boy. QURTUBI-FULL (author): The expression ghimār or khimār of people can mean a hidden place or a place within the crowd. BAYDAWI-FULL (author): Some say the excess of harm over benefit is what prohibits wine, since predominant harm entails prohibition; al-Bayḍāwī rejects this as reviving the Muʿtazilite doctrine previously invalidated. BAYDAWI-FULL (author): Wine is categorically forbidden, as are all intoxicants according to most scholars. ABUHAYYAN-FULL (الرازي عن أبي حنيفة): Al-Rāzī reports Abū Ḥanīfa’s definition of khamr as specifically what is made from grapes. ABUHAYYAN-FULL (author): Abū Ḥayyān calls the sound report from Abū Ḥanīfa that even a drop of these drinks is khamr. SAMIN-DURR (author): It can also encompass the bad words and deeds produced by drinking, and all involved with wine from grape to consumption; the Prophet cursed wine and ten parties connected with it. SAMIN-DURR (author): The famous derivation connects khamr with covering: it veils the mind like a woman's veil. خامري حضاجر is an admonition to a fool to hide; the segment also cites the hadith about covering vessels and poetry using خمر الطريق for concealing vegetation. SAMIN-DURR (author): A second derivation says wine is covered until it ferments and strengthens, illustrated by the hadith about covering vessels. SAMIN-DURR (author): A fourth derivation connects khamr with leaving something until it matures, as dough is left to rise or an opinion until its sound course becomes clear. On all four accounts it is originally a verbal noun used for an active or passive participle. BIQAI-FULL (author): Wine was then allowed during the night of fasting; people commonly drank date and raisin nabīdh, which could ferment in the hot climate. BIQAI-FULL (الحرالي): Harali relates khamr to covering: vegetation hides what is visible, while wine conceals the intellect and obscures the boundary between clear reason and brute animality. BURSEVI (المفسرون): Commentators describe a four-stage prohibition: the first, Meccan verse mentioned wine made from dates and grapes alongside good provision, and Muslims initially drank it as lawful. | mentions 16:67 BURSEVI (author): As outward wine comes from grapes, dates, raisins, and grains, inward wine comes from heedlessness, desire, caprice, and love of the world; these intoxicate the human soul and intellect and are sinful. ALUSI-FULL (الشيخان وأبو داؤد والترمذي والنسائي): Reports in the two Ṣaḥīḥs and Sunan collections state that every intoxicant is wine. ALUSI-FULL (author): A report says wine is forbidden in itself, with a version adding that its small and large amounts are alike; intoxication rules govern other drinks. ALUSI-FULL (أبو داؤد): Abū Dāwūd reports that the prohibition was revealed for wine made from five sources—grapes, dates, wheat, barley, and corn—and that wine is what clouds the mind. ALUSI-FULL (أبو هريرة): Muslim reports from Abū Hurayra that wine comes from these two trees, with the Prophet pointing to the vine and date-palm. ALUSI-FULL (بعضهم): A middle position says Arabic uses “wine” literally for intoxicating grape juice and figuratively for other sources, while Islamic law made it a technical term for every intoxicant, like prayer, fasting, and alms. ELMALILI (author): Elmalılı renders al-khamr as wine and al-maysir as gambling. ELMALILI (İbn Ömer): Ibn ʿUmar reports that at the time the wine prohibition was revealed, hamr came from grapes, dates, wheat, barley, and millet, and meant what clouds the intellect. ELMALILI (Nu‘mân b. Beşir): A report in Abū Dāwūd attributes to the Prophet the statement that wine is made from grapes, dates, honey, wheat, and barley.
