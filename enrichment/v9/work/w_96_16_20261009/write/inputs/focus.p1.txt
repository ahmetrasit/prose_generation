turns on: The transmitted case ending and whether the phrase apposes the earlier noun or begins an implied clause or invective.
- 96:16/q06/p1 All three are genitive, with nāṣiya apposed to the preceding genitive noun.
  reasons: Abū Ḥayyān calls this the majority reading; the second noun repeats the earlier forelock.

# 96:16/q08 How is the hamza in khāṭiʾah treated in recitation? [خَاطِئَةٍۢ · readings]
turns on: Whether a transmitted route applies the regular hamza substitution or preserves it.
- 96:16/q08/p1 Abū Jaʿfar's ten-reader form replaces the hamza after kasrah with yāʾ, without changing the meaning.
  reasons: Al-Nashr describes this as Abū Jaʿfar's common treatment of open hamza after kasrah. Qiraat-Er calls it a recognized dialectal treatment and semantically identical. Al-Nashr compares broader substitutions: Abū Jaʿfar and Warsh generally use wāw after ḍammah before open hamza; most Ibn Wardān routes do so in yuʾayyid; al-Azraq substitutes in muʾadhdhin; Ḥafṣ replaces final root hamza in huzuwan at ten places and in kufuwan.
  holders (6 notes): NASHR(أبو جعفر، ابن وردان، حفص، ورش); QIRAAT-ER
  notes: NASHR:v1p395#2/r1 NASHR:v1p395#2/r2 NASHR:v1p395#2/r3 NASHR:v1p395#2/r4 NASHR:v1p395#2/r5 QIRAAT-ER:96:16:3#1/r1
- 96:16/q08/p2 Some Ibn Wardān and Zayd routes retain the hamza in khāṭiʾah.
  reasons: Al-Nashr lists parallel preservation in al-khāṭiʾah, miʾah and fiʾah. It also notes exceptional retention in yuʾayyid by some Ibn Wardān routes, apparently to avoid three successive weak letters after doubled yāʾ, and al-Aṣbahānī's preservation for Warsh in muʾadhdhin, apparently linked to fa-adhdhin. The author accepts and has read both hamza and substitution in the separate word mawṭiyan.

  notes: NASHR:v1p395#2/r2 NASHR:v1p395#2/r3 NASHR:v1p395#2/r5 NASHR:v1p395#2/r6

# 96:16/q09 What makes the forelock 'lying'? [كَٰذِبَةٍ · interpretation]
turns on: Which of Abū Jahl's false claims the adjective singles out.
- 96:16/q09/p1 Its owner lies in speech, especially in denying Muḥammad's mission and disparaging him.
  reasons: Māwardī and others distinguish false speech from wrongful acts. Al-Rāzī says Abū Jahl denied God's sending Muḥammad and called him a sorcerer, liar, or no prophet; Bursevī repeats denial and the charge of sorcery. Al-Kalbī adds lying about God and polytheism; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ links the term to Qur'anic disbelief and misguidance.

# 96:16/q10 What does khāṭiʾa mean here? [خَاطِئَةٍۢ · meaning]
turns on: Whether khāṭiʾa entails culpable intention or can name inadvertent wrongdoing here.
- 96:16/q10/p1 It means a culpable sinner who deliberately rebels against God, unlike an unaccountable mistaken person.
  reasons: Al-Rāzī contrasts khāṭiʾ with mukhṭiʾ and cites 69:37 for the wrongdoer; Qurṭubī likewise contrasts punishable deliberate wrongdoing with an unintended mistake. Ibn ʿĀshūr derives the active participle from khaṭiʾa, 'to commit a sin.' Al-Kalbī speaks of polytheism; Bursevī cites harming Muḥammad; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ hears disbelief and misguidance in Qur'anic usage.
  holders (9 notes): RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNASHUR; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); BURSEVI; BINTSHATI
  notes: RAZI:96:15/r27 RAZI:96:15/r28 RAZI-FULL:v32p24/r4 QURTUBI:96:16/r2 QURTUBI-FULL:96:15-16/r8 IBNASHUR:96:16/r2 WAHIDI-BASIT:v24p178/r1 BURSEVI:v10p366#2/r11 BINTSHATI:v2p31/r7
- 96:16/q10/p2 It can include unintentional wrongdoing alongside deliberate lies, reflecting Abū Jahl's corrupt disposition.
  reasons: Biqāʿī assigns deliberateness to his lying but says his other errors may be without intent, because most of his conduct is unsound from his bad nature.

  notes: BIQAI:96:16/r2 BIQAI-FULL:v8p486/r6
