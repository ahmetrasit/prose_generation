# 13:9: 15 questions

## 13:9/q01 What do the unseen and the witnessed designate?  [ٱلْغَيْبِ وَٱلشَّهَٰدَةِ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the contrast is drawn by sensory access, privacy, existence, creaturely knowledge, time, or inward capacity; several sources treat these as instances of a comprehensive pair.
- 13:9/q01/p1 What lies beyond creatures' perception and what is present to their senses. — Muqatil includes the fetus while hidden in the womb and when visible, as well as everything else. Ibn Abbas in Wahidi and Razi, al-Rummani in Biqai, and Nasafi frame the contrast relative to creatures. Jishumi, Ibn Atiyya, Razi, Baydawi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi, Alusi and Ibn Ashur specify sensory perception; Bursevi includes concealed knowledge, secrets, the afterlife, perceptible beings and the world. Wahidi, Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi and Ibn Ashur explain that abstract or verbal nouns stand for the absent and witnessed things; Ibn Ashur calls shahada passive in sense and cites 69:38–39. Alusi says the pair intensifies comprehensiveness. Ibn Ashur connects unseen with the beginning of al-Baqara. Ibn al-Jawzi sends readers to 6:73; Asad to his surah 6 note 65; Kuranyolu to 2:3. [24 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(آخرون,ابن عباس,الواحدي); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(الرماني); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 13:9/q01/p2 The secret or private and the public. — Ibn Abbas gives this gloss in Ibn Abi Hatim, al-Durr and Alusi; al-Hasan is reported by Tabrisi. Sulami's report from some interpreters includes a person's concealed thoughts before they appear and intentions behind deeds as they become manifest. Itqan also says hidden and witnessed. [6 notes: IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); SULAMI(بعضهم); TABRISI; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); ITQAN; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 13:9/q01/p3 The nonexistent and the existent. — Tabrisi lists this alternative and prefers a general scope including every object of knowledge, existent or nonexistent. Abu Hayyan, Alusi and Elmalili also transmit or state the pairing. Razi reports a variant formulation: the hidden is what is known, while the manifest is what exists. Ibn Ashur instead speaks of the two classes of existent things. [6 notes: TABRISI; RAZI-FULL(بعضهم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); ELMALILI; TABRISI+]
- 13:9/q01/p4 What creatures do not know and what they know. — Razi reports this formulation; Wahidi says much of the witnessed is known to creatures. Tabatabai says the distinction is relational: one being may see what lies beyond another's limits. Bursevi reports the view that the attribution of unseen knowledge is relative to humans, since nothing is unseen to God. Alusi objects to using that relativity to deny that God knows the unseen. [4 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; RAZI-FULL(آخرون); BURSEVI(بعضهم); TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)-]
- 13:9/q01/p5 What has not yet occurred and what has already occurred. — Abu Abd Allah's report through Tha'laba ibn Maymun gives the temporal contrast. Tabatabai limits the first term to potential reality awaiting actualization, not sheer nonexistence, and calls the report one instance of the wider concept. [2 notes: TABATABAI(أبو عبد الله)]
- 13:9/q01/p6 Latent human receptivities and the perfections already manifest in them. — Qashani reads the pair as unseen capacities and the realized perfections corresponding to them. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 13:9/q02 What is the scope and implication of God's knowledge here?  [عَٰلِمُ ٱلْغَيْبِ وَٱلشَّهَٰدَةِ · theology]
turns on: Whether the verse's unqualified attribution of knowledge applies at every theological level, and how creaturely limits relate to divine knowledge.
- 13:9/q02/p1 God knows every hidden and manifest thing without limitation. — Thalabi, Baghawi and Ibn Kathir state the comprehensive claim; Ibn Kathir says nothing is hidden and all submit willingly or unwillingly. Tabrisi expressly prefers every knowable, existent or nonexistent. Razi divides knowables into existent and nonexistent, each necessary or contingent, with every property known; Imam al-Haramayn says they and their possible states are infinite, down to each possible location and quality of an atom. Bursevi takes the definite article as comprehensive. Ibn Ashur says hidden and manifest exhaust existing things. Kuranyolu contrasts God's infinite, complete, certain knowledge from conception to death with human knowledge that is limited, incomplete and changeable. Islahi says people know God's plans only insofar as He reveals them. Tabatabai says God's unbounded existence encompasses all, so both categories are witnessed to Him. [12 notes: THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; RAZI-FULL(إمام الحرمين); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ELMALILI; TABRISI+]
- 13:9/q02/p2 Knowledge of the unseen belongs to God alone; creatures cannot claim certainty about it. — Qurtubi says the verse praises exclusive divine knowledge; physicians can infer from signs and use experience, since custom may fail, but certain claims from such indications are unbelief because knowledge does not change. Tabatabai reasons that finite beings cannot encompass what exceeds their existential limits. Islahi warns against comparing divine knowledge with human limits. [4 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 13:9/q02/p3 At the level of pure essence beyond all relations, knowledge is not predicated; it pertains to attributes and determinations. — Bursevi reports this view from some Sufi masters: without any scientific relation the pure essence has no unseen knowledge in that respect. Alusi rejects the related claim that God does not know the unseen because nothing is unseen to Him and nonexistent things are witnessed, calling it audacious and contrary to 13:9. [1 notes: BURSEVI(بعض سادات الصوفية); ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)-]

## 13:9/q03 How is ʿālim syntactically construed?  [عَٰلِمُ · grammar]
turns on: The case and attachment of ʿālim to the prior sentence or the following divine names.
- 13:9/q03/p1 It is the predicate of an omitted subject, understood as 'He is Knower.' — Nahhas, al-Samin, Bursevi and Alusi give this nominative analysis. [4 notes: NAHHAS; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 13:9/q03/p2 It is a subject whose predicate is al-kabīr al-mutaʿāl. — Nahhas and al-Samin give this analysis; Alusi specifies al-kabīr as the predicate. [3 notes: NAHHAS; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]
- 13:9/q03/p3 It is an adjective or additional predicate linked to the preceding construction. — Nahhas allows an adjectival analysis; Alusi gives a second-predicate analysis and says al-kabīr can also be a second predicate. [3 notes: NAHHAS; ALUSI-FULL]
- 13:9/q03/p4 It is accusative as an expression of praise. — Nahhas offers the accusative analysis; Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and Alusi report Zayd ibn Ali's accusative reading explicitly as praise. [4 notes: NAHHAS; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(زيد بن علي); SAMIN-DURR(زيد بن علي); ALUSI-FULL(زيد بن علي)]
- 13:9/q03/p5 It is genitive in apposition. — Nahhas gives this additional case analysis. [1 notes: NAHHAS]

## 13:9/q04 What reading of ʿālim is reported?  [عَٰلِمُ · readings]
- 13:9/q04/p1 Zayd ibn Ali reads ʿālim with an accusative ending for praise. — Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and Alusi report Zayd's reading and explain its accusative as praise; Nahhas also allows the grammatical analysis. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(زيد بن علي); SAMIN-DURR(زيد بن علي); ALUSI-FULL(زيد بن علي)]

## 13:9/q05 Why is the unseen named before the witnessed?  [ٱلْغَيْبِ وَٱلشَّهَٰدَةِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the witnessed is delayed because it is more numerous or because rhetorical ascent and equal divine knowledge explain the order; the author disputes the numerical premise.
- 13:9/q05/p1 The witnessed objects are more numerous, so the more praiseworthy witnessed is delayed. — Ibn Abd al-Salam poses the problem of the usual practice of delaying the more praiseworthy and answers by claiming God has more witnessed objects. Biqai's Burhan author objects that the unseen is innumerable. [2 notes: BURHAN(ابن عبد السلام); BURHAN-]
- 13:9/q05/p2 The order rises to the more praiseworthy witnessed while showing both classes are equal in God's knowledge. — The Burhan author argues that unseen things are innumerable, cites 16:8, and distinguishes an importance-first poetic maxim from this rule: the former concerns distinct objects or purposes, while delayed praise concerns two qualities of one thing. A cited poet illustrates the importance-first maxim. [3 notes: BURHAN(الشاعر); BURHAN+]

## 13:9/q06 What does al-kabīr mean here?  [ٱلْكَبِيرُ · meaning]
turns on: Whether greatness is explained chiefly through rank and majesty, sovereign power, knowledge, incomparability, or inward effects.
- 13:9/q06/p1 God is supremely great in majesty and rank, with everything else beneath Him. — Muqatil says none is greater; Wahidi says divine rank and entitlement to exalted attributes make every other greatness small; Ibn Atiyya calls it unrestricted majesty; Ibn al-Jawzi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Alusi and Elmalili combine rank with the inferiority of everything else. Damghani glosses it great or majestic and notes 4:34. Ibn Ashur explains figurative size and multitude as established metaphors for abstract majesty; Tabatabai likewise traces the word from bodily size to God's possession and encompassing of every perfection, with more beyond. Al-Harali in Biqai describes the evident disparity of degree: only God has absolute greatness, and human pretensions end in humiliation at resurrection. Ibn Kathir says He is greater than all. [17 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; DAMGHANI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(أبو الحسن الحرالي); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 13:9/q06/p2 God is the sovereign, master, and one able to do all things. — Nahhas and Tabrisi give sovereignty and omnipotence; Razi says greatness cannot refer to bodily size or volume and instead denotes power and divine measures. [3 notes: NAHHAS; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL]
- 13:9/q06/p3 His greatness entails that nothing escapes His knowledge. — Baydawi and Bursevi link majesty to all-encompassing knowledge; Bursevi also calls Him majestic in status. [2 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 13:9/q06/p4 His perfect attributes place everything beneath Him and beyond resemblance to creatures. — Tabrisi gives this as an alternative; Ibn al-Jawzi adds that God is unlike created things. Alusi reports an interpretation of both names as disavowing creaturely traits ascribed by unbelievers, citing 37:159. [3 notes: TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; ALUSI-FULL]
- 13:9/q06/p5 He encompasses all capacities and unfailingly grants each its due. — Qashani reads greatness as never failing to supply what a capacity requires. [1 notes: QASHANI]
- 13:9/q06/p6 His greatness fills gnostics' hearts, making all else small. — Jaʿfar's inward explanation is reported by Sulami; Ibn Ata says God is great in essence. [2 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء,جعفر)]

## 13:9/q07 What does al-mutaʿāl mean here?  [ٱلْمُتَعَالِ · meaning]
turns on: Whether exaltation is explained as sovereign power, transcendence from created traits, absolute inaccessibility, or an inward account of divine giving.
- 13:9/q07/p1 God is exalted above all creation and all things by unrivaled power and dominion. — Muqatil, Tabari, Thalabi, Baghawi, Tabrisi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Baydawi, Bursevi and Elmalili stress power over all; Tabari says all things, Ibn al-Jawzi reports al-Khattabi's 'above creation,' and Tabrisi adds no equal. Jishumi says al-mutaʿāl and al-ʿālī are synonymous or denote incomparable power; Tabrisi reports the same equivalence and an omnipotence no equal shares. Ibn Kathir adds all-subduing power, before which all submit. Tabatabai distinguishes ʿAlī as above and ruling all from mutaʿāl as ultimate height dominating from every direction. Ibn Ashur gives complete invincibility, citing 16:3, as one explanation. [18 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; NAHHAS; THALABI; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الخطابي); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 13:9/q07/p2 God transcends created attributes, defects, impermissible descriptions, and polytheists' claims. — Al-Hasan in Wahidi and Ibn al-Jawzi specifies the polytheists' sayings; al-Azhari specifies heretics' impiety. Razi extends transcendence to all that is impermissible in essence, attributes and acts; Tabrisi likewise covers acts and polytheist claims. Ibn al-Jawzi and Nasafi mention creaturely attributes; Baydawi, Abu Hayyan, al-Kawashi in Bursevi, and Elmalili concur. Ibn Ashur gives freedom from deficiencies as an alternative. Alusi reports both names understood against disbelievers' creaturely descriptions, citing 37:159. [15 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(الأزهري,الحسن); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الحسن); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI(الكواشي); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 13:9/q07/p3 God's elevation is absolute, beyond approach, comprehension, definition, or comparison. — Jishumi says He surpasses every account; Biqai says no approach in essence, attribute or action, while al-Harali says no judgment or proof reaches Him: imagined arguments fall before His decisive proof, and He is exalted in knowledge, judgment and argument, citing 42:16 and 6:149. Alusi names essence, knowledge and every attribute. Asad calls His exaltedness infinite over everything existing or possible, cites 6:100, and records Zamakhshari's point about human definitions. Asad notes this divine attribute occurs only here in the Qur'an. Tabatabai describes height surpassing all heights. [8 notes: JISHUMI; BIQAI-FULL(أبو الحسن الحرالي); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES(Zamakhshari)]
- 13:9/q07/p4 His continuous effusion gives full gifts as soon as readiness arises. — Qashani says God neither delays once a capacity is ready nor gives less than it requires. [1 notes: QASHANI]
- 13:9/q07/p5 Approach to Him depends entirely on His generosity. — Jaʿfar, reported by Sulami, offers this inward sense; Ibn Ata describes Him as exalted in attributes. [2 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء,جعفر)]

## 13:9/q08 What does the form of al-mutaʿāl convey?  [ٱلْمُتَعَالِ · grammar]
- 13:9/q08/p1 It derives from ʿuluww, elevation, in the mutafāʿil or tafaʿul form. — Tabari compares al-mutaqārib from qurb and al-mutadānī from dunūw; Ibn Atiyya derives it from elevation. Ibn Ashur says tafaʿul makes exaltation inherent and necessary rather than conferred; Biqai says the reciprocal form intensifies transcendence. Tabatabai compares the intensive absolute object 'greatly exalted' in 17:43. Jishumi and Tabrisi also report its equivalence to al-ʿālī. [7 notes: TAB-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; JISHUMI; TABRISI; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 13:9/q09 How is the final yāʾ of al-mutaʿāl read?  [ٱلْمُتَعَالِ · readings]
turns on: The transmitted reader and route, regular morphology versus rasm and verse cadence, and disputed reports from Qunbul and Warsh.
- 13:9/q09/p1 It is retained in both connected reading and pause. — Ibn Mujahid names Ibn Kathir and two Abu Amr transmissions; Jishumi and Nashr also name Yaʿqub. Wahidi calls retention the regular form and cites Sibawayh: the definite article favors keeping yāʾ at pause because it survives connection, unlike indefinite qadin. Ibn Atiyya prefers it as regular; Ibn al-Jazari prefers established practice and trusted Qunbul reports over disputed extra-yāʾ reports, against al-Dani's judgment of error. Nashr says both Ibn Kathir transmissions affirm it, despite conflicting Qunbul reports. Qiraat-ER says this completes the active participle morphologically without changing meaning. [16 notes: IBNMUJAHID(ابن كثير، أبو عمرو عن عبد الوارث، أبو عمرو عن أبي زيد); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن كثير,سيبويه); JISHUMI(ابن كثير ويعقوب); NASAFI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن كثير وأبو عمرو); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن كثير); RAZI-FULL(ابن كثير); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن كثير وأبو عمرو); SAMIN-DURR(ابن كثير وأبو عمرو); NASHR(أبو عمرو الداني,ابن كثير، ويعقوب); QIRAAT-ER; IBNATIYYA-FULL+; NASHR(ابن كثير)+]
- 13:9/q09/p2 It is omitted in both connection and pause. — Ibn Mujahid, Jishumi, Ibn Atiyya, Ibn al-Jawzi, Razi, Abu Hayyan and al-Samin attribute omission to the other readers; Jishumi and Razi cite ease, while Abu Hayyan and al-Samin note agreement with the written form. Sibawayh, cited by Wahidi, Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and al-Samin, allows loss at verse endings by analogy to rhyme and indefinite forms, though he regards loss in connected speech as unexpected or poor; Ibn Atiyya adds that tanwin alternates with the definite article. Nahhas and Ibn Ashur cite verse endings, Ibn Ashur noting the cadence of wāl and al-āṣāl (13:11, 13:15) and that retention is more analogical while omission is common Arabic. Nashr records Qunbul reports of omission in both, including a Shanbudh route, but Ibn al-Jazari adopts affirmation. [15 notes: IBNMUJAHID(ابن كثير، أبو عمرو عن عبد الوارث، أبو عمرو عن أبي زيد); NAHHAS; WAHIDI-BASIT(سيبويه); JISHUMI(الباقون); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الباقون,سيبويه); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن شنبوذ عن قنبل); RAZI-FULL(ابن كثير); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن كثير وأبو عمرو); SAMIN-DURR(ابن كثير وأبو عمرو); NASHR(ابن كثير,ابن كثير، ويعقوب); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 13:9/q09/p3 It is retained at pause but omitted in connection. — Ibn al-Jawzi reports this from Ibn Shanabudh through Qunbul, alongside the other readers' complete omission. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن شنبوذ عن قنبل)]
- 13:9/q09/p4 A Qunbul report omits it at pause only. — Nashr records this as a conflicting report alongside complete omission, while Ibn al-Jazari chooses affirmation in both states. [1 notes: NASHR(ابن كثير)]
- 13:9/q09/p5 Warsh may retain or omit yāʾ at pause in this category. — Abu Hayyan reports al-Azraq's account of Warsh's choice for indefinite, declinable words in this category. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو يعقوب الأزرق عن ورش)]

## 13:9/q10 What does the pairing of divine knowledge, greatness, and exaltation convey?  [عَٰلِمُ ٱلْغَيْبِ وَٱلشَّهَٰدَةِ ٱلْكَبِيرُ ٱلْمُتَعَالِ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the two names chiefly reinforce universal knowledge and power or jointly negate false creaturely descriptions.
- 13:9/q10/p1 Knowledge, greatness, and power together mean nothing escapes or overcomes God. — Tabatabai says the combination signals total encompassing and dominance. Al-Tibi in Alusi says the names add greatness and power to knowledge, countering Christian and polytheist claims; he connects this with 13:8. Biqai says preceding descriptions exhaust what human reason can bear, while the reality is absolute greatness and transcendence. Sulami's Ibn Ata says true knowledge belongs to God, great in essence and exalted in attributes. [4 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء); ALUSI-FULL(الطيبي); BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 13:9/q10/p2 Both names jointly reject created attributes ascribed to God by disbelievers. — Alusi reports this alternative with 37:159; al-Tibi's explanation also names Christian and polytheist claims. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الطيبي)]

## 13:9/q11 How does this declaration develop the preceding discussion of knowledge, signs, and resurrection?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the immediate emphasis is comprehensive knowledge, proof of divine power, or the reason and timing for withholding requested signs.
- 13:9/q11/p1 It generalizes the preceding hidden particulars and confirms that God knows all things. — Abu Hayyan says the passage moves from hidden particulars to everything seen and unseen. Alusi says the description is evidence for 13:8's assertion of complete knowledge. Ibn Atiyya says 'everything with Him is measured' is a general statement about all things subject to determination. Ibn Ashur calls this phrase a summary of the reach of knowledge and recalls the unseen at the start of al-Baqara. Study Quran points to 6:59 and 59:22 for the same divine description. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 13:9/q11/p2 The attributes establish God's ability to raise the dead, give requested signs, and punish. — Razi grounds these capacities in complete knowledge, power and transcendence. Biqai compares resurrection to first creation and says distinguishing scattered dust is less astonishing than distinguishing reproductive fluid from mixed water. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 13:9/q11/p3 Requested signs are withheld because the requesters are obstinate or because the signs would not benefit them. — Kashshaf says differing signs are deliberate wisdom and God would grant the requested sign were it beneficial. Biqai says the obstinate requesters do not seek guidance, so God points instead to familiar acts showing knowledge, power and the possibility of resurrection. Elmalili says God knows their desire for guidance or their obstinacy. Razi reports that some explain the delay by divine wisdom, others by divine will; both accept His power to give signs and punish. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 13:9/q11/p4 The timing of signs and punishment follows divine will. — Razi attributes this account of the delay to one group, contrasting it with an explanation by wisdom. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 13:9/q12 How does this verse serve the sūra's overall purpose?  [* · links]
- 13:9/q12/p1 It supports the sūra's portrayal of true revelation whose effects vary with its recipients. — Biqai compares revelation's sound and fame with thunder: it may guide or leave hearers blind, as lightning and rain may help, fail or harm according to where they fall. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 13:9/q13 What revelation setting is given for this sūra?  [* · sciences]
- 13:9/q13/p1 The entire sūra is Makkan, with no Madinan verse. — Islahi rejects the codices' Madinan classification in favor of this account. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR+]

## 13:9/q14 What are tafsīr and taʾwīl, and how do they differ?  [* · meaning]
turns on: The source of interpretation, degree of certainty, unit of explanation, current usage, and etymology.
- 13:9/q14/p1 They are synonymous. — Alusi reports this view from Abu Ubayda. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أبو عبيدة)]
- 13:9/q14/p2 Tafsīr is broader and mainly explains words; taʾwīl treats meanings and whole constructions in divine books. — Alusi reports al-Raghib: tafsir applies to divine and other books, ta'wil to divine books specifically. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الراغب)]
- 13:9/q14/p3 Tafsīr asserts God's intended meaning decisively; taʾwīl prefers a possible meaning without certainty. — Alusi attributes this distinction to al-Maturidi. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الماتريدي)]
- 13:9/q14/p4 Tafsīr relies on transmitted reports; taʾwīl relies on reasoned analysis. — Alusi records this as another proposed distinction. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
- 13:9/q14/p5 In current usage taʾwīl often means spiritual allusions and unveiled insights. — Alusi says earlier conventional definitions do not match this usage; if distinctions are meant literally, they require reconsideration, although every unveiling involves a return and every return an unveiling. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 13:9/q14/p6 Taʾwīl derives from 'return,' not from siyāsa, 'management.' — Alusi rejects deriving it from the interpreter directing speech and placing its meaning. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 13:9/q15 Why is Qur'anic interpretation necessary?  [* · interpretation]
- 13:9/q15/p1 The Qur'an's guidance and rulings require explanation and God's enabling grace to understand. — Alusi calls legal rulings the basis of enduring happiness and the straight path. Even eloquent Companions repeatedly asked the Prophet and sometimes misunderstood, as Adi ibn Hatim did the white and black thread in 2:187; present readers need interpretation at least as much. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
