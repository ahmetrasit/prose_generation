# 89:9: 24 questions

## 89:9/q01 Who were Thamūd?  [وَثَمُودَ · referent]
turns on: The notes differ on genealogy; Elmalılı says ancestry traditions vary.
- 89:9/q01/p1 The ancient Arab people of Ṣāliḥ, associated with al-Ḥijr. — Muqātil, Ibn ʿAbbās, and al-Qurṭubī identify Ṣāliḥ's people. Bursevī places al-Ḥijr between the Hijaz and Tabuk, calls them idol worshippers and descendants of Thamūd, brother of Jadīs; al-Ālūsī traces them through ʿĀbir, Iram, Sām, and Noah. Elmalılı notes divergent genealogies and leaves the line uncertain. The Study Quran locates them in northern Arabia and cites their narratives elsewhere. [15 notes: MUQATIL; TAB(ابن عباس); BURSEVI; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; BINTSHATI; STUDYQURAN]
- 89:9/q01/p2 Thamūd descended from ʿĀd and inherited its building arts. — Iṣlāḥī calls them the second ʿĀd and says they carved mountain homes in Wādī al-Qurā. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 89:9/q02 What is the origin of the name Thamūd?  [وَثَمُودَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether its source is Arabic thamd or a foreign name.
- 89:9/q02/p1 It is Arabic, related to thamd, a small unreplenished supply of water. — Al-Ālūsī adduces mithmūd for one whose fluid or wealth is exhausted. [2 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 89:9/q02/p2 It is a foreign proper name. — Al-Rāghib explains its diptote form by foreign origin and proper-name status; al-Ālūsī reports this. [2 notes: ALUSI(الراغب); ALUSI-FULL]

## 89:9/q03 How is Thamūd declined and read?  [وَثَمُودَ · readings]
turns on: Whether Thamūd functions as a feminine tribal proper name or a name for the people.
- 89:9/q03/p1 It is normally indeclinable without tanwīn. — Al-Naḥḥās treats it as a tribal name; al-Ālūsī calls it a feminine proper name, while Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it the known people's name. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn contrast the majority reading with Ibn Waththāb. [7 notes: NAHHAS; ABUHAYYAN(ابن وثاب); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 89:9/q03/p2 Ibn Waththāb reads it with tanwīn, declining it as a people or clan. — Reported by Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn, and al-Ālūsī. Al-Naḥḥās also allows declension when the word names the people; he says tanwīn can disappear before two adjacent sukūns. [8 notes: IBNATIYYA(يحيى بن وثاب); IBNATIYYA-FULL(يحيى بن وثاب); ABUHAYYAN(ابن وثاب); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; NAHHAS]

## 89:9/q04 How does the conjunction attach Thamūd?  [وَثَمُودَ · grammar]
- 89:9/q04/p1 It carries forward the oath preposition: 'and by Thamūd'. — Al-Baghawī explains the conjunction as extending the earlier oath construction. [1 notes: BAGHAWI]
- 89:9/q04/p2 It coordinates Thamūd with ʿĀd. — Al-Ālūsī names the preceding people as the coordinate. [1 notes: ALUSI]

## 89:9/q05 How does alladhīna attach, and why is it masculine plural?  [ٱلَّذِينَ · grammar]
turns on: Attachment or detachment of the relative, and grammatical treatment of a tribal name.
- 89:9/q05/p1 It may be an attached genitive adjective. — Al-Naḥḥās gives this as one iʿrāb; al-Samīn says its attachment follows the analyses of the preceding relative. [2 notes: NAHHAS; SAMIN-DURR]
- 89:9/q05/p2 It may be detached in the accusative, 'I mean those who'. — Al-Naḥḥās gives the implicit verb; al-Samīn allows detachment. [2 notes: NAHHAS; SAMIN-DURR]
- 89:9/q05/p3 It may be detached in the nominative, 'they are those who'. — Al-Naḥḥās supplies this understood subject-predicate construction; al-Samīn allows detachment. [2 notes: NAHHAS; SAMIN-DURR]
- 89:9/q05/p4 Its masculine plural agreement treats the tribe as people. — Ibn ʿĀshūr describes a shift from expected feminine agreement for stylistic variety. [2 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 89:9/q06 What does jābū mean as an Arabic verb?  [جَابُوا۟ · meaning]
turns on: The basic cutting sense is shared; the disagreement concerns how 'answer' derives from it.
- 89:9/q06/p1 Its basic sense is cutting, piercing, or entering through; it also describes traversing land. — Al-Ṭabarī cites al-Nābigha's yajūbu; al-Layth compares cutting a garment opening; al-Farrāʾ cites yajību and travel across lands; other commentators cite a camel's jābat in poetry, and the garment's jayb or ijtāba. Al-Rāghib begins with a hollow in land and extends the verb to crossing land. [33 notes: MAJAZ; TAB; TAB-FULL(النابغة); NAHHAS; WAHIDI-BASIT(الليث، الفراء); RAZI(الفراء,الليث); RAZI-FULL(الفراء,الليث); QURTUBI(الشاعر); QURTUBI-FULL(الشاعر); TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN(الشاعر); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الشاعر); IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI; DURR(أمية); ITQAN; ALUSI(شاعر); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(أمية); BINTSHATI+]
- 89:9/q06/p2 The related word jawāb is a reply because it bridges speaker and listener. — Al-Rāghib pictures a gap cut from the respondent's mouth to the listener's ear. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports and rejects that account. [4 notes: ALUSI(الراغب); ALUSI-FULL(الراغب); BINTSHATI(الراغب); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الراغب); BINTSHATI-IJAZ-]
- 89:9/q06/p3 Jawāb's connection with cutting is figurative decisive penetration or resolution. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ finds al-Rāghib's physical gap implausible and compares other Qur'anic uses of cutting and naqb at 50:36. [3 notes: BINTSHATI(الراغب); BINTSHATI-IJAZ; BINTSHATI-IJAZ+]

## 89:9/q07 What did Thamūd do to the rock?  [جَابُوا۟ ٱلصَّخْرَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the cutting makes rock dwellings, supplies stone after travel, or carries/stores water.
- 89:9/q07/p1 They cut, pierced, or hollowed rock and mountains to make homes. — Al-Ṭabarī, al-Māwardī, Ibn ʿAbbās and others cite 15:82, 26:149, or 7:74; Muqātil describes large mountain houses with stone doors and locks. Ibn ʿAbbās also mentions pools and other buildings. Qatāda says bored and carved; al-Ḍaḥḥāk says cut stones. Poetry about powerful arms striking hard rock, including Umayya's ear-piercing verb, supports cutting. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ prefers this and rejects other readings. [90 notes: MUQATIL; FARRA; MAJAZ; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB(ابن زيد,ابن عباس,الضحاك,قائل غير مسمى,قتادة,مجاهد); TAB-FULL(ابن زيد,ابن عباس,الضحاك,قتادة,مجاهد); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس,مجاهد); THALABI; MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL(الشاعر); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس,الفراء); WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(مجاهد); BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA(كثير من المفسرين); IBNATIYYA-FULL(جمهور المفسرين); TABRISI(ابن عباس); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); QURTUBI(المفسرون,قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(المفسرون,قيل); BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR(ابن عباس ومجاهد وقتادة والضحاك وابن زيد,شاعر غير مسمى); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس ومجاهد وقتادة والضحاك وابن زيد); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; DURR(أمية,ابن عباس,مجاهد); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,مجاهد); ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI(İbn Abbas); QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI(الفخر الرازي); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن عباس); CORPUSCORANICUM; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BINTSHATI+]
- 89:9/q07/p2 They traversed the valley to obtain stone, or crossed through it. — Al-Māwardī cites a poem of a camel carrying a load through a land; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports crossing as an alternative. [4 notes: MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; BINTSHATI(بعضهم); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(قيل); BINTSHATI-]
- 89:9/q07/p3 They cut through the valley's rock to channel water or form a storage basin. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya's group describes bringing water through split rock as evidence of strength and ambition; Elmalılı says the water entered a rock reservoir. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports the feat as requiring strength and understanding. [7 notes: IBNATIYYA(قوم); IBNATIYYA-FULL(قوم); ABUHAYYAN(قيل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ELMALILI; BINTSHATI(بعضهم); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(قيل); BINTSHATI-]

## 89:9/q08 What is reported about the scale and novelty of Thamūd's stonework?  [جَابُوا۟ ٱلصَّخْرَ · reports]
turns on: The reports give different building counts and vary in how much detail they claim.
- 89:9/q08/p1 They were the first stone or mountain carvers and built 1,700 stone cities. — Reports vary between mountains, rock, marble, and carved figures. Al-Ālūsī doubts the numerical building claim; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ rejects accumulated extravagant historical details as intrusive lore. Al-Qurṭubī additionally gives 2.7 million houses. [20 notes: KASHSHAF(قيل); KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI(المفسرون); QURTUBI-FULL(المفسرون); NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN(قيل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI(أبو حيان); BIQAI-FULL(أبو حيان); BURSEVI; ALUSI(قيل); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI(الفخر الرازي); BINTSHATI-]
- 89:9/q08/p2 They were the first stone carvers and built 2,700,000 stone houses. — Al-Thaʿlabī attributes this number to historians; al-Qurṭubī combines it with 1,700 cities. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ objects to later extravagant tales. [3 notes: THALABI(أهل السير); QURTUBI(المفسرون); QURTUBI-FULL(المفسرون); BINTSHATI-]
- 89:9/q08/p3 They were the first stone carvers, without a stated building count. — Jishumī attributes the claim to Mujāhid; Ibn ʿĀshūr notes it as a report. [2 notes: JISHUMI(مجاهد); IBNASHUR]

## 89:9/q09 What does al-ṣakhr denote?  [ٱلصَّخْرَ · meaning]
- 89:9/q09/p1 Large or hard stones, including mountain rock. — Al-Baghawī gives ṣakhra as the singular; Ibn ʿĀshūr says large stones. The commentators repeatedly describe the rock as hard mountain stone. [4 notes: BAGHAWI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BURSEVI]

## 89:9/q10 What is the function of bi-l-wād in the sentence?  [بِٱلْوَادِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether bāʾ is locative or instrumental/causal; the locative also permits several attachments.
- 89:9/q10/p1 The bāʾ marks location: they cut rock in the valley. — Al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī allow attachment to jābū or an omitted circumstantial phrase describing the cutters or the rock; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it locative. [5 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 89:9/q10/p2 The bāʾ marks instrument or cause: their valley enabled or occasioned the cutting. — Al-Ālūsī describes a place for water and an arena for the powerful, but judges this less apparent than the locative construal. [2 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 89:9/q11 What does wād mean?  [ٱلْوَادِ · meaning]
- 89:9/q11/p1 A gap or low ground between mountains, whether or not it has water. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya expressly allows a dry valley; al-Qurṭubī includes a passage between hills that carries runoff or affords transit; Ibn ʿĀshūr adds the sense of a large watercourse and forms ending in dāl or quiescent yāʾ. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL(جمهور المفسرين); QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 89:9/q12 Which valley does the verse mean?  [ٱلْوَادِ · referent]
turns on: Whether the named site is Wādī al-Qurā or al-Ḥijr; some sources identify the names.
- 89:9/q12/p1 Wādī al-Qurā, Thamūd's home. — Ibn Isḥāq and Muqātil identify it; Asad places it north of Medina on the South Arabia–Syria caravan route. - 89:9/q12/p2 Wādī al-Ḥijr, the al-Ḥijr/Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ site. — Al-Biqāʿī gives al-Ḥijr beside Wādī al-Qurā; Ibn ʿĀshūr treats these as names for the same home. Corpus Coranicum identifies the rock-cut structures there as tombs and cites later parallels and a poem locating Thamūd there. [5 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 89:9/q13 How is the final yāʾ of al-wādī read?  [ٱلْوَادِ · readings]
turns on: Transmission differs by reader and by pause versus connection; reports on Nāfiʿ, Warsh, and Qunbul vary.
- 89:9/q13/p1 It is omitted in both connected reading and pause. — Al-Baghawī attributes this to the others; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Ibn ʿĀshūr call omission the majority reading. Al-Samīn cites muṣḥaf spelling and end rhyme. [7 notes: BAGHAWI(الآخرون); BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن كثير ويعقوب وورش); IBNATIYYA(ابن كثير وأكثر السبعة ونافع); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن كثير والجمهور ونافع); SAMIN-DURR; IBNASHUR(الجمهور وابن كثير ويعقوب وورش عن نافع); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 89:9/q13/p2 It is retained in both connected reading and pause. — Ibn Kathīr and Yaʿqūb are named by al-Baghawī, Ibn al-Jazarī and Ibn ʿĀshūr; al-Thaʿlabī also names al-Bazzī and al-ʿAwwāsh, and Ibn al-Jawzī reports al-Ḥasan. Al-Samīn also reports Warsh this way, and Qunbul has a both-position report. Ibn ʿĀshūr says oral transmission governs despite a yāʾ-less rasm. [15 notes: THALABI(أبو جعفر); BAGHAWI(ابن كثير ويعقوب); BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن كثير ويعقوب وورش); IBNATIYYA(ابن كثير وأكثر السبعة ونافع); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن كثير والجمهور ونافع); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الحسن); SAMIN-DURR; NASHR; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR(الجمهور وابن كثير ويعقوب وورش عن نافع); IBNASHUR-FULL; QIRAAT-ER; FARISI-HUJJA(ابن كثير)]
- 89:9/q13/p3 It is retained in connected reading but omitted at pause. — Warsh, and in al-Thaʿlabī's account Abū Jaʿfar and Abū Ḥātim, are reported thus. Qunbul has a connected-only report; most Qunbul reports delete at pause. Qiraat-ER describes the two forms without changing root or syntax. [11 notes: THALABI(أبو جعفر); BAGHAWI(ورش); BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن كثير ويعقوب وورش); IBNATIYYA(ابن كثير وأكثر السبعة ونافع); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن كثير والجمهور ونافع); SAMIN-DURR; NASHR; IBNASHUR(الجمهور وابن كثير ويعقوب وورش عن نافع); IBNASHUR-FULL; QIRAAT-ER]

## 89:9/q14 Why is the final weak letter omitted in al-wād?  [ٱلْوَادِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether cadence explains deletion or the spelling belongs to a wider usage.
- 89:9/q14/p1 Deletion fits the verse endings and cadence. — Al-Farrāʾ and Ibn Sinān al-Khafājī say yasri and bi-l-wād have weak letters deleted for rhyme; al-Samīn mentions rhyme and muṣḥaf spelling, and Ibn ʿĀshūr compares 89:4. [4 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الفراء); SAMIN-DURR; IBNASHUR(الجمهور وابن كثير ويعقوب وورش عن نافع); IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI-IJAZ-]
- 89:9/q14/p2 The deletion is a broader Qur'anic usage, not merely rhyme. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites al-Dānī's examples of weak-letter omission within clauses and connected speech at 11:105, 17:11, 54:6, 54:8, 50:41, 79:15–16, 28:30, and 20:12. Al-Naḥḥās mentions a weak letter omitted at a verse ending and a kasra indicating it. [2 notes: NAHHAS; BINTSHATI-IJAZ+]

## 89:9/q15 What happens to the written alif in jābū al-ṣakhr at pause?  [جَابُوا۟ ٱلصَّخْرَ · readings]
- 89:9/q15/p1 All readers restore at pause an alif omitted in connection to avoid adjacent sukūns. — Ibn al-Jazarī treats this as the general rule for written letters lost in liaison and includes jābū al-ṣakhr. [1 notes: NASHR]

## 89:9/q16 What did the Prophet say while passing Thamūd's valley?  [ٱلْوَادِ · reports]
- 89:9/q16/p1 During Tabūk he told the party to hurry through because the valley was cursed. — Abū al-Ashhab through Abū Naḍra supplies a gray horse in al-Māwardī and Bursevī; al-Qurṭubī records the command and its reason. [5 notes: MAWARDI(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); MAWARDI-FULL(أبو الأشهب عن أبي نضرة); QURTUBI(أبو نضرة); QURTUBI-FULL(أبو نضرة); BURSEVI(أبو نضرة)]

## 89:9/q17 Who inhabited the valley after Thamūd, and what later happened there?  [ٱلْوَادِ · reports]
- 89:9/q17/p1 Jews and then Arab groups lived there; the Prophet later conquered it in year seven. — Ibn ʿĀshūr names Qudāʿah, Juhaynah, ʿUdhrah and Balī among the Arab groups; he says local Arabs accepted Islam and Jews agreed to jizya. [4 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 89:9/q18 What does the rock-cutting image convey?  [جَابُوا۟ ٱلصَّخْرَ · interpretation]
- 89:9/q18/p1 It displays formidable strength, skill, and the ability to make enduring dwellings. — Al-Biqāʿī sees an attempt to defy time; Elmalılı calls their skill a divine gift; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says hard rock yielded to their cutting before the blast. Corpus Coranicum sees the monumental works as attempts at self-perpetuation rendered futile by punishment and cites Labīd on transient humans versus enduring mountains and towers. [11 notes: IBNATIYYA(قوم); IBNATIYYA-FULL(قوم); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; BINTSHATI; BINTSHATI-IJAZ; CORPUSCORANICUM(Labīd)]

## 89:9/q19 Why does the sūra turn from ʿĀd to Thamūd and Pharaoh?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes give different roles to the sequence in the sūra.
- 89:9/q19/p1 Thamūd follows the more astonishing ʿĀd because it is nearer in time and similar; Pharaoh then widens a familiar warning. — Al-Biqāʿī contrasts ʿĀd's unusual story with Thamūd's closer circumstances; Ibn ʿĀshūr says Arabs knew ʿĀd and Thamūd and knew Moses' mission to Pharaoh through scriptural communities. Ibn Kathīr says ʿĀd's fuller account had already appeared in al-Aʿrāf; he also reports Ibn Jarīr's view that Iram might be a tribe or town, though he prefers a tribal context. [6 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن إسحاق,ابن جرير)]
- 89:9/q19/p2 The historical examples build suspense for 'Your Lord is indeed watching' or substantiate an omitted oath-answer. — Ibn ʿĀshūr presents both syntactic links for the sequence. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 89:9/q20 What moral lesson does the example of Thamūd teach?  [* · interpretation]
- 89:9/q20/p1 Powerful nations cannot escape God's judgment when they reject signs and transgress. — Al-Biqāʿī says Thamūd answered gardens, springs, crops, dates and the miraculous she-camel with ingratitude and chose blindness. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says the Qur'an omits minor particulars to focus on tyrants' fate and treats later embellished tales as Israelite lore; she cites 11:49 for revelation's unseen history. Iṣlāḥī says God grants respite, then seizes nations and will judge individuals fully; Kuran Yolu says the first audience knew stories or ruins. Jishumī links the chapter to God's watchful judgment in the prior chapter. [12 notes: JISHUMI; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BINTSHATI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; YKHULI-TAJDID; BINTSHATI+]

## 89:9/q21 What happened between Thamūd and Ṣāliḥ?  [* · reports]
turns on: The Qur'anic narrative and the cited poem assign different causes and details.
- 89:9/q21/p1 They rejected Ṣāliḥ and the she-camel, chose blindness, and were destroyed while believers were rescued. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites Qur'anic accounts of their refusal, the hamstrung camel, the blast or thunderbolt, and the survivors; Kuran Yolu attributes their fate to calling Ṣāliḥ a liar. A separate verse note says ʿĀd rejected Hūd and rebelled; Ibn Abī Ḥātim and al-Durr pair Thamūd's buildings with Pharaoh's stakes as troops or people fixed to stakes. [8 notes: BINTSHATI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس,مجاهد); DURR(ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,مجاهد)]
- 89:9/q21/p2 A pre-Islamic poem makes the dead she-camel's calf curse the people without a rejected divine warner. — Corpus Coranicum considers Umayya's poem likely authentic and contrasts its account with the Qur'anic one; it notes Aḥmar attached to ʿĀd in Zuhayr, perhaps because stories shifted or names overlapped. [2 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 89:9/q22 How do the brief historical allusions relate to older poetry and later Qur'anic narratives?  [* · links]
- 89:9/q22/p1 They presume familiar story outlines and form an early core of punishment narratives. — Corpus Coranicum says pre-Islamic poetry names ʿĀd and Thamūd and hints at destruction but rarely its manner; a Zuhayr line naming Pharaoh resembles the Qur'anic catalogue while listing individuals rather than peoples. It links 89:6–13 with 85:17–18, 73:15–16 and 91:11–15, with sūra 105 an earlier, different narrative type. [3 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 89:9/q23 What further valley passages does the Qur'an mention?  [ٱلْوَادِ · links]
- 89:9/q23/p1 It mentions ʿĀd's valleys, Abraham's dry valley, the ants' valley, and the sacred valley of Moses. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites 46:37, 14:37, 27:18, 20:12, 28:30, and 79:16. [1 notes: BINTSHATI]

## 89:9/q24 What unrelated verse-adjacent observations do these sources record?  [* · interpretation]
- 89:9/q24/p1 The al-Fajr recitation report promises forgiveness and resurrection light. — Jishumī reports a prophetic promise through Ubayy ibn Kaʿb for reciting al-Fajr, with light for daily recitation. [1 notes: JISHUMI(أبي بن كعب)]
- 89:9/q24/p2 Aklan later in the sūra is an absolute object, modified by lammā. — Jishumī states this grammatical analysis. [1 notes: JISHUMI]
- 89:9/q24/p3 The preceding account of ʿĀd leaves Iram's identity open. — Ibn Kathīr reports Ibn Jarīr's tribe-or-town analysis and favors the tribe in context. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن جرير)]
- 89:9/q24/p4 A note on ʿĀd describes rejecting Hūd and rebelling. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites a series of Qur'anic cross references to their denial and arrogance. [1 notes: BINTSHATI]
- 89:9/q24/p5 A discussion of majāz separates related figurative transfer from unrelated homonyms. — Al-Jurjānī excludes homonyms such as thawr for curd and names of birds, and illustrates with eating 'day' and 'night'; proper names are transfers, not metaphors. He allows motivated transfer by container, part, agent, or cause, including eye for scout and sky or rain for effects. [4 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR]
