# 96:5: 26 questions

## 96:5/q01 What did God teach the human that was previously unknown?  [عَلَّمَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ مَا لَمْ يَعْلَمْ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the pen clause limits the teaching to writing or this covers broader knowledge, and whether the recipient is a named person.
- 96:5/q01/p1 Writing and script with the pen. — Ibn Zayd and Qatāda explicitly gloss it as writing; al-Kalbī specifies writing in a book. Ibn al-Zubayr's explanatory reading names script; Ibn ʿAbbās is also reported to give this gloss. [15 notes: TAB(ابن زيد); TAB-FULL(ابن زيد); IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); MAWARDI(قتادة وابن زيد); MAWARDI-FULL(قتادة وابن زيد); WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; KASHSHAF(ابن الزبير); ALUSI-FULL(ابن الزبير); DURR(قتادة); DURR-FULL(قتادة); BINTSHATI(ابن عباس)]
- 96:5/q01/p2 All formerly unknown knowledge, including writing, guidance, religion, crafts and insight. — Ṭabarī adds other unknown things to writing. Al-Thaʿlabī and Baghawī name guidance and expression; Ṭabrisī adds religion, law and rulings. Qushayrī includes necessary and acquired knowledge; Ibn al-Jawzī writing and crafts; Bursawī universal, particular, manifest and hidden knowledge. Biqāʿī adds Scripture, Sunna, dealings, reasoning and immediate insight; ordinary learning alone would not explain unequal ability and learning time. Ālūsī includes teaching with and without writing; Qutb all discoveries. [25 notes: MUQATIL; TAB; TAB-FULL; THALABI; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI; QUTB-ZILAL; ELMALILI]
- 96:5/q01/p3 Every craft people learn. — Ibn Shajara gives this explanation. [2 notes: MAWARDI(ابن شجرة); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن شجرة)]
- 96:5/q01/p4 Evidence from humanity's initial creation of its Creator and His power to move it. — Al-Māwardī proposes creation itself as the instruction. [2 notes: MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL]
- 96:5/q01/p5 The names of everything taught to Adam. — The commentators cite 2:31. Qurṭubī says Adam displayed the names to angels, demonstrating his rank and prophethood; descendants inherited his knowledge. Wāḥidī notes that this detaches verse 5 from the pen clause. [9 notes: THALABI; BAGHAWI(قيل); BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); WAHIDI-BASIT(بعض المفسرين); QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); STUDYQURAN(some commentators)]
- 96:5/q01/p6 The Qur'an and previously unknown law, rulings, stories or Book and Wisdom taught to Muhammad. — Muqātil names the Qur'an; others cite 4:113. Qurṭubī places this teaching in the future because the passage was early; Wāḥidī calls the reading weak since Muhammad then knew little. [7 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI; BAGHAWI(قيل); BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); STUDYQURAN(some commentators); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس)-]
- 96:5/q01/p7 Complete guidance especially for the unlettered Arab first audience. — Islāḥī links 62:2 and says the audience must honor this favor. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 96:5/q01/p8 Spiritual truths and moral standards given through prophetic revelation. — Asad says human experience and reason alone cannot establish them unequivocally. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES]

## 96:5/q02 Who is 'the human' whom God taught?  [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · referent]
turns on: Whether the definite noun denotes the species or a particular person.
- 96:5/q02/p1 Humanity as a species. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls this clearer; Abū Ḥayyān calls it apparent. Qurṭubī cites 16:78: humans leave their mothers knowing nothing. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the generic noun broadens the teaching. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says it is unrestricted; Study Quran reports it as the majority. [12 notes: QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(some commentators); BINTSHATI+; IBNATIYYA-FULL+; IBNATIYYA+]
- 96:5/q02/p2 Adam. — The reading ties to 2:31 and the teaching of the names; Qurṭubī says he recited these to angels and descendants inherited them. Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls this restriction weak; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ rejects it. [10 notes: THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(بعض المفسرين); BAGHAWI(قيل); BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); ITQAN; STUDYQURAN(some commentators); TABATABAI(قيل)-; BINTSHATI(بعض المفسرين)-]
- 96:5/q02/p3 Muhammad. — The reading cites 4:113. Qurṭubī says teaching is future because the passage came early; Wāḥidī calls that reading weak for already known law. Ibn ʿAṭiyya prefers generic humanity. [10 notes: THALABI; BAGHAWI(قيل); BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); IBNATIYYA(قيل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); ABUHAYYAN(قيل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; STUDYQURAN(some commentators); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس)-; IBNATIYYA-; IBNATIYYA-FULL-]
- 96:5/q02/p4 Idris, reported as the first writer. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls this remote; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ rejects restricting the human to the first writer. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الضحاك، كعب); TABATABAI(قيل)-; BINTSHATI(بعض المفسرين)-]
- 96:5/q02/p5 Every prophet who wrote. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports this and regards it as remote. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الضحاك، كعب); TABATABAI(قيل)-]
- 96:5/q02/p6 A literate person. — Al-Suyūṭī lists this as a possible referent of the second al-insān. [1 notes: ITQAN]

## 96:5/q03 By what means does God teach unknown knowledge?  [عَلَّمَ مَا لَمْ يَعْلَمْ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether learning is confined to writing or includes oral, rational, revealed and inspired routes.
- 96:5/q03/p1 By the pen and writing, which transmit knowledge. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says books carry learning across peoples and generations; Asad adds cultures and cumulative experience. [10 notes: MUQATIL; TAB(ابن زيد); TAB-FULL(ابن زيد); WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي,صاحب النظم); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES]
- 96:5/q03/p2 Through faculties, signs, reasoning, oral teaching, revelation and direct inspiration as well as writing. — Ṭabrisī cites intuition, rational evidence, angels and messengers. Bayḍāwī cites faculties, signs and revealed verses. Biqāʿī includes uncaused insight and inference through premises and middle terms; Ibn ʿĀshūr distinguishes written, oral and intellectual routes. Ālūsī says with or without pen. [16 notes: QUSHAYRI; TABRISI; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 96:5/q04 How is this teaching clause constructed grammatically?  [عَلَّمَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ مَا لَمْ يَعْلَمْ · grammar]
turns on: The notes give different clause and predicate analyses.
- 96:5/q04/p1 'Taught' takes two objects. — Al-Naḥḥās states the verb's transitivity. [1 notes: NAHHAS]
- 96:5/q04/p2 The clause is the predicate of 'your Lord is most generous'; intervening words are parenthetical. — Ibn ʿĀshūr gives this parsing. [2 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:5/q04/p3 It is a fresh explanatory statement; this or 'who taught by the pen' may be predicate. — Ibn ʿĀshūr's fuller discussion allows either predicate. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:5/q04/p4 It parallels the earlier create/create-human pair. — Al-Samīn says the same grammatical analyses apply. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 96:5/q05 How does this clause relate to 'who taught by the pen'?  [عَلَّمَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ مَا لَمْ يَعْلَمْ · links]
turns on: Whether it specifies the pen clause or adds further teaching.
- 96:5/q05/p1 It explains and specifies the pen teaching. — Ṣāḥib al-Naẓm says the prior object is omitted and supplied here, and repetition emphasizes teaching; Rāzī reports this. Ālūsī calls it substitution yet includes teaching without writing. [5 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(صاحب النظم); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 96:5/q05/p2 It adds further teaching beyond the pen. — Rāzī posits an omitted conjunction and compares successive clauses about honoring, helping and enriching. Ibn ʿĀshūr sees broader generic teaching. [4 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 96:5/q06 What explanatory reading explicitly mentions script?  [عَلَّمَ · readings]
- 96:5/q06/p1 Ibn al-Zubayr reads 'taught script by the pen'. — Al-Kashshāf and Ālūsī cite الذي علم الخط بالقلم; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports a similar Ibn ʿAbbās gloss, which Abū Ḥayyān calls explanatory. [3 notes: KASHSHAF(ابن الزبير); ALUSI-FULL(ابن الزبير); BINTSHATI(ابن عباس)]

## 96:5/q07 What does the wording of this clause emphasize?  [عَلَّمَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ مَا لَمْ يَعْلَمْ · rhetoric]
- 96:5/q07/p1 Prior ignorance and unspecified knowledge stress God's power and generosity. — Al-Irshād says the omitted first object and stated prior ignorance hint at knowledge beyond human comprehension for Muhammad. Bursawī includes universal, particular, manifest and hidden knowledge. [3 notes: ALUSI(الإرشاد); ALUSI-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 96:5/q07/p2 Repeating 'taught' emphasizes and explains the pen clause. — Ṣāḥib al-Naẓm notes the repetition. [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(صاحب النظم)]
- 96:5/q07/p3 Knowledge follows a prior state of ignorance. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says inability to read need not prevent later learning. [2 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:5/q07/p4 Repeated 'human' creates paronomasia. — Al-Suyūṭī says the first denotes the species, the second Adam, a literate person or Idris, and the third Abū Jahl. [1 notes: ITQAN]

## 96:5/q08 How does the verse answer Muhammad's inability to read?  [عَلَّمَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ مَا لَمْ يَعْلَمْ · interpretation]
- 96:5/q08/p1 God can teach him Qur'anic recitation directly despite his inability to write. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the verse answers 'I cannot read', by dictation, instruction or inspiration as God taught Adam names without literacy; with or without the hadith it reassures him. Biqāʿī says he will surpass writers; Ālūsī says God strengthens retention and proclamation. [12 notes: BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 96:5/q09 What does divine teaching show about God and humanity?  [* · theology]
- 96:5/q09/p1 It shows generosity, lordship and wisdom, raising humanity from ignorance to knowledge. — Zamakhsharī and Nasafī contrast darkness and light. Ibn Kathīr calls these first verses mercy and blessing. Bayḍāwī and Biqāʿī describe a rise from lowest to highest, rational proof then transmitted or sensory knowledge. Al-Malawī argues knowledge is noblest since it follows 'most generous'. Ṭabrisī says knowledge comes from a knower. Ibn ʿĀshūr says 'most generous' intensifies generosity rather than comparing God with another; divine gifts begin with creation and sustenance. [22 notes: THALABI(قتادة); KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI(الرازي,الملوي); BIQAI-FULL(الرازي); TABRISI; ELMALILI(Beyzâvî); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:5/q09/p2 Knowledge dignifies humanity and distinguished Adam from the angels. — Ibn Kathīr distinguishes mental, verbal and written knowledge; writing entails the other two but not conversely. Biqāʿī notes generosity of teachers; Kuryolu says knowledge distinguishes people from other living things, citing 2:31. [7 notes: IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 96:5/q09/p3 God is the source of all human knowledge and discovery. — Qutb includes existence, life and self. Ibn ʿĀshūr says lordship implies existence and oneness, teaching and creation divine acts, and 'most generous' perfection. [3 notes: QUTB-ZILAL; TABRISI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:5/q09/p4 Divinely granted knowledge can protect from self-assertion and transgression. — Qāshānī says the Prophet received knowledge before being established in abiding, guarding against a claim to independent perfection. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 96:5/q10 Why is writing singled out in this passage?  [عَلَّمَ مَا لَمْ يَعْلَمْ · interpretation]
- 96:5/q10/p1 It preserves and transmits knowledge, wisdom, reports and revelation, sustaining religious and worldly life. — Zamakhsharī and Nasafī say affairs fail without writing. Qatāda says knowledge sustains religion and livelihood. Biqāʿī extends benefits to both worlds. Ālūsī calls pen and script a sign of wisdom and cites poems about their reach. Asad stresses cumulative learning; Kuryolu includes computers and information exchange. [13 notes: THALABI(قتادة); KASHSHAF; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI; ALUSI-FULL(شاعر غير مسمى); ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:5/q10/p2 It encourages recording revelation and literacy. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says Muhammad appointed scribes from the start; his remaining unlettered completes the miracle, citing 29:48. Elmalılı says he dictated and relied on God's promise not to forget in 87:6. [5 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 96:5/q11 Who first learned or introduced writing, and how did scripts spread?  [عَلَّمَ · reports]
turns on: Reports differ about first writers and the route of writing to Arabia.
- 96:5/q11/p1 Writing was divinely taught; Adam and other prophets may have learned it. — Zarkashī cites pen teaching and 68:1; Ibn Fāris places letters among the names taught Adam. Ālūsī says the verse leaves the teacher unnamed and doubts alphabet invention claims. [4 notes: BURHAN; ITQAN(ابن فارس); ALUSI-FULL(الضحاك، كعب)]
- 96:5/q11/p2 Ishmael established Arabic writing first. — Ibn ʿAbbās says so; Zarkashī notes conflicting reports. [1 notes: BURHAN(ابن عباس)]
- 96:5/q11/p3 Idris and Adam, or every writing prophet, were first writers. — Ālūsī transmits al-Ḍaḥḥāk and Kaʿb but doubts alphabet invention claims. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الضحاك، كعب)]
- 96:5/q11/p4 Writing was old outside Arabia and later reached the Hijaz through Harb ibn Umayya. — Al-Kalbī and al-Haytham trace Harb's learning near al-Anbar through Aslam ibn Sidra to Marāmir ibn Murra. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الكلبي، الهيثم بن عدي)]
- 96:5/q11/p5 Himyar used a separate Musnad script taught with permission. — Ālūsī allows twelve main script families only as origins because derivatives exceed counting. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 96:5/q12 What does the history of writing imply for Qur'anic orthography?  [عَلَّمَ · interpretation]
- 96:5/q12/p1 Early Muslims knew orthography and the first codex spelling should remain. — Zarkashī cites wāw/yāʾ distinctions and hamza omission. Farrāʾ favors codex spelling with an Arabic basis; Mālik requires first spelling including extra written wāw or alif, with al-Dānī reporting no dissent. [3 notes: BURHAN(الفراء,مالك)]
- 96:5/q12/p2 Grammar and prosody existed before their later codifiers. — Zarkashī says Abū al-Aswad and al-Khalīl revived older sciences; he counters the claim ancient Arabs lacked letter names by citing the names taught Adam. [2 notes: BURHAN]

## 96:5/q13 Can acting on known knowledge bring further divine knowledge?  [مَا لَمْ يَعْلَمْ · interpretation]
- 96:5/q13/p1 Whoever acts on what he knows will receive knowledge he did not know. — Ibn Kathīr transmits this maxim at adjacent verse locations; Suyūṭī cites it as a hadith on inspired knowledge. [6 notes: IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ITQAN]

## 96:5/q14 How much of al-ʿAlaq was the first revelation?  [* · sciences]

Suyūṭī calls this soundest. Ibn Shihāb's scholars, al-Rāzī as cited by Bint al-Shāṭiʾ, and most commentators say the rest came later; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites intervening opposition. - 96:5/q14/p2 The entire surah was the first revelation. — Islāḥī reports some people hold this; the majority favors the first five on Bukhārī and Muslim. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR(some people)]

## 96:5/q17 What followed Muhammad's receipt of the opening verses?  [* · reports]

## 96:5/q18 What revelations followed the opening passage?  [* · sciences]
- 96:5/q18/p1 Nūn and the Pen, al-Muddaththir and al-Ḍuḥā are listed after Iqraʾ. — ʿĀʾisha names these; Mujāhid adds Nūn and the Pen next. Ibn Shaddād says al-Ḍuḥā came after a pause and reassured Muhammad in 93:1–3. [8 notes: TAB(عائشة,عبد الله بن شداد,مجاهد); TAB-FULL(عبد الله بن شداد); DURR-FULL(عبد الله بن شداد)]

## 96:5/q19 What is the surah's classification, verse count and reported virtue?  [* · sciences]
turns on: The verse counts differ.
- 96:5/q19/p1 It is Meccan and has seventeen verses. — Al-Jishumī gives this count. [1 notes: JISHUMI]
- 96:5/q19/p2 It is Meccan and has nineteen verses. — Al-Suyūṭī gives this count. [1 notes: DURR-FULL]
- 96:5/q19/p3 Reciting it brings reward like reciting all shorter surahs. — Al-Bayḍāwī transmits the report. [1 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 96:5/q20 How do these verses connect to the surah's next turn and Qur'anic themes?  [* · links]
- 96:5/q20/p1 Teaching calls for gratitude, yet the human transgresses. — Ālūsī supplies an implicit transition after verse 5. Biqāʿī says people cling to their present state and forget what God does not preserve; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ warns of arrogance in knowledge and status. Ibn Masʿūd contrasts knowledge, which increases divine approval (35:28), with wealth seeking, which deepens transgression (96:6). [4 notes: ALUSI(بعضهم); BIQAI; BINTSHATI; DURR-FULL(ابن مسعود)]
- 96:5/q20/p2 The first five verses preview recitation, God's name, law, religion and divine teaching. — Suyūṭī calls the opening a possible title for the Qur'an; Elmalılı says it joins rational and transmitted proof, reading, writing and knowledge. [2 notes: ITQAN; ELMALILI]
- 96:5/q20/p3 Verse 5 closes the first unit; verse 6 opens a new topic. — Abū Ḥātim uses the first five and later kalla at 96:6 to argue kalla opens speech and means alā. Ibn ʿĀshūr also marks verses 6–10 as a new subject. [2 notes: BURHAN(أبو حاتم); IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 96:5/q21 What did Muhammad's taḥannuth at Hira mean?  [* · meaning]
- 96:5/q21/p1 Secluded worship. — Bukhārī and Muslim gloss it as worship; ʿĀʾisha describes provisioning, Ibn Hishām a Ramadan retreat with feeding the poor and circumambulation after return. [5 notes: BUKHARI; MUSLIM; IBNHISHAM(عبيد); AZRAQI(عائشة)]
- 96:5/q21/p2 A form of taḥannuf, meaning ḥanīfiyya. — Ibn Hishām says Arabs replace th with f as in jadath/jadaf, citing Ruʾba; Abū ʿUbayda gives fam for thumma. [2 notes: IBNHISHAM(أبو عبيدة,ابن هشام)]

## 96:5/q22 What knowledge and method qualify a person to interpret the Qur'an?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Directly taught unseen matters differ from disciplined inference and ungrounded opinion.
- 96:5/q22/p1 Some unseen and ambiguous passages require direct divine teaching. — ʿĀʾisha says Muhammad explained only what Gabriel taught; Ibn ʿAṭiyya limits that to unseen matters. The Hour is inaccessible by inference, but wording can suggest trumpet blasts or creation sequence. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(عائشة)]
- 96:5/q22/p2 Disciplined interpretation is permitted; ungrounded opinion is condemned. — A prophetic report warns even correct personal opinion errs. Ibn ʿAṭiyya targets disregard of precedent and grammar or legal theory, while trained linguists and jurists may reason; early scholars either abstained or preserved tafsīr. Suyūṭī requires rhetoric, readings, theology, law, occasions, abrogation, fiqh and hadith; these sciences are an endless ocean. [17 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم,سعيد بن المسيب); ITQAN(ابن أبي الدنيا,الزمخشري,غيره)]
- 96:5/q22/p3 ʿAlī and Ibn ʿAbbās were leading early tafsīr authorities. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya ranks ʿAlī first, Ibn ʿAbbās next, then Mujāhid and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr; Ibn ʿAbbās learned from ʿAlī and was praised by ʿAlī and Ibn Masʿūd; the Prophet prayed for his understanding. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس,رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم,عبد الله بن مسعود,علي بن أبي طالب)]

## 96:5/q23 Which transmitted Qur'anic readings may readers use?  [* · readings]
turns on: Established transmission differs from readings outside the codex.
- 96:5/q23/p1 Established readings beyond the seven may be recited when learned. — Ibn al-Jazarī cites Iraqi acceptance of ten or eleven and transmissions from al-Aʿmash, Yaʿqūb, Khalaf, Abū Jaʿfar and Shaybah; readers should not use unlearned readings or rebuke others. Qāḍī ʿIyāḍ's censure concerned anomalous readings outside the codex. [4 notes: NASHR(أئمة أهل العراق,القاضي عياض)]
- 96:5/q23/p2 The seven named readings are distinct from the seven revealed aḥruf. — Ibn al-Jazarī reports agreement on the distinction; later scholars debated whether the codex with seven, ten and other readings represents one ḥarf. [2 notes: NASHR(المتأخرون)]

## 96:5/q24 What grammatical uses can 'mā' have in related constructions?  [مَا · grammar]
- 96:5/q24/p1 It may be declarative or relative before laysa, lam or lā and after illā. — Zarkashī gives general usage examples, without choosing the parsing here. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 96:5/q25 What examples show surahs descending together or in portions?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Reports conflict about whether al-Anʿām came wholly and about the number of angels.
- 96:5/q25/p1 Several short surahs descended whole; al-ʿAlaq's opening descended as a portion. — Suyūṭī names al-Fātiḥa, al-Ikhlāṣ, al-Kawthar, Tabbat, al-Bayyina, al-Naṣr, the two Muʿawwidhatayn and al-Ṣaff. Ibn Masʿūd says al-Mursalāt descended in a cave but was unsure which of two verses ended what he heard; al-Ḍuḥā's first portion is another instance. [5 notes: ITQAN(ابن مسعود)]
- 96:5/q25/p2 Al-Anʿām descended whole, with angels whose reported number varies. — Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn ʿUmar, ʿAṭāʾ and Ubayy say seventy thousand; Mujāhid says five hundred; ʿAlī says a thousand with seventy from each heaven. Suyūṭī says the reports support one another, while noting an abandoned narrator in Ibn ʿUmar's chain and an unknown one in ʿAlī's. [7 notes: ITQAN(أبي بن كعب,ابن عباس,ابن عمر,عطاء,علي,مجاهد)]
- 96:5/q25/p3 Al-Anʿām did not wholly descend at once, or no sound chain establishes that report. — Ibn al-Ṣalāḥ cites three, six or another number of Medinan verses and found no sound chain for Ubayy's report. [2 notes: ITQAN(ابن الصلاح)]

## 96:5/q26 How did Muhammad embody the opening instruction to act in God's name?  [* · interpretation]
- 96:5/q26/p1 He filled ordinary acts with remembrance, prayer and reliance on God. — Qutb states the opening's faith principle and cites Ibn al-Qayyim on remembrance in speech, silence and rulings. Reports detail waking, night prayer, Witr, leaving and entering home or mosque, dawn and evening, clothing, lavatory, ablution, moon sighting and eating; Qutb cites Bukhārī and Muslim and grades some sound or good. They vary by occasion and wording. [25 notes: QUTB-ZILAL(أبو بكر الصديق,أبو سعيد الخدري,ابن عباس,ابن قيم الجوزية,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,عائشة)]
