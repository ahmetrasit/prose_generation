# 24:27: 30 questions

## 24:27/q01 Who is addressed by the call to believers, and why is it collective?  [يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ · referent]
- 24:27/q01/p1 People committed to this religion; the group address also makes believers responsible for restraining one another. — Al-Biqāʿī says the prohibition binds each person while fellow believers should check an individual whom Satan may overcome. Al-Ālūsī says masculine address also covers women because householders can hide private matters from them. [3 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 24:27/q02 What does buyūt mean here?  [بُيُوتًا · meaning]
- 24:27/q02/p1 Homes or residential shelters, including built houses, rooms and nomadic dwellings. — Al-Dāmghānī cites 33:53, 33:55, and 24:61. Ibn al-Jawzī defines shelter from weather, built roofed enclosures, and wool tents; he also records derivation from spending the night, nocturnal plotting and figurative concern, supported by unnamed and Hudhalī poetry. Ibn ʿĀshūr stresses shelter from weather and private exposure. Iṣlāḥī takes it as family residences, clarified by the next verse. [10 notes: DAMGHANI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(الشاعر,الهذلي,بعض المفسرين); IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ELMALILI]

## 24:27/q03 How is buyūtan pronounced?  [بُيُوتًا · readings]
turns on: Whether initial kasra is acceptable in the broken-plural pattern or follows the yāʾ.
- 24:27/q03/p1 Biyūtan with initial kasra is an attested dialectal form without a difference in meaning. — Al-Ālūsī relates the kasra to the following yāʾ; Qirāʾāt Encyclopedia calls it a dialectal broken plural. Al-Zajjāj reports it but criticizes its pattern. [4 notes: ZAJJAJ; ALUSI-FULL; QIRAAT-ER(vw)]
- 24:27/q03/p2 Buyūtan with initial ḍamma is the regular, common plural. — Al-Zajjāj prefers this form, saying Basrans find initial kasra very poor because Arabic fuʿul is not normally so pronounced. [1 notes: ZAJJAJ+]

## 24:27/q04 Which houses are 'other than your houses'?  [غَيْرَ بُيُوتِكُمْ · referent]
- 24:27/q04/p1 Houses or rooms in which the addressee does not live, including another's premises; title alone does not define one's own house. — Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and Ibn ʿAbbās say houses not belonging to the visitor. Al-Nasafī says neither owned nor inhabited. Ibn ʿAṭiyya defines one's private house as occupied alone, with wife or slave woman. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Bursawī and al-Ālūsī add that tenants and borrowers still respect occupants. Al-Ālūsī also allows the possessive to imply ownership as ordinary usage. Iṣlāḥī specifies family residences. [12 notes: IBNABIHATIM(سعيد بن جبير); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(سعيد بن جبير); IBNATIYYA-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 24:27/q05 What does tastaʾnisū require or mean?  [تَسْتَأْنِسُوا۟ · meaning]

- 24:27/q05/p1 Ask permission to enter, seeking the household's welcome and consent. — Muqātil, Qatāda, Ibn ʿAbbās, Mālik and many commentators gloss it with istiʾdhān. Al-Zajjāj describes seeking assurance whether residents want entry, with 4:6 and 24:28; al-Māwardī says permission puts residents at ease. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls this a subtle metonymy that avoids unsettling the host. Al-Ṭabarī joins request, announcement and assent; Rāzī says mere request cannot be enough without consent. ## 24:27/q06 Was tastaʾnisū also read as tastaʾdhinū, and how are the scribal-error reports assessed?  [تَسْتَأْنِسُوا۟ · readings]

## 24:27/q07 Does the morphology of istiʾnās allow derivation from ānasa?  [تَسْتَأْنِسُوا۟ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the morphological relation between afʿala and istifʿala supports this lexical derivation.
- 24:27/q07/p1 The verb's morphology prevents deriving it from ānasa. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya states this objection; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn transmit it. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية); SAMIN-DURR(ابن عطية)]
- 24:27/q07/p2 The istifʿala form can be connected with sensing or seeking what makes visitor and household at ease. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya describes ānasa as afʿala and istaʾnasa as istifʿala; al-Zajjāj and other lexical explainers use the knowing/perceiving sense and 4:6. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ZAJJAJ]

## 24:27/q08 In what order should one announce, greet and request permission?  [تَسْتَأْنِسُوا۟ وَتُسَلِّمُوا۟ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether recited order governs action, whether the wording is transposed, whether seeing occupants changes the sequence, and whether wāw imposes any chronology.
- 24:27/q08/p1 Greet first, then ask permission; the verse's wording puts the acts in a different order from their performance. — Yaḥyā, al-Ṭabarī, al-Thaʿlabī and al-Wāḥidī cite the fronting and delay; Ibn Masʿūd's codex puts greeting first. Al-Wāḥidī compares ordered pairs in 3:43 and 64:2. Ibn ʿUmar, ʿĀʾisha, the Rawḍa visitor and Kaldah reports demonstrate greeting followed by request. Al-Nawawī prefers greeting first based on reports that it precedes speech and an ungreeted requester is not admitted. Rāzī rejects transposition as weaker than the apparent wording; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects it. Al-Māwardī also reports greeting first as the legal order. Al-Ṭabrisī reports this parsing. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī transmits the Rawḍa instruction. ## 24:27/q09 What counts as a request for entry, and what must the visitor say or do?  [تَسْتَأْنِسُوا۟ وَتُسَلِّمُوا۟ · law]
turns on: Whether the visitor must use the reported formula or any clear, courteous means of requesting entry.
- 24:27/q09/p1 A clear greeting and request such as 'Peace be upon you; may I enter?' is the proper formula; identify oneself, and use a gentle signal if needed. — Reports of the Prophet correcting the blunt visitor and Kaldah establish the formula. Ibn ʿUmar, ʿĀʾisha and Abū Hurayra withhold entry until greeting and explicit invitation. Saying only 'I' fails to identify a caller, as the Jābir incident and later anecdotes illustrate. Al-Māwardī permits verbal, gestural or loud requests; Ibn ʿĀshūr and al-Qurṭubī allow customary wording, illustrated by Persian andar. Rāzī bars pounding and shouting, citing 49:4; Qurṭubī recommends soft knocking, mentions fingernails and reports garden-well knocking. Kuran Yolu applies clear identification to bells and intercoms. ## 24:27/q10 How many times may entry be requested without an answer?  [حَتَّىٰ تَسْتَأْنِسُوا۟ · law]
- 24:27/q10/p1 Ask or greet up to three times, spaced to allow hearing and preparation; leave after the third unanswered or refused request. — The Abū Mūsā and ʿUmar report has corroboration by Ṭalḥa or Abū Saʿīd, with ʿUmar seeking proof rather than suspecting him. Qatāda assigns hearing, preparation and decision to the three requests; al-Ḥasan says permission, consultation and final notice. Rāzī and Elmalılı require intervals; Qurtubi says three usually ensure comprehension and that insistence burdens hosts, citing Saʿd's quiet replies to obtain more greetings. Malik permits another request if certain the first was unheard; Abū Ḥayyān and Kuran Yolu give the same exception. Ibn Kathīr calls the Saʿd report good despite uncertain attribution to Anas and another route. Al-Bayḍāwī also cites the three-request Prophetic rule. ## 24:27/q11 Does seeking permission alone permit entry, and what can count as consent?  [حَتَّىٰ تَسْتَأْنِسُوا۟ · law]
turns on: Whether the assent has already been conveyed by an invitation, authorized person or standing custom.
- 24:27/q11/p1 Entry requires an actual invitation or permission, whether explicit or conveyed by an authorized messenger or invitation. — Rāzī argues the purpose is not met by a bare request and 24:28 requires permission when nobody is found; reports from Abū Hurayra make an invitation-bearing messenger sufficient. ## 24:27/q27 Is the command's legal scope ambiguous because it gives no separate threat?  [لَا تَدْخُلُوا۟ · rhetoric]
- 24:27/q27/p1 Some early authorities classify it as ambiguous in this respect: it forbids a sin without stating a threat as another wrongdoing verse does. — Al-Itqān contrasts the explicit threat in 4:30. [1 notes: ITQAN(بعض الأقدمين)]

## 24:27/q28 How much Arabic learning is needed to interpret a verse such as this?  [* · interpretation]
- 24:27/q28/p1 Detailed linguistic learning beyond basic grammar is needed to interpret Qurʾān responsibly. — Al-Jurjānī argues that neglect of learned grammar and poetry leads to confused tafsīr; he illustrates with the nominative al-ṣābiʾūn in 5:69 and a poetic line. These notes are a general methodological aside rather than a distinct gloss of this verse. [3 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL]

## 24:27/q29 Is the verse cited as an example of unusual word order?  [* · rhetoric]
- 24:27/q29/p1 Yes; it appears among examples of unusual ordering, without an independent gloss in that excerpt. — Al-Zarkashī includes it in the cited section. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 24:27/q30 What did the Prophet say about knowledge in the reported entry incident?  [* · reports]
- 24:27/q30/p1 After teaching a visitor the entry formula, he said some knowledge belongs only to God and recited Luqmān 31:34. — Elmalılı preserves this additional detail of the visitor report. [1 notes: ELMALILI(رسول الله)]
