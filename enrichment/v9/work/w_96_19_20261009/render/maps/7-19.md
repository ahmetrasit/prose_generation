# 7:19: 32 questions

## 7:19/q01 How is Adam's address connected to the preceding speech?  [وَيَٰٓـَٔادَمُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the wāw continues the expulsion speech or introduces an understood new saying, with 2:35 supplying that wording.
- 7:19/q01/p1 An unspoken divine saying introduces the address. — Al-Bayḍāwī supplies 'And We said'; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī infer it from 2:35. Al-Ālūsī favors connection to the earlier angelic command and favors to humanity; the direct call gives the command weight. Al-Kashshāf simply expands the call as God's address to Adam. [5 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 7:19/q01/p2 The address continues the same quoted speech as Iblis's expulsion. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says wāw joins the speech of 7:18 and the speaker turns to Adam without repeating 'We said'; his prophetic legal-case example and 12:28–29 illustrate successive addressees. This juxtaposition humiliates Iblis and honors Adam. Al-Sāmarrāʾī contrasts this shared saying with the explicit 'We said' in 2:35. Al-Ālūsī reports an argument for this connection but finds it against the apparent reading. [6 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(النبيء صلى الله عليه وسلم); SAMARRAI-ASRAR; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)-]

## 7:19/q02 What does the call to Adam convey?  [يَٰٓـَٔادَمُ · rhetoric]
- 7:19/q02/p1 It draws attention to Adam and honors him. — Al-Ālūsī says direct address makes the command important and singles out Adam's priority; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the vocative honors him before the gathered company and sharpens Iblis's humiliation. Study Quran contrasts this intimate direct address with God's later call from a distance, signaling alienation. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN(Qu)]

## 7:19/q03 What does the command to dwell mean for Adam?  [ٱسْكُنْ · interpretation]
- 7:19/q03/p1 Take up or continue residence in the Garden. — Al-Ṭabrisī and al-Nasafī distinguish residence from mere stillness; al-Ālūsī explicitly rejects the sense of immobility. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says a command to continue an existing action means persistence. Ibn ʿĀshūr says if Adam was created there it means remain, and if elsewhere it grants entry as an honor; either taunts Iblis. Al-Bursevī adds that the residence honors the pair and permits their stay. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 7:19/q04 Why is the independent pronoun 'you' stated?  [أَنتَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether coordination requires both nouns to be independently eligible for the same verb form.
- 7:19/q04/p1 It reinforces Adam as the subject and enables coordination with his wife. — Al-Zajjāj prefers the pronoun because 'go and Zayd' without one is awkward. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the hidden subject of 'dwell' particularly needs reinforcement; al-Burhān says a connected subject is conventionally reinforced before a coordinated noun. Al-Biqāʿī adds that it confirms Adam himself, rather than a metonymic associate, is addressed. Ibn ʿĀshūr sees an indirect contrast with Iblis, applicable also to 2:35, while denying that pronouns inherently carry contrast. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ZAJJAJ+]
- 7:19/q04/p2 The wife's mention represents a separate, omitted command to dwell. — Ibn ʿUmarūn and Ibn Mālik require both coordinated nouns to be eligible for the same grammatical role. Abū Ḥayyān counters with 'Hind and Zayd rise': masculine Zayd may be coordinated after a feminine verb although he could not independently be its subject. [1 notes: BURHAN(ابن عمرون وابن مالك); BURHAN(أبو حيان)-]

## 7:19/q05 Who is Adam's spouse, and what is reported of her origin?  [وَزَوْجُكَ · referent]
turns on: The reported account of Eve's origin: formed from Adam or already among the Garden's women.
- 7:19/q05/p1 She is Eve, created from Adam. — Al-Māwardī, al-Jishumī, al-Bursevī, and Study Quran identify her as Ḥawwāʾ/Eve; Study Quran notes the Quran never names her. Al-Suddī reports that Adam awoke to a companion made from his rib, named Ḥawwāʾ because she came from living matter. Study Quran says the rib account appears in Hadith and many read 4:1 as alluding to her origin; it notes that the Quran omits the origin and that primordial Adam represents male and female humanity. Kuranyolu says Adam was made on earth from special soil and his mate from him. [8 notes: IBNABIHATIM(السدي); MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI; BURSEVI; STUDYQURAN; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR+]
- 7:19/q05/p2 Ḥawwāʾ was one of the Garden's women. — Ashʿath al-Ḥaddānī adds that her pregnancy was so transparent the child's sex could be seen. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(أشعث الحداني)]

## 7:19/q06 Why is the wife described as Adam's 'zawj'?  [وَزَوْجُكَ · rhetoric]
- 7:19/q06/p1 The expression concisely conveys their paired relationship and shared address. — Al-Bursevī says zawj names one counterpart and zawjān the pair. Al-Jishumī and al-Ṭabrisī say the possessive relation makes a feminine form redundant. Al-Ālūsī says Eve follows Adam in the residence command, whereas both are addressed equally in eating and prohibition; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī likewise says the ruling includes her though Adam alone is directly addressed. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ notes that the Quran also uses zauj for Adam's spouse in the parallel addresses at 2:35 and 20:117. [6 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI-IJAZ+]

## 7:19/q07 Which Garden did Adam and his wife inhabit?  [ٱلْجَنَّةَ · referent]
turns on: Whether the setting is final recompense, another heavenly test garden, an earthly garden, or specifically Eden; accounts weigh departure, prohibition, sleep, and Iblis's presence differently.
- 7:19/q07/p1 The Garden of Eternity or final reward. — Al-Māwardī says leaving it was possible because their stay was not permanent reward; al-Jishumī attributes the choice to ʿAlī ibn ʿĪsā. Al-Bursevī calls this the majority view. Kuranyolu prefers it after considering parallel verses: the pair were created on earth, then placed in a Paradise unlike earthly life. [5 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI(علي بن عيسى); BURSEVI(أكثر أهل العلم); ELMALILI(bazı kaviller); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR+; TABATABAI-]
- 7:19/q07/p2 A heavenly garden distinct from the final Garden. — Al-Jishumī attributes a heavenly-gardens view to Abū ʿAlī and Abū Hāshim. Al-Bursevī reports a test garden in heaven as one alternative; its advocates cite prohibitions, sleep, expulsion, and Iblis's entry as unlike eternal reward. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī specifies an intermediate heavenly garden before earthly life and legal responsibility, revealing general human disposition. [7 notes: JISHUMI(أبو علي وأبو هاشم); BURSEVI(بعض المحققين من أهل الظاهر والباطن); ELMALILI(bazı kaviller); TABATABAI]
- 7:19/q07/p3 An earthly garden. — Al-Māwardī reports an earthly garden without duties, although Adam was obligated. Al-Bursevī prefers earth: a report says Adam's sons sought Garden grapes at his death and angels buried him; he also interprets the Nile's Garden source as earthly. Kuranyolu gives the lexical earthly-garden view. Other test-garden advocates mention an earthly elevated garden and objections to identifying the site with final reward. [7 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; BURSEVI(بعض المحققين من أهل الظاهر والباطن); ELMALILI(bazı kaviller); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Bir görüş); BURSEVI+]
- 7:19/q07/p4 The Garden of Eden, distinct from Eternity. — Ibn ʿAbbās identifies the place of Adam and expelled Iblis as Eden. Elmalılı glosses adn with unvowelled dāl as residence, linked to maʿdin, and suggests Eden was the first afterlife garden on the way to Eternity, where beginning and return meet, citing 43:72. [3 notes: ELMALILI(İbn Abbas)]

## 7:19/q08 When were Adam and his wife settled in the Garden?  [ٱسْكُنْ ٱلْجَنَّةَ · reports]
turns on: Different reports supply relative sequence and a specific day; the details are not mutually exclusive.
- 7:19/q08/p1 After Iblis was expelled; Adam had previously been alone there. — Al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Nasafī, and al-Bursevī place the divine address after Iblis's expulsion. Al-Suddī says Adam first lived alone, then awoke to his wife. [5 notes: TAB-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(السدي); QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 7:19/q08/p2 Adam was created and placed in Firdaws on Friday. — Abū al-ʿĀliya reports both events on that day. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(أبو العالية)]

## 7:19/q09 How does 'then eat' relate to dwelling, compared with 2:35?  [فَكُلَا · rhetoric]
- 7:19/q09/p1 Fāʾ marks eating as following residence; 2:35's wāw gives broader conjunction. — Al-Rāzī and Tafsīr al-Kabīr say succession is a special case of conjunction, resolving the variation. Al-Biqāʿī says eating accompanies settlement. Al-Itqān says taking residence comes first and min ḥaythu is narrower. Ibn ʿĀshūr says fāʾ conveys prompt, complete favor and distress to Iblis; his general principle allows Quranic variation where both formulations fit. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says wāw leaves timing and choice open while fāʾ orders eating immediately; his Lamāsāt cites 45:24, 42:3, and 5:6 to show wāw can indicate order without requiring it. [11 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ITQAN; IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMARRAI-ASRAR(التفسير الكبير); SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 7:19/q10 How is the imperative 'eat' formed?  [فَكُلَا · grammar]
- 7:19/q10/p1 It comes from akala with its first root letter omitted in the imperative. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya also mentions avoidance of a difficult vowel on a weak letter. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL]

## 7:19/q11 What does eating here mean?  [فَكُلَا · meaning]
- 7:19/q11/p1 Actual eating. — Al-Dāmghānī adduces the parallel in 20:121. [1 notes: DAMGHANI]

## 7:19/q12 How far does the permission to eat extend?  [فَكُلَا مِنْ حَيْثُ شِئْتُمَا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the locution primarily ranges over places or over the fruits available there; both remain bounded by the exception.
- 7:19/q12/p1 It permits broad, free use of the Garden's food, except the singled-out tree. — Al-Ṭabarī mentions fruit wherever they wish; Mujāhid says no accounting for where they eat. Al-Jishumī calls it permission to enjoy blessings. Al-Ṭabrisī allows any place or manner; al-Nasafī, al-Bursevī, al-Ālūsī, al-Biqāʿī, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, al-Iṣlāḥī and Study Quran include the exception. Elmalılı insists this bounded license grants neither unlimited freedom nor ownership; the tree marks a spatial and practical limit. [15 notes: TAB-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(مجاهد); JISHUMI; TABRISI; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 7:19/q12/p2 It permits eating anywhere in the Garden. — Al-Māwardī records this spatial reading as one of two ways to take the permission. [1 notes: MAWARDI-FULL]
- 7:19/q12/p3 It permits eating any of the Garden's fruits. — Al-Māwardī supports this alternative because the prohibited tree bore fruit. [1 notes: MAWARDI-FULL]

## 7:19/q13 What is the force of 'from wherever you wish' here compared with 2:35?  [مِنْ حَيْثُ شِئْتُمَا · rhetoric]
- 7:19/q13/p1 It qualifies eating alone and is narrower than 2:35's wording. — Al-Itqān contrasts min ḥaythu with the broader ḥaythu shiʾtumā. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says the latter can govern both dwelling and eating, whereas this phrase governs eating only; he connects the wider Baqara wording to its honorific setting. [4 notes: ITQAN; SAMARRAI-ASRAR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 7:19/q14 Why is 'abundantly' absent here, unlike 2:35?  [فَكُلَا مِنْ حَيْثُ شِئْتُمَا · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the omission primarily reflects brevity and distributed detail or each sūra's distinct honor and reproach setting.
- 7:19/q14/p1 The sense remains understood or was already stated in al-Baqara. — Abū Ḥayyān calls the Meccan Aʿrāf passage briefer than Medinan al-Baqara; al-Samīn says context supplies raghadan, and al-Ālūsī says it was expressed at 2:35. Al-Biqāʿī still reads ample provision here. Ibn ʿĀshūr sees favors distributed among accounts to vary narration and sustain moral instruction. [7 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 7:19/q14/p2 Al-Baqara's extra word suits its more honorific account; al-Aʿrāf emphasizes reproach. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī and al-Kirmānī link raghadan to al-Baqara's explicit divine 'We said' and greater honor; the al-Aʿrāf story shares the expulsion saying and later rebukes Adam. Al-Sāmarrāʾī also compares Moses' food commands in 2:58 and 7:161. His Lamāsāt repeats the honoring-tone explanation. [4 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR(الكرماني); SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 7:19/q15 Why does al-Aʿrāf omit al-Baqara's 'from it' after eating?  [فَكُلَا · rhetoric]
- 7:19/q15/p1 Al-Baqara repeats the Garden reference to fit its more honorific presentation. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī compares the added pronoun there with its omission here. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR]

## 7:19/q16 What is the force of 'do not approach' the tree?  [وَلَا تَقْرَبَا · interpretation]
- 7:19/q16/p1 Avoid it, especially eating from it; barring approach blocks the path to the forbidden act. — Al-Jishumī glosses it as avoidance; al-Ṭabrisī and al-Ālūsī take eating to be forbidden. Al-Biqāʿī and Ibn ʿĀshūr say banning proximity is stronger than simply banning fruit, because approach may lead to eating. Al-Dāmghānī identifies lā as prohibitive; Ibn ʿAṭiyya notes the verb can be vocalized qaruba/yaqrubu. [7 notes: DAMGHANI; JISHUMI; TABRISI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL]

## 7:19/q17 Was the tree prohibition binding, advisory, or a different kind of command?  [وَلَا تَقْرَبَا · law]
turns on: Whether the utterance legislates a duty, advises against harm, or operates ontologically; disobedience is cited against the last view.
- 7:19/q17/p1 A binding prohibition and test of obedience. — Al-Jishumī attributes this to most; Qatāda says all creation is tested and Adam violated the trial. Al-Māwardī says the prohibition preserved knowledge and supplied a recompensable test. Elmalılı says obligation and divine limits applied even in Paradise. Al-Naḥḥās calls the wording a prohibition. [5 notes: IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); NAHHAS; MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI(الأكثر); ELMALILI]
- 7:19/q17/p2 A nonbinding discouragement or guidance against self-harm. — Al-Jishumī reports a nonbinding view; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says Adam was before earthly legal responsibility and the warning guided him away from harm, not violation of legislation. [3 notes: JISHUMI(الأكثر); TABATABAI]
- 7:19/q17/p3 An ontological command. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports this view of the prostration and tree prohibition, but rejects it because ontological commands cannot be disobeyed whereas Iblis and Adam did disobey. [0 notes: TABATABAI-]

## 7:19/q18 What kind of thing can be called a 'tree'?  [ٱلشَّجَرَةَ · meaning]
- 7:19/q18/p1 Anything having a trunk. — Al-Jishumī gives this lexical range. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 7:19/q19 Which tree was forbidden?  [هَٰذِهِ ٱلشَّجَرَةَ · referent]
turns on: The species or special character assigned by different reports; al-Māwardī contests the colocynth report and Ibn ʿĀshūr doubts intrinsic knowledge effects.
- 7:19/q19/p1 Wheat or a wheat stalk. — Muqātil calls it a wheat stalk; al-Zajjāj, Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Māwardī, al-Jishumī, and Study Quran give wheat. [5 notes: MUQATIL; ZAJJAJ(بعضهم); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس); JISHUMI(قيل); STUDYQURAN]
- 7:19/q19/p2 A grapevine. — Al-Zajjāj reports this alternative. Ibn Abī Ḥātim names Ibn ʿAbbās, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, al-Shaʿbī, Jaʿd ibn Hubayra, al-Suddī, and Muḥammad ibn Qays; al-Māwardī attributes it to al-Suddī. Al-Jishumī and Study Quran also report it. [5 notes: ZAJJAJ(بعضهم); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); MAWARDI-FULL(السدي); JISHUMI(قيل); STUDYQURAN]
- 7:19/q19/p3 A fig tree. — Ibn Abī Ḥātim attributes it to Qatāda, Mujāhid, and Ibn Jurayj; al-Māwardī gives Ibn Jurayj, and Study Quran records the species. [3 notes: IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); MAWARDI-FULL(بان جريج); STUDYQURAN]
- 7:19/q19/p4 The tree of immortality or eternity, whose fruit angels consumed. — Muqātil reports angels rubbing against it for immortality; Wahb describes intertwined branches and fruit eaten by angels. Ibn Judʿān calls it the tree of eternity; Study Quran notes the Tree of Everlastingness in 20:120. [4 notes: MUQATIL; IBNABIHATIM(وهب بن منبه); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن جدعان); STUDYQURAN]
- 7:19/q19/p5 A barley tree. — Wahb says each grain was as large as a cow's liver, softer than butter and sweeter than honey. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(وهب بن منبه)]
- 7:19/q19/p6 A date palm. — Abū Mālik gives this identification. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(أبو مالك)]
- 7:19/q19/p7 A camphor tree. — Al-Māwardī attributes this to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib. [1 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب)]
- 7:19/q19/p8 The tree of knowledge. — Al-Māwardī attributes the name to al-Kalbī; Ibn ʿĀshūr reports a Torah-like explanation that the tree intrinsically awakened knowledge of good and evil, but finds that intrinsic mechanism unlikely. Study Quran contrasts the Biblical knowledge tree with Quranic identifications. [2 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(الكلبي); IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL-]
- 7:19/q19/p9 A colocynth tree. — Ibn Isḥāq transmits it from the People of the Two Books; al-Māwardī knows no basis for the report. [0 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(محمد بن إسحاق)-]

## 7:19/q20 Why is the forbidden tree not specified by name?  [هَٰذِهِ ٱلشَّجَرَةَ · interpretation]
- 7:19/q20/p1 Knowing its species would bring no benefit. — Al-Bursevī cites Aḥkām al-Marjān: God would have named it if doing so benefited people. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the species reports are weak and refers the question to al-Baqara; Ibn ʿAṭiyya likewise points back to his Baqara discussion. [3 notes: BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL]

## 7:19/q21 Why was this tree placed under prohibition?  [هَٰذِهِ ٱلشَّجَرَةَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the point lay in a moral test, a physical consequence, or a supposed intrinsic cognitive effect.
- 7:19/q21/p1 It served as a trial that set a limit amid abundant permission. — Qatāda says God tests all creation for obedience. Al-Māwardī says it preserved Adam's knowledge and gave an occasion for recompense. Al-Qushayrī calls spouse and tree means of ordeal within God's hidden allotment; al-Iṣlāḥī calls the one tree the pair's trial. Elmalılı says its vicinity tested their observance of limits. Ibn ʿĀshūr favors a training of resistance to appetite and developing human cognition through the first ancestor; he rejects an intrinsic knowledge-tree mechanism and compares the development with 2:31. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī makes it a manifestation of humanity's general disposition, followed by maturation under earthly commands. [9 notes: IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNASHUR-FULL+]
- 7:19/q21/p2 Eating its fruit caused bodily evacuation, unsuitable for the Garden. — Abū al-ʿĀliya supplies this physical consequence. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(أبو العالية)]
- 7:19/q21/p3 Its own nature awakened knowledge of good and evil. — Ibn ʿĀshūr reports the Torah-like explanation, but calls it unlikely, preferring human cognition's development through the test and comparing 2:31. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL-]

## 7:19/q22 How is 'this' in 'this tree' understood?  [هَٰذِهِ · referent]
turns on: Whether the demonstrative binds the ban to one visible specimen or uses that specimen for the species.
- 7:19/q22/p1 One indicated individual tree. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya records this as a possible scope of the demonstrative; the lapse account attributed to Abū ʿAlī says Adam took the ban this way and ate another of the kind. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; SULAMI(قيل); JISHUMI(أبو علي)+; IBNATIYYA-FULL-]
- 7:19/q22/p2 The indicated tree stands for the whole kind. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya compares generic singular usage. Al-Sulamī's account says God intended the whole kind although Adam understood one individual; al-Jishumī reports the same mismatch. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; SULAMI(قيل); JISHUMI(أبو علي)]

## 7:19/q23 How is 'this' pronounced and derived?  [هَٰذِهِ · readings]
- 7:19/q23/p1 Ibn Muḥayṣin reads hādhī, preserving an older yāʾ form. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the final hāʾ of hādhihi replaces yāʾ at pause and survives connected speech. Al-Bayḍāwī derives hādhī from diminutive dhayyā, with hāʾ substituted for yāʾ. Al-Ālūsī instead treats hāʾ as compensation for deleted yāʾ, not a pause marker; he reports Ibn Jinnī's derivation of dhī from masculine dhā and dhayyā, reduction of one yāʾ and conversion of the other to alif to ease the ending. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن محيصن); BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(ابن جني)]

## 7:19/q24 How is 'then you become' parsed?  [فَتَكُونَا · grammar]
turns on: Whether fāʾ marks the forbidden act's causal outcome (subjunctive) or coordinates a second prohibition (jussive).
- 7:19/q24/p1 Subjunctive: becoming wrongdoers is the consequence of approaching. — Al-Zajjāj prefers this; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Ibn ʿĀshūr hold it. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the causation runs from the forbidden action, not from uttering the prohibition. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Burhān, and al-Ālūsī allow it as one parse. [7 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURHAN; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ZAJJAJ+]
- 7:19/q24/p2 Jussive: it is coordinated with the prohibition. — Al-Zajjāj reports 'do not approach and do not become wrongdoers'; al-Naḥḥās calls the latter a coordinated consequence. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Burhān and al-Ālūsī allow it. Ibn ʿĀshūr says this parse treats the result as an answer to the prohibiting speech act, by analogy with a condition; al-Burhān cites the formulation as a parallel. [7 notes: ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURHAN; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 7:19/q25 What does becoming 'wrongdoers' mean?  [ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the description foregrounds harm to the pair, lost reward, or breach of God's commanded right; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī denies a legislative breach.
- 7:19/q25/p1 They would wrong or harm themselves. — Muqātil, al-Jishumī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Bursevī, and Ibn ʿĀshūr give self-harm; al-Jishumī calls the warning a threat. Al-Biqāʿī compares the wrongdoer to someone walking in darkness and says disobedience can destroy a high station. Al-Ṭabrisī specifies forfeiting a great reward. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī insists this is harm under guidance, not breach of a binding legislative command. [8 notes: MUQATIL; JISHUMI; TABRISI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 7:19/q25/p2 They would violate God's command and right to obedience. — Al-Ṭabarī calls wrongdoing doing what they had no right to do and gives the same gloss at 2:35. Ibn ʿĀshūr includes violation of God's right as an alternative route to the description; Elmalılı says crossing divine limits makes a person unjust to himself. [3 notes: TAB-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 7:19/q26 How could Adam come to eat despite the prohibition?  [وَلَا تَقْرَبَا ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether Adam acted knowingly, misunderstood the demonstrative, forgot the warning or command, or whether the account stresses the divine purpose of his lapse.
- 7:19/q26/p1 He knowingly intended the forbidden act after Iblis's promise. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls this the majority exegetical view: Iblis claimed the ban withheld immortality or angelhood. He argues that such a promise would not persuade Adam to violate something he already considered lawful. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 7:19/q26/p2 He mistook the ban as applying only to the indicated tree and ate another of the kind. — Al-Sulamī says Adam repented for lack of caution, not disobedience. Al-Jishumī calls Abū ʿAlī's version the soundest and says forgetfulness would remove binding force. Ibn ʿAṭiyya rejects it: Iblis's promise would be pointless if Adam believed the other tree permitted. [2 notes: SULAMI(قيل); JISHUMI(أبو علي)+; IBNATIYYA-FULL-]
- 7:19/q26/p3 They forgot the warning and thought the ban was merely discouragement. — Al-Jishumī records this explanation separately from forgetting the ban itself. [1 notes: JISHUMI(قيل)]
- 7:19/q26/p4 They forgot the prohibition itself. — Al-Jishumī reports this and notes that a forgotten prohibition is not binding, using the point in support of Abū ʿAlī's explanation. [1 notes: JISHUMI(قيل)]
- 7:19/q26/p5 God allowed a lapse so its lesson and proof would emerge. — Al-Kharrāz says even Paradise's command and prohibition unsettled Adam; God allowed the lapse, expelled and reproached him, and 20:115 is cited. [1 notes: SULAMI(الخراز)]

## 7:19/q27 What default ruling applies to acts or new cases without an explicit ruling?  [فَكُلَا وَلَا تَقْرَبَا · law]
turns on: How silence in revelation bears on a new act and whether reasons based on divine or another's property can establish a prior rule.
- 7:19/q27/p1 Default to prohibition and caution. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports jurists who say silence does not license people to legislate permission for a new case without text, Prophetic report, or consensus. He reports a rationalist form: use of another's property is encroachment, especially God's property. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 7:19/q27/p2 Default to permission until a ban arrives. — Jurists argue that God completed religion and would not hide an intended prohibition. A rationalist version permits harmless use of another's property, such as shade from a wall, especially when God suffers no harm. Some exempt unavoidable breathing and movement from a ban; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says necessity could permit them. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(بعض هذه الفرقة,بعضهم)]
- 7:19/q27/p3 Suspend judgment while seeking a general or specific revealed ruling. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls this the clearest juristic response; cases still require revealed authority and further inquiry. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(بعضهم)+]

## 7:19/q28 Can reason alone establish a binding moral ruling before revelation?  [فَكُلَا وَلَا تَقْرَبَا · theology]
turns on: Whether moral obligation comes from reason independently or from revelation, and whether a person without revelation is a meaningful case.
- 7:19/q28/p1 No; binding judgments arise through revelation. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya distinguishes jurists' derivation from revelation from rational good and evil. He says their new-case debate is not a denial of this principle and rejects Ibn al-Bāqillānī's accusation that jurists unwittingly adopted a Muʿtazilī argument. The people of truth and Sunna state the principle expressly. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(أهل الحق والسنة)]
- 7:19/q28/p2 Reason can judge good and evil in a hypothetical absence of revelation. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya attributes this framing to Muʿtazila and like-minded thinkers, who imagine a person or period without revelation or Adam before earthly commands. Ibn al-Bāqillānī says jurists borrowed this argument without grasping its implications; Ibn ʿAṭiyya objects to that charge. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(القاضي ابن الباقلاني,المعتزلة ومن قال بقولهم); IBNATIYYA-FULL-]
- 7:19/q28/p3 The supposed revelation-free case never arises. — Some argue reason is never without revelation; Adam received Paradise commands, including praise after sneezing and directions to dwell, eat, and avoid the tree. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(بعضهم)]

## 7:19/q29 Can believers' sins be placed on unbelievers?  [ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · theology]
turns on: How to reconcile 6:164's individual burden with 29:13's additional burdens.
- 7:19/q29/p1 God may forgive believers and place their sins on Jews and Christians while punishing unbelievers for their own sins. — Al-Bursevī cites a report of mountain-sized forgiven sins and interprets it as grace to believers and justice to sinners. Against a Muʿtazilī appeal to 6:164, he cites 29:13 on unbelievers bearing their own and added burdens. [3 notes: BURSEVI]
- 7:19/q29/p2 No; 6:164 bars transferring another's burden. — Al-Bursevī reports the Muʿtazilī objection but rejects its application by citing 29:13. [0 notes: BURSEVI-]

## 7:19/q30 How does this telling fit the story and audience of al-Aʿrāf?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes offer complementary explanations: immediate sūra audience and the broader function of repeated narration.
- 7:19/q30/p1 It instructs humanity through Adam's favored beginning, temptation, and fall, with special force for al-Aʿrāf's audience. — Elmalılı says humanity's first station was elevated: angels prostrated before Adam's Garden residence. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the scene honors Adam in front of punished Iblis and warns polytheists who chose Satan as patron; 2:35 addresses Israelites wary of Satan and recalls favor, sin, repentance. Asad says appetite, vanity, and false self-interest expose human weakness and the need for continuing prophetic guidance. Study Quran describes 7:11–27 as creation, temptation, and expulsion. [6 notes: ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN]
- 7:19/q30/p2 The recurring story reaches different listeners and uses varied wording to teach. — Al-Jishumī says revelation over twenty-odd years amid changing visitors required retelling; distinct eloquent formulations also demonstrate Quranic inimitability. Ibn ʿĀshūr says variation refreshes repeated material and distributes favors for moral instruction. [4 notes: JISHUMI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 7:19/q31 How does the rest of Adam's account differ between al-Aʿrāf and al-Baqara?  [* · links]
- 7:19/q31/p1 Al-Baqara's wording honors and softens Adam's case; al-Aʿrāf's wording emphasizes reproach and the consequences of deception. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī says 2:36 calls the offense a slip, whereas al-Aʿrāf describes being lowered; reproach appears in 7:22 and answers the descendants' ingratitude at 7:10. Baqara omits explicit confession and reports accepted repentance; Aʿrāf gives the plea in 7:23 without acceptance, echoing the punished peoples' confessions at 7:4–5. Adam's verbal 'we wronged' signals a passing slip, unlike descendants' nominal 'we were wrongdoers' and settled persistence. Baqara's 2:38 offers freedom from fear and grief in the afterlife; Ṭā Hā's 20:123 uses an intensive 'follow' for guidance through worldly struggle and afterlife success, matching its third-person attribution and 20:117's suffering, whereas Baqara's lighter verb suits direct divine attribution and recurs there especially often. Lamāsāt reiterates the slip/lowering contrast. [15 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 7:19/q32 Where do commentators direct readers for the fuller explanation of this verse?  [* · links]
- 7:19/q32/p1 To the parallel at 2:35, sometimes also 20:120. — Al-Wāḥidī's two commentaries and al-Qurṭubī refer back to al-Baqara; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī likewise send readers to their earlier Garden discussions. Al-Rāzī lists ten deferred questions: dwelling as worship or permission, Eve's creation, Garden location, eating permission, the ban's force, tree identity and kind, sin gravity, injustice and condemnation, and whether Adam was already a prophet, mentioning 11:18. Asad adds 20:120. Study Quran notes the 2:35 parallel. [8 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN]
