# 96:11/q01 How is أَرَءَيْتَ read? [أَرَءَيْتَ · readings]
turns on: The pronunciation of the hamza or vowel length, without semantic difference.
- 96:11/q01/p1 Its hamza is eased by tashīl.
  reasons: QIRAAT-ER says the softened hamza becomes a glide and leaves the meaning unchanged.
  holders (1 notes): QIRAAT-ER
  notes: QIRAAT-ER:96:11:1#1/r1
- 96:11/q01/p2 Its hamza is omitted.
  reasons: QIRAAT-ER identifies this as a canonical pronunciation, contrasting it with Ḥafṣ's retained hamza; the difference is phonological.
  holders (1 notes): QIRAAT-ER
  notes: QIRAAT-ER:96:11:1#2/r1
- 96:11/q01/p3 The vowel before yāʾ is lengthened by madd.
  reasons: QIRAAT-ER says the lengthening adds rhetorical weight but has the same meaning as the Ḥafṣ form.
  holders (1 notes): QIRAAT-ER
  notes: QIRAAT-ER:96:11:1#3/r1
sources: QIRAAT-ER = QIRAAT-ER

# 96:11/q02 What does the question أَرَءَيْتَ do? [أَرَءَيْتَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the verb is an idiom of inquiry or is distributed between visual and cognitive senses.
- 96:11/q02/p1 It prompts the hearer to consider or tell what he thinks, rebuking and astonishing him.
  reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it an interrogative challenge rather than knowledge-based vision with two objects. Bursawī calls it inward seeing meaning 'tell me.' Al-Ālūsī derives this usage from seeing leading to knowledge and allows visual or mental seeing. Elmalılı says it seeks attention, admonition, rebuke or wonder rather than information. Al-Rāghib, cited by Bint al-Shāṭiʾ, compares it to akhbirnī; she cites Meccan parallels in 107:1, 19:77, 25:43, 45:23, 53:33 and the araytum series in 56:58, 63, 68, 71, plus 10:59, 26:75, 35:40, 39:38, 53:19, 46:4.
  holders (15 notes): IBNATIYYA; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ELMALILI; BINTSHATI(الراغب); WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL
  notes: IBNATIYYA:96:11/r1 BURSEVI:v10p365#2/r8 ALUSI:96:11/r13 ELMALILI:96:9-19/r1 BINTSHATI:v2p28/r3 BINTSHATI:v2p29/r1 BINTSHATI:v2p30/r2 WAHIDI-QT:96:11/r1 WAHIDI-WAJIZ:96:9-17/r3 BAGHAWI:96:13/r2 BAGHAWI-FULL:v8p480#2/r4 BIQAI:96:11/r1 BIQAI-FULL:v8p484/r1 IBNASHUR:96:11/r1 IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p446/r7
- 96:11/q02/p2 It can represent literal sight for the first question and inward cognition for the later questions.
  reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports a parse in which the first occurrence needs no second object while the later two are cognitive; another view makes all three cognitive.

# 96:11/q03 Who is addressed by أَرَءَيْتَ? [أَرَءَيْتَ · referent]
turns on: The pronoun sequence, contextual exclusion of the two men, or an intentional shift like a judge addressing opposing parties.
- 96:11/q03/p1 The Prophet is addressed throughout.
  reasons: Al-Rāzī says matching the preceding and following addresses preserves order. Abū Ḥayyān says the pronoun sequence supports it; al-Baḥr prefers it. Al-Māwardī allows an address to the Prophet alone.

# 96:11/q04 How do the repeated أَرَءَيْتَ questions and conditional answers fit grammatically? [أَرَءَيْتَ إِن كَانَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether raʾayta needs two syntactic objects, whether the conditional can serve as its second object, and whether an interrogative answer can lack fāʾ.
- 96:11/q04/p1 The first question takes the forbidding man as its first object; later objects and conditional answers are omitted, and the final question about God's sight supplies their sense.
  reasons: Al-Ṭabarī calls the repetitions elliptical substitutions. Al-Bayḍāwī says the conditional serves as second object. Al-Zamakhsharī, as reported by Abū Ḥayyān, places the relative and conditional clauses as two objects and lets 'did he not know?' supply the answer without fāʾ. Bursawī, al-Ālūsī and al-ʿĀshūr recover omitted objects or answers from 96:14 or the prior prohibition; al-Ālūsī says this is semantic ellipsis, not tanāzuʿ because clauses cannot be pronominalized. Elmalılı favors omitted consequences and an independent continuation.
