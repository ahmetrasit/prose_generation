# 96:11/q10 What consequence does the verse's conditional imply? [إِن كَانَ عَلَى ٱلْهُدَىٰٓ · interpretation]
turns on: Which antecedent is chosen for kāna and whether the omitted result is a rebuke to the forbider, a missed alternative for him, God's sight, or astonishment.
- 96:11/q10/p1 Would the opponent still forbid someone on guidance, and is his opposition not ruinous?
  reasons: Al-Qurṭubī stresses the opponent's doom. Ibn Kathīr and Ibn ʿĀshūr frame it as the shock of stopping even a guided person; the latter reconstructs 'would he forbid him too?' Al-Qushayrī asks whether guidance or enjoining piety would not be better for the worshipper. Al-Jishumī says the mistaken tyrant destroys himself.

# 96:11/q11 Why pair guidance here with commanding piety in the following clause? [ٱلْهُدَىٰٓ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the clauses describe the worshipper's prayer and preaching or the forbider's supposed command to piety.
- 96:11/q11/p1 Prayer reforms the Prophet himself, while enjoining piety reforms or calls others; both angered Abu Jahl.
  reasons: Al-Rāzī says Abu Jahl especially resented prayer and calling people to God. The Prophet's prayer also itself preached by example, softening hearts toward faith more powerfully than words. Al-Farrāʾ, cited by Abū Ḥayyān, makes the self/other distinction; al-Ālūsī says forbidding can target prayer and its enacted call, and action precedes verbal enjoining as the stronger call.

# 96:11/q13 What happened when Abu Jahl tried to stop the Prophet's prayer? [* · reports]
turns on: The reports differ in whether they narrate the initial threat and revelation or the subsequent retreat and angelic barrier.
- 96:11/q13/p1 Abu Jahl threatened or forbade Muhammad's prayer, occasioning or illustrating the passage.
  reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās reports a threat, Abu Jahl's boast of his council, revelation of 96:10–13, and angels seizing him if summoned. Another Ibn ʿAbbās report has Abu Jahl encounter and forbid the Prophet, prompting the passage through the lying forelock.
