# 91:7: 16 questions

## 91:7/q01 How does the wāw put the soul into the oath?  [وَنَفْسٍۢ · grammar]
- 91:7/q01/p1 It is an oath particle governing the genitive soul and the other sworn objects. — Jishumī lists bāʾ, its derivative wāw, and tāʾ restricted to God's name. Kashshāf explains that the first wāw substitutes for the omitted oath verb, while coordinated wāws inherit its capacity to govern; Qurṭubī and Ibn ʿĀshūr call the nouns genitive oath objects. [6 notes: JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BURHAN]

## 91:7/q02 Whose soul, or what kind of soul, is meant by the indefinite nafs?  [وَنَفْسٍۢ · referent]
turns on: The indefinite noun can denote a particular magnified soul or a genus; the following inspiration, purification and corruption are used to test the scope.
- 91:7/q02/p1 Human souls generally, each human self or person. — Māwardī reports every soul; ʿAṭāʾ and Baghawī extend it to created jinn and humans. Kashshāf, Rāzī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Bursawī and Ālūsī say indefiniteness can signal multitude, citing 81:14 (Nasafī cites 69:2). Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān read a generic human genus, confirmed by inspiration and purification; Abū Ḥayyān, Ālūsī, Ṭabāṭabāʾī and Elmalılı prefer this over Adam because the sequel addresses all souls. Ibn ʿĀshūr takes it as a species including every self (82:5); Corpus Coranicum compares 75:2. Burhān explains an affirmative indefinite singular can generalize, with kull making it explicit. Rāzī notes distinct animal kinds and unknowable traits, citing 16:8, while Elmalılı restricts the morally inspired rational soul to humans. [38 notes: MAWARDI(ابن جريج,مجاهد); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن جريج,مجاهد); BAGHAWI(عطاء); BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI(عطاء); KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(عطاء); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURHAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; CORPUSCORANICUM; ASAD-NOTES; ALUSI+; ELMALILI+; TABATABAI+]
- 91:7/q02/p2 Adam's particular soul. — Al-Ḥasan is named by Māwardī and later compilers. Kashshāf, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Bursawī and Ālūsī say indefiniteness magnifies him. Ālūsī calls the later change of reference istiḫdām; Abū Ḥayyān finds Adam unlikely because subsequent descriptions suit a generic soul. Elmalılı says this reading needs a special pronoun relation. [17 notes: MAWARDI(الحسن); MAWARDI-FULL(الحسن); TABRISI(عطاء); KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الحسن); QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI(قيل); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ABUHAYYAN(الزمخشري)-; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري)-; ALUSI-; TABATABAI-]
- 91:7/q02/p3 A unique sacred prophetic soul, especially the chief prophet, or the prophetic kind. — Rāzī argues by a hierarchy: composites are led by animals, animals by humans, humans by prophets, and prophets by one supreme leader. Elmalılı lists Muhammad, Adam, prophets collectively, and the rational human kind as magnified readings; specific prophetic readings require a special pronoun relation in the response. [4 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 91:7/q02/p4 The whole human composite, comprising body and spirit or personality. — Rāzī considers nafs as body, and Elmalılı as the spirit-body entity; Asad identifies the whole person, physical body together with elusive life essence. Qāshānī includes the composite among several possible souls. [5 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; ELMALILI; ASAD-NOTES; QASHANI]
- 91:7/q02/p5 The spirit or rational faculty governing the body. — Rāzī distinguishes this from body; Elmalılı likewise allows the governing spirit. Qāshānī distinguishes rational soul and animal potency impressed in animal spirit. [4 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; ELMALILI; QASHANI]
- 91:7/q02/p6 The indefinite marks the soul's grandeur, distinctive significance, or remarkable abilities. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī allows an honorific or distinctive sense. Islāḥī prefers magnitude over multitude or scarcity, citing 85:3 and 90:3. Ālūsī and Bursawī also allow grandeur without limiting the referent. [4 notes: TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 91:7/q03 What does mā in wa-mā sawwāhā denote?  [وَمَا · grammar]
turns on: Whether mā is relative or infinitival, and whether the following fa-alhamahā needs an expressed divine agent.
- 91:7/q03/p1 A relative pronoun denoting God, the One who fashioned the soul. — Akhfash says God swears by Himself; Ṭabarī substitutes man. Abū ʿUbayda similarly uses man in the three clauses, citing 92:3 and 91:6. Ibn Qutayba says mā and man share an origin but normally divide nonhumans and humans; Abū ʿAmr and Meccan thunder speech show mā can be relative as 'that which.' Kashshāf, Nasafī, Bayḍāwī and Elmalılı favor the relative because an infinitive leaves the next verb's subject implicit or disrupts its sequence. Abū Ḥayyān cites al-Ḥasan, Mujāhid, Abū ʿUbayda and Ṭabarī and says mā can cover rational and nonrational beings. Jishumī reports an oath by each created object and its Maker; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the Shaper has power, knowledge and wisdom. [32 notes: AKHFASH; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL(أبو عبيدة,أبو عمرو); TAB; MAWARDI(الحسن); MAWARDI-FULL(الحسن); WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); JISHUMI(الحسن); IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أبو عمرو,أهل مكة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الحسن ومجاهد وأبو عبيدة); QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); ALUSI(الإمام); ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحسن ومجاهد وأبو عبيدة والطبري)+; ELMALILI+; KASHSHAF-FULL+; KASHSHAF+; NASAFI-FULL+; NASAFI+; NAHHAS-; STUDYQURAN(some commentators)-]
- 91:7/q03/p2 An infinitive or verbal noun: the soul's fashioning is sworn by. — Ṭabarī offers this alternative; al-Farrāʾ, Qatāda, al-Mubarrad and al-Zajjāj are named by Ālūsī and Elmalılı. Naḥḥās says mā, rather than man, fits the fashioning itself. Qāḍī ʿAbd al-Jabbār wants the Creator's oath to follow oaths by created things; Study Quran reports an attribution to Qurṭubī preferring nominal phrases. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the clauses effects of divine power; Islāḥī sees workmanship and accountability. Kashshāf, Bayḍāwī and Nasafī object that the next fa-alhamahā lacks an expressed agent or coordinated structure. Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī reply that God is understood from context and verb-noun coordination is possible; Ālūsī adds that temporal order may be customary and that the objection burdens both parses. Elmalılı concedes coordination but prefers an expressed Maker. [26 notes: TAB; NAHHAS; WAHIDI-QT; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(قتادة والزجاج); QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قتادة والمبرد والزجاج); ALUSI(الفراء والزجاج والمبرد وقتادة,القاضي عبد الجبار); ALUSI-FULL(الفراء); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ELMALILI(الفراء، الزجاج، المبرد، قتادة)+; STUDYQURAN(al-Qurṭubī)+; KASHSHAF-; KASHSHAF-FULL-; BAYDAWI-; BAYDAWI-FULL-; NASAFI-; NASAFI-FULL-; ALUSI(الزمخشري)-]
- 91:7/q03/p3 Mā refers to the wise cosmic order or purposes behind the acts. — Ālūsī records the proposal that building, spreading, shaping and inspiration are metaphorically predicated of this order, but dismisses it as far-fetched. [0 notes: ALUSI-; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 91:7/q03/p4 Mā means what each feminine object built or formed. — Corpus Coranicum appeals to feminine -hā agreeing with sky, earth and soul, and distinguishes the construction from 92:3, where mā may refer to what God created. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM+]

## 91:7/q04 Why use mā rather than man for the Shaper?  [وَمَا · rhetoric]
turns on: The sources dispute whether mā can point to God and whether it adds a descriptive or enigmatic effect.
- 91:7/q04/p1 It evokes the Maker's power and wisdom, or suspense and wonder. — Kashshāf and Nasafī say mā supplies a descriptive force, as in mighty builder and wise shaper; Abū Ḥayyān objects that neither mā nor man can function as a descriptive adjective. Elmalılı says the ambiguity provokes wonder and introduces an unknown God by His works; Corpus Coranicum says the enigmatic Creator is explicitly named at 91:14. [7 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ELMALILI; CORPUSCORANICUM; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]
- 91:7/q04/p2 The ordinary distribution favors man for a person and mā for a thing or act. — Ibn Qutayba gives this human/nonhuman distribution; Naḥḥās says referring to God as 'the One who proportioned' would require man; the grammarians reported by Elmalılı prefer the infinitive to avoid mā referring to God. [3 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; NAHHAS; ELMALILI(الفراء، الزجاج، المبرد، قتادة)]

## 91:7/q05 Is there a reading with man in place of mā?  [وَمَا · readings]
- 91:7/q05/p1 Abū ʿImrān al-Jawnī and others read man in all three parallel clauses. — Ibn al-Jawzī reports 'who built, spread and fashioned' for the sky, earth and soul. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو عمران الجوني)]

## 91:7/q06 What does the suffix -hā in sawwāhā refer back to?  [سَوَّىٰهَا · grammar]
- 91:7/q06/p1 It refers to the feminine soul, as the parallel suffixes refer to sky and earth. — Corpus Coranicum says feminine agreement excludes referring the suffix to mā. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 91:7/q07 What does fashioning or proportioning the soul consist of?  [سَوَّىٰهَا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether taswiyah describes bodily symmetry, the rational faculty, preparation for perfection, or a moral-spiritual balance.
- 91:7/q07/p1 Forming and evenly proportioning its body, limbs, senses and outward organs. — Ṭabarī, Thaʿlabī, Mujāhid, Ibn ʿAbbās and others gloss creation and its balanced limbs. Jishumī adds senses and channels; Rāzī appeals to anatomy; Biqāʿī includes substances, accidents and contrary humors. Ibn ʿĀshūr says without disparity, compares 82:7 and links taswiyah to precise making in 75:38 and 87:2. Corpus Coranicum notes 75:38, 87:2 and 82:7. Ibn al-Jawzī refers back to 82:7. [23 notes: TAB; THALABI; MAWARDI(مجاهد); MAWARDI-FULL(مجاهد); WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); JISHUMI(قتادة وأبي علي); BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI(مجاهد); QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI; DURR(ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,مجاهد); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 91:7/q07/p2 Perfecting intellect, discernment and inward faculties. — Māwardī says reason distinguishes humans from animals; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān say completed intellect and reflection prepare inspiration. Rāzī names hearing, sight, imagination, thought and memory. Ālūsī and Kur'an Yolu include spiritual powers, their functions and organs. Qāshānī describes readiness for perfection between bodily and spiritual worlds. [15 notes: MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; QASHANI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 91:7/q07/p3 Preparing the soul or body for its intended perfection and the breathing of spirit. — Qurṭubī glosses sawwā as preparing. Bursawī says creation endowed readiness for perfection. Elmalılı distinguishes bodily preparation for spirit (15:29; 38:72) from ordering outward and inward powers; Asad calls it forming by an intended pattern (87:2). Islāḥī treats taswiyah as the final stage of creation (87:2). [9 notes: QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 91:7/q07/p4 Creating the soul upright upon a sound natural disposition. — Ibn Kathīr invokes 30:30, the Prophet's report that newborns have fitra before parents turn them toward other religions (Bukhārī and Muslim), and ʿIyāḍ's report that God made servants upright monotheists before devils diverted them. [3 notes: IBNKATHIR(أبو هريرة,عياض بن حمار المجاشعي)]
- 91:7/q07/p5 Making souls equal in health and equal in punishment. — Māwardī attributes this detail, within the universal-soul view, to Ibn Jurayj. [2 notes: MAWARDI(ابن جريج); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن جريج)]
- 91:7/q07/p6 Balancing the animal, composite or rational soul between bodily lowliness and spiritual elevation. — Qāshānī likens the balance to neither east nor west (24:35), a proportioned temperament, or readiness for perfection between two worlds, depending on the soul meant. [2 notes: QASHANI]

## 91:7/q08 How is fashioning linked by the following fāʾ to inspiration?  [وَمَا سَوَّىٰهَا · links]
turns on: Whether fāʾ demands immediate temporal succession, and whether inspiration can share the syntax of the oath clause.
- 91:7/q08/p1 Fashioning makes intellect ready, then God inspires the soul. — Abū Muslim connects the fāʾ to the shaping clause; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān say completed intellect and reflection explain the subsequent inspiration. Ālūsī says adjusted organs and thought are accompanied by guidance along two paths (90:10), which grows with the faculties. [7 notes: JISHUMI(أبو مسلم); IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ALUSI]
- 91:7/q08/p2 The sequence is problematic if inspiration is taken as immediately after bodily formation. — The reported objection says formation comes before breathing the soul, while inspiration comes after maturity. Ālūsī replies that guidance begins with the soul's formation and develops with it; elsewhere he says the fāʾ may express customary rather than literal temporal order. Ṭībī appeals to balanced cadence against verb coordination, which Ālūsī says assumes an independent following clause. [6 notes: ALUSI(الطيبي); ALUSI(قيل)+; ALUSI-]

## 91:7/q09 What does the fashioned soul's nature teach about moral choice and accountability?  [وَنَفْسٍۢ وَمَا سَوَّىٰهَا · theology]
turns on: The notes set human capacity and choice beside reports that deeds and destinations are divinely decreed.
- 91:7/q09/p1 It is equipped to discern and choose good or evil, so purification brings success and corruption loss. — Ibn Qutayba says even the ignorant recognize righteous and wicked deeds. Quṭb traces equal readiness to earthly clay and breathed spirit. Biqāʿī says capacity and choice attend differing dispositions; Ibn al-Zubayr calls the capacity acquired, not independent, citing 90:10, 76:3 and 37:96. Islāḥī calls the soul a witness to its Maker and accountability, comparing closing one's eyes while walking; divine favors should be used gratefully. Kur'an Yolu says human superiority entails moral powers. Corpus Coranicum reads verse 8's opposed capacities and verses 9–10's outcomes as the oath's moral movement. [17 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; BIQAI(الأستاذ أبو جعفر ابن الزبير); BIQAI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; CORPUSCORANICUM; ASAD-NOTES]
- 91:7/q09/p2 God decreed and prepared each person's deeds and final destination. — The Prophet, in Ibn Abī Ḥātim's report, says deeds are decreed yet people act because each is prepared for the created destination. Thaʿlabī's Abū al-Aswad answers ʿImrān that all is decreed and God owns creation (21:23); another Thaʿlabī note says God prepares the two destinations. Ālūsī transmits the two Muzayna men's question and the Prophet's answer citing 91:7–8, with Bukhārī, Muslim and Abū Dāwūd named. [4 notes: IBNABIHATIM(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); THALABI(أبو الأسود الدؤلي عن عمران بن حصين); ALUSI(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 91:7/q10 What is the oath's answer or implied conclusion?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the complement is read as the explicit purification contrast or as a broader implicit warning.
- 91:7/q10/p1 The answer is that whoever purifies the soul succeeds, while whoever corrupts it fails. — Akhfash identifies qad aflaḥa man zakkāhā. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the oath prepares this contrast; Islāḥī says the soul's innate moral awareness witnesses the same outcome. Corpus Coranicum reads verses 9–10 as the antithetical result of the oath series. [5 notes: AKHFASH; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 91:7/q10/p2 Its implied conclusion is God's differing dispositions and the need to purify oneself in view of resurrection. — Biqāʿī says God prepared souls for purification or corruption through capacity and choice. [2 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL]
- 91:7/q10/p3 Its suppressed complement includes the threatened destruction of rebellious nations. — Islāḥī says ellipsis permits everything evident from the oaths; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the oath confirms the implicit destruction threat carried by the news. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 91:7/q11 How does the soul oath relate to the preceding cosmic oaths and the sūra's argument?  [* · links]
turns on: How the sequence shifts from cosmic signs to the human moral subject and the Creator.
- 91:7/q11/p1 The series moves from visible cosmic creation to the human soul and, through it, to the unseen Creator and moral duty. — Rāzī says heaven and earth are simple upper and lower bodies, with ensouled beings the noblest composites; sensory signs lead reason to divine majesty. Biqāʿī calls the soul a comprehensive miniature cosmos, whose wise order leads to its Maker and self-reform. Bursawī and Elmalılı likewise move from visible creation to the Maker and the moral soul. Corpus Coranicum sees sun/moon, night/day and heaven/earth pairs narrowing vertically to the moral subject; a pause after verse 4 shifts to divine creative activity in verses 5–8. Asad sees bodily, emotional and intellectual personhood following cosmic contemplation. Qurṭubī says created wonders point to God, and Rāzī says oaths invite reflection and gratitude. [21 notes: RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI(الإمام); ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; CORPUSCORANICUM; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 91:7/q11/p2 The soul's inner constitution mirrors the entire outward cosmos. — Biqāʿī maps seven heavens to head layers and faculties; sun to spirit, night to temperament, day to heart, moon to soul; organs to stars; tears, grief, joy, speech, salt and breath to rain, heat, cold, thunder, lightning and winds; body layers, bones and fluids to earth layers, mountains and waters; hair and parasites to vegetation and animals. Islāḥī calls the soul a mirror of the larger world and its moral balance. [7 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 91:7/q11/p3 The oath recasts a traditional cumulative oath form for creation and judgment. — Corpus Coranicum cautiously compares pre-Islamic soothsayers and points to parallels at 93:1–2; it says the eight-oath series repurposes the form for biblical creation ideas. Its cosmic-to-human movement resembles 81:1–14, though that passage depicts eschatological dissolution. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the series displays wise and powerful creation; Burhān lists oaths by God, His acts and created things. [6 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM; IBNASHUR-FULL; BURHAN]
- 91:7/q11/p4 This sūra continues the warning of Sūrat al-Balad to Quraysh. — Islāḥī says Balad warned that Mecca's prosperity came through Abraham's prayer and the House (90:4), while Shams warns what follows if Quraysh persist in arrogance. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 91:7/q12 What inward correspondences are read into the oath's images?  [* · inward]
turns on: The authorities give different correspondences between the oath's created images and inner realities.
- 91:7/q12/p1 The cosmos is read as the human body and its faculties. — Biqāʿī gives detailed correspondences: heavens/head, sun/spirit, moon/soul, day/heart, night/temperament, stars/organs, weather/feelings and speech, earth/body, vegetation/hair and animals/parasites. [5 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 91:7/q12/p2 The images map divine, spiritual and material levels. — Ālūsī records one scheme with sun as necessary existence or divine essence, moon as fixed entities or first determination, day/night as manifestations or beauty/majesty, heaven/earth as subtle/dense worlds, and camel as spiritual or legal path. Another makes sun divine essence, brightness Muhammadan reality, moon possible-being, day manifest world, night contingent veiling, heaven intellect, earth body, and camel/drink longing and remembrance or reflection. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعض آخر,بعض أهل التأويل)]
- 91:7/q12/p3 The soul stands balanced between lordly light and bodily darkness. — Qāshānī reads animal potency, composite or rational soul as proportioned temper or readiness for perfection, likening the balance to neither east nor west (24:35). [2 notes: QASHANI]

## 91:7/q13 What relation does this verse's sawwāhā have to later wording in the sūra?  [سَوَّىٰهَا · links]
- 91:7/q13/p1 It echoes later sawwāhā as a verbal and moral link between creation and punishment. — Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the echo with fa-sawwāhā complete paronomasia. Corpus Coranicum describes the second use, God's leveling in punishment at verse 14, as sarcastically enforcing the obligation established by formation of the soul. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 91:7/q14 What was reported about reciting this sūra aloud?  [* · reports]
- 91:7/q14/p1 The Prophet raised his voice with the Sun and Night sūras at midday so listeners learned their times. — Al-Durr reports Anas and Ubayy's question whether the Prophet had been commanded to recite them. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(أنس)]

## 91:7/q15 How are human souls related to pure, evil or celestial spirits?  [وَنَفْسٍۢ · theology]
turns on: The reported accounts differ over whether lower spirits exist and how other spirits influence human souls.
- 91:7/q15/p1 Affinity joins luminous souls to pure spirits and dark souls to evil spirits. — Rāzī presents an affinity principle: assisting spirits reinforce virtue or sin and aggression according to the soul's quality. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 91:7/q15/p2 A reported third group denies lower spirits but assigns human souls to distinct celestial spirits. — Rāzī reports celestial souls assigned to spheres, first tied to a heart or planet and then radiating influence, analogous to bodily spirits moving through arteries and nerves. Planetary rays engender individual human souls; souls from one planet, such as Saturn, have mutual affinity and differ from those of Jupiter. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL(الفريق الثالث)]

## 91:7/q16 How does Ibn ʿAbbās gloss the sūra's imagery?  [* · reports]
- 91:7/q16/p1 He explains the cosmic oaths as light, succession, illumination, divine building, and spreading, and the soul verses as guidance and corruption. — Al-Durr reports Ibn ʿAbbās: the sun is its light, the moon follows it, daylight illuminates it, God builds heaven, earth is spread out, inspiration shows good and evil, and corruption leads astray. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]
