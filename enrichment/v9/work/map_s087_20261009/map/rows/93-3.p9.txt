[BINTSHATI:v1p23/r7] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | Reports of occasions are contextual clues, not necessarily causal legal explanations; early authorities admit uncertainty and disagreement, and the verses came down around the event.
[BINTSHATI:v1p26/r1] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | prefers | The daily alternation from bright duha to a quiet night is a sensory image that prepares for the unseen pause in revelation, without suggesting that heaven abandoned earth.
[BINTSHATI:v1p26/r2] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | The sensory contrast between light and darkness can clarify unseen guidance and truth or misguidance and falsehood.
[BINTSHATI:v1p27/r1] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · فخر الدين الرازي ونظام الدين النيسابوري | reports | They also say the oath answers the disbelievers' taunt: they were unable to prove their claim that God had abandoned Muhammad.
[BINTSHATI:v1p27/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الشيخ محمد عبده | reports | Abduh says the Prophet's distress during the pause was relieved by renewed revelation: duha represents life-giving first revelation, while quiet night restores strength for later work.
[BINTSHATI:v1p27/r4] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | rejects | The author's sensory analogy is nearly lost in Abduh's account of night as rest and preparation for work.
[BINTSHATI:v1p27/r5] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · ابن قيم الجوزية | reports | Ibn al-Qayyim comes close to matching the bright duha after darkness with revelation after its pause, but frames the oath as proof of God's lordship, wisdom, and mercy.
[BINTSHATI:v1p28/r1] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | rejects | The author says Ibn al-Qayyim's appeal to doubts about God's lordship misses the stated occasion, which concerns a claim that God abandoned Muhammad.
[BINTSHATI:v1p28/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | rejects | The author finds no clear connection between the oath and its answer in the accounts of al-Tabari, al-Zamakhshari, and Abu Hayyan, and notes they do not compare it with God's explicit denials of oaths.
[BINTSHATI:v1p32/r2] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | rejects | The author excludes esoteric readings that turn duha and night into the Prophet's face and hair, male and female relatives, knowledge and forgiveness, or Islam's rise and later estrangement.
[BINTSHATI:v1p32/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | The majority read wadda'aka with a doubled dāl, while some read the verb with a light dāl; eloquent Arabic generally avoided the simple triliteral form.

[BINTSHATI:v1p33/r1] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · أبو حيان | reports | Abu Hayyan says eloquent Arabic dispensed with the simple triliteral form, and a lexicon commentator's objection does not undo that point.
[BINTSHATI:v1p33/r2] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الزمخشري | reports | Wada' means leaving, with tawdi' intensifying the sense because a person who bids farewell leaves you emphatically.

[BINTSHATI:v1p34/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الطبري وأبو حيان | reports | Al-Tabari and Abu Hayyan explain the omitted addressee through the preceding mention of the Prophet and as concise expression.
[BINTSHATI:v1p34/r4] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · النيسابوري | reports | Al-Nisaburi adds that omission preserves the rhyme endings of the surah.
[BINTSHATI:v1p34/r5] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الرازي | reports | Al-Razi gives three reasons: the earlier kaaf suffices, rhyme requires omission, or the unrestricted object includes all who love the Prophet; the author rejects the expansive reading as unsupported by context.
[BINTSHATI:v1p35/r1] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | rejects | The author rejects rhyme alone as the reason for omission: the Qur'anic style is not governed by form alone, and the surah's last verse does not follow a th-rhyme or use a substitute verb to force one.
[BINTSHATI:v1p35/r2] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | prefers | The addressee is clear from what precedes; omitting the harsh verb comforts the Prophet, since qala implies expulsion and severe hatred, while tawdi' can suggest reluctant parting and hoped-for return.
[BINTSHATI:v1p35/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الرازي والنيسابوري | reports | Some explain the delay by the Prophet's failure to say God willing after promising answers to Jewish questions, or by a puppy or untrimmed nails preventing Gabriel's entry; the author rejects these as strained, and notes the puppy story conflicts with the children's post-Hijra birth.
