[ELMALILI:6:75-82/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Abraham’s words “this is my Lord” on seeing a star first serve as a taunt to those around him, questioning whether a star could nurture a human being.
[ELMALILI:6:75-82/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | By saying he does not love those that set, Abraham teaches that love grounds lordship and worship, while motion and setting show createdness, subjection, change, and perishability; the true Lord creates, acts, and is free from extinction.
[ELMALILI:6:75-82/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The argument about setting also exposes the contradiction of replacing setting stars with idols: if the original star is inadequate, its fashioned image is even less worthy.

[IBNASHUR-FULL:v7p320/r2] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | The rational masculine plural الآفلين reflects Ibrahim’s people’s belief that stars are intelligent agents governing the universe, since a worshiped being must know.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v7p320/r3] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | Setting disqualifies a star because a deity must continuously oversee servants, while the absent star cannot attend to their needs; Ibrahim argues from his people’s view that setting means absence from this world.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v7p320/r4] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | The argument is not that change disproves divinity: his people did not accept that entailment, and setting is only an appearance to observers while the star remains in its orbit and returns.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v7p320/r5] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | He argues from setting rather than rising because the earlier setting was not witnessed, making the point shorter than explaining that the rising star had previously set.
