[TABATABAI:7:180-186#7/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The promise to answer a caller (2:186) requires a true request directed to God; one who turns from every other cause reaches the reality of the name suited to the need, whose effect follows its scope. | mentions 2:186
[TABATABAI:7:180-186#7/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The reports and prayers about the greatest name should be understood as connection to the name's reality; teaching a servant a name opens this way of turning to God, while words and meanings only preserve realities.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186#7/r5] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | A name may be exclusive to God; al-Ilāh is exclusive as a proper name, whereas al-Raḥmān has a shared meaning as one of the beautiful names.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186#8/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Allah is a proper name exclusive to God rather than a name in the technical sense under discussion; al-Raḥmān has a shared meaning, and the legal inquiry lies outside this exegetical discussion.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186#8/r2] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Scripture does not set a fixed number of beautiful names: 20:8, 7:180, and 59:24 indicate that every name whose meaning is most beautiful belongs to God. | mentions 20:8, 7:180, 59:24
[TABATABAI:7:180-186#8/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The author enumerates the divine names found in the Qur'an, grouping them alphabetically by their Arabic initials.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186#8/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The names belong to God essentially and originally; others possess their meanings only derivatively by God's gift, and that gift does not remove them from God's ownership.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186#9/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Comparative forms such as al-Aʿlā and al-Akram, and genitive forms such as best of judges and providers, imply that divine and human referents share a basic meaning.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186#9/r2] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | rejects | There is no exegetical proof that divine names are restricted to transmitted forms; the opposing argument assumes both a covenantal reading of the article and that illicit naming means exceeding transmitted names, and both assumptions are questionable.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186#9/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | rejects | The well-known report that God has ninety-nine names and that whoever enumerates them enters Paradise does not prove that names are restricted to that number.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186#9/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Exegetically the names are not restricted to transmitted forms, while legal caution favors using only transmitted names for formal naming; ordinary predication is less restricted.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186#9/r5] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · علي عليه السلام | reports | A report transmitted in al-Tawḥīd through al-Riḍā and his forefathers says that invoking God by ninety-nine names brings a response and enumerating them leads to Paradise; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī explains enumeration as faith in all their meanings.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186#9/r6] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Abū Hurayra reports the Prophet's saying that God has ninety-nine names and whoever enumerates them enters Paradise, for God is singular and loves the singular.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | These verses continue the preceding argument by restating that guidance and misguidance turn on calling God by His beautiful names or distorting them.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186/r2] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | By natural disposition, people agree that the visible world depends on God, who orders it, originates and receives all things, and gives it its beauty and perfection.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | One group names God only with attributes of perfection or names that negate every defect, thereby calling on Him by the beautiful names.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | A second group distorts divine names by assigning creation, life, or provision to matter or time, good to idols, divine prerogatives to prophets or saints, or independent power to created causes.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186/r5] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | A third group believes in God but attributes defects to Him, including body, location, sensory apprehension, humanlike knowledge, will, or power, temporal persistence, injustice, and ignorance.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186/r6] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The three groups reduce to two: those who worship God by His beautiful names are rightly guided, while those who misname Him or assign His name elsewhere are astray and headed for the Fire according to their error.
[TABATABAI:7:180-186/r7] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Guidance originates wholly with God; misguidance itself is a privation, but God makes it persistent by withholding success as recompense, through gradual enticement and respite. | mentions 7:179
[TABATABAI:7:180/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | A name linguistically denotes an object, either with descriptive meaning or as a bare reference to the essence; the phrase beautiful names points to descriptive names, not bare proper names.
[TABATABAI:7:180/r2] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Not every descriptive meaning is a beautiful name: a name must express the best perfection when considered with the divine essence, without inseparable deficiency.
[TABATABAI:7:180/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Courage and chastity are unsuitable divine names because they imply bodily particularity, whereas generosity, justice, and mercy can be purified of such limitation.
[TABATABAI:7:180/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Human terms such as knowledge, life, and power acquire bodily limitations in their created instances, but when abstracted to their perfect meanings they may be attributed to God without those limitations.
