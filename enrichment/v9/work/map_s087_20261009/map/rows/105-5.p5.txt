[ASAD-NOTES:105:1#n3/r1] ASAD-NOTES, d. 1412 · some authorities | reports | Some authorities regard this passage as continued in the next surah, making the two surahs one part.
[ASAD-NOTES:105:intro/r1] ASAD-NOTES, d. 1412 · author | holds | Asad dates the campaign against Mecca to 570 CE: Abyssinian Yemen’s viceroy Abrahah built a cathedral at Sana to redirect pilgrimage, then marched to destroy the Kaaba when that failed.
[ASAD-NOTES:105:intro/r2] ASAD-NOTES, d. 1412 · author | holds | Abrahah’s large force included war elephants, astonishing to Arabs; it was destroyed on the march, probably by smallpox or typhus, and Abrahah died on returning to Sana.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:105:1-5:kommentar/r1] CORPUSCORANICUM · Horovitz | reports | Horovitz calls Q105 the earliest example of a punishment legend.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:105:1-5:kommentar/r2] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | Although kaid may morally evaluate the elephant army, the surah lacks the explicit charge of denial or rebellion typical of later punishment legends; its focus is God's power and protection of Mecca's sanctuary, rescue more than punishment.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:105:1-5:kommentar/r4] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The surah likely invokes a familiar Meccan legend, and later Quranic punishment stories borrow its language, suggesting Q105 helped establish the genre.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:105:1-5:kommentar/r5] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | Q105 and Q106 portray human affairs, especially Mecca's fate, as subject to divine intervention rather than mere worldly causation; Allah's direct destruction of the elephant army was significant because the pre-Islamic Allah was often conceived as inactive.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:105:5:anmerkung/r1] CORPUSCORANICUM · Neuwirth | reports | Neuwirth compares the verse with Psalms 37:1–2 and 58:8, where the wicked wither like grass, and says the episode is biblically colored.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:105:5:anmerkung/r2] CORPUSCORANICUM · Neuwirth | reports | Neuwirth concludes that the episode is thereby biblically inflected.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p460/r2] ISLAHI-TADABBUR · author | holds | Calling the enemy eaten straw names it by its eventual fate; human stone-throwing is distinguished from God's power that completed the destruction with a stone-hurling wind.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:105:1/r10] KURANYOLU-TEFSIR · author | holds | Verses 3–5 recount God-sent flocks of birds pelting the elephant army with baked-brick-type stones and turning them into crop remnants eaten by animals and vermin.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:105:1/r13] KURANYOLU-TEFSIR · author | reports | Older tafsirs treat the entire elephant event as a miracle.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:105:1/r17] KURANYOLU-TEFSIR · author | holds | The aggressor who tried to destroy God's House was punished through a miracle, while Meccans who could not resist and fled to the mountains escaped unharmed.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:105:1/r19] KURANYOLU-TEFSIR · author | holds | ʿAṣf means crop chaff and husks eaten by moths, insects, and worms and then scattered by the wind.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:105:1/r21] KURANYOLU-TEFSIR · author | holds | The final verse shows the scale of Abraha's army's calamity and its eventual destruction.
[SAMARRAI-ASRAR:r15#3/r5] SAMARRAI-ASRAR · author | holds | Araf uses “sent” rather than “sent down,” which the author says intensifies punishment; he points to the Elephant passage, where sent birds pelted them and left them like chewed straw. | mentions 2:59, 7:162, 105:3, 105:4, 105:5
[STUDYQURAN:105:5/r1] STUDYQURAN · author | holds | Devoured husks are animal-eaten leaf remains; the surah contrasts Arabia’s greatest army with something among its most insignificant things.
[STUDYQURAN:105:intro/r1] STUDYQURAN · author | holds | The early Meccan surah recounts Abrahah’s 570 campaign against the Kaʿbah after he built a church in Sanʿāʾ to divert pilgrims; God repelled his army with birds and stones.
