[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#2/r3] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | prefers | The clearest governor of إذ قال is حاج: he disputed when Abraham said this.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#2/r4] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · أبو البقاء | reports | Abū al-Baqāʾ makes إذ depend on آتاه; the author questions this because granting kingship was not the time Abraham made his statement, except by loose extension of the time phrase.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#2/r5] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · الزمخشري | reports | Al-Zamakhsharī allows إذ as appositive to أن آتاه if that clause means at the time; the author considers this weak because the time expressions differ unless construed loosely. Abū al-Baqāʾ rejects apposition if أن is causal, unless إذ itself means the verbal noun أن, an attested usage.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#2/r6] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · مكي | rejects | Makkī makes إذ depend on تر, but the author rejects this: seeing, on either interpretation of the parallel expression, did not occur when Abraham said this.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#2/r7] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | holds | ربي is a subject with a relative-clause predicate, and the whole clause is governed in the accusative by قال; أنا أحيي is likewise a subject-predicate clause governed by قال.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#2/r8] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | holds | Abraham states God's power through a relative clause, which implies exclusivity; the disputant's finite clause does not. He did not claim unique possession of that lowly power for himself.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#3/r1] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | holds | أنا is a separate nominative pronoun; its underlying form is أن, with added alif to mark the vowel at pause and ordinarily deleted in connected speech. Some retain it throughout, explained either by extending pause pronunciation into liaison or by dialect.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#3/r2] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | prefers | The sound account recognizes two dialects: Tamīm retains alif in both pause and liaison, which explains Nāfiʿ's reading before hamza with ḍamma or fatḥa and his differing report before kasra, and Ibn ʿĀmir's form noted later. The other dialect retains alif only at pause; liaison retention is poetic necessity. | mentions 7:143, 26:115
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#3/r3] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | reports | Other forms are أنا, أن, and آن; one account explains آن by moving alif before nūn and associates it with time. In أنه, the hāʾ is pause hāʾ rather than a substitute for alif, as cited poetry illustrates.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#3/r4] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | reports | Nāfiʿ retains alif before hamza either by combining the two dialects or because hamza is difficult to pronounce and the long vowel eases it.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#4/r1] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | holds | The fāʾ answers an omitted condition: Abraham effectively says if you claim or pretend that, then God... Without the omitted condition, a quotation after قال would have no fāʾ and would read قال إبراهيم إن الله يأتي.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#4/r2] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · أبو البقاء | reports | Abū al-Baqāʾ says the fāʾ connects the statement to what preceded: if the opponent claims life and death without understanding, the proof is that God brings the sun.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#4/r3] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | holds | The bāʾ in بالشمس makes the verb transitive: أتت الشمس means the sun came, while أتى الله بها means He brought it; من المشرق and من المغرب modify their respective verbs.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#4/r4] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · أبو البقاء | rejects | Abū al-Baqāʾ also allows the directional phrases as circumstances meaning subjugated or compliant, after previously denying that analysis; the author wishes he had maintained his initial rejection.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#4/r5] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | holds | The majority read بهت passively: الذي is raised as the passive subject, with Abraham as the understood agent; alternatively, the original agent may be the speech implied by قال.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#4/r6] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · ابن السميفع | reports | Ibn al-Sumayfiʿ reads بهت with fatḥa on both consonants as active: Abraham may be subject and the disbeliever object, meaning Abraham defeated him in argument; alternatively the disbeliever is subject and Abraham an omitted object as the disbeliever resorted to slander when silenced.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#4/r7] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | reports | On Ibn al-Sumayfiʿ's active reading, an intransitive parse takes الذي as subject, matching the passive reading's meaning of bewilderment; it can also mean to produce a falsehood.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#4/r8] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · أبو حيوة | reports | Abū Ḥaywa reads fatḥa on bāʾ and ḍamma on hāʾ with the relative pronoun as subject; al-Akhfash reports a kasra on hāʾ, also intransitive. The author summarizes three forms: both fatḥa, ḍamma, and kasra; the fatḥa form can be transitive or intransitive.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#4/r9] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | holds | بهت means bewilderment and astonishment; bāhata or bahtahu can mean confronting someone with a lie. The cited saying calls the Jews people of falsehood because lies bewilder the person lied to.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258#5/r1] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | holds | A lie confounds the person against whom it is directed, explaining the use of بهت for bewilderment.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258/r1] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | holds | This opening was discussed at the parallel expression in 2:243; ʿAlī is reported to read تر with a still rāʾ, whose analysis was also given there. | mentions 2:243
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258/r2] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | prefers | The pronoun in ربه most clearly refers to Abraham; another analysis refers it to the disputant.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258/r3] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | holds | حاجه means that he displayed rivalry in argument with Abraham, trying to prevail through his proof.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258/r4] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | prefers | The clearest parse makes أن آتاه a causal object with a deleted preposition, because God gave him kingship. It can then be accusative or genitive; the preposition is omitted because this cause clause lacks the same subject, a deletion allowed with لام before أن.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258/r5] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | reports | One causal interpretation is ironic reversal: the man should have thanked God for kingship but instead made it an occasion for disputation, like the reversal in 56:82 and the example of opposing someone because he treated you well. | mentions 56:82
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258/r6] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · author | reports | The other causal interpretation is that receiving kingship made him arrogant and insolent, which led to his dispute with Abraham.
[SAMIN-DURR:2:258/r7] SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 · الزمخشري | reports | Al-Zamakhsharī instead lets أن and its clause function as a time expression: he disputed when God gave him kingship. The Shaykh questions this because the dispute was not simultaneous with the grant unless time is loose, and notes grammarians' objection to a clause replacing a temporal noun; he counters that maṣdariyya ما can do so.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v1p686#2/r1] IBNKATHIR-FULL, d. 774 · كثير من المنطقيين | rejects | Many logicians say Abraham abandoned the first proof for a clearer second one; Ibn Kathir rejects this account of the argument.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v1p686#2/r2] IBNKATHIR-FULL, d. 774 · author | holds | The first proof serves as a premise for the sun challenge: together they expose Nimrod's false claim at both stages.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v1p686#2/r3] IBNKATHIR-FULL, d. 774 · السدي | reports | Al-Suddi says the debate took place after Abraham came out of the fire, when he met the king for the first time that day.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v1p686#2/r4] IBNKATHIR-FULL, d. 774 · زيد بن أسلم | reports | Zayd ibn Aslam reports that Nimrod withheld provisions from Abraham, who carried home saddlebags of dirt to distract his family; Sarah found them filled with fine food, which Abraham recognized as God's provision.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v1p686#2/r5] IBNKATHIR-FULL, d. 774 · زيد بن أسلم | reports | In Zayd ibn Aslam's report, the king refused an angel's three calls to believe and challenged God to assemble forces; a swarm of gnats destroyed his army, one tormented him in the nose for four hundred years, and it killed him.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v1p686/r1] IBNKATHIR-FULL, d. 774 · author | holds | The disputant is Nimrod, king of Babylon, whom Ibn Kathir identifies as Nimrod son of Canaan, son of Cush, son of Shem, son of Noah.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v1p686/r10] IBNKATHIR-FULL, d. 774 · author | holds | Abraham's challenge shows that the true giver of life and death controls creation and the heavenly bodies; if Nimrod were divine, he should bring the sun from the west.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v1p686/r11] IBNKATHIR-FULL, d. 774 · author | holds | Nimrod was struck dumb and unable to speak when his incapacity became clear, and Abraham's proof stood established.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v1p686/r12] IBNKATHIR-FULL, d. 774 · author | holds | God does not inspire unjust people with proof; their arguments are invalid, and they face divine anger and severe punishment.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v1p686/r2] IBNKATHIR-FULL, d. 774 · author | reports | The source also gives an alternate lineage for Nimrod: son of Falikh, son of Eber, son of Shalikh, son of Arpakhshad, son of Shem, son of Noah.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v1p686/r3] IBNKATHIR-FULL, d. 774 · مجاهد | reports | Mujahid says the four who ruled the world were the believers Solomon and Dhul-Qarnayn and the unbelievers Nimrod and Nebuchadnezzar; Ibn Kathir adds that God knows best.
[IBNKATHIR-FULL:v1p686/r4] IBNKATHIR-FULL, d. 774 · author | holds | The address means to see with the heart, addressed to Muhammad.
