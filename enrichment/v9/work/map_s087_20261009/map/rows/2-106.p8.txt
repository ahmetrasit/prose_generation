[TABRISI:2:106#5/r3] TABRISI, d. 548 · قيل | reports | Some take the address as directed to the Prophet; others take it to all morally responsible people, meaning any listener or person.
[TABRISI:2:106#5/r4] TABRISI, d. 548 · author | holds | On one reading, God can produce verses and sūras like the Qurʾan to replace an abrogated command with something equally beneficial.
[TABRISI:2:106#5/r5] TABRISI, d. 548 · قيل | reports | If addressed to Muhammad, the question means God can help him and defeat his enemies; another view makes it general to everything.
[TABRISI:2:106#5/r6] TABRISI, d. 548 · من زعم أنه لا يجوز نسخ القرآن بالسنة المعلومة | reports | Those denying abrogation of Qurʾan by definitive Sunna argue that the verse attributes the replacement to God, while Sunna is not literally attributed to Him, and that the verse concerns God’s unique power over the miraculous Qurʾan.
[TABRISI:2:106#5/r7] TABRISI, d. 548 · author | rejects | The author holds that definitive Sunna may abrogate Qurʾan: it comes by God’s revelation and command, and ‘better’ means more beneficial for faith and reward, whether delivered by Qurʾan or Sunna.
[TABRISI:2:106#5/r8] TABRISI, d. 548 · author | holds | The verse shows the Qurʾan is created and distinct from God: an eternal thing cannot be abrogated, and anything under God’s power is an originated act.
[TABRISI:2:106/r1] TABRISI, d. 548 · ابن عامر | reports | Ibn ʿĀmir reads nunsikh with a damma on nūn and kasra on sīn; the other readers open both vowels.
[TABRISI:2:106/r2] TABRISI, d. 548 · ابن كثير وأبو عمرو | reports | Ibn Kathīr and Abū ʿAmr read nansaʾhā with both vowels open and a hamza; the rest read nunshihā with a damma, kasra, and no hamza.
[TABRISI:2:106/r3] TABRISI, d. 548 · author | prefers | The best explanation of Ibn ʿĀmir’s form is that nasakha and ansakha are two synonymous dialect forms; the ordinary open-vowel reading is clearer.
[TABRISI:2:106/r4] TABRISI, d. 548 · author | holds | In Arabic, naskh means removing something and putting another thing in its place, as the sun removes and replaces the shade.
[TABRISI:2:106/r5] TABRISI, d. 548 · ابن دريد | reports | Ibn Durayd generalizes the root: anything that replaces another thing has superseded it.
[TABRISI:2:106/r6] TABRISI, d. 548 · علي بن عيسى | rejects | Alī ibn ʿĪsā defines abrogation as lifting an obligation and replacing it, but the author rejects this as too broad, giving inability to pray standing and a ruling against reason as counterexamples.
[TABRISI:2:106/r7] TABRISI, d. 548 · author | holds | Abrogation is a later legal proof that a ruling established by an earlier text will not apply in the future, though absent the later proof the earlier text would still govern.
[TABRISI:2:106/r8] TABRISI, d. 548 · أبو بكر | reports | The first kind of Qurʾanic abrogation lifts both a verse’s ruling and its recitation; Abū Bakr is reported to have cited a formerly recited warning about disowning fathers.

[IBNJAWZI-NUZHA:v1p154/r10] IBNJAWZI-NUZHA, d. 597 · بعض المفسرين | reports | Āya can mean a bounded unit of Qur’anic text called a verse. | mentions 13:1, 12:1, 16:101
[IBNJAWZI-NUZHA:v1p154/r2] IBNJAWZI-NUZHA, d. 597 · ابن قتيبة | reports | Ibn Qutayba defines an āya as a group of letters.

[IBNJAWZI-NUZHA:v1p154/r4] IBNJAWZI-NUZHA, d. 597 · الزجاج | reports | Al-Zajjāj gives the forms āya, āy, and āyāt.

[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v1p127#2/r2] IBNJAWZI-ZAD, d. 597 · الفراء | reports | One meaning of delaying a verse is to leave it unabrogated.

[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v1p127/r2] IBNJAWZI-ZAD, d. 597 · الزجاج | reports | Al-Zajjāj says naskh linguistically means abolishing one thing and installing another in its place, like the sun removing shade and taking its place.

[IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v1p127/r7] IBNJAWZI-ZAD, d. 597 · أبو علي | reports | Abū ʿAlī explains this reading as God finding the verse abrogated through His act of abrogating it.
