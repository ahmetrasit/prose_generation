# 26:77: 14 questions

## 26:77/q01 What does the opening fa connect to?  [فَإِنَّهُمْ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the fa states a conclusion, supplies an implied condition or conclusion, or introduces a direct predication.
- 26:77/q01/p1 It draws a conclusion from Abraham's question about their worship. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the question makes their worship appear astonishing, so the declaration follows as its conclusion. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 26:77/q01/p2 It is an expressive fa implying a condition: if they examine the idols, they will know they are his enemies. — Ibn ʿĀshūr finds this analysis the most apparent. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL+]
- 26:77/q01/p3 The clause explains the unstated conclusion that Abraham does not worship the idols, or that worshipping them is invalid. — Al-Ālūsī reports both formulations of the implied conclusion. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
- 26:77/q01/p4 The clause directly states the reality of the things his people had worshipped. — Al-Ālūsī reports this as an alternative connection. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]

## 26:77/q02 Who are 'they' in the declaration?  [فَإِنَّهُمْ · referent]
turns on: Whether the plural points to worshipped things, the people who worship them, both worship and idols, or Abraham's people and forebears.
- 26:77/q02/p1 The objects Abraham's people worshipped, especially their idols. — Ibn ʿĀshūr traces the pronoun to the objects of worship and parses 'your fathers' with the subject of 'you worshipped.' Al-Ālūsī says it returns to mā by its plural sense. Al-Ṭabrisī notes that this may include God among what some people worshipped. [34 notes: MUQATIL; NAHHAS(الفراء); THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء,الكلبي,مقاتل); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(قيل); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(قيل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(بعض الأجلة); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]
- 26:77/q02/p2 The idol worshippers themselves, or the worshippers supplied as an omitted noun. — Al-Rāzī says the pronoun can refer to worshippers despite the immediately prior objects of worship. Abū Ḥayyān reports an ellipsis; al-Samīn rejects the need for it because the idols can be called enemies as written. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); STUDYQURAN(R); SAMIN-DURR-]
- 26:77/q02/p3 Both idolatrous worship and its idols are Abraham's enemies. — Al-Ṭabrisī explicitly includes the act of worship with its objects. [1 notes: TABRISI]
- 26:77/q02/p4 His people and their ancestors are his enemies, except ancestors who worshipped God. — Al-Ḥasan identifies the people and forebears as the hostile party and restricts the exception to godly ancestors. [1 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(الحسن)]

## 26:77/q03 Why is a rational plural pronoun used for the worshipped things?  [فَإِنَّهُمْ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the pronoun reflects personification, agreement with the antecedent's meaning, or inclusion of God.
- 26:77/q03/p1 The idols are personified as rational because people worship or imagine them as aware beings. — Al-Ṭabrisī says describing the idols as enemies licenses the rational form; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ says being worshipped implies awareness and cites frequent Qur'anic usage. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the wording reflects worshippers' belief that they perceive. [4 notes: TABRISI(قيل); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 26:77/q03/p2 The plural follows the plural meaning of mā, its antecedent. — Al-Ālūsī gives grammatical agreement with meaning rather than the singular form. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 26:77/q03/p3 The rational form prevails because God was worshipped alongside the idols. — Al-Ṭabrisī reports this possibility in explaining the plural. [1 notes: TABRISI(قيل)]

## 26:77/q04 In what sense are the idols an enemy to Abraham?  [عَدُوٌّۭ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether enmity is future repudiation, Abraham's present opposition, a reversal, a simile for harm, Satan's agency, human opposition, or simply unfitness for worship.
- 26:77/q04/p1 They truly become enemies by disowning or reproaching their worshippers on Judgment Day. — Al-Farrāʾ, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Nasafī and Abū Ḥayyān invoke 19:82; al-Jishumī cites 21:98. Al-Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān report that God will bring idols to life for repudiation. Several state the conditional 'if Abraham worshipped them.' Al-Zamakhsharī adds that Satan who prompts their worship is humanity's greatest enemy. [14 notes: NAHHAS(الفراء); THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء); JISHUMI(قيل); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل)]
- 26:77/q04/p2 Abraham is their enemy: the wording reverses the relationship because opposition is reciprocal. — Ibn Qutaybah, al-Farrāʾ and others argue that whoever one opposes also opposes one; the author of al-Naẓm and al-Fārisī say lifeless idols cannot hate or will hostility. Al-Ṭabrisī adds that opposition to Abraham opposes God. Al-Wāḥidī questions a later attribution of this view to al-Farrāʾ. Al-Ālūsī calls the inversion untenable, and Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn say the direct wording works. [15 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; THALABI(الفراء); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن قتيبة); JISHUMI(الفراء); BAGHAWI-FULL(الفراء); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(الفراء); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة); QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); NASAFI-FULL(الفراء); BURHAN(الفارسي); BURSEVI(الفراء); WAHIDI-BASIT(صاحب النظم)+; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; SAMIN-DURR-; ALUSI-FULL-; WAHIDI-BASIT-]
- 26:77/q04/p3 The idols are called enemies figuratively because their worship harms and misleads people. — Al-Rāzī says idolaters glorify and hope in them as though alive, but they lead from happiness to misery. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Būrsawī say the harm exceeds that of a human enemy. Al-Ālūsī reports the devotees' love of idols as they love God and prefers the direct forceful simile to attributing Satan's enmity to idols. Ibn ʿĀshūr says idols cannot perceive, but Abraham hates and harms them as one does enemies; he compares the injunction to take Satan as an enemy at 35:6. [8 notes: RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(بعض الأجلة); IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN(al-Rāzī); ALUSI-FULL+]
- 26:77/q04/p4 Abraham disavows, opposes and refuses to worship the idols. — Muqātil and al-Kalbī gloss the declaration as freedom from the idols; al-Jishumī gives disavowal as the sense of enmity. Al-Baghawī says he neither befriends nor seeks gain from idols. Ibn Kathīr reads a challenge for them to harm him; al-Biqāʿī cites his condemnation and plan to destroy them at 21:54, 21:57 and 21:67. Ibn ʿĀshūr sees confidence that his safety will expose their impotence. Al-Nasafī says 'enemy' can mean adversary or one opposed. Ibn ʿĀshūr derives the term from aggression, harming or hatred; al-Naẓm's author also gives hatred, disavowal and refusal to agree, with a root sense of passing beyond. Al-Fārisī distinguishes willing reciprocal hostility from one-sided leaving behind; al-Burhān likewise distinguishes ʿādaytuhu from ʿadawtu. [15 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي,مقاتل); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(قيل); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(النسفي); IBNASHUR-FULL; BURHAN; STUDYQURAN; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 26:77/q04/p5 The real enemy is Satan, whose inducement to idol worship is figuratively attributed to idols. — Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Bayḍāwī call Satan humanity's greatest enemy; Abū Ḥayyān adds that he incites their worship. Al-Ālūsī reports the causal attribution but judges the direct simile clearer. Iṣlāḥī calls idols Satan's lures to bar the straight path. [5 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 26:77/q04/p6 Their worshippers are enemies, and hostility to the objects is hostility to their worshippers. — Al-Jishumī reports both a transfer from idols to worshippers and the thought that, if idols lived and invited worship, they would merit enmity. Iṣlāḥī says a prophet wishes people well, but their resistance and attempt to draw him into their ways make them his enemies; Abraham assigns enmity to them, not himself. [3 notes: JISHUMI(أبو علي,قيل); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 26:77/q04/p7 The phrase means the idols are unfit for worship. — Al-Jishumī reports this without requiring literal enmity. [1 notes: JISHUMI(قيل)]

## 26:77/q05 Why is 'enemy' singular with a plural subject?  [عَدُوٌّۭ · grammar]
turns on: Whether singularity comes from verbal-noun force, collective reference, distributive ellipsis, relational form, one shared quality, or the faʿūl pattern.
- 26:77/q05/p1 ʿAduww has the force of an invariant verbal noun, so one form describes one or many. — Al-Wāḥidī, al-Jishumī, al-Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn, al-Burhān, al-Itqān, al-Ālūsī and al-Kashshāf give this explanation; parallels include khaṣm, ʿadl, qabūl and walūʿ, with 18:50, 63:4, 4:92 and poetry cited. Al-Wāḥidī says an adjectival verbal noun may sometimes be inflected. Al-Farrāʾ permits ʿaduww or ʿaduwwa of a woman; ʿAlī ibn Sulaymān distinguishes the tāʾ form as 'hostile' and the form without tāʾ as relational. Al-Marzūqī's discussion of Jaʿfar ibn ʿUlba's poem expressly cites this verse for the generic singular: the poem laments enemies overwhelming kin and dependents. His contextual explanations cover talahhuf as grief at a near loss, Ahlafa as vocative or possessive and its lengthened lamenting alif, ahlabāt as help generalized from milking, walaya as saddlecloths alluding to women or helpless people, and an alternate mawālī for cousins whose hostility cuts deeper; al-mubāsil derives from basāla, bravery, and its singular description follows the form of al-ʿaduww rather than its collective sense; he adduces related elegy and proverb. Al-Sāmarrāʾī compares invariant ṭāghūt and khaṣm, derives ṭāghūt from ṭughyān on the faʿlūt pattern with sound substitution and parallels malakūt, jabarūt and rahabūt, and defines it broadly as a leader of misguidance or worshipped object, illustrated by a hadith and 24:31, 24:59. [29 notes: NAHHAS(الفراء,علي بن سليمان); HAMASA; WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURHAN; ITQAN; BURSEVI(الفراء); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL(الكشاف); SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 26:77/q05/p2 The singular word can have a collective sense and serve as the group's predicate. — Al-Farrāʾ says singular can represent a group, and al-Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Rāzī and others cite collective usage. Al-Zamakhsharī says enemy and friend can be singular or collective, citing poetry and 18:50; al-Rāzī cites another poem and compares 'we are the messenger' at 26:16. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the form can apply to either gender. [13 notes: NAHHAS(الفراء); THALABI; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 26:77/q05/p3 The singular distributes over the group: each idol is an enemy. — Al-Jishumī and Ibn al-Jawzī give the distributive sense; al-Ālūsī supplies an understood kull, 'each one.' [3 notes: JISHUMI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; ALUSI-FULL]
- 26:77/q05/p4 It is an invariant relational adjective meaning one possessing enmity. — ʿAlī ibn Sulaymān distinguishes the form without feminine tāʾ this way; al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī give the relational explanation, and al-Būrsawī reports it beside the verbal noun analysis. [4 notes: NAHHAS(علي بن سليمان); BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI(الفراء); ALUSI-FULL]
- 26:77/q05/p5 All the idols share a single quality of enmity. — Al-Ālūsī reports this account of their single predicate. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 26:77/q05/p6 The faʿūl form functions as an active participle and normally remains masculine singular. — Ibn ʿĀshūr derives ʿaduww from aggression and says this pattern is invariant here. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 26:77/q06 Why does Abraham say 'to me'?  [لِّىٓ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the first-person form primarily softens counsel to others or expresses Abraham's own challenge.
- 26:77/q06/p1 He presents his rejection as counsel applied first to himself, so his audience can accept it without direct accusation. — Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī and al-Būrsawī stress indirect persuasion. Al-Ālūsī reports al-Nasafī's self-address analogy and illustrates tact with anecdotes about al-Shāfiʿī and a man discussing a stone. Abū Ḥayyān calls al-Zamakhsharī's elaboration excessive. [8 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(النسفي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]
- 26:77/q06/p2 He declares his own opposition and fearlessness, challenging the idols to harm him. — Ibn Kathīr describes a challenge to the idols; Ibn ʿĀshūr says his indifference and safety demonstrate their inability to hurt him. Al-Biqāʿī links the posture to his intention to destroy idols. [3 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 26:77/q07 How is the yāʾ in 'lī illā' read?  [لِّىٓ إِلَّا · readings]
turns on: Whether the pronoun yāʾ takes fatḥa before illā.
- 26:77/q07/p1 The yāʾ is opened, producing liya as a separate pronoun syllable. — Ibn al-Jazarī attributes opening this yāʾ and the one in 'wa-ghfir li-abī innahu' to Abū ʿAmr and the two Medinan readers. Qirāʾāt-ER calls this a canonical reading. [2 notes: NASHR(أبو عمرو والمدنيان); QIRAAT-ER]
- 26:77/q07/p2 The yāʾ remains unvowelled in the long lī pronunciation. — Qirāʾāt-ER identifies it as the other canonical reading. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER]

## 26:77/q08 How does the exception 'except the Lord of the worlds' work?  [إِلَّا رَبَّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether God was among the objects worshipped, whether the exception instead supplies 'but,' an omitted worshipper, or a reordered 'besides,' and whether the wording asserts exclusive worship.
- 26:77/q08/p1 It is a disconnected exception: the idols are enemies, but the Lord is Abraham's ally, benefactor and object of worship. — The grammarians cited by al-Zajjāj and Ibn al-Jawzī take God to be outside the class of idols. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī and al-Nasafī gloss illā as 'but'; Ibn al-Jawzī gives other uses at 11:43 and 4:22. Al-Būrsawī says God is a friend in both lives and continually benefits Abraham; al-Biqāʿī ties this to God's rule of all worlds. Al-Wāḥidī replies to an objection that a prophet knows God is his ally. Ibn ʿĀshūr finds the disconnected reading historically likely if the Chaldeans did not acknowledge a creator; he cites 21:63 and 6:80 and describes a sun or Baal cult with creation assigned to time and procreation, citing 45:24. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn keep the exception attached in its apparent place after the enmity clause. [27 notes: ZAJJAJ(النحويون); NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق); THALABI(الفراء); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أكثر النحويين); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو إسحاق الزجاج); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الفراء والزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الفراء والزمخشري); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL+]
- 26:77/q08/p2 It is a connected exception from everything the people worshipped: Abraham rejects all of it except God. — Al-Zajjāj and Abū Isḥāq allow this because the people worshipped God alongside idols; Muqātil says they acknowledged Him as Creator, which counted as worship. Other notes allow God-worshipping forebears or occasional worship of God. Al-Ālūsī says naming idols rebuts them without asserting exclusive idol worship. Ibn ʿĀshūr offers recognition of a supreme Creator with idols as partners governing creation; the Study Quran compares the Quraysh. Al-Jishumī notes rabb is accusative by exception, as though Abraham said he worshipped the Lord. Ibn ʿĀshūr says either parsing links divine creation to exclusive devotion, comparing 6:79. [26 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(الكلبي); ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق); THALABI(الفراء); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,مقاتل); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن زيد); QURTUBI-FULL(أبو إسحاق الزجاج); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزجاج); SAMIN-DURR(الزجاج); ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج); IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 26:77/q08/p3 An omitted 'worshipper' makes the exception refer to people who worship the Lord. — Al-Ḥasan, al-Kalbī and al-Ḥusayn ibn al-Faḍl take the exception to mean God-worshipping people or ancestors. Al-Ḥusayn ibn al-Faḍl prefers it and treats it as an exception outside the first group; al-Qurṭubī explicitly supplies omitted ʿābid. [6 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(الحسن); WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); BAGHAWI-FULL(الحسين بن الفضل); QURTUBI-FULL(الكلبي); STUDYQURAN(Q); WAHIDI-BASIT(الحسين بن الفضل)+]
- 26:77/q08/p4 The exception is moved in sense to the prior 'what you worshipped'; illā means 'apart from' the Lord. — The author of al-Naẓm and al-Jurjānī propose fronting and delay; al-Qurṭubī illustrates illā as dūn or siwā with 44:56. Sibawayh's discussions offer comparative exception syntax: two exceptions cannot both be nominative appositions; mixed cases can be parsed; an advanced substitute can follow illā; ghayr may mean 'like,' an adjective, or an exception; repeated illā may emphasize an identical referent or correct a slip; Yūnus and al-Khalīl allow accusative continuation or nominative new clause. Al-Wāḥidī calls the rearrangement strained, Abū Ḥayyān unnecessary, and al-Ālūsī notes its weakness. [11 notes: SIBAWAYH(ابن أبي إسحاق,يونس والخليل); WAHIDI-BASIT(صاحب النظم); QURTUBI-FULL(الجرجاني); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجرجاني); SAMIN-DURR(الجرجاني); WAHIDI-BASIT-; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL(الجرجاني)-]
- 26:77/q08/p5 The exception means Abraham worships none but the Lord. — Al-Baghawī states the exclusivity directly, and al-Jishumī paraphrases the accusative rabb as 'I worship the Lord of the worlds.' [2 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL; JISHUMI]
- 26:77/q08/p6 It means the matter is with the Lord of the worlds. — Al-Ḥasan ibn al-Faḍl gives this brief paraphrase. [1 notes: THALABI(الحسن بن الفضل)]

## 26:77/q09 What does 'Lord of the worlds' designate?  [رَبَّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ · meaning]
- 26:77/q09/p1 God, the creator, ruler and benefactor of everything He created. — Al-Zajjāj equates the worlds with all created things, citing 6:164; Qatāda glosses the title by everything in His creation. Muqātil says the idolaters knew God as their Lord and Creator, and al-Biqāʿī says He governs the cosmos and benefits Abraham. Al-Zajjāj elsewhere praises the Qur'an's most eloquent nominative wording 'rabb al-ʿālamīn al-raḥmān al-raḥīm' over possible colloquial case alternatives. [8 notes: MUQATIL; ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 26:77/q10 How is the word 'worlds' formed and declined?  [ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ · grammar]
- 26:77/q10/p1 It is the plural of ʿālam, a collective of different kinds, with an opened plural nūn to distinguish the dual. — Al-Zajjāj says the singular form gathers one kind, while the plural gathers different kinds and has no singular of the same collective sense. He reports Sibawayh's explanation that the plural nūn is fatḥa and dual nūn kasra. Al-Zajjāj also explains fatḥa at a meeting of still letters: kasra after wāw or yāʾ is heavy, comparing sawfa and ayna Zayd. [3 notes: ZAJJAJ(سيبويه)]

## 26:77/q11 What spiritual reading is given to Abraham's enmity and exception?  [* · inward]
turns on: Whether the seeker is at the stage of detaching from creation or seeing God in creation after attachment has fallen away.
- 26:77/q11/p1 Sound love of God requires detachment from every created thing, treating the worlds as enemies while turning solely to Him. — Saḥnūn and Samnūn say love becomes sound by viewing all worlds as enemies. Al-Būrsawī reads this as Abraham's perfect love and says a traveler should look at other-than-God only contemplatively; his two-eye image contrasts attention to physical and spiritual realms, and his prayer asks for absorption in God. A Sufi master explains Abraham's sense of unique intimacy with God in his age as enmity with all creation. [9 notes: SULAMI(سحنون); BURSEVI(بعض الكبار,سمنون); STUDYQURAN(Su)]
- 26:77/q11/p2 After transcending attachment and seeing God in everything, former enemies become friends. — Al-Būrsawī says creation is an effect of divine theophany yet becomes an enemy when made an idol or attachment; crossing both physical and spiritual levels toward divine omnipotence dissolves otherness, and the world becomes a mirror of the Beloved's beauty. [3 notes: BURSEVI]

## 26:77/q12 What does Abraham's declaration teach about worship and preaching?  [* · interpretation]
- 26:77/q12/p1 False objects are to be rejected and God alone worshipped as the source of creation and blessings. — Al-Rāzī says Abraham's teaching rejects their gods, praises God, then prays, showing a preacher should teach religious practice. Iṣlāḥī argues only the giver of blessings deserves worship, since stone idols cannot hear, see, help or harm. Kuran Yolu lists God's creation, guidance, food, drink, healing, life, death and forgiveness against fabricated gods. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cautions that semantic distinctions between apparently synonymous Qur'anic words are sometimes hard to perceive, while Ibn al-Aʿrāb holds that such pairs always differ in nuance. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن الأعرابي)]

## 26:77/q13 How is this declaration linked to the wider Abraham story and sura?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the link foregrounds Abraham's parallel declarations or this sura's narrative sequence and transition.
- 26:77/q13/p1 It echoes Abraham's other repudiations and the model of prophets confronting idolatry. — Ibn Kathīr connects Noah's challenge at 10:71, Hūd's at 11:54–56, Abraham's argument at 6:81, and his example at 60:4 and 43:26–28. Iṣlāḥī links the declaration of acquittal to migration at 37:99 and 29:26. [2 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 26:77/q13/p2 The Abraham narrative continues the sura's repeated preaching pattern and fills the interval between Noah and Moses. — Iṣlāḥī says this account and the preceding anecdote share a preaching purpose and close with recurring verses; the preceding sūra leaves a gap after Noah, and Abraham is the prominent messenger between Noah and Moses. Al-Ṭībī says Abraham's speech gracefully moves from divine attributes to a request for judgment and company with the righteous. [3 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ITQAN(الطيبي)]

## 26:77/q14 What report echoes the exception for the Lord of the worlds?  [* · reports]
- 26:77/q14/p1 Ibn ʿAbbās reports that the Prophet left Mecca for Medina fearing only the Lord of the worlds. — Al-Nasāʾī records the report without a grading; its wording echoes the verse's exception. [1 notes: NASAI(ابن عباس)]
