# 7:57: 25 questions

## 7:57/q01 How are wind and its verse description read and understood?  [ٱلرِّيَٰحَ بُشْرًۢا · readings]
turns on: The readings vary between singular and plural wind and between bāʾ for glad tidings and nūn for spreading, revival, or fragrance; grammatical analyses also vary.
- 7:57/q01/p1 Plural winds with bāʾ describe bearers of good news of rain. — ʿĀṣim is repeatedly named; 30:46 is cited by al-Ṭabarī, al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, and others. The form is explained as plural bashīr or bashīra, a lightened plural, or a verbal noun; Ibn ʿAbbās says people rejoice at the winds. Al-Ṭabarī accepts its meaning but does not favor the reading. Some derive a plural of nashūr or nāshir, an active or passive form, or a verbal noun. Abū ʿUbayda describes dispersal; al-Farrāʾ and Imruʾ al-Qays support gentle fragrant wind; other poets support life and revival. ## 7:57/q02 What does God’s sending of the winds convey?  [يُرْسِلُ ٱلرِّيَٰحَ · interpretation]
turns on: Descriptions range from physical release and cloud transport to a deliberate divine sign and inward winds.
- 7:57/q02/p1 God releases and moves the winds to prepare rain and demonstrate His governance. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya glosses irsāl as setting in motion; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls dispatch a messenger metaphor within the air sphere. Al-Rāzī argues motion is not essential to air and must have a choosing agent; Elmalılı says regular heat and cold remain under God. Al-Suddī describes gathering clouds and opening heaven; Abū Bakr b. ʿAyyāsh names four winds. Winds also pollinate, heal, destroy, or cleanse air; rain is measured according to Abū Hurayra. [80 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(السدي); IBNABIHATIM(أبو هريرة,ابن كعب,السدي); THALABI(أبو بكر بن عياش); IBNATIYYA-FULL(السدي); BAGHAWI-FULL(أبو هريرة); RAZI-FULL(ابن عمر,السدي,الفلاسفة,المنجمون,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,كعب); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أهل الحق,ابن عمران,السدي); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(السدي); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عمر,النبي,غير واحد من العلماء,كعب الأحبار); ELMALILI(Ka‘b,Prophet,İbn Ömer); IBNASHUR-FULL; QIRAAT-ER; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 7:57/q02/p2 Some philosophers and astrologers attribute wind motion to natural particles or stars. — Al-Rāzī reports philosophers' heated particles and astrologers' stellar forces, then objects that particles cool, descend vertically, cannot explain violent or clear sea winds; constant stellar nature would yield constant worldwide winds. Abū Ḥayyān reports this refutation. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL(الفلاسفة,المنجمون); RAZI-FULL-; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; BIQAI-FULL-]

## 7:57/q03 What is the mercy before which the winds arrive?  [بَيْنَ يَدَىْ رَحْمَتِهِۦ · meaning]
turns on: Nearly all identify rain; the dispute concerns whether calling rain mercy is lexical, metonymic, or a literal instance of mercy.
- 7:57/q03/p1 The mercy is rain, and the winds precede and signal it. — Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn ʿAṭiyya explain 'between the hands' as the Arabic idiom 'before', literal with a person and figurative here. Ibn ʿĀshūr says it implies near succession; al-Rāzī notes the verse makes no universal weather claim. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls rain the product of mercy rather than an established lexical sense; al-Shihāb and al-Ālūsī defend lexical attestation, while al-Ālūsī also permits rain as an instance of mercy. [52 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; TAB-FULL(السدي); ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(السدي); NAHHAS; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,أبو بكر,ابن عباس,الكلبي); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أهل التفسير); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(السدي); ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(أبو بكر الأنباري,ابن هشام,الشهاب,غالب المفسرين); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN(al-Zamakhsharī); ALUSI-FULL+]

## 7:57/q04 How does the clause beginning 'until, when' relate to sending the winds?  [حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَآ أَقَلَّتْ · grammar]
- 7:57/q04/p1 It marks the endpoint of the winds' advance, developed through carrying, driving, and rainfall. — Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn, Bursevī and Ibn ʿĀshūr say the stages are cumulative; the shift to God's plural pronoun highlights His favor and command. [7 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 7:57/q05 What does 'aqallat' mean and what do the winds lift?  [أَقَلَّتْ سَحَابًۭا ثِقَالًۭا · meaning]
- 7:57/q05/p1 It means the winds lifted and carried clouds laden with water. — Al-Zajjāj gives wind as the implicit subject. Al-Zamakhsharī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Rāzī and others derive carrying from deeming a load small or light; al-Samīn mentions load jars. Ibn ʿĀshūr describes vapor rising and condensing; Elmalılı sees the suspension of heavy droplets by moving air as a scientific sign, rejecting hollow-bubble theory. ## 7:57/q06 How does the collective word 'cloud' govern agreement and the pronoun in 'We drove it'?  [سَحَابًۭا سُقْنَٰهُ · grammar]
- 7:57/q06/p1 Saḥāb can denote one or many clouds; the singular masculine pronoun follows its word form, while the plural adjective follows its sense. — Al-Wāḥidī compares tamr/tamra; al-Zamakhsharī notes feminine agreement would follow the collective sense. Abū Ḥayyān cites other Qurʾanic agreements and Ibn ʿĀshūr cites a Prophetic expression. Ibn Fāris derives the name from clouds' motion; al-Biqāʿī sees one cohesive mass. ## 7:57/q07 What does the lām in 'for a dead land' mean?  [لِبَلَدٍۢ مَّيِّتٍۢ · grammar]
turns on: It may mark destination or delivery, or purpose and benefit; Abū Ḥayyān distinguishes assured arrival from acting for someone's sake.
- 7:57/q07/p1 The lām marks destination or conveyance to the land. — Al-Naḥḥās equates it with ilā; al-Jishumī cites 99:5 and 7:43. Abū Ḥayyān prefers delivery, noting its entailment of reaching the recipient. [11 notes: NAHHAS; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو بكر); JISHUMI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ); ALUSI-FULL(البحر); BIQAI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+]
- 7:57/q07/p2 The lām marks purpose: the cloud is driven for the land's revival or watering. — Al-Zamakhsharī is reported for this reading; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the wording points to divine care, while Abū Ḥayyān observes purpose alone does not guarantee delivery. [13 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو بكر); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]

## 7:57/q08 What is a 'dead land' and what does balad denote?  [بَلَدٍۢ مَّيِّتٍۢ · meaning]
- 7:57/q08/p1 It is barren land with no growth, needing rain; balad can mean any tract or a broad expanse. — Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Kalbī gloss absence of vegetation. Al-Wāḥidī and al-Rāzī include inhabited and uninhabited places, citing al-Aʿshā's desert verse; al-Ṭabrisī narrows it to populated territory, unlike steppe. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls death figurative for lost plants. Related lexical notes give nakd as scant and harsh and list other balda homonyms. - 7:57/q09/p1 Readers use a full geminated mayyit or a shortened mayt; the contracted form can denote an already dead state. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya names Abū ʿAmr, ʿĀṣim and al-Aʿmash for the shortened form; NASHR names Abū Jaʿfar for doubling. ## 7:57/q10 What does the first 'by it' refer to when God sends down water?  [فَأَنزَلْنَا بِهِ ٱلْمَآءَ · referent]
turns on: The closest land supports a locative bāʾ; cloud supports instrumental or causal bāʾ; driving, wind, and a 'from' construction are also proposed.
- 7:57/q10/p1 It refers to the dead land: God sends rain in or upon it. — Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn prefer the nearest expressed antecedent and locative bāʾ. Bursevī takes bāʾ as attachment. Al-Zajjāj and Ibn al-Anbārī permit it. [17 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج وابن الأنباري); THALABI(قيل); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن الأنباري); RAZI-FULL(الزجاج وابن الأنباري); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج وابن الأنباري); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 7:57/q10/p2 It refers to the cloud, which is the instrument or cause of rainfall. — Al-Zajjāj and Ibn al-Anbārī allow instrumental bāʾ; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn report causal bāʾ. Al-Qurṭubī also reports bāʾ for 'from', citing 76:6; Abū Ḥayyān rejects forcing the preposition to mean min. [16 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج وابن الأنباري); THALABI(قيل); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); RAZI-FULL(الزجاج وابن الأنباري); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج وابن الأنباري); ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]
- 7:57/q10/p3 It refers to the act of driving the cloud, or to the wind. — Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Bayḍāwī and others allow the implicit dispatch or driving as cause; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn reject an unexpressed verbal noun when a stated antecedent is available. [6 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج وابن الأنباري); ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; SAMIN-DURR-]

## 7:57/q11 What does the second 'by it' refer to, and how are fruits brought out?  [فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِۦ مِن كُلِّ ٱلثَّمَرَٰتِ · referent]
turns on: Water is the nearest and most natural means for many; land and cloud remain grammatically possible, and sources differ over whether water has natural causal power.
- 7:57/q11/p1 It refers most naturally to the water by which God brings forth fruits. — Al-Wāḥidī, al-Rāzī, al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī prefer water; fruit emergence follows rainfall. Al-Biqāʿī includes crops figuratively. Al-Jurjānī says assigning fruit production to a tree or earth is a causal metaphor for God's act. [20 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JURJANI-ASRAR; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن الأنباري); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ALUSI-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR+; ZAJJAJ+]
- 7:57/q11/p2 It may refer to the land, or less directly to cloud, wind, or driving. — Al-Zajjāj permits land because no particular land is specified. Al-Zamakhsharī extends the first pronoun's alternatives to the second; Abū Ḥayyān notes an awkward proposal shifting antecedents without a separator. [13 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج); KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن الأنباري); RAZI-FULL(الزجاج); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 7:57/q11/p3 God creates plants by customary conjunction with water and could create them without it. — Most theologians of justice and other theologians reject natural production: uniform water and earth cannot explain the different parts of one grape, so a free agent creates them. Al-Jurjānī similarly names God the true agent. [5 notes: TABRISI(أكثر أهل العدل); RAZI-FULL(أكثر المتكلمين,المتكلمون); JURJANI-ASRAR]
- 7:57/q11/p4 Natural powers placed in water produce effects when it mixes with earth, while God remains their ultimate agent. — Abū al-Qāsim al-Balkhī accepts direct and mediated divine action; philosophers allow specific natural powers. The theologians of justice object to making water a producer by nature. [3 notes: TABRISI(أبو القاسم البلخي); RAZI-FULL(جمهور الحكماء); TABRISI(أكثر أهل العدل)-; RAZI-FULL(أكثر المتكلمين,المتكلمون)-]

## 7:57/q12 What is meant by 'of every fruit'?  [مِن كُلِّ ٱلثَّمَرَٰتِ · grammar]
turns on: Some understand every kind within ordinary or local scope; Ibn ʿĀshūr argues exhaustive scope across all dead lands. Min is variously partitive, generic, or source marking.
- 7:57/q12/p1 It means every kind, understood with ordinary contextual limits rather than every individual fruit. — Al-Bayḍāwī, Bursevī and al-Ālūsī distinguish kinds from individuals. Al-Ālūsī prefers abundance; al-Suhaylī and al-Burhān see min as generic and fronted kull as avoiding an adverbial misreading. [11 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; BURHAN(السهيلي); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 7:57/q12/p2 Across dead lands it is truly exhaustive; for one land it means all its familiar fruits. — Ibn ʿĀshūr treats min as partitive. Al-Ṭabrisī and al-Samīn also allow partitive; they offer generic or source alternatives. [3 notes: TABRISI; SAMIN-DURR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 7:57/q13 What does 'likewise We bring out the dead' assert, and how is resurrection related to rain?  [كَذَٰلِكَ نُخْرِجُ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ · interpretation]
turns on: Some take the scene as an analogy proving God's power; others also take it as a literal rain-assisted process between trumpet blasts. Accounts differ over duration and mode.
- 7:57/q13/p1 The revival of barren earth and fruit is an analogy proving God can raise the dead. — Al-Ṭabarī addresses idolaters; al-Rāzī reasons that one who creates moisture and taste can create life in a body. Al-Zamakhsharī says both are renewed after prior creation; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the manner of resurrection unknown. Al-Ālūsī prefers creation and enlivening after nonexistence to importing reassembly into the comparison; another account reassembles parts. A dry valley turning green is the Prophet's reported sign to Abū Razīn. - 7:57/q13/p2 Rain will literally fall on the dead, split the earth, grow bodies, and precede restored souls. — Mujāhid describes earth splitting and spirits returning. Abū Hurayra's fuller account has water of life from beneath the Throne for forty years after the first trumpet, growth like crops, sleep, second trumpet, and 36:52; Ibn ʿAbbās compares it with semen or gestation. Another report gives forty days; Muslim's report likens rain to dew. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says al-Suddī's weather sequence is not Prophetic; al-Rāzī denies that rain is necessary yet permits God to choose it by custom. ## 7:57/q14 What should the audience remember from this sign?  [لَعَلَّكُمْ تَذَكَّرُونَ · interpretation]
- 7:57/q14/p1 They should infer God's oneness, power, mercy and resurrection, and take admonition. — Al-Zajjāj says laʿalla addresses humans by their hoped response without limiting God's knowledge. Ibn ʿĀshūr says believers deepen faith while polytheists abandon denial; other writers connect hope and fear, invocation of God alone, and moral awakening. Al-Ṭabarī specifically addresses idolaters. [25 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس,الزجاج); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]

## 7:57/q15 How is 'tadhakkarūn' formed and read?  [تَذَكَّرُونَ · readings]
- 7:57/q15/p1 It derives from tatadhakkarūn: one tāʾ is omitted, or the second assimilates to dhāl. — Ibn ʿĀshūr reports the majority's doubled dhāl and Ḥafṣ's light dhāl; QIRĀʾĀT-ER says full assimilation emphasizes effort. [5 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL(الجمهور,عاصم في رواية حفص); QIRAAT-ER]

## 7:57/q16 How does this verse connect with its surrounding discourse and other verses?  [* · links]
- 7:57/q16/p1 It extends the prior signs of creation and mercy into a sign of resurrection and divine lordship. — Al-Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān move from upper-world signs to wind, rain and plants; Ibn ʿĀshūr links 7:54 and 7:56, and sees a rebuke of polytheism. Ibn Kathīr sees provision and raising the dead. Islahi connects the lessons to earlier sūrah themes. The wind image recurs in 25:48, 27:63 and 30:46; rain-revival analogies recur in 35:9, 36:33 and 43:11. Some adjacent rhetorical notes illustrate verse order and presentation. [49 notes: AKHFASH(الشاعر,بعضهم); JURJANI-ASRAR; JURJANI-DALAIL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 7:57/q17 Why is the wind sent in the present tense here?  [يُرْسِلُ · rhetoric]
- 7:57/q17/p1 It fits the surrounding present or future oriented discourse and presents the act as ongoing or vividly present. — Al-Kirmānī contrasts past forms in al-Furqān and Fāṭir with this context and al-Rūm. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says present verbs stage a scene vividly. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الكرماني); SAMIN-DURR(الكرماني); QIRAAT-ER; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 7:57/q18 What inward sense is drawn from the winds, rain, and revived earth?  [* · inward]
- 7:57/q18/p1 The sequence depicts divine care reviving a withered heart through guidance, love, nearness, and spiritual fruit. — Al-Sulamī relates winds to repentance, fear, hope, nearness and longing. Al-Qushayrī compares early signs of union to a breeze and a withered seeker to renewed land; Bursevī assigns winds to care, cloud to guidance, water to love and fruit to unveilings. Qushayrī also says purity of root yields good deeds, while deprivation leaves some unmoved. [11 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم); QUSHAYRI(بعضهم,قائلهم); BURSEVI]

## 7:57/q19 What additional reports accompany the rain and wind imagery?  [* · reports]
- 7:57/q19/p1 Reports describe measured rain, water sources, wind categories and the Prophet's response to wind. — Ibn Abī Ḥātim reports rain by measure, water from the seventh heaven, and some sea-fed clouds whose water requires thunder and lightning to grow crops. Ibn ʿUmar's eight winds split between mercy and punishment; Kaʿb says three windless days would make earth foul. Prophetic reports counsel asking for good and refuge from evil. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites the Prophet's three-soil parable of receptive, retaining, and barren ground. [22 notes: IBNABIHATIM(أبو هريرة,خالد بن يزيد,عكرمة); BAGHAWI-FULL(أبو هريرة); RAZI-FULL(ابن عمر,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,كعب); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو عبيد,ابن عمران); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عمر,النبي,غير واحد من العلماء,كعب الأحبار); ELMALILI(Ka‘b,Prophet,İbn Ömer); TABATABAI(محمد)]

## 7:57/q20 What ancillary grammatical and narrative comparisons are recorded beside this verse?  [* · reports]
- 7:57/q20/p1 The notes preserve collateral examples of plural formation, human artifacts, and other narratives. — Sībawayh's examples analyze nisba to collectives and broken plurals. Al-Azraqī records Qullays architecture, Black Stone traditions, and mosque expansion. These passages are ancillary to the verse notes and do not resolve a reading of 7:57. ## 7:57/q21 What additional lexical forms are cited for the winds?  [ٱلرِّيَٰحَ بُشْرًۢا · meaning]
- 7:57/q21/p1 Riyāḥ is the plural of rīḥ; nushuran may denote scattered affairs, and rīda or raydāna also denotes wind. — Ibn ʿĀshūr points to the prior lexical discussion in al-Baqara. Ibn Qutayba invokes the idiom for gathering scattered affairs. Al-Ṭabrisī cites poetry for the rare wind names. Al-Burhān notes that a small-count plural can denote abundance. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TABRISI; BURHAN]

## 7:57/q22 What additional syntax is noted in the wind and revival clauses?  [بُشْرًۢا فَأَنزَلْنَا بِهِ ٱلْمَآءَ كَذَٰلِكَ · grammar]
- 7:57/q22/p1 The wind description can modify sender or wind, the rain pronoun can refer to water, and likewise is an accusative comparison. — Al-Samīn allows the circumstantial phrase to describe the subject or object, governed by yursilu or bushran. Ibn ʿAṭiyya finds water the most apparent antecedent among cloud, land, and water. Al-Naḥḥās puts the kāf of kadhālika in the accusative. [3 notes: SAMIN-DURR; IBNATIYYA-FULL; NAHHAS]

## 7:57/q23 What other wording distinguishes this verse from its parallels?  [* · links]
- 7:57/q23/p1 Al-Aʿrāf uses a lām before the land, whereas Fāṭir uses ilā. — Al-Burhān records the prepositional contrast without giving an additional explanation. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 7:57/q24 What collateral grammar is cited for mercy in the nearby context?  [بَيْنَ يَدَىْ رَحْمَتِهِۦ · grammar]
- 7:57/q24/p1 The preceding feminine mercy with masculine qarīb receives several grammatical explanations. — Al-Naḥḥās reports Abū ʿUbayda's masculine 'place' referent, ʿAlī b. Sulaymān's case objection, Sībawayh's rare permission with poetry, al-Akhfash's masculine treatment of feminine nouns, and adjectival relation like ṭāliq; Abū Isḥāq rejects mismatch. [6 notes: NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق,أبو عبيدة,الأخفش,سيبويه,علي بن سليمان)]

## 7:57/q25 What further explanation is given for God's driving of clouds and the Prophet's wind prayer?  [سُقْنَٰهُ ٱلرِّيَٰحَ · interpretation]
- 7:57/q25/p1 Driving pictures urging a cloud onward; a Prophetic prayer asks for beneficial plural winds. — Ibn ʿĀshūr calls saq a metaphor for causes moving a cloud like a handler urges a mount. Ibn Kathīr says winds spread before rain clouds. Al-Ṭabrisī transmits the Prophet's supplication for winds of mercy rather than destructive wind.
