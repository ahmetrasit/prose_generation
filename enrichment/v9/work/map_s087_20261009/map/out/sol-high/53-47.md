# 53:47: 18 questions

## 53:47/q01 How does wa anna connect this clause to its context?  [وَأَنَّ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the opening is read wa anna as a continued clause or wa inna as a fresh assertion.
- 53:47/q01/p1 It continues the series of truths conveyed in the scriptures of Moses and Abraham. — Study Quran says all verses 42–50 except 46 begin wa anna and continue the account of those scriptures in 36–41. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]
- 53:47/q01/p2 Read as wa inna, it opens an independent emphatic statement: truly or surely. — Study Quran reports this alternative vocalization for the repeated clauses. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 53:47/q02 What does 'upon Him' signify here?  [عَلَيْهِ · theology]
turns on: Whether ʿalā denotes rational obligation, God's self-binding promise, assignment to His power, or emphasis directed at deniers.
- 53:47/q02/p1 Resurrection is assured by God's own promise and wisdom, without an obligation imposed on Him. — Al-Tabrisi says promised reward and compensation for earthly suffering require a further life of judgment and justice. Al-Razi cites God's promise in 36:12. Al-Baydawi, al-Biqai, Bursawi, al-Alusi, Ibn Ashur, and Tabatabai stress fulfillment of a promise or irrevocable decree; al-Biqai says God does not change His word, Ibn Ashur likens the wording to an assured right and cites 6:12, and Tabatabai says He has said He will not break His promise. Bursawi also says wisdom calls for bodily recompense and believers' gradual perfection: this world cannot contain one person's full reward, demanding immediate results is discourteous, and a purified divine gift should be accepted with trust in God's choice. [9 notes: TABRISI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; RAZI-FULL+]
- 53:47/q02/p2 Reason or divine wisdom makes resurrection incumbent on God so that good and evil receive their due. — Al-Jishumi invokes the force of ʿalā and says resurrection is owed to those due reward or compensation, against the claim that nothing is incumbent on God. Al-Kashshaf and al-Zamakhshari as reported by al-Razi and al-Alusi ground obligation in wise requital; al-Alusi questions that Muʿtazilite approach. [3 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL(الزمخشري); JISHUMI+; RAZI-FULL-; BIQAI-FULL-; BURSEVI-; ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري)-; IBNASHUR-FULL-]
- 53:47/q02/p3 Resurrection rests with God as the one able and assigned to bring it about. — Al-Razi offers this as an alternative to rational obligation; Asad renders the phrase literally as the second coming to life resting upon God. [2 notes: ASAD-NOTES; RAZI-FULL+]
- 53:47/q02/p4 The emphatic 'upon Him' addresses deniers, making resurrection sound as if God has imposed it upon Himself. — Al-Alusi reports this explanation from Abu Hayyan's al-Bahr. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان)]

## 53:47/q03 What does al-nashʾa mean?  [ٱلنَّشْأَةَ · meaning]
- 53:47/q03/p1 A newly brought into being, fashioned creation. — Al-Jishumi calls it newly made creation and distinguishes initial life from resurrection. Al-Tabrisi says it is fashioned origination, as distinct from merely willing something. Al-Razi derives both noun forms from intransitive nashaʾa. [3 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL]

## 53:47/q04 How is al-nashʾa read and formed?  [ٱلنَّشْأَةَ · readings]
turns on: The presence or absence of an alif after shīn, with corresponding verbal-noun patterns.
- 53:47/q04/p1 The short nashʾa, without an alif after shīn, is read. — Ibn Atiyya calls this the common reading, with still shīn and hamza. Ibn al-Jazari says the remaining readers omit the alif here and in al-ʿAnkabut and al-Waqiʿa; al-Biqaʿi also contrasts them with the lengtheners. Al-Razi analyzes it as a faʿla verbal noun for one occurrence. Qiraat-ER calls both forms canonical. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الناس); RAZI-FULL; NASHR(الباقون); BIQAI-FULL; QIRAAT-ER(Lvw)]
- 53:47/q04/p2 The long nashāʾa, with an alif after shīn, is read. — Al-Kashshaf records the form; Ibn Atiyya attributes it to Abu ʿAmr and al-Aʿraj, while al-Qurtubi, al-Baydawi, Ibn al-Jazari, al-Biqaʿi and al-Alusi attribute it to Ibn Kathir and Abu ʿAmr. Ibn al-Jazari gives the same distribution in al-ʿAnkabut and al-Waqiʿa. Al-Razi calls it a faʿāla verbal noun; al-Baydawi and al-Alusi also say it is a Form I nashaʾa verbal noun. Al-Qurtubi associates the reading with promised resurrection; Qiraat-ER calls both forms canonical and suggests the long form may stress an extended rather than punctual process. [9 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبو عمرو والأعرج); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن كثير وأبو عمرو); BAYDAWI-FULL(ابن كثير); NASHR(ابن كثير، وأبو عمرو); BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(ابن كثير وأبو عمرو); QIRAAT-ER(Lvw)]

## 53:47/q05 Why say al-nashʾa rather than al-inshāʾ?  [ٱلنَّشْأَةَ · rhetoric]
- 53:47/q05/p1 It asserts that the new creation actually comes to exist. — Al-Razi says al-inshāʾ would be the expected noun for causing to arise, but it could denote an attempt with no result, whereas al-nashʾa confirms the result. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 53:47/q06 What does calling it 'the other creation' add?  [ٱلنَّشْأَةَ ٱلْأُخْرَىٰ · rhetoric]
- 53:47/q06/p1 It identifies a second creation already known and due from God, rather than merely saying creation happens once more. — Al-Razi says 'once more' need not presuppose a known first creation, whereas 'the other creation' points to the known second one. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 53:47/q07 What is the other creation?  [ٱلنَّشْأَةَ ٱلْأُخْرَىٰ · referent]
turns on: Whether the nearby creation and provision sequence indicates fetal ensoulment or the destination and recompense context indicates resurrection.
- 53:47/q07/p1 The resurrection or recreation of people after death for the afterlife. — Muqatil, Abu Ubayda, Ibn Qutayba, al-Thaʿlabi, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Nasafi and others identify raising after death. Al-Nahhas speaks of recreating the pair; Ibn Atiyya specifies restoration of bodies after decay in soil; al-Qurtubi specifies spirits returned to bodies. Al-Jishumi contrasts initial life and resurrection. Al-Tabrisi, al-Kashshaf, al-Baydawi, Tabatabai and Elmalili connect it with reward, punishment or compensation. Al-Razi reports this as most exegetes' view and notes contextual support from the final destination and full recompense (53:42, 53:41) and the use of the phrase in 29:20, while answering that this passage has just described creation from a drop. Ibn Kathir and Islahi argue that the first creation establishes God's ability to recreate; al-Biqaʿi says the first proves the second and God's promise ensures it. Islahi adds that justice and mercy fully appear in resurrection; Bursevi adds that wisdom gives believers their fitting perfection. Qurtubi's reading note calls it promised resurrection; Asad calls it the second coming to life, and al-Alusi and Kuranyolu give the usual afterlife explanation. [29 notes: MUQATIL; MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; NAHHAS; THALABI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(أكثر المفسرين); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن كثير وأبو عمرو); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; RAZI-FULL-]
- 53:47/q07/p2 The ensoulment of the embryo, joining a noble human spirit to the body. — Al-Razi proposes this as an honor giving humans angel-like perception and cites 23:14: after sperm develops into bones, ensoulment makes humans distinct from animals and is called another creation. He argues that this passage has just recalled creation from a drop, and its sequence then moves from male and female to the human spirit and to provision in 53:48: mother's milk and father's support in childhood, earning in adulthood. Kuranyolu reports his suggestion and its preceding creation context. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî); RAZI-FULL+]

## 53:47/q08 Must creatures perish before the second creation?  [ٱلنَّشْأَةَ ٱلْأُخْرَىٰ · theology]
turns on: Whether a second creation requires the first substances to cease existing.
- 53:47/q08/p1 Yes; the second creation entails prior annihilation and restoration. — Al-Jishumi reasons that what persists can cease only through an opposite or something functioning like one, so God annihilates then restores creation. [1 notes: JISHUMI]
- 53:47/q08/p2 No; substances do not perish once they have come into existence. — Al-Jishumi reports this as the view of some. [1 notes: JISHUMI(منهم)]

## 53:47/q09 How do substances come to cease?  [ٱلنَّشْأَةَ ٱلْأُخْرَىٰ · theology]
turns on: Whether cessation is direct annihilation, loss of renewed persistence, or cessation of a sustaining state.
- 53:47/q09/p1 God directly annihilates them. — Al-Jishumi reports Abu al-Husayn al-Khayyat's account. [1 notes: JISHUMI(أبو الحسين الخياط)]
- 53:47/q09/p2 God stops creating persistence in them. — Al-Jishumi reports Abu al-Qasim's account. [1 notes: JISHUMI(أبو القاسم)]
- 53:47/q09/p3 God creates imperishable states in them, and they cease when those states cease. — Al-Jishumi reports this as another position. [1 notes: JISHUMI(منهم)]

## 53:47/q10 How are perished substances restored?  [ٱلنَّشْأَةَ ٱلْأُخْرَىٰ · theology]
turns on: Whether restoration needs a distinct causal meaning beyond God's creating the substance again.
- 53:47/q10/p1 God creates them again without a separate cause called restoration. — Al-Jishumi holds this and objects that positing an additional restoring meaning produces an infinite regress of meanings. [1 notes: JISHUMI+]
- 53:47/q10/p2 An original existent comes to be by an agent, while a restored existent returns through a distinct cause called restoration. — Al-Jishumi reports this view from some and argues that it entails an infinite regress of meanings. [1 notes: JISHUMI(بعضهم); JISHUMI-]

## 53:47/q11 What inward resurrection parallels bodily resurrection?  [ٱلنَّشْأَةَ ٱلْأُخْرَىٰ · inward]
- 53:47/q11/p1 Spiritual resurrection is a second birth into enduring divine attributes and heavenly realities after spiritual annihilation. — Bursawi compares bodily resurrection after full bodily annihilation and readiness for spirit with shedding human traits and readiness for divine effusion. Both births require a sound constitution: a malformed embryo miscarries and a malformed womb cannot conceive. He cites Jesus saying no one enters heaven's kingdom without being born twice, explaining the second birth as access to heavenly lights and secrets through prophets and saints. [2 notes: BURSEVI]

## 53:47/q12 Why is there no emphatic huwa in this clause?  [* · rhetoric]
- 53:47/q12/p1 No rival could plausibly claim responsibility for resurrection, so the separator pronoun is unnecessary. — Abu Hayyan extends this to creating the pair and destroying ʿAd. Al-Suhayli, as reported in al-Itqan, says huwa appears where a quality was claimed for someone besides God, such as making people laugh and weep (53:43), and is absent where no rival claim existed (including 53:45, 53:47, 53:50). [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ITQAN(السهيلي)]

## 53:47/q13 How does this clause follow the creation account and lead to what follows?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the surrounding human creation and provision sequence points forward to resurrection or continues with ensoulment.
- 53:47/q13/p1 Human reproduction prepares an argument for resurrection, which the preceding account of life's beginning makes timely. — Ibn Ashur says reproduction, though known to the audience, independently proves resurrection, as in 21:104; that independent force explains the conjunction before this clause. Islahi says creation from a drop shows God can recreate, and Elmalili says paired creation in this world points to an afterlife. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ELMALILI]
- 53:47/q13/p2 The sequence proceeds from sexual creation to ensoulment and then to worldly provision in the next verse. — Al-Razi describes care through mother's milk and father's provision in childhood and earning in adulthood, matching 53:48. [2 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî); RAZI-FULL+]

## 53:47/q14 Which earlier scriptural words does this resurrection claim recall?  [* · links]
- 53:47/q14/p1 It echoes Abraham's declaration that God will bring him back after death in 26:81. — Islahi says the divine acts in this section echo Abraham's declaration of acquittal against his people in 26:78–82, with slight variations; the resurrection claim corresponds to 26:81. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 53:47/q15 How does this verse fit the sūra's appeal to Moses' and Abraham's scriptures?  [* · links]
- 53:47/q15/p1 The section moves from reflection on human creation to enduring truths in those scriptures, with this clause in the continuing series. — Study Quran calls this the third section, says it turns to perennial truths attested in Moses' and Abraham's scriptures, and links the wa anna clauses of 42–50 to the scriptural discussion in 36–41. [3 notes: STUDYQURAN]
- 53:47/q15/p2 Ancestry from Abraham and Moses does not entitle people to Paradise; their teaching and sacrifices are the point of the appeal. — Islahi condemns reliance on descent from those prophets and redirects attention to their teaching and sacrifices. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 53:47/q16 What does this assertion teach about God's power and governance?  [* · interpretation]
- 53:47/q16/p1 The creator of the first life has power to recreate people after death. — Ibn Kathir argues from initial creation to return on Judgment Day; al-Biqaʿi says the first creation proves the second, and Islahi makes the same argument from each person's creation from a drop while adding that resurrection permits divine justice and mercy to appear fully. [3 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 53:47/q16/p2 All events of life, death and the universe depend on God's will and power. — Kuranyolu reads verses 43–49 as examples of this comprehensive dependence. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 53:47/q17 What ruling about an unconscious pilgrim is noted with this verse?  [* · law]
- 53:47/q17/p1 Companions may perform the rites when customary conduct supplies the pilgrim's consent. — Al-Jishumi treats customary consent as though the pilgrim had spoken. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 53:47/q18 What example illustrates that word order can add meaning?  [* · rhetoric]
- 53:47/q18/p1 Different arrangements of the same comparison can make its likeness more emphatic. — Al-Jurjani says two Zayd-and-lion similes share the basic comparison, but kaʾanna Zaydan al-asad makes Zayd's bravery virtually indistinguishable from a lion's through its word order and combination. [1 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL]
