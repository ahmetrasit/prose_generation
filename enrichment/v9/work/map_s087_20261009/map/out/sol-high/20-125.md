# 20:125: 14 questions

## 20:125/q01 How does 'he said' connect to the preceding account?  [قَالَ · grammar]
- 20:125/q01/p1 It begins a fresh statement in the dialogue, explaining what the preceding account prompts one to ask. — Bursevi calls it an explanatory resumption; al-Alusi specifies an implied preceding question; Ibn Ashur says God's ensuing reply is separated because the dialogue continues. [3 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 20:125/q02 Why does the speaker address God as 'Lord'?  [رَبِّ · rhetoric]
- 20:125/q02/p1 He appeals to God's lordship and former blessings to seek mercy. — Al-Biqai says a benefactor tends to maintain a blessing while reform remains possible; Bursevi identifies Rabb as the address to the Lord. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI]

## 20:125/q03 What is the speaker asking with 'why'?  [لِمَ · interpretation]
- 20:125/q03/p1 He asks which sin earned this punishment, believing himself guiltless or not knowing the cause. — Al-Tabari says the question does not challenge God's sovereignty: the speaker seeks to learn his deserved penalty. Al-Qurtubi says he thinks himself guiltless. Al-Alusi allows either ignorance of the cause or an assumption of innocence. [3 notes: TAB-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 20:125/q04 How is the first-person yāʾ of 'You gathered me' read?  [حَشَرْتَنِىٓ · readings]
turns on: The readers differ over opening or quiescing the first-person suffix before the following hamza.
- 20:125/q04/p1 The suffix yāʾ is opened with fatḥa before the following hamza. — Ibn Mujahid assigns this to Nafi and Ibn Kathir through their rules for the listed first-person yāʾs. Al-Samin names Nafi and Ibn Kathir; al-Nashr adds Abu Jafar and separately calls the two Medinese and Ibn Kathir readers of the open yāʾs. QIRAAT-ER transcribes ḥashartaniya and says sense stays the same while rhythm changes. [6 notes: IBNMUJAHID(ابن كثير,نافع); SAMIN-DURR(نافع وابن كثير); NASHR(ابن كثير ونافع وأبو جعفر,المدنيان وابن كثير); QIRAAT-ER(Y-iḍ)]
- 20:125/q04/p2 The suffix yāʾ is quiescent, with sukūn. — Ibn Mujahid expressly assigns sukūn here to Abu Amr and, by their rule for all listed first-person yāʾs, to Hamza, al-Kisai, Ibn Amir and Abu Bakr from Asim. [2 notes: IBNMUJAHID(أبو عمرو,حمزة، الكسائي، ابن عامر، عاصم عن أبي بكر)]

## 20:125/q05 What blindness and former sight does the speaker describe?  [أَعْمَىٰ بَصِيرًۭا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the contrast concerns eyesight, proof and argument, spiritual insight, or access to reward and escape; al-Tabari stresses that the wording itself does not restrict sight, while al-Wahidi and al-Qurtubi question whether a denier ever had a valid proof.
- 20:125/q05/p1 He is physically blind on resurrection after having eyesight in worldly life. — Al-Tabari reports both an unnamed literal view and a report from Mujahid; al-Thaʿlabi, al-Wahidi al-Wajiz, al-Nasafi, Ibn Kathir, Bursevi, al-Alusi and Asad give the same contrast. Abu Hayyan calls it the majority reading and cites another verse; Ibn Atiyya argues unbelievers would not newly feel loss of insight they already lacked. Ibn Ashur distinguishes figurative blindness in the earlier instance from real blindness here. Tabatabai says this is the first impression of the question, while citing 32:12 and 17:14 as an apparent difficulty. Bint al-Shati connects the raising blind to the warning with ḍank. Razi calls the blindness requital for abandoning guidance. Qurtubi and al-Alusi note that the prior sight is worldly; al-Alusi adds that most rejecters could see although some were blind from birth. [16 notes: TAB-FULL(آخرون,مجاهد); THALABI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; BINTSHATI-IJAZ; RAZI-FULL]
- 20:125/q05/p2 He lacks a proof or defense at resurrection although he knew, saw, or thought he could argue his case in life. — Mujahid and Muqatil gloss former sight as knowledge of proof; Muqatil links the loss to 69:29, 'my proof has gone.' Yahya reports Qatada and al-Suddi on lacking argument, and explains the denier's earlier self-confidence despite rejecting revelation. Al-Tabari, al-Thaʿlabi, al-Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, al-Durr and al-Alusi transmit forms of this gloss, with reports attributed to Mujahid, Ibn Abbas, al-Dahhak, Muqatil and Abu Salih. Al-Damghani and Ibn al-Jawzi give lexical senses of aʿmā as lacking proof and baṣīr as insight into proof. Al-Alusi says abandoned proofs leave him without an argument that helps him. Al-Wahidi al-Basit and al-Qurtubi object that an unbeliever possessed no real proof in either world; al-Qurtubi calls the attributed gloss far-fetched. [20 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(قتادة والسدي); TAB-FULL(مجاهد); FARISI-HUJJA(بعض المفسرين); THALABI(مجاهد); DAMGHANI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس ومجاهد); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(مجاهد,مجاهد والضحاك ومقاتل وأبو صالح); DURR-FULL(مجاهد); ALUSI-FULL(مجاهد ومقاتل والضحاك وأبو صالح وابن عباس); WAHIDI-BASIT-; QURTUBI-FULL(القشيري)-]
- 20:125/q05/p3 Both his physical sight and his ability to argue are taken from him, though he had both in life. — Al-Tabari prefers the inclusive sense because the verse leaves 'sight' unrestricted rather than confining it to either eyes or proof. [1 notes: TAB-FULL+]
- 20:125/q05/p4 The blindness is blindness of heart or insight into truth and guidance, reflecting his worldly condition. — Yahya transmits Qatada's statement that he was blind to truth in life. Al-Tabari reports Qatada's contrast between far-sightedness and blindness to truth. Qushayri cites the report that one meets God in the state in which one lived: the blind of heart and ignorant rise so. Razi speaks of souls departing ignorant and spiritually disconnected, with intensified pain. Ibrahim ibn Arafa argues every blameworthy scriptural blindness concerns the heart, citing 22:46; al-Alusi reports Ibn Urfa favored the insight reading. Ibn Ashur says resurrection blindness depicts earlier blindness to guidance and salvation, God's anger, and exclusion from mercy. Islahi says people fail to recognize their earthly blindness until Judgment unveils reality, addressing 50:22. [8 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(قتادة); TAB-FULL(قتادة); QUSHAYRI; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(إبراهيم بن عرفة); ALUSI-FULL(مجاهد ومقاتل والضحاك وأبو صالح وابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 20:125/q05/p5 He is blind to the paths that lead to reward or salvation, while other perception may remain. — Al-Farisi explicitly proposes blindness to the paths of reward. Tabatabai says one can be blind to felicity and salvation yet see the proof against oneself, Judgment's terrors and what deepens punishment; he cites 83:15 and rejects assuming afterlife perception follows worldly rules. [2 notes: FARISI-HUJJA; TABATABAI]
- 20:125/q05/p6 He cannot find his way. — Abu Hayyan reports this among alternative glosses and says al-Jubbai adopted it. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قول آخر)+]
- 20:125/q05/p7 He is helpless to escape punishment. — Abu Hayyan records this as one of the alternative explanations. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قول آخر)]
- 20:125/q05/p8 He sees nothing except Hell. — Abu Hayyan records this as another alternative explanation. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قول آخر)]

## 20:125/q06 How is the vowel of aʿmā read here and at its preceding occurrence?  [أَعْمَىٰ · readings]
turns on: The readers' treatment of the final alif, its yāʾ origin, and whether the first occurrence's verse ending affects its pronunciation.
- 20:125/q06/p1 Both occurrences are pronounced with fatḥa, without imāla. — Al-Farisi lists Hafs from Asim, Abu Amr, Ibn Kathir and Ibn Amir; Ibn al-Jawzi describes fatḥa on the two mīms and likewise lists Ibn Kathir, Abu Amr, Ibn Amir and Hafs. Al-Farisi says both imāla and its absence are acceptable, referring to earlier discussion. [3 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(حفص عن عاصم); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن كثير وأبو عمرو وابن عامر وحفص عن عاصم)]
- 20:125/q06/p2 Both occurrences receive imāla or a kasra-like pronunciation. — Al-Farisi names Abu Bakr from Asim, Hamza and al-Kisai. Ibn al-Jawzi calls the vowel kasra on the two mīms. Al-Baydawi names Hamza and al-Kisai and gives the derivation of the alif from yāʾ as reason; al-Alusi adds Khalaf and invokes the defective-yāʾ form. Al-Farisi accepts imāla alongside its absence. [5 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(أبو بكر عن عاصم وحمزة والكسائي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(حمزة والكسائي وأبو بكر عن عاصم); BAYDAWI-FULL(حمزة والكسائي); ALUSI-FULL(حمزة والكسائي وخلف)]
- 20:125/q06/p3 Nafi reads between imāla and fatḥa. — Both al-Farisi and Ibn al-Jawzi distinguish Nafi's intermediate realization from the two endpoint pronunciations. [2 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(حفص عن عاصم); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(نافع)]
- 20:125/q06/p4 Abu Amr distinguishes the first occurrence at the verse ending from the second here. — Al-Baydawi says the first is a pause position favoring a change; this account differs in detail from al-Farisi's and Ibn al-Jawzi's assignment of fatḥa to Abu Amr in both occurrences. [1 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL(أبو عمرو)]

## 20:125/q07 What is the grammatical role of 'while I had been sighted'?  [وَقَدْ كُنتُ بَصِيرًۭا · grammar]
- 20:125/q07/p1 It is a circumstantial clause describing the object 'me' in 'You gathered me.' — Al-Samin identifies وقد كنت بصيرا as ḥāl from the object of حشرتني. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 20:125/q08 When was the speaker previously sighted?  [وَقَدْ كُنتُ بَصِيرًۭا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether 'I had been sighted' refers to earthly life or an earlier stage of resurrection.
- 20:125/q08/p1 He had sight in worldly life. — Mujahid and the literal interpreters reported by al-Tabari locate former sight in life; al-Qurtubi, al-Durr, Bursevi and al-Alusi do likewise. Al-Alusi observes that this fits most rejecters but some had been blind from birth. The claim can concern eyesight or, in the proof gloss, knowledge of an argument. [14 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(آخرون,مجاهد); THALABI(مجاهد); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس ومجاهد); DURR-FULL(مجاهد); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(مجاهد ومقاتل والضحاك وأبو صالح وابن عباس)]
- 20:125/q08/p2 He was sighted at the start of resurrection and became blind at the gathering place. — Abu Hayyan reports Ibn Abbas's sequence; al-Alusi cites the same report and says the blindness apparently is later removed as well. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس)]

## 20:125/q09 How can his blindness coexist with passages where sinners see at Judgment?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the apparent conflict is resolved by changing states, different objects of perception, or unveiling an unrecognized worldly blindness.
- 20:125/q09/p1 They pass through different states, seeing at one time and blind at another. — Al-Jishumi allows two states to reconcile the statement with sharp sight that day. Abu Hayyan reports Ibn Abbas that sight precedes blindness at the gathering place; al-Alusi adds apparent later restoration. Tabatabai reports both a sequence from sight to blindness and a proposal of alternating states in response to 32:12 and 17:14. [5 notes: JISHUMI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); TABATABAI(بعضهم)]
- 20:125/q09/p2 He can be blind to salvation while seeing the evidence and terrors that punish him. — Tabatabai rejects imposing worldly rules of perception on the afterlife; he says sight of incriminating proof, terrors and painful scenes can coexist with blindness to felicity and cites 83:15. [1 notes: TABATABAI]
- 20:125/q09/p3 Judgment exposes an earthly blindness he had not recognized. — Islahi uses this disclosure to reconcile the contrast with Qaf 50:22. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 20:125/q10 In what setting does the speaker lose his argument and ask this question?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports describe different moments or wording in the resurrection scene.
- 20:125/q10/p1 His limbs testify against him for association and disbelief, leaving him without a defense. — Muqatil places the utterance at the testimony of the person's limbs; he also connects the lost argument with 69:29. [2 notes: MUQATIL]
- 20:125/q10/p2 The resurrected initially ask who raised them, until the truth becomes unavoidable. — Qushayri connects their question from the resting place to 36:52. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]

## 20:125/q11 What conduct accounts for the blindness and what does it signify?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes variously explain blindness as requital for rejected guidance, one kind of neglect included in that rejection, or persistence of an ignorant soul's state.
- 20:125/q11/p1 Turning away from God's reminder and guidance brings blindness as punishment and marks exclusion from mercy. — Razi describes requital for abandoning guidance; Ibn Ashur calls the blindness a depiction of worldly estrangement from guidance and salvation and a mark of God's anger. Asad says the divine reply cites ignored messages and being forgotten. Bint al-Shati links the unique ḍank to warning those who turn away from the reminder and are raised blind. Al-Alusi says abandoned proofs leave the person without an aid and blind forever. Al-Biqai gives two possibilities for 'thus' in the reply: the person acted shamefully in this way or events stand as he described, the reply explaining the former or its cause in the latter. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; BINTSHATI-IJAZ; ALUSI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 20:125/q11/p2 Neglect of Qur'an recitation, retention, and care is included in turning away from remembrance. — Ibn Kathir says some commentators include this grave negligence, but it is only part of the verse's intent. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 20:125/q11/p3 A soul that leaves its body ignorant and disconnected from spiritual beings remains so and suffers intensified spiritual pain. — Razi offers this as an alternative and connects it to rejecting evidence during life. Qushayri cites a report that a person meets God in the state in which they lived, applying it to the blind of heart and the ignorant. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; QUSHAYRI]

## 20:125/q12 What makes a variant reading acceptable as Qur'an?  [* · readings]
turns on: Whether tawātur must attach to every variant letter, to the accepted transmission collectively, or whether sound transmission is the formal condition.
- 20:125/q12/p1 A reading established as mutawātir from the Prophet is accepted even against the written rasm; each variant letter need not separately be mutawātir. — Ibn al-Jazari in al-Nashr rejects an individual-letter tawātur requirement because it would exclude many established readings. He says he once inclined toward that stricter condition, then followed early and later authorities in judging it unsound. [2 notes: NASHR+]
- 20:125/q12/p2 Collective transmission, broad acceptance across groups, no objection and wide circulation suffice for treating readings as mutawātir, and are the minimum where individual tawātur is lacking. — Al-Nashr reports Abu Shama's formulation of the collective-transmission standard. [1 notes: NASHR(أبو شامة)]
- 20:125/q12/p3 Sound transmission is the sole formal condition; Arabic conformity and rasm agreement follow necessarily. — Al-Nashr reports al-Jabari's argument that knowledge of transmitters, Arabic and the written codex settles the question. [1 notes: NASHR(إبراهيم بن عمر الجعبري)]

## 20:125/q13 What aspects of Qur'anic coherence and expression does Suyuti study?  [* · links]
turns on: These are complementary topics in Suyuti's program, not competing explanations of this verse.
- 20:125/q13/p1 He studies how verse endings suit their verses. — Suyuti names this as one dimension of coherence. [1 notes: SUYUTI-TANASUQ]
- 20:125/q13/p2 He studies how a surah's name relates to its contents. — Suyuti includes this among the topics of coherence. [1 notes: SUYUTI-TANASUQ]
- 20:125/q13/p3 He explains why one synonym is selected over another. — Suyuti presents this as a question of Qur'anic word choice. [1 notes: SUYUTI-TANASUQ]
- 20:125/q13/p4 He studies famous and anomalous readings and their meanings as part of inimitability. — Suyuti explicitly includes knowledge of readings in his method. [1 notes: SUYUTI-TANASUQ]
- 20:125/q13/p5 He studies differences among repeated passages, including addition, omission, reordering and substituted words. — Suyuti lists these forms of variation. [1 notes: SUYUTI-TANASUQ]
- 20:125/q13/p6 He wrote a concise work on coherence in surah order, selecting views he approved. — Suyuti says the work was largely his own thinking because few had studied this subject; he renamed it Tanāsuq al-Durar from Nata'ij al-Fikr because the later title better fitted its subject and added wordplay. [2 notes: SUYUTI-TANASUQ]

## 20:125/q14 Was surah order set by prophetic instruction or Companion judgment?  [* · sciences]
turns on: The differing early codex orders and the report of Uthman's arrangement favor Companion judgment; the account of Gabriel assigning each surah and verse its place supports prophetic instruction. Both sides agree on verse order.
- 20:125/q14/p1 Surah order came through the Companions' independent judgment. — Suyuti reports this as one side of the dispute, held by Malik, Qadi Abu Bakr in one opinion and decisively by Ibn Faris. Early codices differed: Ali's followed revelation order; Ibn Masud began al-Baqara, al-Nisa, Al Imran; Ubayy's and other codices differed too. Ibn Ashta transmits that Uthman ordered the long surahs together. [4 notes: SUYUTI-TANASUQ(جماعة الاجتهاد,عثمان,مالك والقاضي أبو بكر)]
- 20:125/q14/p2 Surah order was fixed by prophetic instruction. — Suyuti reports another group including Qadi Abu Bakr's other opinion. Ibn al-Anbari says Gabriel showed the Prophet each surah's and verse's place after the Qur'an descended first to the lowest heaven and then over twenty-odd years; changing the sequence corrupts its order. Both sides agree verse order was instructed. [3 notes: SUYUTI-TANASUQ(أبو بكر بن الأنباري,القاضي أبو بكر)]
