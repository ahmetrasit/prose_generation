# 51:2: 21 questions

## 51:2/q01 What does al-ḥāmilāt mean as a word?  [ٱلْحَٰمِلَٰتِ · meaning]
- 51:2/q01/p1 Those that carry a burden. — Kuranyolu gives the literal sense as carriers of a load or weight. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 51:2/q01/p2 The word can describe pregnant females. — Jishumi distinguishes ḥaml, a womb or tree load, from ḥiml, a load on the back, and cites a poem using ḥāmilah of a pregnant woman. He reports two explanations for ḥāmil: a feminine-only adjective like ṭāliq and ḥāʾiḍ, or 'possessor of pregnancy.' Qurtubi compares fruit-bearing palms to pregnant women and notes the muwaqqir, muwaqqarah, and muwaqqar forms, including irregular ones; he cites Labid's palm verse and the plural mawāqir. [5 notes: JISHUMI; QURTUBI-FULL(لبيد)]

## 51:2/q02 Who or what are the burden bearers?  [ٱلْحَٰمِلَٰتِ · referent]
turns on: Whether the participle denotes the rain-laden cloud, its moving wind, a loaded ship, a pregnant creature, or a wider set of causes; the wind and cloud sequence and the scope of the plural are cited.
- 51:2/q02/p1 Rain-bearing clouds. — Mujahid, Ali, Ibn Abbas and later commentators identify clouds. Muqatil, Wahidi, Baghawi and others specify their water weight; Tabrisi says they carry it between lands. Biqai describes wind dispersing and gathering vapor into clouds bearing water, lightning, and other unseen contents. Kuranyolu reports this as a common interpretation. [34 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); ABDURRAZZAQ(علي); ZAJJAJ(علي); MAWARDI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL(علي); IBNATIYYA-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); TABRISI(علي); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(علي); IBNKATHIR-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); DURR-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); ELMALILI; TABATABAI(علي عليه أفضل السلام); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Hz. Ali,müfessirler); STUDYQURAN(Bḍ, JJ, Q, Ṭ); ALUSI-FULL+; BAGHAWI-FULL+; BIQAI-FULL+; BURSEVI+; IBNATIYYA-FULL+; IBNJAWZI-ZAD+; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB+; KASHSHAF-FULL+; MAJAZ+; MUQATIL+; NAHHAS+; NASAFI-FULL+; QUSHAYRI+; TABATABAI+; TABRISI+; WAHIDI-BASIT+; WAHIDI-WAJIZ+]
- 51:2/q02/p2 Winds carrying clouds or water-bearing vapor. — Nahhas reports this because winds carry clouds. Ibn Bahr adds that a wind precedes each cloud and another bears it, since clouds move only by wind. Kashshaf and Abu Hayyan explain the whole series as wind functions; Razi, as reported by Kuranyolu, considers this stronger and says vapor clouds may be heavier than mountains. Islahi sees a wind subject and cites 7:57 for clouds as the conventional burden. Baydawi and Elmalili allow this alongside other referents. [12 notes: NAHHAS(قيل); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن بحر); KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(müfessirler); ISLAHI-TADABBUR+; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî)+; TABATABAI-]
- 51:2/q02/p3 Ships laden with people and goods. — Nahhas reports ships; Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan attribute the people-and-belongings formulation to Ibn Abbas, echoed by Kuranyolu. Muqatil instead makes ships the next oath object, the smooth-running ones. [4 notes: NAHHAS(قيل); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس وغيره); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(İbn Abbas)]
- 51:2/q02/p4 Pregnant women or animals. — Mawardi suggests women weighed down by pregnancy; Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan report pregnant animals; Qurtubi and Study Quran name women. Baydawi and Elmalili allow pregnant women among several possibilities. Ibn Atiyya says the interpretations all offer reflection. [8 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(جماعة من العلماء); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Bir grup âlim); STUDYQURAN(Bḍ, Q)]
- 51:2/q02/p5 The phrase may include the causes of the carriers as well. — Baydawi and Elmalili explicitly add the causes of rain-bearing clouds, wind-borne clouds, and pregnancy; Elmalili allows the carriers to be the same forces as the preceding oath or different ones. [2 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 51:2/q03 What does wiqr mean?  [وِقْرًۭا · meaning]
turns on: The notes distinguish kasra wiqr for a burden from fatḥa waqr for ear heaviness.
- 51:2/q03/p1 A heavy carried load, especially on the back or in the belly. — Nahhas says any load on the back; Mawardi, Tabrisi, Qurtubi and Tabatabai include the womb or belly. Qurtubi says it commonly names a mule or donkey load, whereas wasq names a camel's. Jishumi derives it from heaviness and uses the heavily fruited palm, muwqir. Islahi says wind-borne dust and pebbles fit the sense but clouds are its conventional referent, citing 7:57. [8 notes: NAHHAS; MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 51:2/q03/p2 With fatḥa, waqr denotes heaviness in the ear or deafness. — Mawardi and Tabrisi distinguish this from kasra wiqr. Qurtubi gives waqirat udhunuhu for an ear going deaf and calls the sukūn in the verbal noun unusual. [4 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL]

## 51:2/q04 How is wiqran read and explained in the variant reading?  [وِقْرًۭا · readings]
- 51:2/q04/p1 It is read waqran with fatḥa on the wāw. — Kashshaf and Baydawi say the verbal noun names the carried thing, in place of ḥamlan; Samin reports both this object-naming explanation and the possibility of a verbal noun governed by the preceding sense of carrying, since ḥaml and waqr overlap though ḥaml is broader. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(قراءة غير مسماة); BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]

## 51:2/q05 What grammatical role does wiqran have?  [وِقْرًۭا · grammar]
turns on: Whether the word names the thing carried as an object or, with fatḥa, retains its verbal-noun force.
- 51:2/q05/p1 It is the object of al-ḥāmilāt. — Ibn Atiyya, Nasafi, Samin and Bursevi explicitly parse it as the carried object; Nasafi and Bursevi combine this with the cloud reading. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI]
- 51:2/q05/p2 Under the fatḥa reading, it can remain a verbal noun governed by the preceding verbal meaning. — Samin says ḥaml and waqr overlap in sense, though ḥaml is broader. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 51:2/q06 How is al-ḥāmilāt joined syntactically to the surrounding oaths?  [فَٱلْحَٰمِلَٰتِ · grammar]
- 51:2/q06/p1 It is coordinated with the preceding oath and is genitive as an oath term. — Nahhas explicitly calls it coordinated with the preceding oath phrase. Zajjaj calls the listed objects genitive under the oath whose answer is 51:5. [2 notes: NAHHAS; ZAJJAJ]

## 51:2/q07 What does the fāʾ sequence imply about the four oath descriptions?  [فَٱلْحَٰمِلَٰتِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether fāʾ orders different classes of creatures or successive properties and actions of a single subject; Islahi invokes an Arabic rule, while the reported Ali interpretation supplies distinct referents.
- 51:2/q07/p1 It orders distinct referents and the effects or signs linking them. — Kashshaf orders winds, the clouds they drive, ships moved by wind, and angels apportioning provision through rain and trade. Baydawi calls it increasing evidence of power; Biqai gives vapor, clouds and water distribution as a causal chain. Abu Hayyan says distinct referents are successive groups. Tabatabai orders clouds after winds; Ali's transmitted fourfold identification supports this. The oath is read as winds, clouds, ships, and angels by Ali and many transmitters. [14 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(علي); KASHSHAF-FULL(علي); TABRISI(علي); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري,علي); TABATABAI(علي عليه أفضل السلام); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Zemahşerî ve Râzî); BIQAI-FULL+; KASHSHAF-FULL+; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Hz. Ali)+; TABATABAI+; ISLAHI-TADABBUR(those who regard them as adjectives of separate nouns)-]
- 51:2/q07/p2 It orders successive acts or qualities of winds alone. — Kashshaf says winds scatter dust and gravel, form and carry clouds, move and spread them, and distribute rain. Abu Hayyan compares a poem ordering a raid, booty-taking, and return. Baydawi recognizes this single-kind sequence; Kuranyolu reports Razi preferring it. Islahi argues that fāʾ linking adjectives makes them qualify the same noun, citing Arabic sequence rules and Quranic parallels. [8 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Zemahşerî ve Râzî); ISLAHI-TADABBUR+; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî)+; TABATABAI-]
- 51:2/q07/p3 The one-subject pattern can be illustrated with horses in classical Arabic. — Islahi says an opening-oath series about horses follows ordered attributes of one subject and cites Ibn Zayyabah's couplet as an illustration of the style. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 51:2/q08 What is the oath's answer and what does it affirm?  [* · interpretation]
- 51:2/q08/p1 What people are promised is true, and judgment or recompense will occur. — Zajjaj cites 51:5; Qushayri says the promised thing is true; Kuranyolu cites 51:5–6 for the certain Day of Judgment. Tabatabai says the successive oaths intensify promised recompense. Islahi says wind effects and striped clouds substantiate both the threatened punishment and the Hereafter's reward and punishment. [6 notes: ZAJJAJ; QUSHAYRI; QASHANI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 51:2/q08/p2 The combined consequence of preparers, receptive capacities, and emanators must occur. — Qashani gives the inward oath's corresponding necessary consequence. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 51:2/q09 Why are these things sworn by, and what do they show?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes variously stress signs of divine power, evidence for resurrection, reflection and affirmation, and Farahi's theory of an oath's semantic purpose.
- 51:2/q09/p1 They display God's making, power, blessings, and wise governance, inviting reflection. — Jishumi says winds and clouds show power and blessings; Baghawi says created things demonstrate God's making and power. Tabrisi cites their human benefits and signs of oneness and artistry. Biqai says measured winds, suspended clouds, ship movement, and angelic distribution show a wise Creator; excess, cessation, or stillness can destroy. Ibn Barjan says each oath object has significant qualities whose signs distractions may hide. Tabatabai sees general causes governing land, sea and sky through angels. Kuranyolu links the oaths to earthly and heavenly provision and the surah's 51:22, citing 51:7, 20, 23, 47, 48. [8 notes: JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; BIQAI-FULL(ابن برجان); TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 51:2/q09/p2 The displayed power and extraordinary order establish God's ability to resurrect. — Biqai reports Baydawi's inference from extraordinary phenomena; Kuranyolu repeats it as the inference from striking order and balance. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL(البيضاوي); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Beyzâvî)]
- 51:2/q09/p3 The oath strengthens belief and prompts thought, while a human oath calls God as witness. — Biqai reports Razi contrasting divine oaths by created wonders with human oaths by God, who knows claims in public and secret. Kuranyolu says the oaths attract attention and mark the subject's seriousness. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL(الرازي); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 51:2/q09/p4 An oath chiefly indicates meaning and reinforces the speaker's assertion and resolve. — Kuranyolu reports Farahi that glorifying the object is not the Quranic oath's primary purpose, though it may be implied. Farahi says it reinforces a statement and shows firm resolve to act or refrain; Arab oaths can honor an object or addressee, elevate the speaker, or appeal to fear of perjury's curse. Kuranyolu notes Farahi's study of Ibn Qayyim and Razi and the limited older literature devoted solely to oaths. [4 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Abdülhamîd el-Ferâhî)]

## 51:2/q10 By whom is the oath formally made?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether an implied divine object is required, or God's oath may name creatures, or an oath need not posit an object at all.
- 51:2/q10/p1 The oath is by the Lord and His power over the created things. — Qushayri says this of the outward sequence. Tabrisi reports a view supplying 'Lord of these things' because only God may be sworn by. [2 notes: QUSHAYRI; TABRISI(قيل)]
- 51:2/q10/p2 God may swear by creatures, though people may swear only by God. — Tabrisi transmits Abu Ja'far and Abu Abd Allah's distinction. [1 notes: TABRISI(أبو جعفر وأبو عبد الله)]
- 51:2/q10/p3 No omitted object of oath has to be supplied. — Kuranyolu reports Farahi: an oath can work without naming an object sworn by, and interpretation need not assume glorification of an object. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Abdülhamîd el-Ferâhî)]

## 51:2/q11 Why are the oath bearers left unnamed?  [ٱلْحَٰمِلَٰتِ · rhetoric]
- 51:2/q11/p1 The participles concisely name striking qualities and leave several bearers imaginable. — Kuranyolu reports Ibn Ashur's account of elegant concision and says the plural participles have prompted several interpretations. [2 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(İbn Âşûr)]

## 51:2/q12 How does the opening oath connect to the surah's theme and other verses?  [* · links]
- 51:2/q12/p1 It underpins the surah's promise of resurrection and recompense against deniers. — Tabatabai says the mission warns of worldly ruin and everlasting punishment, and that accountability gives effect to belief in oneness and prophethood. He says deniers target resurrection to undermine religion; the surah opens and closes on the promised Day, and the opening section calls denial conjecture before describing the pious and deniers. Kuranyolu links the atmospheric and angelic signs to the recurring earth and sky theme and 51:22. [6 notes: TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 51:2/q12/p2 Its assertion of judgment continues Sūrat Qāf's treatment of death and the afterlife. — Biqai quotes Ibn al-Zubayr, who links this oath to 50:19 and warnings that God gives wrongdoers respite rather than overlooking them, citing 14:42 and 3:178. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL(أبو جعفر بن الزبير)]

## 51:2/q13 What is reported about the surah's revelation and length?  [* · sciences]
- 51:2/q13/p1 Al-Dhāriyāt is Meccan. — Durr states this and transmits Ibn Abbas and Ibn al-Zubayr. Tabatabai says the context supports it and reports no dispute. [4 notes: DURR-FULL(ابن الزبير,ابن عباس); DURR-FULL+; TABATABAI+]
- 51:2/q13/p2 It has sixty verses. — Durr gives this count. [1 notes: DURR-FULL]

## 51:2/q14 What happened in the reported questioning of Ali about the oaths?  [* · reports]
- 51:2/q14/p1 Ali was questioned in public and explained the four oath figures as winds, clouds, ships, and angels. — Abu al-Tufayl says Ali invited questions, claiming knowledge of future matters and of whether each verse descended by night or day, on plain or mountain. Ali's answer is transmitted by Abd al-Razzaq, Kashshaf, Tabrisi, Abu Hayyan, Ibn Kathir, Durr, and others; Tabrisi also names Ibn Abbas and Mujahid as transmitters. Ali rebuked Ibn al-Kawwa for provocative questioning rather than seeking knowledge. [9 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(أبو الطفيل,علي); KASHSHAF-FULL(علي); TABRISI(علي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(علي); IBNKATHIR-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); DURR-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Hz. Ali)]

## 51:2/q15 What other answers are attached to Ali's reported exchange?  [* · reports]
- 51:2/q15/p1 Ali answered questions about Dhū al-Qarnayn, the rainbow, and al-Bayt al-Maʿmūr. — Abd al-Razzaq reports that Dhū al-Qarnayn was neither prophet nor king but a righteous servant who loved and served God, called people to guidance, and was struck on each side of his head. The rainbow was a sign between Noah and his Lord and protection from drowning. Al-Bayt al-Maʿmūr is below the Throne in the seventh heaven and receives seventy thousand angels daily who never return. [3 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(علي)]

## 51:2/q16 How is a related raised report graded, and why was Ṣubaygh punished?  [* · reports]
- 51:2/q16/p1 The report raised to the Prophet is weak; its nearest sound form stops at Umar. — Ibn Kathir says Umar punished Ṣubaygh because his questions appeared stubborn and contentious, and cites al-Bazzar's criticism of two transmitters. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL]

## 51:2/q17 What reports describe the source and effects of cloud-borne rain?  [ٱلْحَٰمِلَٰتِ · reports]
turns on: The reports give different accounts of where clouds come from, what they do to rain, and what each drop produces.
- 51:2/q17/p1 A tree in paradise bears clouds, with black mature clouds bringing rain and white clouds not raining. — Bursevi reports this from Khalid ibn Ma'dan. [1 notes: BURSEVI(خالد بن معدان)]
- 51:2/q17/p2 Clouds sieve rain so it does not destroy what it strikes. — Bursevi reports this from Ka'b. [1 notes: BURSEVI(كعب)]
- 51:2/q17/p3 Clouds hold people's provision, but sins and hopes can deprive them of it. — Bursevi reports al-Hasan saying this to companions looking at clouds. [1 notes: BURSEVI(الحسن)]
- 51:2/q17/p4 Every heavenly drop grows a herb on earth or a pearl in the sea. — Bursevi reports this from Ikrima. [1 notes: BURSEVI(عكرمة)]

## 51:2/q18 What may wind and cloud effects bring to people?  [فَٱلْحَٰمِلَٰتِ · interpretation]
- 51:2/q18/p1 They bring mercy or punishment according to where the water or storm falls. — Biqai says the wind-to-cloud causal chain divides water among distant lands as mercy or punishment. Islahi contrasts mercy for one people with punitive storms for another. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 51:2/q19 How are the carriers understood inwardly?  [ٱلْحَٰمِلَٰتِ · inward]
turns on: The inward readings differ in what is borne: separation, unveiled knowledge, divine mercy, or lovers' longing.
- 51:2/q19/p1 Clouds can pour the reproach of absence and the pain of separation. — Qushayri says people of insight recognize these clouds and pray for protection; he cites a poem depicting estrangement raining separation upon lovers. [2 notes: QUSHAYRI]
- 51:2/q19/p2 They are luminous incoming states that carry lasting realities and unveiled knowledge to receptive hearts. — Qashani links them to prepared souls and capacities; the inward oath by preparers, receptivity and emanation makes their consequence necessary. [2 notes: QASHANI]
- 51:2/q19/p3 Clouds of divine kindness carry mercy that revives truthful hearts. — Bursevi compares rain reviving earth with divine effusion reviving heart and spirit. [1 notes: BURSEVI]
- 51:2/q19/p4 Morning winds bear lovers' groans and return with a scent of truth that relieves longing. — Bursevi says the lovers seek divine favors and the scent reaches the secrets of love. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 51:2/q20 How does fertility figure in Bursevi's inward reading?  [ٱلْحَٰمِلَٰتِ · inward]
- 51:2/q20/p1 The fertile woman images the perfect human, productive of effects and lights. — Bursevi cites an ungraded saying attributed to the Prophet preferring a fertile dark-skinned woman to a beautiful barren one. He contrasts the generative perfect human with the inert incomplete human, likening the latter to innamā, limited to emphasis and restriction. [2 notes: BURSEVI(النبي)]

## 51:2/q21 How is the oath sequence classified metrically?  [* · rhetoric]
- 51:2/q21/p1 It has been classified as basīṭ meter. — Burhan reports this classification for openings of al-ʿĀdiyāt and al-Dhāriyāt, citing 100:1–2 and 51:1–3. [1 notes: BURHAN(قالوا)]
