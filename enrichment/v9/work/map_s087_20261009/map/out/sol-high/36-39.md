# 36:39: 24 questions

## 36:39/q01 What does qamar name here?  [ٱلْقَمَرَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the verse follows restricted lexical usage or the familiar name of the celestial body.
- 36:39/q01/p1 The familiar moon throughout its monthly course. — Al-Ālūsī prefers this ordinary sense because the moon is paired with the sun; al-Qurṭubī connects the name to its brightness whitening the sky. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 36:39/q01/p2 Strict lexical usage calls it qamar chiefly from about the third through twenty-sixth night, and hilāl near conjunction. — Al-Ālūsī attributes the distinction to lexicographers. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(اللغويون)]

## 36:39/q02 How is wa-l-qamar read and construed?  [ٱلْقَمَرَ · readings]
turns on: The recited case ending and whether the preceding nominal signs or the surrounding verbs govern the construction.
- 36:39/q02/p1 Nominative: the moon begins a nominal clause or is another sign alongside the night and sun. — Ibn Kathīr, Nāfiʿ, Abū ʿAmr, and in later lists Rawḥ/Yaʿqūb, Abū Jaʿfar, Ibn Muḥayṣin, al-Ḥasan, al-Aʿraj or Sahl are reported, with transmission variants for al-Ḥasan. The subject may have qaddarnāhu as predicate, or an implied āyatun lahum precedes it, with an understood predicate 'visible/existing' and ensuing explanatory clauses; al-Fārisī compares 36:37, 5:9, 4:11. Al-Farrāʾ, al-Naḥḥās and Abū Ḥātim prefer the nominal connection to earlier signs; Abū Ḥātim says the verb already bears an object pronoun. Al-Samīn says the nominal opening of wa-l-shams tajrī licenses it and judges both cases sound. - 36:39/q02/p2 Accusative: the moon is a fronted object governed by an implied verb explained by qaddarnāhu, or is attached to an earlier verbal construction. — Kufans, Ibn ʿĀmir and other readers are reported. Al-Zajjāj supplies 'We appointed'; Sībawayh and al-Fārisī compare Zaydan ḍarabtuhu and allow linkage to naslakh. Abū ʿUbayd prefers accusative because verbs flank it; al-Ālūsī says the shift emphasizes divine determination and lunar month reckoning; al-Biqāʿī sees grandeur and speed, a month's travel matching the sun's year. Al-Samīn rejects al-Akhfash's condition of a pronoun referring to the sun: four readers and 55:5,7 counter it. Al-Samīn treats both cases as sound, following the verbal ending of wa-l-shams tajrī. ## 36:39/q03 How is the rāʾ of qaddarnāhu read?  [قَدَّرْنَٰهُ · readings]
turns on: The transmitted vowel of the rāʾ.
- 36:39/q03/p1 With ḍamma on the rāʾ. — Ibn Kathīr, Nāfiʿ, Abū ʿAmr and Rawḥ transmit it. [1 notes: NASHR(ابن كثير ونافع وأبو عمرو وروح)]
- 36:39/q03/p2 With fatḥa on the rāʾ. — Ibn al-Jazarī assigns it to the other readers. [1 notes: NASHR(ابن كثير ونافع وأبو عمرو وروح)]

## 36:39/q04 What did God measure or appoint?  [قَدَّرْنَٰهُ مَنَازِلَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the implicit object of determination is the course, the light, or the moon itself.
- 36:39/q04/p1 God appointed the moon's course and stations. — Al-Bayḍāwī gives course or course through stages; al-Ālūsī and Abū Ḥayyān understand the moon's path or place of travel; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī speaks of ordained stations; al-Burhān supplies 'for it.' [7 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURHAN; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان); TABATABAI]
- 36:39/q04/p2 God measured its light, which grows and diminishes. — Al-Nasafī gives light as one possible omitted object; Abū Ḥayyān includes it; al-Ālūsī reports an explanation from changing sun-derived illumination and possible Earth eclipse, judging it more probable but uncertain. [4 notes: NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 36:39/q04/p3 God determined the moon itself through appointed stations or phases. — Al-Bayḍāwī permits direct determination; the ordinary explanation follows the moon through its changing monthly forms. [2 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(Q)]

## 36:39/q05 How does manāzil function grammatically?  [مَنَازِلَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether manāzil is an object, an understood prepositional phrase, or a locative, especially given its bounded place sense.
- 36:39/q05/p1 It is a second object or an omitted 'possessor of stations' construction. — Al-Naḥḥās and al-Qurṭubī supply dhā; al-Ṭabrisī says the omitted possessed noun leaves manāzil in its place, since the moon itself is not stations. Al-Samīn allows a second object if qaddara means 'made,' or a circumstantial dhā manāzil. Al-Ālūsī supplies the moon's course as first object; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī finds the 'possessing stations' sense clearest. [7 notes: NAHHAS; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(أبو السعود); TABATABAI+]
- 36:39/q05/p2 It stands for 'stations for it,' with an omitted preposition. — Al-Naḥḥās and al-Qurṭubī compare omitted prepositions in 12:82 and 7:155; Abū al-Suʿūd prefers this parse as al-Ālūsī reports; al-Burhān also supplies 'for it.' [5 notes: NAHHAS; QURTUBI-FULL; BURHAN; ALUSI-FULL(أبو السعود)+; TABATABAI+]
- 36:39/q05/p3 It is an adverbial expression for the moon's course through stations. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn allow the parse. Al-Ṭabrisī objects that a bounded place cannot directly function as a locative after a verb: one says 'sat in the mosque,' not 'sat the mosque.' [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان); TABRISI-]

## 36:39/q06 What are the moon's manāzil?  [مَنَازِلَ · meaning]
turns on: The counting convention: the Arab twenty-eight visible stations versus the Indian astronomers' rounding of the sidereal period.
- 36:39/q06/p1 Twenty-eight named lunar stopping places or stellar markers. — Kuran Yolu explains manzil as a stopping place. Ibn Qutayba, al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, Ibn ʿAbbās in al-Durr, Elmalılı and others give the twenty-eight-name sequence from al-Sharaṭān/al-Saraṭīn to al-Rishā or Batn al-Ḥūt, with variants such as al-Kharatān for al-Zubra, al-ʿAqrab for al-Ghafr, Cancer for the first, and al-Zabābayn for al-Zubānā. Arabs divided fourteen Syrian/northern from fourteen Yemeni/southern. Each zodiac sign covers two and a third stations. Al-Ālūsī says Arabs used visible stars near the lunar path as markers, dividing the circle into about 12°51′ segments; Ibn ʿAbbās and others connect named stars with true lunar stations. Al-Sharaṭān: two bright Aries horn stars and a small neighbor, called al-Nātiḥ; its setting signaled rain and wind. Al-Buṭayn: three faint Aries thigh stars in a triangle. Al-Thurayyā: a six-star fan/grape cluster, with four Taurus-rump/Aries-tail stars noted; a cited poetic comparison has uncertain attribution to Aḥīḥah or Qays. Al-Dabarān: red Taurus eye after Pleiades, also Mijdah, amid eye, face and muzzle stars. Al-Haqʿa: three faint Orion-head dots, also Athāfī; al-Hanʿa: Orion shoulder or horse-neck brand, two stars; al-Dhirāʿ: two bright stars over Gemini, Lion's outstretched arm, against folded arm Sirius. Al-Nathra: Lion-nose gap, two faint stars and Cancer cloud patch, between the donkey stars; al-Ṭarf: two stars before Lion's eyes and paw; al-Jabha: four bent Lion-forehead stars, including bright Heart of the Lion; al-Zubra: two bright Lion mane/shoulder/nape stars; al-Ṣarfa: single Lion-tail star marking cold's turning; al-ʿAwwā: five bent Virgo stars, explained as howling dogs, cold-chaser, or bent shape, alternatively a person's lower part or Lion's hip. Al-Simāk al-Aʿzal: bright Virgo-shoulder mansion; al-Rāmiḥ opposite it is not a mansion. Al-Ghafr: two or three faint Virgo-tail/hind-leg stars, sometimes put in Libra, named for covering/dimness; a report calls it fortunate, rising with prophets. Al-Zubānā: two Libra-shoulder stars, or Scorpion horns. Al-Iklīl: three or four Scorpion-head stars like a crown; al-Qalb: bright red Scorpio-heart star with Niyāṭayn; al-Shawla: two bright Scorpion-tail stars; al-Naʿāʾim: Archer stars by the Milky Way, incoming and departing fours, likened to well beams; al-Balda: empty Archer sky patch like a den/desert/brow gap. Saʿd al-Dhābiḥ: two Goat-horn stars and a small 'sheep'; Saʿd Bulaʿ: two Water Bearer stars with a swallowed-looking neighbor, connected also to 11:44 or the missing sheep; Saʿd al-Suʿūd: two or three Goat-tail stars whose rising marks livelihood; Saʿd al-Akhbiya: four Water Bearer/Archer stars like tents, alternatively named for hidden creatures emerging in warmth. The two Farghs are bright Winged Horse/Andromeda pairs likened to a bucket spout and seasonal rain; Batn al-Ḥūt/al-Rishā is a bright Andromeda star in an imagined fish. Al-Ālūsī records moon alignments, passages and occasional eclipses for individual markers, including Pleiades, donkeys, Lion-heart, Simāk, Qalb and other stars, and varying proximity at each station. [74 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس وغيره,غير واحد); ELMALILI; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 36:39/q06/p2 Twenty-seven stations in the Indian astronomers' reckoning. — Al-Ālūsī explains that the moon completes the zodiac in twenty-seven and a third days; the fraction is dropped because it is under a half. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 36:39/q07 How does the moon move through its stations?  [قَدَّرْنَٰهُ مَنَازِلَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether one station per night is taken as exact or as an approximate marker against visible stars.
- 36:39/q07/p1 It traverses roughly one station nightly, waxing to fullness and waning before disappearing and beginning again. — Muqātil, al-Kalbī, al-Ḥasan and later authors describe continual increase or decrease. Ibn Qutayba, al-Wāḥidī, al-Ṭabrisī, al-Bayḍāwī and al-Nasafī give nights one through twenty-eight; al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ṭabrisī say it neither skips nor falls short, while Ibn ʿAṭiyya says slightly less than one nightly. Ibn Kathīr puts fullness on night fourteen and lists three-night phase names ghurar, nafl, tisʿ, ʿashr, bīḍ, diraʿ, thulum, hanādis, daʿādiʿ, maḥāq; Abū ʿUbayd rejects tisʿ and ʿashr. It is unseen for one or two nights according to month length; al-Biqāʿī and Bursawī specify two after a thirty-day month, one after twenty-nine. Al-Qurṭubī says its nightly relation to the sun shifts and it rises again after the sun's station. Al-Māwardī reports a view linking the cycle to thirteen lunar months in a solar year. Elmalılı says the sun moves steadily while each lunar station changes the moon's form. [27 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(الحسن,الكلبي); IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو عبيد); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(Q); DURR-FULL(قتادة)]
- 36:39/q07/p2 Its speed varies, so apparent station crossings do not always occur one per night. — Al-Ālūsī says the moon may cross a station midway, remain across two nights, or appear between stations, correcting a precise daily alignment rule. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 36:39/q08 How were the lunar stations used for seasons and weather?  [مَنَازِلَ · sciences]
turns on: The observed stellar spacing and whether dawn visibility follows a fixed fourteen/fourteen division.
- 36:39/q08/p1 They marked the solar year, seasonal work and the rising and setting periods called anwāʾ. — Ibn Qutayba notes Arab linkage to rain omens. Al-Qurṭubī gives four seasons: spring from 15 Ādhār for 92 days across Aries, Taurus, Gemini; summer from 15 Ḥazīrān for 92 across Cancer, Leo, Virgo; autumn from 15 Aylūl for 91 across Libra, Scorpio, Sagittarius; winter from 15 Kānūn I for 90 or 91 across Capricorn, Aquarius, Pisces; each crosses seven mansions. Most Syriac months are 31 days, four 30, Shubāṭ 28¼. Al-Ālūsī says Arabs used stations to follow the solar year despite drifting lunar years, observed three hidden by sunlight, reckoned about thirteen days per appearance and 364 days, adding a day at Ghafr for 365. A mansion emerging from dawn light rises; its opposite sets. Al-Qutb calls rainy-season risings anwāʾ and other opposites bawāriḥ. Al-Jawharī defines nawʾ by dawn westward setting and opposite eastern rising, thirteen days except Jabha's fourteen; Abū ʿUbayd knows nawʾ as setting here, while al-Aṣmaʿī assigns weather to the rising star rather than the setting one. Al-Ālūsī gives anwāʾ and dual nuʾān forms. Al-Ṭībī's al-Marzūqī schedule variously gives Sharaṭān 3, Buṭayn 3, Thurayyā 5, Dabarān 3, Haqʿa 6, Nathra 7, Ṭarf 3, Jabha 7, Zubra 4, Ṣarfa 3, ʿAwwā 1, Simāk 4, Ghafr 3 or 1, Zubānā 3, Iklīl 4, Qalb and Shawla 3, Naʿāʾim 1, Balda 3 or 1, Dhābiḥ 1, other Saʿd and front Fargh 3, rear Fargh 4; Hanʿa is grouped with Jawzāʾ, Dhirāʿ has none and his copy omits Rishā. [20 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(أبو عبيد,الأصمعي,الجوهري,الطيبي,الطيبي عن المرزوقي,القطب)]
- 36:39/q08/p2 The supposed fourteen visible and fourteen hidden stations at each moment is false. — Al-Ālūsī says the stellar markers are not equally spaced along one path; sixteen or seventeen may be visible and thirteen hidden. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 36:39/q09 What causes the observed waxing and waning?  [ٱلْقَمَرَ مَنَازِلَ · interpretation]
turns on: Physical changing illumination versus the two reported accounts of removed original light.
- 36:39/q09/p1 The changing position of a sunlit moon relative to Earth and sun changes the illuminated part seen from Earth. — Abū Ḥayyān calls the lunar body dark and sunlight-reflecting like a polished mirror. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says roughly half is sunlit and emphasizes appearance to Earth's inhabitants, with effects on land, sea and life; Kuran Yolu describes the daily changing sunlit face and names new, first quarter, full and last quarter. Al-Qushayrī describes waxing as moving away from the sun and waning as drawing near. Kuran Yolu says it reflects a small fraction of sunlight and lights Earth at night. [7 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(أبو القاسم القشيري); TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 36:39/q09/p2 A reported cosmology attributes its diminished light to Gabriel erasing it. — Al-Qurṭubī reports sun and moon as fire clothed with light, the sun's from the Throne and moon's from the Kursī; Gabriel's wing erased lunar light. The moon moves in water and wanes from the side unseen by the sun. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]
- 36:39/q09/p3 A reported tale attributes its diminution to self-admiration and Gabriel striking its face. — Bursawī quotes Kashf al-asrār: the moon originally had complete light, admired itself, was struck and excluded from the divine court; Ibn ʿAbbās is cited on the remaining marks as testimony to oneness and messengership. Angels interceded and it could prostrate monthly on the fourteenth; its light grows as it approaches, then fades as it recedes. [3 notes: BURSEVI(ابن عباس,كشف الأسرار)]

## 36:39/q10 What does the moon's return describe?  [عَادَ · interpretation]
- 36:39/q10/p1 It becomes a thin crescent again at the end of its cycle, near the next month's beginning. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it reappearance; al-Wāḥidī says mention of stations compresses the motion through them; Ibn al-Shaykh locates it at one month's end and the next one's start. Al-Ālūsī notes nearness to the sun late in the course, and Elmalılı places the final crescent before conjunction. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; BURSEVI(ابن الشيخ); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 36:39/q11 What is the ʿurjūn?  [كَٱلْعُرْجُونِ · meaning]
turns on: Which part of the palm or fruit cluster the noun designates.
- 36:39/q11/p1 It is the date-cluster stalk or base, especially what remains attached to the palm after fruit stems are removed. — Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatāda, Mujāhid and many authors describe a dried, bent stalk or cluster base. Al-Zajjāj specifies the stem supporting little branches; al-Layth and al-Khalīl describe a broad yellow base; Thaʿlab and al-Jawharī describe the remainder after cutting. Al-Majāz calls it ihān and distinguishes ʿidhq with fatḥa as a palm tree; al-Ṭabrisī lists ʿuthkul, ʿithkal, kabasa and qinw, and al-Qurṭubī adds Egyptian isbāṭa. Bursawī says ʿidhq with kasra is the date cluster and shamarīkh the smaller fruit-bearing stems, with variant plurals. Al-Ālūsī calls this the well-known explanation and prefers it. Al-Aʿshā's verse compares the yellow moon with ʿurjūn; Ruʾba's muʿarjan line describes a curved form and is cited for the word. [46 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(مجاهد); MAJAZ; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس,الحسن); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,الكلبي,الليث,رؤبة,مقاتل); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; TABRISI(رؤبة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن بن أبي الحسن); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL(الأعشى بن قيس,الجوهري,الخليل,الزجاج,ثعلب,قتادة); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزجاج); SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس,مجاهد); BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,الحسن,قتادة,مجاهد); ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(İbn Âşûr); STUDYQURAN(Q, Z); ALUSI-FULL(الحسن وقتادة)+]
- 36:39/q11/p2 It is the small twig of a cluster that itself bears unripe dates. — Al-Ālūsī reports this alternative and favors the better-known cluster-stalk explanation. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 36:39/q11/p3 It is a bent palm tree. — Al-Māwardī reports this from al-Ḥasan. [1 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(الحسن)]

## 36:39/q12 What is the derivation and form of ʿurjūn?  [كَٱلْعُرْجُونِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether nūn belongs to the root or is an addition.
- 36:39/q12/p1 Its nūn is original; its pattern is faʿlūl. — Al-Ṭabrisī gives faʿlūl; al-Jawharī, al-Rāghib, al-Samīn and al-Qāmūs count nūn original. Al-Layth treats it as quadriliteral and connects ʿarjana with depicting date stems; Ruʾba's muʿarjan is cited as evidence. Al-Biqāʿī notes the textile-depiction derivation. Al-Zamakhsharī also gives a variant form on al-furjūn's pattern for the stalk between branchlets and base. [8 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(الليث,رؤبة); TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL(الجوهري,الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الراغب والسمين وصاحب القاموس)]
- 36:39/q12/p2 Its nūn is added; its pattern is faʿlūn from ʿ-r-j, bending. — Al-Zajjāj, Abū Isḥāq, al-Bayḍāwī and Bursawī derive it from inclination or inʿirāj; al-Qurṭubī also reports al-Zajjāj's added nūn. The old date stem visibly bends. [8 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL(الزجاج); BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج)]

## 36:39/q13 How is ʿurjūn vocalized?  [كَٱلْعُرْجُونِ · readings]
turns on: The transmitted dialectal vowels.
- 36:39/q13/p1 The common form has ḍamma on both ʿayn and jīm. — Abū Ḥayyān calls it the majority form; al-Bayḍāwī and al-Samīn treat the two vocalizations as dialect forms, comparing bizyūn. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(سليمان التيمي); BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(سليمان التيمي)]
- 36:39/q13/p2 A transmitted dialect form has kasra on ʿayn and fatḥa on jīm. — Sulaymān al-Taymī is reported by Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī; Ibn al-Jawzī also names Abū Majliz, Abū Rajāʾ, al-Ḍaḥḥāk, ʿĀṣim al-Jaḥdarī and Ibn al-Sumayfaʿ. Al-Ālūsī specifies rāʾ sukūn. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(سليمان التيمي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو مجلز وأبو رجاء والضحاك وعاصم الجحدري وابن السميفع); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(سليمان التيمي); SAMIN-DURR(سليمان التيمي); ALUSI-FULL(سليمان التيمي); BAYDAWI-FULL]

## 36:39/q14 In what respects is the moon like the old stalk?  [عَادَ كَٱلْعُرْجُونِ ٱلْقَدِيمِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the visual comparison is limited to crescent shape or also sketches its monthly path.
- 36:39/q14/p1 The final crescent and the aged stalk are both thin, curved and yellow or weathered. — Many compare the last visible moon, and sometimes the first crescent, to a dried stalk; Ibn Qutayba names night twenty-eight, al-Zajjāj allows either end or beginning, and al-Ḥasan invokes observation. Al-Aʿshā's poetic yellow moon supports color. Bursawī says this is how an observer sees the immense moon. Al-Itqān classifies it with comparisons of two sensible things. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls the stalk curved and yellow. [29 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(مجاهد); IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(الحسن); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن بن أبي الحسن); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL(الزمخشري); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ITQAN; BURSEVI(ابن الشيخ); BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(İbn Âşûr); STUDYQURAN(Q, Z)]
- 36:39/q14/p2 The curved stalk also evokes the moon's path around Earth over a month. — Elmalılı says the image outlines the orbital path as well as the first and last crescents, and that 'old' imagines the lunar body at every station; ancient astronomers missed this subtlety. [2 notes: ELMALILI]

## 36:39/q15 What does qadīm mean and how long must something be old?  [ٱلْقَدِيمِ · meaning]
turns on: Whether oldness is relative to custom or has a stated threshold of a year or six months.
- 36:39/q15/p1 Old or weathered, without a fixed duration in ordinary context. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya says long-lasting; al-Zamakhsharī explains worn, changed, thin, bent and yellow; al-Jishumī relates qadīm, awwal and sābiq to precedence and contrasts ḥadīth. Abū Ḥayyān and Bursawī say custom determines age rather than an exact year; al-Rāzī likewise rejects a necessary year. Al-Ṭabarī permits 'dry.' Al-Ālūsī says time has dried the stalk; al-Bayḍāwī offers simply 'aged.' [12 notes: JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الزمخشري,ثعلب); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî,Taberî)]
- 36:39/q15/p2 A year old or more. — Muqātil describes a year-old stalk; Ibn Qutayba gives the same explanation; al-Bayḍāwī, Bursawī and al-Ālūsī report it as an alternative. Kuran Yolu translates 'years old' on this usage. [7 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة); BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 36:39/q15/p3 Six months can count as qadīm. — Al-Ṭabrisī reports this for a drying cluster; an Imāmī report from al-Riḍā applies it to owned slaves. [2 notes: TABRISI(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(أبو الحسن الرضا)]

## 36:39/q16 Can qadīm linguistically name anything besides God?  [ٱلْقَدِيمِ · theology]
turns on: The difference between theologians' technical designation and ordinary linguistic use.
- 36:39/q16/p1 In theologians' convention it is reserved for God; Abū ʿAlī extends the restriction to linguistic usage. — Al-Jishumī distinguishes technical naming from the disputed ordinary language and reports Abū ʿAlī's stricter view. [2 notes: JISHUMI(أبو علي)]
- 36:39/q16/p2 Ordinary language can call something besides God qadīm. — Al-Jishumī reports Abū Hāshim's position; the verse itself calls the stalk old in ordinary speech. [2 notes: JISHUMI(أبو هاشم)]

## 36:39/q17 What does an old-slave manumission include?  [ٱلْقَدِيمِ · law]
turns on: Which period of ownership makes 'old' apply in the legal declaration.
- 36:39/q17/p1 A slave owned for at least a year. — Al-Qurṭubī applies this to a will or declaration; reports from al-Ḥasan ibn al-Walīd say Yaʿqūb ruled so and cited the old date cluster; Bursawī gives the oath example. [4 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; DURR-FULL(الحسن بن الوليد); BURSEVI]
- 36:39/q17/p2 A slave owned for at least six months. — Al-Ṭabrisī relates al-Riḍā's judgment in a deathbed declaration; the disputation with the Wāqifī Abū Saʿīd al-Makkārī includes an analogy to Jesus and Mary, and reports Abū Saʿīd's later blindness and begging. Al-Ālūsī notes Imāmī transmission. [3 notes: TABRISI(أبو الحسن الرضا); ALUSI-FULL(أبو الحسن الرضا)]

## 36:39/q18 What guidance do the lunar stations give for reckoning time?  [قَدَّرْنَٰهُ مَنَازِلَ · interpretation]
- 36:39/q18/p1 Their phases mark months, years, calendars and ritual dates. — Ibn Kathīr links the stations with crescent dates for people and pilgrimage (2:189) and reckoning years (10:5); the sun marks day and night, has another light and seasonal course (17:12). Bursawī says the Muslim community reckons by lunar months rather than solar days. Kuran Yolu notes sun and moon's shared timekeeping role. Al-Qurṭubī directs readers to al-Baqara for lunar-crescent rulings. [6 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 36:39/q19 What does the verse establish about God and worship?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The general lesson from ordered signs and the pointed force of the waning-moon comparison.
- 36:39/q19/p1 The ordered heavenly signs display God's power, sole divinity and a call to gratitude. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya sees proof of divine power and exclusive divinity; al-Qurṭubī invites contemplation. Iṣlāḥī says gratitude necessarily takes monotheistic form and implies reward and punishment; the moon cannot govern itself. Kuran Yolu notes such visible bodies have been deified. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 36:39/q19/p2 Its return to a crooked dry twig rebukes worship of the moon. — Iṣlāḥī says an object that repeatedly wanes to this state cannot be worthy of worship. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 36:39/q20 How does this verse fit the adjoining signs?  [* · links]
- 36:39/q20/p1 The moon follows the night and sun as a corresponding sign of the night, distinct in light and course. — Al-Biqāʿī explicitly pairs it with the daytime sign; al-Farrāʾ treats it as a third sign after night and sun; Ibn Kathīr contrasts the sun's seasonal rising, setting and daytime rule with the moon. A reported statement from Abū Jaʿfar says God created sun before moon and light before darkness. [4 notes: BIQAI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء); IBNKATHIR-FULL; TABATABAI(أبو جعفر)]

## 36:39/q21 How does the passage display eloquence?  [* · rhetoric]
- 36:39/q21/p1 Each Qur'anic word is eloquent, and their composition adds beauty and instruction beyond human speech. — Al-Bāqillānī applies this to the adjacent verses 36:37–39. [1 notes: BAQILLANI]

## 36:39/q22 What do rain attributions to mansions imply?  [مَنَازِلَ · theology]
turns on: Whether the speaker assigns independent agency to stars or speaks of a cause under God's decree.
- 36:39/q22/p1 Believing the star itself sends rain is disbelief or apostasy. — Al-Shāfiʿī and others say an unrepentant speaker may be executed; al-Ālūsī cites al-Rawḍa for apostasy when true agency is assigned to a mansion. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الشافعي وغيره)]
- 36:39/q22/p2 Calling a mansion a divinely decreed cause is permitted, though some call the phrase ingratitude. — Al-Ālūsī calls permissibility the sound view when God remains the true agent; Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr regards even that phrase as ingratitude and ignorance of divine wisdom. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(ابن عبد البر)]

## 36:39/q23 What reports are cited about lunar months in Ramadan and the festivals?  [* · reports]
turns on: The two different reports concern historical Ramadan lengths and the merit of a short festival month.
- 36:39/q23/p1 The Prophet fasted eight or nine Ramadans, five of twenty-nine days and the rest of thirty. — Bursawī cites the count while discussing lunar months. [1 notes: BURSEVI]
- 36:39/q23/p2 The two festival months do not fall short in merit when one has twenty-nine days. — Bursawī explains an authenticated report this way. [1 notes: BURSEVI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 36:39/q24 What inward meaning is given to the moon and its stations?  [* · inward]
turns on: Whether the lunar course maps the heart's ordered stations or a seeker's changing states.
- 36:39/q24/p1 The moon is the heart passing through spiritual stations toward absorption in spirit and God. — Al-Qāshānī lists fear, hope, patience, gratitude, trust and contentment; the old stalk marks the heart approaching the secret, face lit toward spirit before annihilation, while full luminosity veils soul and faculties. Bursawī's Taʾwīlāt Najmiyya equates twenty-eight stations with Qur'anic letters: first fourteen intimacy, righteousness, repentance, steadfastness, communion, forbearance, sincerity, devotion, humility, compassion, nearness, safety, yearning and truthfulness; remaining harm, quest, thirst, love, jealousy, chivalry, proximity, generosity, gentleness, nobility, light, sainthood, guidance and certainty. Its course begins in intimacy and ends with Qur'anic character, God's bond and certainty (15:99); a report 'Read and ascend' depicts higher nearness, and another makes its disappearance true poverty. The Night Journey (53:9; 93:8) illustrates the Prophet's needy approach and enrichment; the saying 'Poverty is my pride' is cited. [11 notes: QASHANI; BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 36:39/q24/p2 The moon is the servant of changing spiritual states, contracting and expanding until refined. — Bursawī contrasts the sun as a servant constantly illuminated in knowledge of God; the moon-seeker rises toward union, lapses and is restored. A couplet describes repeated descents through bewildering stations of love. [3 notes: BURSEVI]
