# 19:47: 16 questions

## 19:47/q01 What does Abraham's “peace be upon you” mean here?  [سَلَٰمٌ عَلَيْكَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether salām functions as assurance, farewell, formal greeting, forbearance, or a prayer; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī contests a farewell because departure came later.
- 19:47/q01/p1 He assures his father of safety from harm or insult at his hands. — Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, Ibn al-Jawzī, and al-Qurṭubī say Abraham had no command to fight his father for unbelief. Al-Biqāʿī qualifies the assurance by any contrary divine command. Ibn Kathīr grounds it in filial respect. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī links the assurance to the threat, the promised prayer, and his later withdrawal; Study Quran says it ends confrontation without fighting. Al-Bursawī and al-Ālūsī include refraining from hurtful speech. [14 notes: THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL(الجمهور); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(Quran commentators); IBNATIYYA-FULL+]
- 19:47/q01/p2 It is a peaceful farewell and separation from his father. — Ibn Baḥr says he leaves because of unbelief. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Bursawī, and al-Ālūsī compare 28:55 and 25:63. Al-Bayḍāwī calls it kindness in reply to injury; al-Nasafī allows conciliatory parting. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the farewell comes first to show no resentment at leaving for God. Iṣlāḥī says salām serves as an affectionate farewell formula. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī objects that actual departure occurred later. [17 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(ابن بحر); BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(الجبائي وأبو مسلم); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الجمهور); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNATIYYA-FULL+; TABATABAI-]
- 19:47/q01/p3 It is a courteous greeting that answers his father's harshness with filial kindness. — Al-Māwardī stresses his father's status and past upbringing, and prefers this to a mere farewell. Al-Ṭabrisī says Abraham leaves without disobedience. Abū Ḥayyān calls it filial duty; Ibn ʿĀshūr describes kindness at their last encounter. Iṣlāḥī notes salām is also a blessed greeting on meeting. [6 notes: TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; TABATABAI; MAWARDI-FULL+]
- 19:47/q01/p4 It is a forbearing reply to ignorant or abusive speech. — Al-Suddī calls it a good response and al-Ḥasan a word of forbearance. Abū Bakr ibn Ṭāhir compares 25:63 after the father's idolatrous invitation and threat; al-Naqqāsh calls Abraham the forbearing man addressing a fool. Al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, and Study Quran also cite the peaceful reply to ignorance; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī adds 25:72. [11 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(الحسن,السدي); SULAMI(أبو بكر بن طاهر); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); IBNATIYYA-FULL(النقاش); QURTUBI-FULL(النقاش); TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN; ALUSI-FULL(أبو بكر بن طاهر)]
- 19:47/q01/p5 He prays for his father's safety to win him over. — Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī infer conciliatory prayer from the immediately following promise to seek forgiveness. [2 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL]
- 19:47/q01/p6 In an inward reading, peace asks God to remove the father's material veils. — Al-Qāshānī says his father's essence lies hidden behind material veils. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 19:47/q02 What does salām mean in Arabic usage?  [سَلَٰمٌ · meaning]
turns on: These are distinct Arabic uses of the word; the notes apply safety to this verse.
- 19:47/q02/p1 Safety or release from harm, including a prayer for safety. — Al-Zajjāj lists the verbal noun of salima and a plural of salāmah; as a prayer it seeks safety in religion, person, and property. He explains reconciliation as safety from evil, stones called salām for hardness, and a ladder carrying one to safety or a desired place. Ibn ʿĀshūr takes it as safety, with ʿalā picturing its establishment, and compares 19:15. [4 notes: IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(الزجاج); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 19:47/q02/p2 As a divine name, al-Salām names the possessor of peace and safety. — Al-Zajjāj says God owns safety understood as release from harm. [2 notes: IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(الزجاج)]
- 19:47/q02/p3 Salām also names a large, strong tree. — Al-Zajjāj lists the tree; Ibn al-Jawzī suggests its name reflects freedom from afflictions. [2 notes: IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(الزجاج)]
- 19:47/q02/p4 Salām can mean goodness. — Some interpreters give this fourth sense; Ibn Qutayba applies it to the salām of the Night of Decree. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(بعض المفسرين)]

## 19:47/q03 How is salāmun ʿalayka parsed and how do analogous nominal formulas behave?  [سَلَٰمٌ عَلَيْكَ · grammar]
turns on: The case and meaning depend on whether the expression is a nominal invocation, an accusative verbal noun, a wish, or a rhetorical prompt.
- 19:47/q03/p1 The nominative salāmun begins a nominal sentence or invocation. — Al-Naḥḥās permits an indefinite subject because the expression conveys greeting and parting, like the greeting of the ignorant. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says it is specified and can convey the sense of an accusative 'I greet you peacefully,' citing Sibawayh's fierce beast example. Al-Qurṭubī says its restriction and definite complement license the indefinite subject. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī parse it as a nominal sentence or noun. Sibawayh says adding lā to an already governed invocation noun preserves its nominal form; lā salām ʿalayka and Jarīr's lā salām ʿalā ʿAmr parallel the verbal prayer lā sallama Allāhu ʿalayhi. He supports the nominal pattern by the corresponding verbal construction and notes that no imperative lā iḍrib is available. [8 notes: SIBAWAYH; NAHHAS; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 19:47/q03/p2 An accusative salāman is an absolute verbal noun expressing a prayer for safety. — Abū Ḥayyān treats it as a verbal noun and conciliatory address; al-Ālūsī calls it an absolute verbal noun. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says even nominative salāmun carries the sense of an accusative peaceful greeting. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 19:47/q03/p3 Related nominal invocation and lā constructions retain or change case according to their particular function. — Sibawayh says lā bika al-sūʾ prays 'may God not harm you'; lā enters wishing and welcome formulas as in verbal equivalents. Lā sawāʾ and lāhā Allāhi dhā replace a prior predicate; lā nawlaka substitutes for lā yanbaghī; bi-lā dhanb means 'without'; lā shayʾ diminishes a matter. In ḥīna lā ḥīn, lā is null; lā barāḥ and lā mustaṣrikh are commonly accusative but may be nominative. A bare predicate lā fāris is awkward without a contrast, although a Banū Sulūl poem attests bare lā nafʿ. Lā governs questions as statements, illustrated by Ḥassān. With wish meaning, interrogative alif plus lā governs accusative without nunation; the form is not nominative because it neither answers a question nor means laysa. In alā māʾan wa-labanan bāridan ḥulwan each adjective qualifies a separate noun, and alā ghulāman afḍala minka is an accusative wish for a boy without a predicate. [14 notes: SIBAWAYH]
- 19:47/q03/p4 Alā rajulan can be a rhetorical prompt rather than a wish. — Al-Khalīl paraphrases it as 'Do you not show me a man whom God rewarded well?' Sibawayh presents this reading. [1 notes: SIBAWAYH(الخليل)]
- 19:47/q03/p5 The nunation in alā rajulan can be poetic necessity. — Yūnus supplies this alternative; Sibawayh calls it possible while presenting al-Khalīl's prompt reading for others. [1 notes: SIBAWAYH(يونس)]

## 19:47/q05 May one initiate salām to a non-Muslim, and what does this verse imply?  [سَلَٰمٌ عَلَيْكَ · law]
turns on: Whether Abraham's salām is a prescribed greeting or only peaceful disengagement, and how reports forbidding first greetings relate to need, mixed company, and 60:8.
- 19:47/q05/p1 One may initiate such a greeting, at least to a non-hostile unbeliever. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Qurṭubī report this from those who take Abraham's words as a real greeting. Sufyān ibn ʿUyayna cites 60:8 and 60:4; al-Qurṭubī prefers his reading. Abū Usāma greeted all passersby because Muslims are told to spread salām. Al-Awzāʿī says greeting and withholding it both have righteous precedent. The permissive report contrasts with a prohibitive report, which al-Ṭabarī treats as general and specific. Al-Ālūsī reports the view but rejects its proofs. [9 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(سفيان بن عيينة); IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو أسامة,الأوزاعي,سفيان بن عيينة); ALUSI-FULL(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL+; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 19:47/q05/p2 An initial greeting is prohibited without a qualifying reason. — The stricter report says greeting honors unbelievers; Anas calls salām the greeting of Paradise. The majority reading makes Abraham's words peaceful disengagement, not a salutation. Abū Hurayra's sound report forbids first greeting Jews and Christians and directs road precedence. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī say this report outweighs the verse as a prooftext for permission. Al-Qurṭubī confines prohibition to cases with no need or social right. [9 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(أبو هريرة,الجمهور); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL+; QURTUBI-FULL-]
- 19:47/q05/p3 An initial greeting is permitted for need, companionship, neighborly right, travel, or a mixed gathering. — Al-Nakhaʿī allows it for a need involving a Jew or Christian. Al-Qurṭubī lists need, companionship, neighborhood, and travel; Ibn Masʿūd greeted a Persian travel companion because companionship has a right. Al-Ḥasan permits greeting a mixed gathering, and the report of Usāma says the Prophet greeted a mixed gathering before Badr. [5 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(أسامة بن زيد,ابن مسعود,الحسن البصري,النخعي)]
- 19:47/q05/p4 Greeting a non-Muslim is permitted in wartime but not in peace. — Al-Wāḥidī reports Sufyān ibn ʿUyayna's formulation with 60:8 and 60:4; he says Abraham's example excludes asking forgiveness, not greeting. This wording differs from the non-hostile formulation reported by al-Qurṭubī. [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(سفيان بن عيينة)]

## 19:47/q06 What conduct does Abraham's reply teach toward a harsh or obstinate person?  [سَلَٰمٌ عَلَيْكَ · interpretation]
turns on: The notes give an ethical reading and a mystical application.
- 19:47/q06/p1 Answer abuse gently while fulfilling filial duty, then leave an unreceptive person peacefully. — Al-Qushayrī says Abraham replied from settled insight while retaining hope after a threat. Al-Rāzī and al-Bursawī infer departure after fruitless advice and kindness toward abuse. Abū Ḥayyān and Ibn ʿĀshūr stress filial duty and forbearance. Ibn Ṭāhir says 25:63 supports shunning wicked people for religion; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls peaceful reply generous conduct. Iṣlāḥī says the promise shows tenderness and love despite callousness. [11 notes: QUSHAYRI; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(أبو بكر بن طاهر); TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 19:47/q06/p2 In an inward reading, the peace marks turning from worldly others while remaining gentle to the ignorant. — Al-Ālūsī records this mystical application. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 19:47/q07 What does Abraham promise by 'I shall ask forgiveness for you'?  [سَأَسْتَغْفِرُ لَكَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether forgiveness is sought through future faith, under an unstated condition, in hope before prohibition or final enmity is known, or only as relief from worldly punishment; 9:114, 60:4, and 26:86 are central.
- 19:47/q07/p1 He will ask God to guide his father to faith and repentance, through which forgiveness becomes possible. — Al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ibn Kathīr, al-Biqāʿī, al-Bursawī, al-Ālūsī, and Ibn ʿĀshūr make guidance a prerequisite. Ibn ʿAṭiyya prefers it to supposing forgiveness of persistent unbelief. Al-Zamakhsharī and Abū Ḥayyān compare commands to unbelievers that presuppose faith and prerequisites; 26:86 and 9:114 support the explanation. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says he hopes his father will receive mercy and believe. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls this the fairest restricted explanation but somewhat remote from the appeal's tenderness. [19 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن الأنباري); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; IBNATIYYA-FULL+; TABATABAI+; ALUSI-FULL-; TABATABAI-]
- 19:47/q07/p2 He hopes to pray for forgiveness directly before a prohibition or his father's final unbelief is known. — Al-Wāḥidī, Ibn al-Jawzī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Ṭabrisī, and al-Biqāʿī say the ban or the father's enmity was not yet known. Ibn ʿAṭiyya allows that Abraham first received the later revelation, through death or disclosure. Al-Zamakhsharī says reason alone did not forbid the prayer; 60:4's exception would be pointless for a merely conditional promise. Al-Rāzī offers uncertainty of punishment under Abraham's law and possible permission while faith was hoped for. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī treats the promise as hopeful intercession, not a guarantee, and notes that his father might have been ignorantly receptive under 4:98–99. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 9:114 and 26:86. [19 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(الجبائي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن الأنباري); RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية,الزمخشري، author); ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري); TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; TABATABAI-]
- 19:47/q07/p3 The promise is conditional on his father abandoning idols and believing. — Al-Māwardī, al-Ṭabrisī, al-Rāzī, and al-Ālūsī report this condition. Al-Rāzī reads 9:114 as the promise ceasing when the father did not believe. Al-Zamakhsharī objects that 60:4 would not except such a lawful conditional promise; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says no such restriction is expressed. [6 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(الجبائي); RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري); KASHSHAF-FULL-; TABATABAI-]
- 19:47/q07/p4 He prayed because his father had promised to believe. — Al-Zamakhsharī reports this account with 26:86 and 9:114, and al-Ālūsī repeats it. Al-Zamakhsharī and Abū Ḥayyān counter that 9:114 makes Abraham the promiser; Ḥammād's reading waʿadahā abāhu supports this. Al-Ālūsī reports a variant phrased 'his father' rather than 'to him.' [2 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري); KASHSHAF-FULL(حماد الراوية)-; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري، author)-]
- 19:47/q07/p5 He asks God to avert his father's punishment in this world. — Al-Aṣamm supplies this interpretation; al-Rāzī compares 45:14. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī objects that the verse states no restriction to worldly punishment. [2 notes: TABRISI(الأصم); RAZI-FULL; TABATABAI-]
- 19:47/q07/p6 In an inward reading, he asks God to veil his father's essence in light and erase selfish dispositions. — Al-Qāshānī makes this dependent on its being possible. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 19:47/q11 What does ḥafiyyan mean of God's relation to Abraham?  [حَفِيًّۭا · meaning]
turns on: The root is explained through gracious care, regular answered prayer, attentive knowledge, or earnest inquiry; several sources combine these senses.
- 19:47/q11/p1 Exceptionally kind, gentle, merciful, gracious, or attentive to Abraham. — Al-Majaz, al-Ṭabarī, al-Zajjāj, al-Wāḥidī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, and others derive this from treating someone gently; al-Kisāʾī gives ḥafāwah and ḥafwah as dialectal forms, and al-Rāzī compares 47:37. Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Zayd, al-Kalbī, Muqātil, and others are reported with kind, gentle, or merciful glosses. Al-Nasafī adds honoring; al-Rāghib says the care meets fine needs successively. Al-Ṭabrisī contrasts khafiyy as gently extending favor with ḥafiyy as probing. Ibn Kathīr reports Ibn ʿAbbās taking this kindness as guidance to sincere worship. Al-Naḥḥās combines Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Kisāʾī, al-Farrāʾ, and Abū Isḥāq's glosses. [39 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(الكلبي,بعضهم); FARRA; MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(ابن زيد,ابن عباس); ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS; THALABI(ابن عباس ومقاتل); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس ومقاتل); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(ابن عباس ومقاتل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن أبي طلحة عن ابن عباس,الضحاك عن ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس، الكلبي، القتبي، السدي); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس); BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI(الراغب); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 19:47/q11/p2 God regularly answers Abraham's prayers and has accustomed him to such answers. — Al-Ḥasan contrasts accepting prayer with rejecting it; al-Farrāʾ says God answers whenever Abraham prays. Ibn Qutayba, al-Ṭabarī, Mujāhid, Qatāda, al-Kalbī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Rāzī, and others mention habitual response. Al-Rāzī says this gives Abraham confidence that repentance would bring forgiveness; al-Biqāʿī says it explains his promised prayer. [15 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(الحسن); FARRA; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL; NAHHAS; THALABI(الكلبي,مجاهد); BAGHAWI-FULL(الكلبي,مجاهد); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); IBNKATHIR-FULL(مجاهد، قتادة); BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(مجاهد)]
- 19:47/q11/p3 God knows Abraham and his hopes, and attends closely to his affairs. — Al-Farrāʾ combines knowledge with answering prayer. Al-Kalbī and the ʿAṭāʾ report from Ibn ʿAbbās gloss it as knowing; Jābir calls God a helper. Al-Ṭabrisī says God knows Abraham's effort to guide his father. Al-Suddī says God cares for his affair; al-Rāghib emphasizes attention to fine needs. [11 notes: FARRA; NAHHAS; THALABI(الكلبي); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي وابن عباس، رواية عطاء); BAGHAWI-FULL(الكلبي); TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس، الكلبي، القتبي، السدي); IBNKATHIR-FULL(السدي); TABATABAI(الراغب)]
- 19:47/q11/p4 Ḥafiyy can signify earnest, thorough, insistent investigation or supplication. — Al-Jishumī derives assiduousness from lavish honoring and intense questioning. Al-Ṭabrisī says ḥafiyy investigates insistently. Ibn ʿAṭiyya adds earnest supplication to gentleness. [3 notes: JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI]

## 19:47/q14 When and how did Abraham's prayer for his father end, and how does this relate to other verses?  [سَأَسْتَغْفِرُ لَكَ · links]
turns on: The notes distinguish Abraham's continued hopeful prayer from the later knowledge that ended it; the dawn-delay report is rejected as transferred from another story.
- 19:47/q14/p1 He prayed while hoping for faith, then stopped and disavowed his father when enmity or final unbelief became clear. — Yaḥyā ibn Sallām cites 9:114 as the promised prayer. Al-Wāḥidī, al-Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Bursawī, al-Ālūsī, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, Iṣlāḥī, Kuran Yolu, and Study Quran connect the later cutoff to 9:113–114 and 60:4. Ibn Kathīr says Abraham continued after migration, building the sanctuary, and having children, citing 14:41; early Muslims followed his example until the exception and ban were revealed. Al-Ṭībī reads 60:1, 60:3, and 60:4 as urging estrangement from hostile unbelieving relatives, with Abraham's compassionate promise excepted because their unbelief was already known. Al-Rāzī says Abraham's concern remained even as he left. [14 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; WAHIDI-BASIT; RAZI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الطيبي); TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 19:47/q14/p2 One report says he delayed the prayer until dawn. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya rejects Makkī's report from al-Suddī as strained and says the dawn delay belongs to Jacob and his sons. [0 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL-]

## 19:47/q15 Was asking forgiveness for his unbelieving father permissible, and why?  [سَأَسْتَغْفِرُ لَكَ · theology]
turns on: The dispute concerns whether reason or only revelation bars the prayer, whether hope of future faith remains, and what the exception to Abraham's example in 60:4 entails.
- 19:47/q15/p1 It was permissible before revelation prohibited it; reason alone did not forbid it. — Al-Zamakhsharī cites 60:4: if merely conditioned on faith, the promise need not be excepted. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī report his argument. Al-Ālūsī says Abraham's action was not inherently wrong, though later believers are forbidden by 9:113; he reports disagreement over whether initial permission came from reason, as Muʿtazila held, or revelation, as Sunnis argued from kindness to parents and compassion for those invited to faith. Al-Ālūsī distinguishes reason's possible permission for a dead unbeliever from revealed prohibition. Al-Jishumī reports rational permission or permission conditional on later belief. [10 notes: JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري، author); ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري,الكشف,قيل); TABATABAI-]
- 19:47/q15/p2 Prayer for a living unbeliever is permissible while faith remains possible, but forgiveness in persistent unbelief or after a known unbelieving death is forbidden. — Al-Bursawī and al-Ālūsī distinguish guidance for someone of unknown fate from prayer once final disbelief is known, citing 9:113–114. Al-Ālūsī says forgiveness during continuing unbelief trivializes faith's supreme obligation, a rationale that might apply to other sins unless unbelief differs. He says reason alone does not bar prayer for one already dead in unbelief, but revelation does. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites 60:8 on kindness to non-hostile people and describes the father's possible vulnerability under 4:98–99. [7 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 19:47/q15/p3 The prayer was sinful because the father was an unbeliever and revelation forbids seeking forgiveness for unbelievers. — Al-Rāzī reports critics of prophetic impeccability citing 26:86, 9:113, 60:4, and consensus on the father's unbelief. His replies include uncertain legal knowledge, possible worldly relief, and hope for future faith; he also says an excluded act need not be sinful. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(من طعن في عصمة الأنبياء); RAZI-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 19:47/q15/p4 The prayer might have been permissible yet less fitting for a prophet. — Al-Rāzī gives this fourth reply: good deeds of the righteous may be shortcomings for those nearest God. He also says 60:4's exclusion can remove imitation without proving sin, because prophets have lawful acts peculiar to them. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الكشف)]
- 19:47/q15/p5 The exception in 60:4 shows Abraham's prayer should not be imitated. — Al-Zamakhsharī takes exception from the required example as evidence against extending Abraham's promise. Al-Ālūsī says the act is called objectionable and excluded from the good model, and a duty to follow the model is stated elsewhere. Al-Kashf records al-Rāzī's objection and replies that the act was lawful for Abraham but barred for later believers. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري,الكشف); RAZI-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL(صاحب التقريب)-]
- 19:47/q15/p6 Exclusion from the example removes an obligation to imitate but does not itself establish impermissibility. — Al-Rāzī notes prophet-specific lawful acts. The author of al-Taqrīb adds that 60:6 does not itself impose a duty to imitate; al-Ālūsī answers that the prayer is excluded as objectionable and that the duty is stated elsewhere. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(صاحب التقريب); ALUSI-FULL-]
- 19:47/q15/p7 A pre-prohibition prayer for Abu Talib is reconciled by chronology or by a restricted understanding of unforgivable shirk. — Al-Ālūsī raises the objection that the Prophet's prayer for Abu Talib would conflict with an earlier prohibition and replies he might not yet have received it. One report places 9:113 after Abu Talib's unbelieving death and 4:48 later; another says the Prophet understood unforgivable shirk as lacking intercession or involving every limb and thought Abu Talib's case differed until prohibited. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
