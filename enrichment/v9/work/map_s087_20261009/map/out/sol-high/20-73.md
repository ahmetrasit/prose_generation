# 20:73: 25 questions

## 20:73/q01 What does their profession of faith affirm?  [إِنَّآ ءَامَنَّا بِرَبِّنَا · interpretation]
- 20:73/q01/p1 They affirm and trust in their Lord, including His oneness, promise, and warning. — Muqatil specifies divine oneness; al-Tabari adds belief in God's promise and threat. Al-Biqa'i says they trust the Lord who cared for them even during disbelief, without supposing their faith benefits or harms Him. Tabataba'i says calling Him their Lord fits faith and worship because lordship entails ownership and governance. [4 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 20:73/q02 Why do they declare faith in order to seek forgiveness?  [ءَامَنَّا بِرَبِّنَا لِيَغْفِرَ لَنَا · interpretation]
- 20:73/q02/p1 They choose faith and forgiveness for the afterlife over safety, favor, or longer life under Pharaoh. — Bursevi and al-Alusi contrast pardon and rescue from afterlife punishment with fleeting worldly life or reward; al-Alusi says this lessens fear of Pharaoh. Tabataba'i says their request explains refusal to prefer Pharaoh: they choose and believe in the God who created them. Al-Biqa'i stresses their trust despite their earlier disbelief. [4 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; BIQAI-FULL]

## 20:73/q03 What does forgiveness mean here?  [لِيَغْفِرَ · meaning]
turns on: The inward reading applies the image of covering to spiritual traits; the other notes describe pardon of sins and punishment.
- 20:73/q03/p1 God conceals or overlooks sins and shields the servant from their deserved punishment. — Al-Tabari says forgive and conceal. Bursevi derives maghfira from covering something against impurity and explains it as protection from deserved punishment through overlooking sins. [2 notes: TAB-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 20:73/q03/p2 Inwardly, forgiveness covers dark traits from worldly and natural attachments with divine light. — Al-Qashani names the dark forms and blameworthy traits arising from attachment to natural pleasures and worldly adornment. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 20:73/q04 What are their خطايانا?  [خَطَٰيَٰنَا · meaning]
turns on: Some notes identify a particular former sin, while others give the general scope of خطايا; the details can overlap.
- 20:73/q04/p1 Their sins or misdeeds generally, including disbelief and other disobedience. — Al-Jishumi calls them sins and disobedient acts; al-Tabrisi and al-Baydawi include association or disbelief and other disobedience. Tabataba'i identifies khataya as the plural of khati'a, close to sayyi'a, a misdeed. Bursevi and al-Alusi also mention disbelief and other sins. [6 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; BAYDAWI-FULL; TABATABAI; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 20:73/q04/p2 Their former association of partners with God is the sin specified. — Al-Wahidi reports Ibn Abbas's identification of idolatry and repeats it in al-Wajiz; Ibn al-Jawzi likewise names their former association. [3 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNJAWZI-ZAD]
- 20:73/q04/p3 Their sins include their magic, especially the magic Pharaoh forced them to perform. — Muqatil explicitly includes coerced magic among their errors; Ibn Kathir says they especially seek pardon for using magic against God's sign and the prophet's miracle. Bursevi and al-Alusi say the singled-out magic was already within the sins. [5 notes: MUQATIL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 20:73/q05 What did Pharaoh force the magicians to do with sorcery?  [وَمَآ أَكْرَهْتَنَا عَلَيْهِ مِنَ ٱلسِّحْرِ · interpretation]
turns on: The reports locate coercion at training, at the summons or contest, at both stages, or at removal from home; several accounts allow more than one stage.
- 20:73/q05/p1 He forced them to learn sorcery, sometimes by making older practitioners teach children. — Al-Farra and al-Zajjaj cite this tafsir report; al-Tabari says Pharaoh ordered learning, with Ibn Zayd adding that they left God's Book and had their people teach it. Al-Wahidi gives learning as one transmitted account. Al-Hasan in al-Baghawi says this kept the art from dying out, although the sorcerers came voluntarily to the present contest. Ibn al-Jawzi transmits Ibn Abbas through Ibn al-Anbari that subjects had to teach children. Al-Jishumi and al-Tabrisi explain a royal custom of requiring elders to teach youths, retaining royal control of the craft; al-Alusi reports al-Hasan's account of seizing children. [13 notes: FARRA(التفسير); TAB-FULL(ابن زيد); ZAJJAJ(الراوي); WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(الحسن); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(الحسن,المهدوي); ALUSI-FULL(الحسن)]
- 20:73/q05/p2 He forced them to confront Moses or perform magic against him, including summoning them from their districts. — Al-Wahidi preserves this as the second transmitted explanation; Abu Muslim in al-Jishumi says they were gathered from towns and regions. Ibn Atiyya, al-Baydawi, al-Qashani, Ibn Kathir, al-Biqa'i, Bursevi, al-Alusi, Ibn Ashur and Asad describe a forced contest or performance. Al-Hasan as reported by Razi says assembly, and perhaps the performance too, was compelled. Ibn al-Jawzi reports that recognition of Moses' piety or fear of losing face before the crowd and kings led them to resist, but Pharaoh insisted; al-Jishumi likewise reports resistance after the staff's nonmagical sign. Tabataba'i locates possible compulsion in the summons or in the dispute and whispering before the contest. [16 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI(أبو مسلم); IBNATIYYA-FULL(فرقة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(الحسن); BAYDAWI-FULL; QASHANI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(السحرة); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES]
- 20:73/q05/p3 His coercion covered both learning magic and practicing it. — Al-Tabari says they learned and practiced under compulsion. Al-Jishumi explicitly names learning and practice; Ibn Atiyya reports taking children for training and later forced practice. Razi transmits Ibn Abbas that some had to learn and older practitioners had to teach; al-Mahdawi allows childhood training even while noting coercion is not sinful. [5 notes: TAB-FULL; JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL(فرقة); RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(المهدوي)]
- 20:73/q05/p4 He forced them out of their homelands because of the magic. — Ibn al-Jawzi gives this as Ibn al-Anbari's fourth explanation. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن الأنباري)]

## 20:73/q06 How can coercion fit their apparent voluntary participation, and what counts as coercion?  [أَكْرَهْتَنَا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether coercion occurred earlier in training or at the contest, and whether expected harm must accompany a ruler's summons.
- 20:73/q06/p1 They were compelled as children to learn, even if they later practiced willingly or came voluntarily to the contest. — Al-Hasan in al-Baghawi distinguishes early forced learning from their voluntary arrival; al-Hasan in al-Qurtubi says they later practiced voluntarily. Al-Mahdawi allows childhood coercion, and notes that compulsion itself is no sin. [3 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(الحسن); QURTUBI-FULL(الحسن,المهدوي)]
- 20:73/q06/p2 A tyrant ruler's command already amounts to coercion because disobedience brings expected harm. — Al-Alusi reports Abu Ubayda and Hanafi authorities on implicit coercion even without a spoken threat. Amr ibn Ubayd similarly calls a ruler's summons coercion, but Razi objects that a summons without fear does not suffice. Al-Alusi says their oath by Pharaoh's might and their request for reward may have preceded compulsion or been bravado, citing 26:44 and 7:113. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أبو عبيد وساداتنا الحنفية); RAZI-FULL(عمرو بن عبيد)-]
- 20:73/q06/p3 A mere summons without fear is not coercion. — Razi rejects Amr ibn Ubayd's inference from the ruler's summons alone; he requires fear for compulsion. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(عمرو بن عبيد)+]

## 20:73/q07 Why ask forgiveness for magic said to have been coerced?  [لِيَغْفِرَ لَنَا خَطَٰيَٰنَا وَمَآ أَكْرَهْتَنَا عَلَيْهِ مِنَ ٱلسِّحْرِ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether their plea concerns culpable resistance or use of magic, emphatic repudiation of a past sin, or acts excused by actual coercion.
- 20:73/q07/p1 They single out an already included sin to show utter rejection of the magic and desire for pardon; coercion also pleads for mercy. — Bursevi and al-Alusi say the explicit addition conveys thorough repulsion from their old craft; al-Alusi adds that mention of coercion supplies an excuse and seeks mercy. [2 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 20:73/q07/p2 They should have resisted the forced challenge, and using magic against God's sign was sinful even under pressure. — Al-Biqa'i says God deserved greater reverence than Pharaoh, so resistance was more fitting. Ibn Ashur says they now recognize that trying to disprove God's divinity with magic was false and sinful; Ibn Kathir specifies opposition to God's sign and His prophet's miracle. [3 notes: BIQAI-FULL(السحرة); IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 20:73/q07/p3 Coercion itself is not a sin, even if they were forced to learn magic as children. — Al-Mahdawi makes this qualification in al-Qurtubi. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(المهدوي)]

## 20:73/q08 What do reports say about the identity and number of Pharaoh's trained magicians?  [أَكْرَهْتَنَا عَلَيْهِ مِنَ ٱلسِّحْرِ · reports]
turns on: The transmitted number differs: seventy-two leaders, with disagreement about their ethnic composition, or forty trained Israelite boys; some versions name no number.
- 20:73/q08/p1 Seventy-two leading magicians included two Copts and seventy Israelites. — Al-Jishumi reports Muqatil's seventy-two Israelites, while al-Baghawi, Razi, al-Biqa'i and al-Alusi specify two Copts and seventy Israelites; the transmitted descriptions thus vary on whether all seventy-two were Israelites. [5 notes: JISHUMI(مقاتل); BAGHAWI-FULL(مقاتل); RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(رُوي); ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
- 20:73/q08/p2 Forty Israelite boys were taken and trained in magic, later joining those who believed in Moses. — Ibn Kathir transmits this from Ibn Abbas, places their training at al-Farama, and gives Abd al-Rahman ibn Zayd ibn Aslam as corroboration; al-Durr and al-Alusi add that Pharaoh wanted them unbeatable. Al-Tabari's Ibn Abbas report mentions boys taught at al-Farama without a number; al-Jishumi and Ibn al-Jawzi transmit versions of boys being handed over or ordered taught. [7 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); JISHUMI(ابن عباس); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس,عبد الرحمن بن زيد بن أسلم); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس)]

## 20:73/q09 What did the magicians observe of Moses before the contest?  [أَكْرَهْتَنَا عَلَيْهِ مِنَ ٱلسِّحْرِ · reports]
turns on: The accounts give different signs by which the magicians judged Moses before Pharaoh insisted on the challenge.
- 20:73/q09/p1 They saw Moses' staff guard him while he slept, recognized that this could not be magic, but Pharaoh still forced the contest. — Abd al-Aziz ibn Aban in al-Jishumi and al-Tabrisi says a magician's spell ceases during sleep. Al-Baghawi says Pharaoh nevertheless forced them to learn; Razi combines the scene with the seventy-two leaders; al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, al-Biqa'i and al-Alusi tell variants, with al-Nasafi adding that Pharaoh harmed himself through ignorance while their knowledge benefited them. [8 notes: JISHUMI(عبد العزيز بن أبان); BAGHAWI-FULL(عبد العزيز بن أبان); TABRISI(عبد العزيز بن أبان); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(رُوي); ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
- 20:73/q09/p2 Moses' invocation of God and pious conduct made them fear that their craft would be exposed. — Ibn al-Jawzi gives this as a second account of why they resisted the challenge. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD]

## 20:73/q10 How is ما in وما أكرهتنا analyzed?  [وَمَآ أَكْرَهْتَنَا عَلَيْهِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether ما names an additional object of forgiveness, negates a clause, or heads a new nominal sentence; the negative reconstruction is challenged by their request for reward.
- 20:73/q10/p1 It is a relative pronoun in the accusative, coordinated with خطايانا as another object of forgiveness. — Al-Farra says its case follows the sense of خطايا; al-Zajjaj paraphrases forgiveness of their sins and Pharaoh's coercion. Al-Nahhas, followed by al-Qurtubi, prefers coordination. Al-Nasafi, al-Samin and Tabataba'i explicitly call it relative or coordinate; al-Alusi also calls the phrase coordinated with 'our sins.' [9 notes: FARRA; ZAJJAJ; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; NAHHAS+; QURTUBI-FULL(النحاس)+; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 20:73/q10/p2 It is a negation with no case position, separating the sins from magic that was not compelled or was excluded from forgiveness. — Al-Nahhas transmits a negative reading; al-Qurtubi's version says 'forgive our sins apart from the magic forced on us.' Abu al-Baqa reconstructs 'sins from sorcery are forgiven although Pharaoh did not compel them'; al-Samin calls that remote. Al-Mahdawi finds negation strained because they had asked about reward at 26:41, and al-Nahhas prefers the accusative alternative. [2 notes: NAHHAS(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); NAHHAS-; QURTUBI-FULL(المهدوي,النحاس)-; SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء)-]
- 20:73/q10/p3 It is a relative pronoun functioning as a nominative subject with an omitted predicate: the compelled magic is set aside or lifted. — Al-Qurtubi supplies an omitted predicate 'set aside'; al-Samin offers 'lifted' or 'not held against them.' [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); SAMIN-DURR]

## 20:73/q11 How does من السحر attach and what does it specify?  [مِنَ ٱلسِّحْرِ · grammar]
turns on: Its attachment changes with the syntactic analysis of ما; al-Samin also distinguishes circumstantial from specifying use.
- 20:73/q11/p1 It describes the compelled thing or its pronoun as a circumstantial phrase, or specifies that thing as magic. — Al-Nasafi makes it circumstantial to the relative pronoun. Al-Samin allows attachment to the pronoun in عليه or to ما, and alternatively says it specifies the kind. Tabataba'i says it explains what they were made to do. Al-Qurtubi attaches it to أكرهتنا under the accusative and nominative analyses. [5 notes: NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; TABATABAI; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 20:73/q11/p2 Under the negation analysis, it attaches to خطايانا, making their sins sins of sorcery. — Al-Qurtubi ties this attachment specifically to the analysis of ما as a negative particle; Abu al-Baqa's rejected reconstruction in al-Samin likewise fronts 'from sorcery' with the sins. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء)-]

## 20:73/q12 What place did magic have in Pharaoh's Egyptian rule?  [ٱلسِّحْرِ · interpretation]
- 20:73/q12/p1 Magic was central to the god-king Pharaoh's religion and something his subjects were expected to accept. — Asad explains Pharaoh as the title of Egypt's indigenous rulers and a god-king embodying Egyptian religion, in which magic was part of ordinary life. Al-Tabrisi says kings wanted teaching controlled so magic would not escape their rule. [2 notes: ASAD-NOTES; TABRISI]

## 20:73/q13 What does والله خير وأبقى mean?  [وَٱللَّهُ خَيْرٌۭ وَأَبْقَىٰٓ · interpretation]
turns on: The comparison can name God Himself, His goodness against Pharaoh's call, His lasting reward alone, or His better reward together with more lasting punishment.
- 20:73/q13/p1 God Himself is better than Pharaoh and abides after Pharaoh perishes. — Muqatil and al-Wahidi stress Pharaoh's mortality versus God's permanence; al-Zajjaj says God is better and more enduring for the magicians than Pharaoh. Yahya ibn Sallam transmits this from some interpreters. Bursevi and al-Alusi say He is better in Himself; Tabataba'i says absolutely none is better or more enduring, relating the preference to forgiveness. Asad calls أبقى literally 'most abiding,' hence eternal, and cites 55:26-27. Ibn Ashur says God is better for them to prefer over Pharaoh. [10 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(بعضهم); ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES]
- 20:73/q13/p2 God is better than what Pharaoh summons them to, and His good and protection exceed Pharaoh's. — Yahya ibn Sallam compares God with Pharaoh's call. The inward interpretation quoted by Bursevi compares God's bringing good and repelling harm with Pharaoh's power, and says both God's good and punishment endure longer. [2 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]
- 20:73/q13/p3 God's reward is better and more enduring than Pharaoh's promised reward. — Al-Wahidi reports Ibn Abbas reading an implicit 'reward'; al-Qurtubi does likewise. Al-Tabrisi gives this as one explanation; Ibn Kathir reports Ibn Ishaq comparing God's reward with Pharaoh's promise. Al-Baydawi lists it as one possible reading; al-Biqa'i says God's promised reward is better and enduring. Asad speaks of God's superior and abiding goodness. [7 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس); BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن إسحاق); BIQAI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES]
- 20:73/q13/p4 God is better in reward for obedience and His punishment for disobedience endures longer than Pharaoh's. — Al-Tabari reports Wahb, Muhammad ibn Ka'b and Muhammad ibn Qays; al-Wahidi reports Abu Ishaq, Ibn Ishaq and Muhammad ibn Ka'b. Al-Mawardi gives two near-identical formulations. Al-Jishumi adds that Pharaoh perishes. Al-Baghawi transmits Ibn Ishaq and Muhammad ibn Ka'b; Ibn al-Jawzi, Razi, al-Nasafi, al-Qurtubi, al-Tabrisi, al-Baydawi and Ibn Kathir give this reward and punishment contrast. Bursevi and al-Alusi include recompense; al-Biqa'i says both God's reward and punishment endure; al-Durr says God's punishment lasts longer than Pharaoh himself. Ibn Ashur and Study Quran contrast both divine and pharaonic reward and punishment; Study Quran cites 28:60, 42:36 and 20:131. [28 notes: TAB-FULL(محمد بن كعب ومحمد بن قيس,وهب بن منبه); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,محمد بن إسحاق,محمد بن كعب); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(محمد بن إسحاق,محمد بن كعب); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن إسحاق,محمد بن كعب القرظي); BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(محمد بن كعب القرظي); BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN(JJ)]

## 20:73/q14 How does their closing comparison answer Pharaoh's threat?  [وَٱللَّهُ خَيْرٌۭ وَأَبْقَىٰٓ · links]
- 20:73/q14/p1 It answers Pharaoh's claim in 20:71 that his punishment would be harsher and longer lasting by invoking God's greater and enduring power or recompense. — Muqatil, al-Zajjaj, Abu Ishaq in al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, Ibn Atiyya, al-Tabrisi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Razi, al-Qurtubi, al-Nasafi, Ibn Ashur and Tabataba'i expressly link the reply to Pharaoh's threat. Ibn Ashur says his threat cannot terrify them once they recognize God's lasting reward and punishment. [14 notes: MUQATIL; ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); BAGHAWI-FULL(محمد بن إسحاق,محمد بن كعب); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 20:73/q15 How does the sentence والله خير وأبقى function in the speech?  [وَٱللَّهُ خَيْرٌۭ وَأَبْقَىٰٓ · grammar]
- 20:73/q15/p1 It may be circumstantial or an inserted conclusion summarizing what the magicians have said. — Ibn Ashur gives both grammatical possibilities. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 20:73/q16 Why call Him Allah in their final comparison?  [وَٱللَّهُ · rhetoric]
- 20:73/q16/p1 The divine name grounds His absolute goodness in His possession of every perfection. — Tabataba'i says goodness rests on perfection, and God possesses every perfection. His three descriptions support a sequence: He created them, He is the Lord in whom they believe, and He is God whom they hold best. Bursevi links God's goodness in Himself to their earlier mention of the One who created them. [3 notes: TABATABAI; BURSEVI]

## 20:73/q17 How does their faith and request for forgiveness support their refusal to choose Pharaoh?  [إِنَّآ ءَامَنَّا بِرَبِّنَا لِيَغْفِرَ لَنَا خَطَٰيَٰنَا · links]
- 20:73/q17/p1 Their faith gives the reason for their refusal: they choose the Lord who created them and seek His lasting forgiveness over Pharaoh's temporary power. — Ibn Ashur calls إنا آمنا بربنا the reason for their preceding words. Tabataba'i says forgiveness explains their refusal in 20:72 and that God's goodness and permanence explain preferring His pardon to Pharaoh's favor. His three divine descriptions form the argument; al-Biqa'i also stresses trust in their Lord. [5 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; BIQAI-FULL]

## 20:73/q18 How does the magicians' answer depict their changed priorities?  [* · interpretation]
- 20:73/q18/p1 Recognition of truth displaced awe of Pharaoh and worldly show; they now hope in and fear only God and choose lasting truth over a worldly mirage. — Tabataba'i recalls their former oath by Pharaoh's might and attraction to finery, then names their release from cowardice, flattery, caprice and longing for glitter. They attach themselves to truth and God's authority, willing what He wills. Study Quran says their certainty makes finite trials and pleasures pale before eternal felicity. [5 notes: TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(K)]

## 20:73/q19 Could a vision of Paradise coexist with their voluntary faith and plea for forgiveness?  [لِيَغْفِرَ لَنَا · theology]
turns on: Whether direct sight of Paradise would force faith, and whether a certain believer can still voice hope for forgiveness.
- 20:73/q19/p1 No: seeing Paradise directly would compel belief, contrary to their voluntary plea for forgiveness. — Al-Qadi in Razi objects to Ikrima's vision report on this ground. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(القاضي)+; RAZI-FULL-]
- 20:73/q19/p2 Yes: certainty or vision need not prevent a believer from hoping aloud for forgiveness. — Razi replies that Abraham expressed hope for forgiveness at 26:82 despite certainty, so the magicians could speak in the same way after a vision. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL+; RAZI-FULL(القاضي)-]

## 20:73/q20 What happened to the magicians after their profession of faith?  [* · reports]
turns on: Whether the reported martyrdom or the verses al-Biqa'i invokes decide their fate.
- 20:73/q20/p1 Pharaoh carried out his threat and they ended the day as martyrs. — Qatadah in Yahya ibn Sallam says they began the day sorcerers and ended as martyrs. Ibn Kathir reports the same from Ibn Abbas and other early authorities, and himself considers their execution apparent. [3 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(قتادة); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس وغيره من السلف); IBNKATHIR-FULL+]
- 20:73/q20/p2 God saved them from Pharaoh. — Abu Hayyan in al-Biqa'i considers rescue apparent; al-Biqa'i supports him with 28:35 and says the end of al-Hadid is explicit about rescue. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL(أبو حيان)]

## 20:73/q21 Whose speech comprises the surrounding verses, and how does it move into 20:74?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the statements are direct quotation or divine narration.
- 20:73/q21/p1 The preceding three verses quote the magicians, whose speech moves from steadfast reply into believers' and criminals' contrasting fates at 20:74. — Al-Kashshaf allows the preceding verses as a quotation; Razi says their speech turns to Resurrection and begins with the criminal's Hell in 20:74. [2 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL]
- 20:73/q21/p2 The preceding three verses are God's account of them rather than a quotation. — Al-Kashshaf gives this as the alternative to direct speech. [1 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL]

## 20:73/q22 Is the change into the magicians' first-person words an instance of rhetorical shift?  [إِنَّآ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the addressed and speaking parties have one referent, as a rhetorical shift requires.
- 20:73/q22/p1 It is not a valid shift from address to first person because the two intended speakers are not the same referent. — Al-Itqan rejects an analysis proposed by some on the requirement that the referent remain the same. [1 notes: ITQAN+]
- 20:73/q22/p2 Some analyze it as a shift from address to first-person speech. — Al-Itqan reports this proposal only to reject it. [0 notes: ITQAN-]

## 20:73/q23 What do the grammatical notes say about إن in إنا?  [إِنَّآ · grammar]
turns on: The notes distinguish complete emphasized clauses from open, noun-like clauses and discuss causal use and whether the open form retains emphasis.
- 20:73/q23/p1 Kasra إن emphasizes an otherwise complete sentence, while open إن turns its clause into a noun-like unit needing another constituent. — Al-Burhan quotes Ibn al-Hajib, whose example is 'I like that Zayd is standing,' and al-Zamakhshari's al-Mufassal for the distinction. [2 notes: BURHAN(ابن الحاجب,الزمخشري)]
- 20:73/q23/p2 إن can convey causation in the verses under discussion. — Al-Burhan calls this agreed and paraphrases the examples as 'because He gave it' and 'so that they would not believe.' [1 notes: BURHAN]
- 20:73/q23/p3 The open doubled form of إن may also emphasize despite a claim that verbal-noun expansion would remove emphasis. — Al-Burhan calls that objection weak. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 20:73/q24 What comparative imagery is discussed alongside خير وأبقى?  [خَيْرٌۭ وَأَبْقَىٰٓ · rhetoric]
- 20:73/q24/p1 Poetic comparisons can rely on imagined or grounded likeness, make abstract opposites visible, reverse expected standards, or transform an ugly scene into honor. — Al-Jurjani's lover calls separation black, then treats a never-loving heart as darker; he contrasts images of a hypocrite's or unbeliever's black heart with Ibn al-Amid's direct 'darker than unbelief.' He allows al-Buhturi's Sunna and innovation line to show truth brightening against falsehood like stars against dark, while noting that abstract ideas receive imagined colors. Ibn al-Mu'tazz makes the moon spotted and albino and less luminous than the sun despite its remaining heat. Abu al-Hasan's elegy turns Ibn Baqiyya's crucifixion into honor by making height distinction, onlookers a prayer delegation, extended hands gifts, and wind a shroud. These are the cited comparisons; the notes do not explicitly apply a particular image to this verse. [6 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR; JURJANI-ASRAR+]
