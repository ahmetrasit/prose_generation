# 20:21: 22 questions

## 20:21/q01 How does qāla connect this speech to the preceding scene?  [قَالَ · rhetoric]
- 20:21/q01/p1 It resumes the narrative as the answer to an implied question about what God said next. — Bursevī identifies the implied question. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 20:21/q02 What is Moses told to take, and with which hand?  [خُذْهَا · referent]
turns on: Whether the reports specify a hand in addition to identifying the serpent.
- 20:21/q02/p1 He is told to take the serpent, the transformed staff. — Al-Ṭabarī identifies the object pronoun as the serpent; Ibn al-Jawzī says it was still alive when taken. [3 notes: TAB-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; ASAD-NOTES]
- 20:21/q02/p2 He is told to take it specifically with his right hand. — Al-Baghawī and al-Ṭabrisī state the right hand; al-Ālūsī attributes this specification to Wahb. [3 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; ALUSI-FULL(وهب)]

## 20:21/q03 How did Moses take the serpent and recover his staff?  [خُذْهَا · reports]
turns on: The reports differ over where Moses grasped the serpent and whether he first used a covering.
- 20:21/q03/p1 He put his bare hand into its mouth or onto its teeth or jaws, and it became the familiar wooden staff. — Rāzī reports the teeth; al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, al-Qurṭubī and Study Quran report jaws or mouth. Al-Biqāʿī and Bursevī say his hand ended between the staff's prongs; Ibn al-Jawzī and Ibn ʿAṭiyya say touching or grasping it restored it. Al-Ālūsī finds direct taking more fitting for prophetic dignity. [12 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL(قيل); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; STUDYQURAN; ALUSI-FULL(وهب)+; RAZI-FULL(بعضهم)+]
- 20:21/q03/p2 He first tried to shield his hand with a cloak or sleeve, then uncovered it and took the serpent. — Wahb's versions say a wool cloak covered the hand; God or an angel asked whether it could protect him if God permitted harm, and Moses acknowledged human weakness. Al-Ṭabrisī places his hand in the mouth, between the prongs; al-Qurṭubī and Ibn ʿAṭiyya give a sleeve version. Al-Ālūsī finds this account less fitting than direct taking. [7 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(وهب); BAGHAWI-FULL(بعضهم); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(وهب); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(وهب)]
- 20:21/q03/p3 He grasped the serpent by its tail, and it became a staff. — Muqātil locates the grasp at the tail. [1 notes: MUQATIL]
- 20:21/q03/p4 He was told to put his hand in its mouth, where it became a staff. — Al-Jishumī presents this as one reported explanation alongside the view that it was a harmless prophetic sign. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 20:21/q04 How many times was Moses called to take it before obeying?  [خُذْهَا · reports]
turns on: The divergent reports count earlier calls differently; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī assess their fit with prophetic dignity.
- 20:21/q04/p1 He obeyed after a third call, having failed to take it at two earlier calls. — Ibn ʿAbbās in al-Ṭabarī and al-Durr describes two earlier calls; Makkī gives three commands. Al-Ālūsī says Makkī puts the restoration promise in the third call. Abū Ḥayyān objects that two refusals are unfitting for a prophet; al-Ālūsī doubts the report's soundness or fit with prophetic dignity. [2 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(مكي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس)-]
- 20:21/q04/p2 He initially refused, then took it after a further command and assurance of safety. — Ibn ʿAbbās as transmitted in al-Durr links his flight, the renewed command, and the assurance that he was secure with 27:10 and 28:31. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 20:21/q04/p3 He took it directly after God's command. — Al-Ālūsī says the direct-taking account better suits prophetic dignity; Abū Ḥayyān argues against repeated disobedience. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(وهب)+]

## 20:21/q05 Why did Moses fear the serpent?  [وَلَا تَخَفْ · interpretation]
turns on: The explanations locate the object of fear in the snake's danger, God's power, Adam's precedent, or loss of the staff's uses.
- 20:21/q05/p1 He reacted with ordinary human fear to a terrifying and unfamiliar serpent. — Al-Rāzī says extreme panic can overwhelm knowledge; al-Iṣlāḥī notes that Moses' staff for killing a snake had itself become one. Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Qurṭubī, al-Biqāʿī, Bursevī and al-Ālūsī call such fear natural; al-Ālūsī says it does not lessen prophetic rank. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ cites 28:31 for flight. [13 notes: TAB-FULL(السدي); THALABI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 20:21/q05/p2 He feared being overwhelmed by divine might, rather than ordinary creaturely danger. — Junayd distinguishes fear of divine power from instinctive fear; al-Wāsiṭī sees on the staff a sign of divine displeasure and punishment, making hidden divine power unsettling. Al-Ālūsī reports a comparison: Moses faced a mysterious act from God, while Abraham faced a known fire from an enemy. [3 notes: SULAMI(الجنيد,الواسطي); ALUSI-FULL]
- 20:21/q05/p3 He feared because a serpent had figured in Adam's misfortune or expulsion. — Al-Rāzī and al-Ālūsī transmit this explanation without adopting it. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL(بعضهم); ALUSI-FULL]
- 20:21/q05/p4 He feared losing the uses and benefits of his staff. — Al-Ālūsī rejects this as misjudging Moses' spiritual standing. [0 notes: ALUSI-FULL-]

## 20:21/q06 What shows that Moses was afraid, and what does that fear imply?  [وَلَا تَخَفْ · interpretation]
turns on: The notes distinguish evidence of fear, its value as a sign of sincerity, and its compatibility with prophetic courage.
- 20:21/q06/p1 His flight shows actual fear; the prohibition alone would not establish it. — Al-Rāzī compares 'do not fear' with 33:1's 'do not obey the disbelievers': a prohibition does not prove the forbidden action. He cites Moses' flight in 27:10 and contrasts Muhammad's freedom from worldly attachment. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ cites 28:31; Study Quran points to 27:10 and 28:31. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]
- 20:21/q06/p2 His fear attests truthful prophethood. — Abū al-Qāsim al-Anṣārī argues that a magician knows a display is illusion and therefore would not fear it. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(أبو القاسم الأنصاري)]
- 20:21/q06/p3 Ordinary prophetic fear differs from blameworthy inner agitation. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ defines khawf as taking precautions against harm and khashya as disturbance of the heart; prophets can experience the first but not cowardly khashya, citing 33:39. [2 notes: TABATABAI]

## 20:21/q07 When and why did Moses cease fearing?  [وَلَا تَخَفْ · interpretation]
turns on: The reports attach reassurance to the command, to the promised restoration, or place residual fear after both.
- 20:21/q07/p1 God's promise to restore the serpent to a staff reassured him. — Study Quran reports that some commentators connect the fear's end to the promise, citing 27:10–11. Al-Ālūsī says the promise explains both obedience and fearlessness. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN(some commentators); ALUSI-FULL]
- 20:21/q07/p2 God's command not to fear brought tranquility and ended his fear. — Study Quran attributes this to other commentators, citing 27:10–11; al-Rāzī reports a view that the command removed fear sufficiently for Moses to grasp the jaws. Bursevī says God removed the initial fear. [3 notes: STUDYQURAN(other commentators); BURSEVI; RAZI-FULL(بعضهم)+]
- 20:21/q07/p3 He remained frightened even while taking it. — Al-Rāzī rejects this report as unlikely after accumulated signs and God's restoration promise. [0 notes: RAZI-FULL-]

## 20:21/q08 What is the force of 'do not fear' beside the command to take it?  [وَلَا تَخَفْ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether 'do not fear' is an independent end or chiefly reassurance attached to taking the serpent.
- 20:21/q08/p1 Fearlessness is itself intended, in addition to enabling Moses to take the serpent. — Al-Ālūsī bases this on coordination of the prohibition with the positive command. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 20:21/q08/p2 The prohibition reassures Moses that the serpent will become his staff again. — Al-Ṭabarī, al-Thaʿlabī and Asad couple reassurance with restoration; al-Ālūsī says the promise is why he should take it without fear. [4 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI; ASAD-NOTES; ALUSI-FULL]

## 20:21/q09 Why was Moses shown and told to take this sign before facing Pharaoh?  [خُذْهَا وَلَا تَخَفْ · interpretation]
turns on: The notes emphasize either preparation for Pharaoh or further proof through the reversal.
- 20:21/q09/p1 The prior experience trained him not to panic when the staff changed before Pharaoh. — Al-Wāḥidī says the miracle prepared Moses and that this verse implies fear which al-Qaṣaṣ states; the exegetes in al-Baghawī call it a sign beyond human ability. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Nasafī, al-Biqāʿī, Bursevī and Study Quran make the same preparatory connection. Al-Ālūsī reports it as a proposed wisdom but calls the further claim that the promise itself is a new reassuring sign unclear. [8 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL(قال المفسرون); QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(al-Qurṭubī)]
- 20:21/q09/p2 Taking the live serpent safely and seeing it become wood added miracles to the first transformation. — Al-Rāzī says the two further signs strengthened the proof; al-Biqāʿī says the sign confirmed that the speaker was the Lord with the most beautiful names. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]

## 20:21/q10 What does sīrah mean here and how did that sense arise?  [سِيرَتَهَا · meaning]
- 20:21/q10/p1 It means a way, manner, form, or condition, extended from movement or travel. — Al-Farrāʾ, al-Zajjāj, al-Jishumī, al-Nasafī, Bursevī, al-Ālūsī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ connect the word to a mode of going; al-Zajjāj notes its use for resemblance though ṭarīqa and sīra often describe acts. Al-Kashshāf, cited by al-Rāzī, compares rukba from riding; al-Samīn compares the same form and cites Khālid al-Hudhalī's poetry. Abū Ḥayyān also cites a poem; al-Ālūsī notes a line for 'way' or school. Ibn ʿAbbās glosses condition, Mujāhid appearance, Abū ʿUbayda original creation. [24 notes: FARRA; MAJAZ; TAB-FULL(ابن جريج,ابن عباس,مجاهد); ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيدة,ابن عباس,مجاهد); JISHUMI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الفراء); RAZI-FULL(صاحب الكشاف); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,مجاهد); ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 20:21/q11 What does God promise by 'We shall restore it to its former sīrah'?  [سَنُعِيدُهَا سِيرَتَهَا ٱلْأُولَىٰ · interpretation]
turns on: The ordinary explanation identifies a literal staff; al-Qāshānī reads the first state as a condition of the soul.
- 20:21/q11/p1 The serpent will return to the staff's original form, state, and familiar uses. — Mujāhid, Qatāda, Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Jurayj, Wahb, ʿAṭāʾ and al-Suddī give forms of this gloss. Al-Ṭabarī invokes Arabic usage for resuming a former state; al-Jishumī defines iʿāda as a second return to an original state. Al-Mubarrad says it will be as before; al-Wāḥidī and al-Bayḍāwī include its former behavior; al-Biqāʿī says God's promise cannot fail. Abū ʿUbayda calls it original creation. Abū Zayd notes the construction can use ilā. [39 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(قتادة وابن مجاهد عن أبيه); FARRA; MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(ابن جريج,ابن عباس,قتادة,مجاهد,وهب بن منبه); ZAJJAJ; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيدة,ابن عباس,المبرد,عطاء والسدي,مجاهد); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الفراء); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,مجاهد); ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN(some commentators)]
- 20:21/q11/p2 In an inward reading, its first condition is lifeless, vegetative capacity without awareness or impulse. — Al-Qāshānī relates the staff to Moses' training under Shuʿayb and says Shuʿayb gave it to him. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 20:21/q12 How is the accusative sīratahā construed?  [سِيرَتَهَا · grammar]
turns on: The alternatives differ over omitted prepositions, direct transitivity, adverbial force, apposition, or an implied verb; Abū Ḥayyān disputes the adverbial analysis.
- 20:21/q12/p1 An omitted preposition, especially ilā, licenses the accusative. — Al-Zajjāj, ʿAlī ibn Sulaymān, Abū Isḥāq and others restore ilā; ʿAlī compares 7:155, 'Moses chose his people.' Al-Ḥawfī says ilā or li; Ibn Mālik adopts and Ibn Hishām approves this analysis according to al-Ālūsī. Al-Burhān instead supplies ʿalā. Al-Bayḍāwī also allows derivation from causative ʿāda. [16 notes: ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS(علي بن سليمان); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL(علي بن سليمان); BAYDAWI-FULL; RAZI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURHAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الحوفى); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحوفي)]
- 20:21/q12/p2 The verb directly governs sīratahā as a second object. — Al-Jishumī allows direct governance; al-Ḥawfī and al-Zamakhsharī analyze ʿāda as 'return to.' Al-Samīn cites Zuhayr's line; al-Ālūsī also cites al-Aṣmaʿī and lexicographers for transitive ʿāda, while his report of the Zuhayr line glosses ʿādaka as 'turned you away.' [8 notes: JISHUMI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحوفي,الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL(الأصمعى,الزمخشرى)]
- 20:21/q12/p3 It is an adverbial accusative, 'in its former manner.' — Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Nasafī allow this. Abū Ḥayyān objects that a specific adverb needs fī and its omission is rare or poetic; al-Ālūsī cites Maghribī grammarians who treat such derived nouns as an indefinite adverbial type, making this regular. [4 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(نحاة المغرب); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري)-; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ)-; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان)-]
- 20:21/q12/p4 It is an apposition of inclusion replacing the object pronoun and specifying its former quality. — Al-Ḥawfī and Abū al-Baqāʾ give the replacement analysis; al-Samīn describes it as apposition of inclusion. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو البقاء,الحوفي); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(أبو البقاء)]
- 20:21/q12/p5 The restoration verb is complete, and sīratahā belongs to an understood verb or clause describing its former movement. — Al-Rāzī estimates tasīru and the staff's former uses; Abū Ḥayyān prefers this third parse. Al-Samīn supplies yasīru as a circumstantial clause. Al-Zamakhsharī, cited by al-Ālūsī, treats it as an absolute object, recalling Moses' leaning on the staff and using it for his flock. Al-Ālūsī likes its meaning but says it departs from the apparent construction. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشرى); ABUHAYYAN-FULL+]
- 20:21/q12/p6 Sīratahā is a verbal noun corresponding to an implied sense 'make it proceed.' — ʿAlī ibn Sulaymān says sanʿīduhā carries the sense sanusayyiruhā. [2 notes: NAHHAS(علي بن سليمان); QURTUBI-FULL(علي بن سليمان)]

## 20:21/q13 What did the serpent look and act like when Moses fled?  [خُذْهَا وَلَا تَخَفْ · reports]
turns on: Reports vary in visual details and in whether the transformed creature posed real danger.
- 20:21/q13/p1 It was a terrifying, powerful serpent that swallowed or crushed objects and made Moses flee. — Ibn ʿAbbās says it swallowed a tree and rock, and Moses heard the rock; Wahb mentions fangs. Al-Ṭabrisī reports a camel-sized creature crossing rock, uprooting a tree, fiery eyes and a meteor-haired neck, with Moses stopping from shame before God. Al-Bayḍāwī says it moved fast and swallowed stones and trees. Bursevī describes blazing eyes, grinding fangs, and jaws forty or eighty cubits apart. [6 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,السدي,وهب بن منبه); TABRISI(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 20:21/q13/p2 The serpent was male. — Ibn ʿAbbās reports the sex in al-Ālūsī; Bursevī also calls it male. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); BURSEVI]
- 20:21/q13/p3 It was a prophetic sign rather than a genuinely dangerous snake. — Al-Jishumī records this explanation as a reason Moses could take it calmly. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 20:21/q14 How do the descriptions jān, ḥayya, and great serpent fit together?  [خُذْهَا · links]
turns on: Whether the verses describe successive stages or simultaneous size and movement.
- 20:21/q14/p1 Jān describes its first stage and great serpent its final stage; ḥayya covers snakes of any size or sex. — Ibn al-Jawzī reconciles the descriptions in 7:107 and 27:10 by sequence and broad usage. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD]
- 20:21/q14/p2 It had a great serpent's body but a jān's speed and light movement. — Ibn al-Jawzī reports al-Zajjāj's reconciliation of 7:107 and 27:10. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج)]

## 20:21/q15 What is reported about the staff's origin?  [خُذْهَا · reports]
turns on: The reports assign the staff's provenance to Paradise, an angelic delivery, or Shuʿayb's gift.
- 20:21/q15/p1 It came from Paradise as Adam's staff and fell to Moses when Shuʿayb sent him to choose one. — Al-Qurṭubī reports this account and separately reports Paradise myrtle as the wood; these details may belong to the same Paradise provenance. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]
- 20:21/q15/p2 An angel brought the staff to Moses. — Al-Qurṭubī gives both an unnamed angel and Gabriel as variants of the bearer. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]
- 20:21/q15/p3 Shuʿayb gave Moses the staff. — Al-Qāshānī mentions this report in his inward account of Moses' training. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 20:21/q16 What further abilities are reported for the staff?  [سِيرَتَهَا ٱلْأُولَىٰ · reports]
- 20:21/q16/p1 It later walked, spoke, carried loads, gave light, served as a bucket, and bore fruit. — Al-Qurṭubī transmits these abilities as an unattributed report. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]

## 20:21/q17 Can the staff really change into a serpent and back?  [سَنُعِيدُهَا · theology]
turns on: Whether essences themselves can change and, if so, how divine creation accounts for changed properties.
- 20:21/q17/p1 Yes; divine creative command can change a thing's reality. — Al-Ālūsī takes the verse as evidence and rejects the denial of transformation. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 20:21/q17/p2 No; essences cannot change into other essences, so such change is impossible. — Al-Ālūsī reports this denial and argues against it. [0 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)-]
- 20:21/q17/p3 Transformation can be described as God creating gold in copper's place. — Al-Ālūsī reports this explanatory model by analogy; it avoids simultaneous contrary identities. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعض المحققين)]
- 20:21/q17/p4 Transformation can be described as removing copper's property and creating gold's property in atoms receptive to both. — Al-Ālūsī attributes this model to some theologians and favors it; copper and gold existing at once in the same thing is the impossible case. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعض المحققين)+]

## 20:21/q18 What inward meaning is given to taking the staff without fear?  [خُذْهَا وَلَا تَخَفْ · inward]
turns on: The inward readers identify the serpent with anger, feared causes, the lower soul, apparent reality, or changing spiritual states.
- 20:21/q18/p1 Moses regains intellectual control over an anger that now moves by God's command. — Al-Qāshānī says the anger no longer hides beneath the heart's light, so Moses need not fear its domination or changes in spiritual state. [2 notes: QASHANI]
- 20:21/q18/p2 Only God causes harm and benefit, so fear and hope should be directed to Him alone. — The Najmī inward reading contrasts Moses' former belief in the staff's benefits with fear of its harm; Bursevī quotes the Mathnawī that one who fears God and has piety need fear no creature. [2 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية,المثنوي)]
- 20:21/q18/p3 The serpent images the unrefined lower soul, which must be conquered and restored to its primordial disposition. — Study Quran attributes this to philosophers and mystics; Mullā Ṣadrā urges purification and an intellectual life oriented toward the Return and Final Abode. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN(Mullā Ṣadrā,philosophers and mystics)]
- 20:21/q18/p4 The change alludes to appearances versus reality and insight granted to chosen servants. — Asad links it to Moses' encounter with the unnamed sage in 18:66–82 and wording in 27:10 and 28:31. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES]
- 20:21/q18/p5 Changing states show God stabilizing servants as He gathers and separates them. — Al-Qushayrī reads the staff's sequence of changes as a pattern of spiritual states. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]

## 20:21/q19 What does the serpent image teach about the forms of deeds and traits?  [* · inward]
turns on: The accompanying analogies concern either the afterlife form of traits or the practical transformation of the knower.
- 20:21/q19/p1 Inner traits and deeds can appear in fitting perceptible forms in the next life. — Bursevī cites a hadith in which unpaid zakat appears as a serpent and 6:108 for deeds being shown; he says love of wealth in the commanding soul appears as a harmful snake, whereas purified love of spending in God's way takes a beautiful form. He extends the analogy to other traits. [4 notes: BURSEVI(النبي عليه السلام)]
- 20:21/q19/p2 Knowledge must turn into action and transform the person. — Bursevī quotes a Persian couplet about leaving base existence for love's alchemy and Jāmī's copper-to-gold image: knowledge without action is poison and achieves nothing. [2 notes: BURSEVI(المولى الجامي)]

## 20:21/q20 How do the prophets' experiences of fear compare?  [وَلَا تَخَفْ · interpretation]
turns on: The comparisons appeal either to differing spiritual firmness or to the nature and source of what each prophet encountered.
- 20:21/q20/p1 Abraham's greater firmness explains why he did not fear the fire as Moses initially feared the staff. — Bursevī says God then removed Moses' fear so he reached Abraham's firmness. [1 notes: BURSEVI]
- 20:21/q20/p2 Moses faced an unknown act from God, whereas Abraham knew the nature of fire brought by God's enemy. — Al-Ālūsī reports this explanation of their different responses. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 20:21/q20/p3 The Prophet likewise initially feared Gabriel, then became ready to see his true form. — Bursevī cites the Miʿrāj and 53:13–14 as a parallel progression. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 20:21/q21 What distinguishes tafsīr and taʾwīl in handling a verse?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The distinction turns on transmitted knowledge of revelation circumstances versus constrained inference from wording and context.
- 20:21/q21/p1 Tafsīr uncovers a verse's meaning and its transmitted revelation circumstances. — Al-Thaʿlabī derives it from uncovering, illustrated by driving a horse or camel until its hair or covering wears away; an unnamed Hudhalī verse supports the camel usage, though its wording is omitted. Scholars define tafsīr as knowledge of revelation, story, and causes; they restrict claims about revelation circumstances to transmitted reports. [6 notes: THALABI(العلماء,بعض الهذليين)]
- 20:21/q21/p2 Taʾwīl turns wording toward a possible contextual meaning, judged by Qurʾan and Sunna. — Al-Thaʿlabī links the word to return and the phrase awwaltuhu, 'I redirected it'; al-Naḍr instead derives it from iyāla, governance, because the interpreter manages speech. Scholars allow inference supported by wording and use Qurʾan and Sunna as its measure. Al-Thaʿlabī says the tafʿīl pattern of both terms implies repeated work through sūras and verses. ## 20:21/q22 What makes an elaborate expression rhetorically effective?  [* · rhetoric]
- 20:21/q22/p1 Careful composition can make a normally heavy construction delicate and witty when it avoids affectation. — Al-Jurjānī cites Ibn al-Muʿtazz, al-Khālidī, Abū Tammām and al-Mutanabbī; even the familiar metaphor of being bound by kindness gains beauty through its path of phrasing.
