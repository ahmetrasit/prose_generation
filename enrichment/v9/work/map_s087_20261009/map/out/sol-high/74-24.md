# 74:24: 14 questions

## 74:24/q01 Who uttered this judgment?  [فَقَالَ · referent]
- 74:24/q01/p1 Al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra, the Quraysh leader called al-Wāḥid. — Jishumī, citing Ibn ʿĀmir, names him al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra al-Makhzūmī, describes his wealth and sons, and says his people called him al-Wāḥid; Ibn Kathīr also identifies him as a Quraysh leader. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk links him to the solitary man of 74:11. [3 notes: JISHUMI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; TAB-FULL(الضحاك)]

## 74:24/q02 What does the fāʾ in fa-qāla convey?  [فَقَالَ · rhetoric]
- 74:24/q02/p1 He voiced the accusation immediately after it occurred to him or after turning away in pride. — Al-Kashshāf, al-Nasafī, and al-Ālūsī say the phrase was uttered without hesitation once conceived; al-Rāzī places it just after his proud turning away. Abū Ḥayyān distinguishes the linked acts: frowning and scowling are close, pride causes turning away, and the fāʾ marks immediate utterance. Al-Bayḍāwī combines the inherited-magic gloss with this temporal point. Al-Ālūsī says immediacy does not conflict with the preceding report; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī traces the sequence from deliberation and looking through frowning, disgust, turning away, and pride. [7 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 74:24/q03 What is the force of in hādhā illā?  [إِنْ هَٰذَآ إِلَّا · grammar]
- 74:24/q03/p1 It restricts his verdict to magic, presenting it as the outcome of rejecting other descriptions. — Bursevī says in is the negative particle mā, as the following illā shows. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the exclusive wording presents magic as the conclusion of his testing and sorting through possible descriptions, following 74:18. [2 notes: BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 74:24/q04 What does hādhā point to?  [هَٰذَآ · referent]
- 74:24/q04/p1 The Qur’an, Muhammad’s recitation or message. — Al-Wāḥidī explicitly identifies the demonstrative as the Qur’an; Bursevī does the same. Other accounts call its immediate object what Muhammad recited or his message. [4 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; BURSEVI; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]

## 74:24/q05 What does siḥr mean in this accusation?  [سِحْرٌۭ · meaning]
turns on: Whether siḥr is explained as deceptive sorcery or as the spellbinding effect of eloquence.
- 74:24/q05/p1 Sorcery or deception: an illusory effect presented as truth. — Al-Qurṭubī glosses it as deception and reports a definition of magic as making falsehood appear true. Al-Biqāʿī says the accusation treats the message as effects without reality, with hidden causes. Asad gives the ordinary sense sorcery or magic and, citing Tāj al-ʿArūs, the primary sense of changing a thing from its natural state; when applied to revelation, it implies deliberate deception or delusion. [5 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قوم); BIQAI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES]
- 74:24/q05/p2 Something with the captivating effect of magic, especially exceptional eloquence. — Al-Māwardī says its sweetness affects souls as magic does. Abū Ḥayyān glosses illā siḥr as something resembling sorcery. Asad explains the figurative use for spellbinding eloquence and reads the charge as old spellbinding eloquence. Iṣlāḥī describes the leader’s misreading of stirring eloquence and says Quraysh adopted the label after they could no longer deny the Qur’an’s eloquence and force. [6 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 74:24/q06 What does yuʾthar mean here?  [يُؤْثَرُ · meaning]
turns on: Whether yuʾthar comes from recounting and transmitting earlier material or from īthār, preferring one thing over another.
- 74:24/q06/p1 It is transmitted, narrated, or learned from others before Muhammad. — Abū Razīn says the supposed magic was taken from someone else, in al-Ṭabarī and al-Durr. Al-Wāḥidī derives athara al-ḥadīth from following a people’s traces after death, later general narration, and cites ʿUmar’s wording about recalling or reporting an oath. Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī give the reporting sense; al-Qurṭubī calls athara a verbal noun and maʾthūr a report from earlier generations, citing verses of Imruʾ al-Qays for what keeps being recounted and al-Aʿshā for al-athīr as hearer or transmitter. Abū Ḥayyān cites poetry for this sense. Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ibn Kathīr, al-Biqāʿī and Bursevī explain the transfer from another, with Bursevī distinguishing learning from performing magic. Bursevī also describes the old-to-general development of the verb. Al-Ālūsī prefers the retold, learned sense and invokes Babylonian magic; Elmalılı says practitioners teach it to successors. Al-Bayḍāwī allows both transmitted and learned. [16 notes: TAB-FULL(أبو رزين); WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الأعشى,امرؤ القيس); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(أبو رزين); BURSEVI; ELMALILI; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 74:24/q06/p2 It is chosen, preferred, or admired over other magic. — ʿAṭāʾ from Ibn ʿAbbās derives it from īthār, preferring one thing, and says the Qur’an would be preferred above magic. Al-Rāzī gives the same alternative; Abū Ḥayyān reports it alongside transmission; Elmalılı describes a dazzling, captivating appeal. Al-Ṭabrisī reports that souls favor and choose it because of sweetness. Al-Ālūsī mentions but does not select this interpretation. [5 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(عطاء عن ابن عباس); TABRISI(قيل); RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ELMALILI; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 74:24/q07 From whom did he allege that the magic was learned?  [سِحْرٌۭ يُؤْثَرُ · referent]
turns on: The reports name a general earlier source, Musaylima, Babylon, or both; Abū Ḥayyān explicitly gives Musaylima or Babylon.
- 74:24/q07/p1 From earlier people or sorcerers, left unnamed. — Al-Ṭabarī says another person; al-Māwardī's Ibn Zayd says earlier people; al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Nasafī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī say sorcerers. Al-Wāḥidī al-Wajīz likewise says sorcerers; al-Biqāʿī adds that a listener passes it to another and that the alleged magic divides spouses and close relatives. Ibn ʿĀshūr describes the alleged sources as ancient people. [10 notes: TAB-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL(ابن زيد); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 74:24/q07/p2 From Musaylima. — Al-Wāḥidī explicitly names Musaylima; al-Farrāʾ, al-Kashshāf and al-Nasafī give him alongside Babylonian sorcerers. Abū Ḥayyān’s debate report gives Musaylima or Babylon as alternatives. [5 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; FARRA; KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL(الوليد); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(غير مسمى)]
- 74:24/q07/p3 From Babylonian sorcerers or the people of Babylon. — Al-Kalbī names the people of Babylon; al-Farrāʾ names Babylonian sorcerers and separately says Muhammad learned it from them. Al-Kashshāf and al-Nasafī pair Babylon with Musaylima; Abū Ḥayyān reports Babylon as an alternative, and al-Ālūsī refers to Babylon and similar sources. [7 notes: FARRA; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL(الوليد); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(غير مسمى); ALUSI-FULL]

## 74:24/q08 Why call the supposed magic inherited?  [سِحْرٌۭ يُؤْثَرُ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the qualifier chiefly answers an objection from the message’s unusual character or explains away its captivating effect as inherited human speech.
- 74:24/q08/p1 To explain why the Qur’an and Muhammad did not resemble familiar magic or magicians. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says he posited unfamiliar magical sayings from ancient people to account for the Qur’an’s distinct language and effects and for Muhammad’s circumstances. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 74:24/q08/p2 To present its powerful effect as familiar human artistry passed down from earlier speakers. — Iṣlāḥī says opponents conceded its spellbinding effect but attributed it to earlier poets and orators, denying that it was divine or unique. Al-Biqāʿī notes the alleged magic spreads powerfully and divides families. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BIQAI-FULL]

## 74:24/q09 What happened before al-Walīd made this accusation?  [* · reports]
turns on: The narratives differ over Abū Jahl’s intervention, a Quraysh council, individual encounters with recitation, and the Fuṣṣilat episode.
- 74:24/q09/p1 He praised the Qur’an, then Abū Jahl pressured him to denounce it. — ʿIkrima in al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr says the recitation softened him, he praised its sweetness and force, and Abū Jahl pressed him. Ibn ʿAbbās in al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr says he initially called it God’s speech, neither poetry, magic nor madness, and Abū Jahl shamed him by fearing conversion. Al-Wāḥidī and Study Quran report praise of its unlike-poetry, sweet and abundant language. Al-Kashshāf and al-Nasafī also record rejecting madness, soothsaying, poetry and lying before the verdict, with Musaylima and Babylon named. Abū Ḥayyān’s second report says visits to Abū Bakr and Quraysh’s suspicions formed Abū Jahl’s pressure. Elmalılı adds the charge that al-Walīd had strayed, his wealth and poetry expertise, public denunciation, the assembly’s applause and dispersal; his praise included beauty, fruitfulness and incomparable height. Ibn Isḥāq and other historians in Ibn Kathīr report a similar account. [14 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,عكرمة); WAHIDI-ASBAB(ابن عباس); KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL(الوليد); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس,عكرمة,محمد بن إسحاق); ELMALILI(الوليد بن المغيرة); STUDYQURAN(Ibn ʿAbbās)]
- 74:24/q09/p2 A Quraysh discussion of possible labels led him to choose magic after rejecting the others. — Mujāhid in al-Wāḥidī gives madman, soothsayer, poet, and liar as rejected labels. Jishumī says al-Walīd chose magician because the Qur’an divided families. Ibn Kathīr’s al-Suddī places a council before the pilgrimage to deter visitors. Al-Durr’s Mujāhid from Ibn ʿAbbās credits al-Walīd’s poetry and soothsaying expertise for rejecting those labels. Abū Ḥayyān reports variants of a debate with Abū Jahl and Quraysh with the same gist, including praise of sweetness and force. Al-Wāḥidī records Quraysh asking for his verdict; Study Quran notes he settled on sorcery after considering alternatives. [7 notes: WAHIDI-ASBAB(مجاهد); JISHUMI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الوليد); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(السدي); DURR-FULL(مجاهد عن ابن عباس); STUDYQURAN]
- 74:24/q09/p3 He heard or examined recitation, deliberated, then pronounced it magic. — Ibn Zayd in al-Ṭabarī says he watched Muhammad reciting in prayer, found the words sweet and heart-gripping, rejected poetry and soothsaying, then allowed inherited magic. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk says Muhammad invited him to Islam and he delayed, pondered, frowned and turned away before the verdict and Saqar. Qatāda in al-Durr reports praise of sweetness, beauty, fruitfulness and dominance despite the magic verdict. Al-Farrāʾ locates a scene near Muslims at the Sacred Mosque, where he called Muhammad a sorcerer, then scowled and turned away. [4 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن زيد,الضحاك); DURR-FULL(قتادة); FARRA]
- 74:24/q09/p4 A reported encounter with the opening of Fuṣṣilat preceded his rejection. — Abū Ḥayyān connects the rejection with his hearing Muhammad recite the opening of Fuṣṣilat and pleading for him to stop at the warning about ʿĀd and Thamūd, 41:13. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 74:24/q10 Which verses did the reported incident occasion?  [* · reports]
turns on: Whether the incident belongs to the opening passages of both sūras or specifically to these verses; Elmalılı cites the recited Ḥā-Mīm passages.
- 74:24/q10/p1 The account concerns the opening of al-Muzzammil and al-Muddaththir. — Elmalılı reports that some attach the incident to the beginnings of both sūras. [0 notes: ELMALILI-]
- 74:24/q10/p2 It concerns these al-Muddaththir verses, including 74:11–24. — Elmalılı argues for these verses because the passages recited were from the Ḥā-Mīms. Al-Ṭabarī’s ʿIkrima and Ibn ʿAbbās reports say the passage was revealed after the charge; al-Wāḥidī specifies 74:11–24. Qatāda in al-Durr says the condemnation from his measuring through becoming sullen was revealed about him; Study Quran’s Ibn ʿAbbās account likewise says the verdict prompted this passage. [6 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,عكرمة); WAHIDI-ASBAB(ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(قتادة); STUDYQURAN(Ibn ʿAbbās); ELMALILI+]

## 74:24/q11 What accusation about the Qur’an does the verse express?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the accusation is explained as inherited sorcery or as a disparaging account of the Qur’an’s captivating eloquence.
- 74:24/q11/p1 He alleges that the Qur’an is humanly transmitted magic, rather than divine revelation. — Muqātil, al-Ṭabarī, al-Wāḥidī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī, Ibn Kathīr, Bursevī, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, and Elmalılı pair this charge with calling it human speech, where stated. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says exegetes understand the claim as falsely presented revelation; al-Wāḥidī says Muhammad took it from Musaylima. Al-Biqāʿī says the alleged effects lack reality and points out Muhammad neither practiced nor learned magic. Iṣlāḥī says opponents used the label as propaganda after its force could no longer be denied. [18 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(أكثر المفسرين); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI(الوليد بن المغيرة); TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 74:24/q11/p2 He treats the Qur’an as captivating human eloquence or influence, comparing it with magic. — Al-Māwardī attributes its effect to sweetness; Asad reads old spellbinding eloquence. Iṣlāḥī says he misjudged the prophet’s stirring eloquence and that opponents compared its effect with earlier poets and orators; Abū Ḥayyān explains the magic phrase as resemblance. [5 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 74:24/q12 What does his verdict reveal about his response to revelation?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the note emphasizes his own conscious rejection or extends the portrayal to similarly dismissive readers.
- 74:24/q12/p1 He knowingly or pridefully rejected the truth and fashioned a false excuse. — Muqātil, al-Ṭabarī, Bursevī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī connect the speech with arrogant turning away. Al-Naḥḥās says he found no argument, disbelieved, then made the claim. Al-Burhān says pride made him recant recognition. Al-Biqāʿī says corrupt worldly calculation ignored God’s judgment and the Hereafter, and that Muhammad’s lack of magical practice or learning exposes the charge. Iṣlāḥī calls it a disdainfully delivered excuse ignoring a warning whose realities he will meet. Ibn ʿĀshūr relates worldly leadership and envy to a worldly threat and denial of revelation to the threat of Hell. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk’s account ends with Saqar. [11 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(الضحاك); NAHHAS; BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 74:24/q12/p2 The condemnation also applies to others who adopt the same unbelieving posture. — Kuranyolu reads 74:18–25 as condemning people like al-Walīd, not only the individual in the report. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 74:24/q13 How is this verdict connected with neighboring verses and other Qur’anic charges?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes connect the verdict either to its immediate continuation and punishment or to parallel Qur’anic accusations.
- 74:24/q13/p1 The following human-speech accusation confirms this one, and the threats answer his worldly motives and denial. — Al-Kashshāf says the second accusation is unjoined because it confirms the first. Ibn ʿĀshūr says worldly threat answers his pursuit of leadership and envy, while Hell answers his attack on the Qur’an and denial of revelation, citing 74:14 and 74:24–26. [2 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 74:24/q13/p2 Other Qur’anic passages portray similar fabricated labels and the deniers’ failure. — Ibn Kathīr says the Qur’an elsewhere describes the disbelievers inventing comparisons and losing the way. Study Quran cites other opponents calling revelations manifest sorcery at 5:110, 6:7, 11:7, 34:43 and 37:15. [2 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]

## 74:24/q14 What ruling about learning and practicing magic is drawn here?  [* · law]
- 74:24/q14/p1 Learning it for a sound purpose is allowed, but believing in its reality and practicing it are disbelief. — Bursevī cites a couplet saying knowledge of evil can protect a person from it. [1 notes: BURSEVI]
