# 2:258: 75 questions

## 2:258/q01 What does 'Have you not seen' mean here?  [أَلَمْ تَرَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the expression is explained by its knowledge/sight sense or its interrogative rhetorical force; these can coexist.
- 2:258/q01/p1 It calls Muhammad or the hearer to know the astonishing account with the certainty of inward sight or a reliable report. — Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr specify sight of the heart; Bursevī says God's report yields the certainty of sight; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the sight figurative. Dāmghānī compares 105:1 and 69:7. [21 notes: TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; DAMGHANI; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ZAJJAJ; TABRISI]
- 2:258/q01/p2 The interrogative arrests attention, affirms the story, and invites amazement at the disputant's audacity. — Al-Farrāʾ, Zajjāj, Wāḥidī, Rāzī, Qurtubī, and Ālūsī take its interrogative form as wonder or confirmation; Burhān likens it to an alerting command. [18 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء); JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURHAN; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 2:258/q02 How is 'Have you not seen' read?  [أَلَمْ تَرَ · readings]
turns on: The final rāʾ's vowel in connected recitation.
- 2:258/q02/p1 ʿAlī reads the final rāʾ with sukūn, dropping the yāʾ or carrying pause pronunciation into connection. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Qurtubī, Abū Ḥayyān, and Samīn transmit it; Samīn recalls its analysis at 2:243. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); QURTUBI-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); SAMIN-DURR]
- 2:258/q02/p2 The majority vocalize the final rāʾ in connected speech. — Qurtubī contrasts this with ʿAlī's pausal/jussive form. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب)]

## 2:258/q03 What does the address imply about Muhammad's knowledge of earlier history?  [أَلَمْ تَرَ · theology]
- 2:258/q03/p1 His knowledge of this earlier episode must come from revelation, confirming his prophethood. — Zajjāj argues that the unlettered Muhammad had neither learned the Torah and Gospel nor had a human teacher, excluding the other proposed sources. [2 notes: ZAJJAJ]

- 2:258/q04/p1 Its wording and analysis were discussed already at parallel occurrences. — Samīn refers to 2:243; Abū Ḥayyān says he does not repeat his earlier treatment. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]

## 2:258/q05 What does the preposition in 'Have you seen to the one' do?  [إِلَى ٱلَّذِى · rhetoric]
- 2:258/q05/p1 It is Arabic idiom for directing attention in wonder to such a remarkable person. — Al-Farrāʾ gives Arab usage; Wāḥidī says sight reaches an extraordinary endpoint; Ṭabrisī likens it to a comparison marker. [8 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء); JISHUMI; TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); BURHAN]

## 2:258/q07 What ancestry and status are reported for Nimrod?  [ٱلَّذِى · reports]
turns on: The competing genealogies and historical descriptions in transmitted accounts.
- 2:258/q07/p1 He was the first tyrant, claimed divinity, wore a crown, and owned or built Babylon's tower. — Qatāda, al-Rabīʿ, and later sources transmit the tower and tyranny; Muqātil also calls him earth's first ruler. [20 notes: MUQATIL; ABDURRAZZAQ(الكلبي وقتادة); TAB-FULL(الربيع,قتادة); IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI(مجاهد); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(قتادة); TABRISI(مجاهد وغيره); QURTUBI-FULL(قتادة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قتادة); IBNKATHIR-FULL; DURR-FULL(قتادة); ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(bazı kaynaklar)]
- 2:258/q07/p2 Four people ruled the world: believing Solomon and Dhū al-Qarnayn, unbelieving Nimrod and Nebuchadnezzar. — Mujāhid or Ibn ʿAbbās transmit this list; Ibn Kathīr adds 'God knows best'; Bursevī gives an additional identification of Nebuchadnezzar with Shaddād. [10 notes: TAB-FULL(مجاهد); THALABI(مجاهد); BAGHAWI-FULL(مجاهد); IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(قوم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(مجاهد); IBNKATHIR-FULL(مجاهد); DURR-FULL(مجاهد); BURSEVI(مجاهد)]
- 2:258/q07/p3 He descends through Canaan and Cush; reports vary in their intermediate names and order. — Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Qurtubī, Ibn Kathīr, and Ālūsī transmit differing Canaan/Cush/Shem or Ham lines; the variation does not change the identity. [9 notes: TAB-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(مجاهد وقتادة والربيع والسدي وابن إسحاق وزيد بن أسلم); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس ومجاهد وقتادة والربيع والسدي وابن إسحاق وزيد بن أسلم وغيرهم,السهيلي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(السهيلي,مجاهد وقتادة والربيع والسدي وابن إسحاق وزيد بن أسلم); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 2:258/q07/p4 He descends through Falikh or Faligh, Eber/ʿĀbir, Shalikh and Arfakhshadh to Shem. — Al-Ṭabarī gives the short Falikh line; Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Qurtubī, and Ibn Kathīr expand it. [4 notes: TAB-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عطية); IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 2:258/q07/p5 He was the first king on earth. — Ibn Jurayj and some reports say so; Ibn ʿAṭiyya rejects the claim and Qurtubī and Abū Ḥayyān repeat his objection. [2 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن جريج); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن جريج)-; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن جريج)-; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن جريج)-]
- 2:258/q07/p6 Nimrod the elder ruled four hundred years; a separate younger Nimrod ruled one year. — Qurtubī transmits this distinction; Ibn Kathīr also mentions four hundred years for the tyrant. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL]

## 2:258/q08 What inward reading is given to Nimrod and his kingdom?  [ٱلَّذِى حَآجَّ إِبْرَٰهِۦمَ ٱلْمُلْكَ · inward]
- 2:258/q08/p1 Nimrod can symbolize the commanding soul, his kingdom bodily powers and the lower world, and Abraham the sacred spirit. — Ālūsī allows this alongside the historical account; he reads Nimrod's life-and-death boast as turning powers toward pleasure or restraining them. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:258/q09 What other historical comparison is reported beside Nimrod?  [ٱلَّذِى · reports]
- 2:258/q09/p1 The tyrant al-Ḍaḥḥāk was killed by Afrīdūn; reports differ on his lineage and associate him with Nimrod or with a distinct earlier ruler. — Qurtubī says he ruled a thousand years and first crucified and amputated; Abū Ḥayyān relays al-Suhaylī and Abū Tammām's poetic comparison; Qurtubī also gives al-Suhaylī's separate lineage. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(السهيلي,حبيب); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(السهيلي)]

## 2:258/q10 What does 'disputed' mean and what was the dispute about?  [حَآجَّ فِى رَبِّهِۦٓ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the focus is the verb's reciprocal force or the precise claim contested.
- 2:258/q10/p1 Ḥājja means to contend by proof and counterproof, seeking to prevail over the other. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya derives the reciprocal form from ḥujja; Ṭabāṭabāʾī traces ḥujja to intention. Ibn ʿĀshūr notes the form lacks a known simple stem and commonly implies contentious, even false, disputation. [17 notes: THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(الصادق); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 2:258/q10/p2 They argued over Abraham's Lord, His existence and exclusive right to be worshipped, against the king's claim of divinity. — Ibn Kathīr compares Pharaoh at 28:38; Ṭabāṭabāʾī sees a contest over exclusive lordship within a culture that admitted other gods. [10 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI; JISHUMI(مجاهد); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]
- 2:258/q10/p3 The king presented a false argument as though it were proof. — Ālūsī explains why the verse still calls the exchange disputation; Ibn ʿĀshūr notes the verb often denotes contentious false debate. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 2:258/q11 When and where did the debate happen?  [حَآجَّ إِبْرَٰهِۦمَ · reports]
turns on: The reported chronology relative to the idols and fire, and the grain-rationing setting.
- 2:258/q11/p1 It followed the breaking of idols and preceded Abraham's being thrown into the fire; Nimrod imprisoned and questioned him. — Muqātil's account has Nimrod release Abraham for burning; al-Ṣādiq alternatively places the dispute before the idols were broken. [15 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI(مقاتل); JISHUMI(مقاتل); BAGHAWI-FULL(مقاتل); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(زيد بن أسلم); RAZI-FULL(مقاتل); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(السدي); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ما روي,مقاتل); ELMALILI]
- 2:258/q11/p2 It occurred after Abraham survived the fire. — Al-Suddī and others place it then, often at the food-distribution court; Ālūsī transmits an al-Ṣādiq report to this effect, conflicting with Ṭabrisī's other attribution. [10 notes: THALABI(آخرون); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(آخرون); IBNATIYYA-FULL(السدي); TABRISI(الصادق); QURTUBI-FULL(السدي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); IBNKATHIR-FULL(السدي); ALUSI-FULL(جعفر الصادق)]
- 2:258/q11/p3 The debate was at Nimrod's food court, where visitors had to acknowledge him as lord to obtain grain. — Ibn Zayd locates Nimrod at Mosul; reports say he refused Abraham food after the exchange. [11 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(زيد بن أسلم); TAB-FULL(عبد الرحمن بن زيد بن أسلم); IBNABIHATIM(زيد بن أسلم); THALABI(زيد بن أسلم عن معمر); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس في رواية عطاء); IBNATIYYA-FULL(زيد بن أسلم); QURTUBI-FULL(زيد بن أسلم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 2:258/q11/p4 One al-Ṣādiq report places it before the idols were broken and before the fire. — This differs from Muqātil's and another al-Ṣādiq chronology. [1 notes: TABRISI(الصادق)]

## 2:258/q12 What provision reached Abraham after Nimrod denied him food?  [حَآجَّ إِبْرَٰهِۦمَ · reports]
turns on: The reported material brought home and the resulting food.
- 2:258/q12/p1 Sand or earth carried home in sacks became fine flour or food for his household. — Ibn Zayd says Sarah found fine grain or flour and Abraham recognized God's provision; accounts vary whether he brought sand to comfort or distract the children. - 2:258/q12/p2 Red earth became red wheat, whose grain grew densely along the stalk. — Abū Ṣāliḥ's report via Ibn Abī Shayba gives this specific variant. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(أبو صالح)]

## 2:258/q13 What prior culture and beliefs frame the king's claim?  [حَآجَّ إِبْرَٰهِۦمَ فِى رَبِّهِۦٓ · theology]
- 2:258/q13/p1 People became attached to sensory idols and intermediary gods; powerful rulers exploited their visible authority to claim superior divinity. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī discusses innate awareness of higher powers, divergent planetary cults, and Pharaoh's claim of supremacy while recognizing other gods. [6 notes: TABATABAI]

## 2:258/q14 How are Abraham's name and the nearby yāʾ read?  [إِبْرَٰهِۦمَ · readings]
- 2:258/q14/p1 The name has a canonical dialectal form Ibrāhām, with long ā replacing ī; the three instances follow the same variant. — Qiraat-ER notes Arabization of the Hebrew name and no difference in reference. [3 notes: QIRAAT-ER]

## 2:258/q15 Whose Lord is meant by 'his Lord'?  [رَبِّهِۦٓ · referent]
turns on: The antecedent of the pronoun; several commentators allow both.
- 2:258/q15/p1 It is Abraham's Lord. — Rāzī compares 6:80; Ṭabāṭabāʾī points to Abraham's later words 'my Lord'; Bursevī and Ālūsī see an honorific assurance of divine support. [9 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; RAZI-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 2:258/q15/p2 It can refer to the disputant's Lord, the same God who is Abraham's Lord. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Nasafī, Biqāʿī, and Samīn admit the grammatical possibility. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL]

## 2:258/q16 How does the grant of kingship relate to the dispute?  [أَنْ ءَاتَىٰهُ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْمُلْكَ · grammar]
turns on: Causal lām versus a temporal interpretation of an and whether the grant and dispute were simultaneous.
- 2:258/q16/p1 The clause is causal: kingship led Nimrod to arrogance and disputation. — Qurtubī and Samīn analyze an omitted causal lām; Ālūsī prefers causation over a strained temporal reading; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says naming the divine favor exposes ingratitude. [32 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; MAWARDI-FULL(الحسن); WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(الحسن والجبائي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء); BIQAI-FULL(الحرالي); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(bazı kaynaklar); ALUSI-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 2:258/q16/p2 The phrase highlights Nimrod's perverse ingratitude: he repaid God's favor with opposition. — Kashshāf and Bayḍāwī compare 56:82; Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites the Sanmar poem and 28:8 for ironic reversal. [12 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI(الشاعر)]
- 2:258/q16/p3 The clause is temporal: he disputed when God gave him kingship. — Kashshāf allows an as a time expression; Abū Ḥayyān, Samīn, and Ālūsī object that the grant and debate were not simultaneous unless the time is extended. [8 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURHAN(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري)-; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري)-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 2:258/q17 What is the grammatical status of 'because God granted him kingship'?  [أَنْ ءَاتَىٰهُ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْمُلْكَ · grammar]
- 2:258/q17/p1 It is accusative under an understood causal lām; a causal clause with distinct agents may also be genitive after deletion of the preposition. — Qurtubī gives the accusative analysis; Samīn gives both case possibilities and explains why it is not a same-agent purpose object; Ālūsī agrees. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:258/q18 Who received the kingship?  [ءَاتَىٰهُ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْمُلْكَ · referent]

- 2:258/q18/p1 God gave worldly kingship to the disputant Nimrod. — Zajjāj says the opponent displayed a king's power while Abraham was a prophet; Rāzī cites majority reports and the arrogance implied by the grant; Ālūsī cites early tradition; Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites Nimrod's visible sovereignty. - 2:258/q18/p2 God gave Abraham kingship, understood as prophetic or lawful authority. — Abū Ḥudhayfa, al-Mahdawī, Abū al-Qāsim al-Balkhī, and others invoke 2:124 and 4:54 or argue God would not give binding authority to an unbeliever; Rāzī notes the nearest-antecedent argument. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Ālūsī reject this as forced or contrary to the reports. ## 2:258/q19 Why is God explicitly named in the grant and sun challenge?  [ءَاتَىٰهُ ٱللَّهُ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ · rhetoric]
- 2:258/q19/p1 The explicit divine name shifts from a pronoun or 'my Lord' to assert God's lordship over both disputants and block Nimrod's verbal misapplication. — Ālūsī notes the pronoun-to-name shift and its reply to Nimrod's 'I'; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says it prevents the king from reusing Abraham's 'my Lord' wording. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 2:258/q20 Why does Nimrod's sovereignty make Abraham's stance striking?  [ءَاتَىٰهُ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْمُلْكَ · rhetoric]
- 2:258/q20/p1 Abraham publicly confronted a powerful unbelieving sovereign while himself lacking the king's worldly power. — Rāzī prefers this as a reason to identify Nimrod as recipient of kingship; al-Aṣamm says a kingly Abraham would have stopped Nimrod's killing, but the Qāḍī replies spiritual and coercive powers can coexist. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL(أبو بكر الأصم); RAZI-FULL+; RAZI-FULL(القاضي)-]

## 2:258/q21 What kind of kingship may God give an unbeliever?  [ٱلْمُلْكَ · theology]
turns on: Whether mulk means factual control or rightful religious authority, and whether Nimrod was an unbeliever when granted it.
- 2:258/q21/p1 Worldly sovereignty, wealth, followers, and coercive power may be given to a disbeliever; it can be a trial. — Zajjāj cites 3:26; Jishumī and Ṭabrisī distinguish worldly dominion from rightful command; Ālūsī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī say this still is God's grant. Bursevī challenges the Muʿtazilite aṣlaḥ doctrine. [27 notes: ZAJJAJ; JISHUMI(أبو مسلم,الحسن وأبو علي وأبو مسلم); KASHSHAF-FULL(قيل); TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور,الزمخشري); BIQAI-FULL(الحرالي); BURSEVI(مجاهد); ALUSI-FULL(بعض الإمامية); ELMALILI; TABATABAI]
- 2:258/q21/p2 Binding, rightful kingship and obligatory obedience cannot be granted to a known corrupter. — Abū al-Qāsim al-Balkhī and some Imāmīs cite divine covenant and separate rightful authority from Nimrod's coercion; Abū Ḥayyān says spiritual authority can coexist with an oppressor's political power. [8 notes: TABRISI(أبو القاسم البلخي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري,المهدوي); ALUSI-FULL(بعض الإمامية); TABATABAI]
- 2:258/q21/p3 Nimrod may have received rule while a believer and later disbelieved. — Abū Muslim and Rāzī present this as a way to reconcile the grant with later unbelief. [2 notes: JISHUMI(أبو مسلم); RAZI-FULL]

## 2:258/q22 What does the episode teach rulers about sovereignty?  [ٱلْمُلْكَ · interpretation]
- 2:258/q22/p1 Sovereignty is a divine trust or deputyship; gratitude and justice preserve it, while arrogant claims to divinity ruin ruler and subjects. — Elmalılı contrasts Nimrod with David and Solomon, says power is a trial, and warns that changing truth and ruling by desire lead to destruction. ## 2:258/q23 What answer is given to the claim that Abraham's family received kingship?  [ٱلْمُلْكَ · interpretation]
- 2:258/q23/p1 The cited grant to Abraham's family does not prove Abraham personally ruled. — Rāzī replies to use of 4:54 for the Abraham-pronoun reading. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 2:258/q24 How is 'when Abraham said' attached syntactically?  [إِذْ قَالَ إِبْرَٰهِۦمُ · grammar]

- 2:258/q24/p1 The time clause is governed by ḥājja: the disputation happened when Abraham spoke. — Kashshāf, Abū Ḥayyān, Samīn, Bursevī, and Ālūsī support this; Samīn calls it clearest. [7 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 2:258/q24/p2 It depends on ātāhu or substitutes for the preceding grant clause as a time expression. — Kashshāf and Abū al-Baqāʾ propose this; Abū Ḥayyān and Samīn object that the two events have different times; Abū al-Baqāʾ conditions it on a temporal, rather than causal, an. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء,الزمخشري)-; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان)-]
- 2:258/q24/p3 It depends on 'have you seen'. — Makkī's proposal is rejected by Samīn since the seeing did not occur at the time Abraham spoke. [0 notes: SAMIN-DURR(مكي)-]

## 2:258/q25 What unstated speech prompts Abraham's reply?  [إِذْ قَالَ إِبْرَٰهِۦمُ رَبِّىَ · rhetoric]
- 2:258/q25/p1 The king first asked Abraham, 'Who is your Lord?' and the question is omitted because Abraham's answer makes it clear. — Rāzī compares Moses and Pharaoh in 43:46 and 26:23; Biqāʿī infers an earlier exchange and claim of shared lordship. [15 notes: THALABI(مقاتل); WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ما روي)]

## 2:258/q26 How is 'my Lord, the one who gives life and death' parsed?  [رَبِّىَ ٱلَّذِى يُحْىِۦ وَيُمِيتُ · grammar]
- 2:258/q26/p1 Rabbī is the subject and its relative clause the predicate; the whole assertion is the object of 'said'. — Abū Ḥayyān and Samīn give the clause analysis; Samīn also parses ana uḥyī analogously. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]

## 2:258/q27 Why use a relative clause for God's action but a finite clause for the king's?  [رَبِّىَ ٱلَّذِى يُحْىِۦ وَيُمِيتُ أَنَا۠ أُحْىِۦ وَأُمِيتُ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the wording marks a mere shared claim or Nimrod's claim of exclusive lordship.
- 2:258/q27/p1 The relative predicate marks God's exclusive possession of life and death; Nimrod's bare verb avoids claiming explicit exclusivity. — Abū Ḥayyān and Samīn say the king could not claim that none other gives life and death, though Ṭabāṭabāʾī reads his reply as claiming exclusive lordship. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]
- 2:258/q27/p2 The king's uncoordinated 'I' asserts that he himself is the Lord Abraham described, claiming supremacy rather than partnership. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī reads the absence of a conjunction and omission of other gods as signs of this aim. [3 notes: TABATABAI]

## 2:258/q28 How is 'my Lord' read?  [رَبِّىَ · readings]
turns on: The vowel or sukūn on the first-person yāʾ.
- 2:258/q28/p1 The yāʾ is quiescent, giving rabbī; Ḥamza and al-Aʿmash are named. — Thalabī, Baghawī, Nashr, and others transmit it; Baghawī lists parallel yāʾ contexts. - 2:258/q28/p2 The yāʾ is pronounced with fatḥa in rabbiya before the following article; this is the common reading. — Jishumī relates the opening to the following alif; Qiraat-ER calls it the more emphatic declaration. [4 notes: THALABI(الأعمش وحمزة وعيسى); JISHUMI(الأعمش وحمزة); QIRAAT-ER]

## 2:258/q29 How does Abraham's opening function as correction of Nimrod's claim?  [رَبِّىَ ٱلَّذِى يُحْىِۦ وَيُمِيتُ · rhetoric]
- 2:258/q29/p1 It reverses Nimrod's mistaken attribution of life and death to himself. — Itqān classifies this as qasr qalb, correction of a reversed attribution. [1 notes: ITQAN]

## 2:258/q30 What does God's giving life and death mean?  [يُحْىِۦ وَيُمِيتُ · interpretation]
turns on: The scope of life and death: origination generally, animal life, or resurrection in particular.
- 2:258/q30/p1 God actually originates life and death in bodies, creates beings after nonexistence, and can restore the dead. — Al-Ṭabarī says He gives and takes life by will; Rāzī argues that changing life and death require a freely acting creator, not a fixed necessary cause; Ṭabāṭabāʾī stresses conscious animal life; Elmalılı includes resurrection. [23 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(الحرالي); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 2:258/q30/p2 The divine act includes giving life by resurrection and death after a former life. — Baghawī says Abraham's first proof concerned resurrection; Tustarī's surrounding readings connect direct witnessing of revival to certainty. [7 notes: TUSTARI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); BAGHAWI-FULL(أكثر المفسرين); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 2:258/q31 Why mention life before death and choose it as the first sign?  [يُحْىِۦ وَيُمِيتُ · rhetoric]
- 2:258/q31/p1 Life is the first foundational blessing and a particularly clear sign of the Creator. — Ṭabrisī stresses life as the first favor and death without bodily injury; Jishumī says the sign recalls divine unity and favor; Rāzī says life's observable marvels outnumber death's, comparing 2:28, 67:2, 26:81. [5 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 2:258/q32 Are the objects of 'gives life and death' deliberately omitted?  [يُحْىِۦ وَيُمِيتُ · grammar]
- 2:258/q32/p1 Yes; the focus is God's power itself, not particular recipients. — Burhān compares Moses' question about idols that neither hear nor see. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 2:258/q33 What is the point of naming life and death as signs of God?  [يُحْىِۦ وَيُمِيتُ · theology]
turns on: The kind of inference drawn from divine acts and the relative force of the two signs.
- 2:258/q33/p1 Acts no creature can originate are the way to infer a freely acting Creator, and life is an especially clear instance. — Rāzī argues contingent changes and diverse animal forms cannot proceed from an unchanging necessary cause; Jishumī and Ṭabrisī infer God from unique acts; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says innate awareness of superior powers survives human neglect. [14 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI]
- 2:258/q33/p2 Creating life is stronger evidence than altering the sun's course, though unjust observers trivialize it. — Elmalılı distinguishes the strength of the sign from audience response. [1 notes: ELMALILI]

## 2:258/q34 Was Nimrod's killing-and-sparing actually performed in the Qur'anic account?  [قَالَ أَنَا۠ أُحْىِۦ وَأُمِيتُ · reports]
- 2:258/q34/p1 The verse reports his claim but does not state that he carried out the killing and sparing. — Rāzī distinguishes the Qur'anic wording from the extra-Qur'anic staging reports. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 2:258/q35 Why might Abraham leave the first false claim unanswered?  [قَالَ أَنَا۠ أُحْىِۦ وَأُمِيتُ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether silence communicates the obviousness of the fallacy or leaves a public doubt.
- 2:258/q35/p1 Its falsehood was obvious, so an immediate verbal refutation was unnecessary. — Bursevī and Ālūsī say the equivocation did not deserve prolonged response; Rāzī objects that leaving a seemingly damaging doubt unanswered could confuse an audience. [3 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; RAZI-FULL-]
- 2:258/q35/p2 A visible objection should be answered at once to avoid making the prophet appear defeated. — Rāzī says the apparent shift might concede weakness; Ālūsī reports and rejects that concern. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); ALUSI-FULL-]

## 2:258/q41 What does the sun challenge prove?  [ٱللَّهَ يَأْتِى بِٱلشَّمْسِ مِنَ ٱلْمَشْرِقِ فَأْتِ بِهَا مِنَ ٱلْمَغْرِبِ · interpretation]
turns on: The force assigned to the westward-sun demand and its eschatological extension.
- 2:258/q41/p1 The real Lord governs the sun; a claimant to comparable divinity must reverse its rising, which Nimrod cannot do. — Al-Ṭabarī states the demand conditionally; Ibn Kathīr joins control of life with heavenly bodies; al-Biqāʿī notes the sun rose long before Nimrod; Ālūsī calls the imperative an incapacitating challenge. ## 2:258/q42 How are the sun's movement and its directions constructed grammatically?  [يَأْتِى بِٱلشَّمْسِ مِنَ ٱلْمَشْرِقِ فَأْتِ بِهَا مِنَ ٱلْمَغْرِبِ · grammar]

- 2:258/q44/p1 God compels the sun against its apparent natural course; Nimrod would have to reverse that imposed motion. — Nasafī compares an ant walking on a millstone while water carries it the opposite way. [2 notes: NASAFI-FULL]

## 2:258/q46 What inward reading is given to the sun challenge?  [ٱلشَّمْسِ مِنَ ٱلْمَشْرِقِ مِنَ ٱلْمَغْرِبِ · inward]
- 2:258/q46/p1 The sun can signify gnosis rising from its eastern source; the westward demand tests the soul or evokes the spirit's return after setting in the body. — Ālūsī offers these alternatives. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:258/q47 What ancillary point is made about imagery of sun, day, and night?  [ٱلشَّمْسِ · rhetoric]
- 2:258/q47/p1 Comparing a benefaction to the sun mainly conveys universality, with comfort and brightness as possible further associations. — ʿAbd al-Qāhir says al-Nābigha's daytime line expects night afterward; a beloved may be called one's day and night, while calling a praised person's anger 'night' directly is disliked because of its darkness. [4 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR]

## 2:258/q48 Why could Nimrod not counter by claiming the eastern sunrise or demanding a western sunrise from God?  [فَأْتِ بِهَا مِنَ ٱلْمَغْرِبِ فَبُهِتَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether his silence was divinely imposed, based on knowledge and fear, obvious to the public, or an objection to the reconstruction.
- 2:258/q48/p1 God deprived him of that reply to support Abraham and establish a sign. — Māwardī, Wāḥidī, Jishumī, Baghawī, Rāzī, and Nasafī transmit this; Baghawī prefers it. Ālūsī calls the forgetfulness explanation weak. [8 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; TABRISI; BAGHAWI-FULL+; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 2:258/q48/p2 He knew from Abraham's survival of the fire or other signs that God could reverse the sun, and feared added disgrace. — Māwardī, Wāḥidī, Jishumī, Rāzī, Baghawī, and Ṭabrisī give this reason. [7 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 2:258/q48/p3 The crowd knew the sun rose before Nimrod; a claim to cause its regular rising would plainly be false. — Legal theorists and Qurtubī say the audience would call him a liar; Abū Ḥayyān says he knew he could not reverse it. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الأصوليون); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 2:258/q48/p4 The supposed counterchallenge exposes a weakness in the familiar reconstruction of the debate. — Rāzī argues that waiting for a westward sunrise would replace the original proof and leave Abraham's silence unexplained; Ālūsī replies the objection also presses Rāzī's version. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); RAZI-FULL+; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام)-]

## 2:258/q49 What does 'he was confounded' mean?  [فَبُهِتَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the context uses the core sense of false accusation or the resulting sense of stunned silence.
- 2:258/q49/p1 He became stunned, perplexed, speechless, and lost his argument. — Al-Ṭabarī distinguishes bewilderment from false accusation; Wāḥidī quotes ʿUrwa's verse; al-Biqāʿī derives a frozen state; most later interpreters treat it as defeat by proof. - 2:258/q49/p2 The root can mean confronting someone with a lie or slander, from which bewilderment is figuratively extended. — Wāḥidī and Ṭabrisī explain the semantic shift; Jishumī and Samīn cite a Prophetic description of Jews as people of falsehood; Tabrisī compares 4:20. [11 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI(النبي); TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(حديث); SAMIN-DURR]

- 2:258/q50/p5 The active reading can mean the defeated Nimrod insulted or falsely accused Abraham. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Qurtubī transmit this alternative; Ibn Jinnī says he may have tried to confound Abraham but failed. ## 2:258/q51 What happened when Nimrod's argument was defeated?  [فَبُهِتَ · reports]
- 2:258/q51/p1 In one account, Nimrod resorted to ordering Abraham burned. — Qurtubī comments that tyrants use punishment when they lose argument and God saved Abraham. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]

## 2:258/q52 Why call the opponent 'the one who disbelieved'?  [ٱلَّذِى كَفَرَ · rhetoric]
- 2:258/q52/p1 The relative description explains his defeat by naming his disbelief as the cause, and marks his disputation itself as disbelief. — Bursevī stresses the causal implication; al-Biqāʿī says it exposes denial of God's exclusive power. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI]

## 2:258/q53 What happened to Nimrod after the encounter?  [ٱلَّذِى كَفَرَ · reports]
turns on: The different durations, forty days versus four hundred years, and the details of his end.
- 2:258/q53/p1 A swarm of gnats or mosquitoes destroyed his army; one entered his nose or brain and killed him after torment. — Muqātil and Ibn ʿAbbās give forty days or nights; Zayd b. Aslam's version gives four hundred years matching his reign, often after an angel's summons and the king's challenge to God. Qurtubī adds hammer blows. [8 notes: MUQATIL; ABDURRAZZAQ(زيد بن أسلم); TAB-FULL(زيد بن أسلم); THALABI; TABRISI(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(زيد بن أسلم); BURSEVI]
- 2:258/q53/p2 His tower reaching toward heaven was struck down at its foundations. — Zayd b. Aslam and Bursevī connect this to 16:26. [3 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(زيد بن أسلم); TAB-FULL(زيد بن أسلم); BURSEVI]

## 2:258/q54 What is the grammatical role of 'the one who disbelieved'?  [ٱلَّذِى كَفَرَ · grammar]
- 2:258/q54/p1 It is nominative as the verb's subject or passive subject, depending on the reading. — Jishumī treats it as nominative by its relation to the verb; Samīn gives the passive-subject analysis. [2 notes: JISHUMI; SAMIN-DURR]

## 2:258/q55 What does 'God does not guide the wrongdoing people' mean here?  [وَٱللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِى ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the withheld guidance concerns argument, enabling aid, faith, or the afterlife, and whom 'wrongdoers' includes.
- 2:258/q55/p1 He does not guide them to a valid proof or successful argument against truth. — Muqātil links the statement to Nimrod and 9:19; al-Ṭabarī says falsehood has no prevailing argument; Rāzī rejects saying there is a valid argument for unbelief that God withholds. [20 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(مجاهد,محمد بن إسحاق); IBNABIHATIM(محمد بن إسحاق); DAMGHANI; JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(مقاتل); NASAFI-FULL; BURHAN; ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; RAZI-FULL(القاضي)-]
- 2:258/q55/p2 He does not lead persistent wrongdoers to faith and truth; their injustice blocks their benefit from clear proof. — Rāzī prefers this contextual account and cites 6:111; Abū Ḥayyān prefers those decreed to persist in unbelief; Ṭabāṭabāʾī makes injustice the cause of withholding guidance, not the effect. Al-Suddī explicitly says faith. [15 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(المهدوي); DURR-FULL(السدي); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES(Muhammad 'Abduh); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; RAZI-FULL+]
- 2:258/q55/p3 He does not assist their corrupt ends or give them enabling grace and success. — Māwardī, Jishumī, Ṭabrisī, Nasafī, and Bayḍāwī present this sense; Rāzī rejects a version that says God withholds a rationally impossible favor. [9 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI; TABRISI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; RAZI-FULL(القاضي)-]
- 2:258/q55/p4 He does not guide them to Paradise or save them from punishment. — Māwardī, Jishumī, Ṭabrisī, Bayḍāwī, Bursevī and Ālūsī report this; Rāzī rejects it as distant from the debate context. [7 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI; TABRISI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; RAZI-FULL(القاضي)-]
- 2:258/q55/p5 The general wording particularly concerns unbelievers or those who die unjust; it does not deny guidance to repentant wrongdoers. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya limits the warning in light of possible repentance; Ṭabrisī distinguishes general and believer-specific senses of guidance, comparing 41:17; Abū Ḥayyān restricts it to decreed persistence. [9 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(المهدوي); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:258/q56 What does the verse imply about divine success or punishment after the debate?  [وَٱللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِى · interpretation]
- 2:258/q56/p1 The proof prevailed but God did not inspire the wrongdoer to accept it; his false argument and punishment follow. — Ibn Kathīr stresses invalid proof and divine anger; Elmalılı says acceptance of truth would have prevented the darkness. [2 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 2:258/q57 How does divine guidance operate when injustice obstructs it?  [لَا يَهْدِى · theology]
- 2:258/q57/p1 God guides every creature through real causal paths to its ends; injustice substitutes false paths and so fails. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī compares 20:50 and 11:56, fire and plant growth, education, corrupt offices, and the eventual exposure of false acts; truth endures while imitation fails. [7 notes: TABATABAI]

## 2:258/q58 Does God’s lack of guidance here conflict with guidance given elsewhere to wrongdoers?  [لَا يَهْدِى · theology]
- 2:258/q58/p1 No; guidance has general and special senses, and guidance toward a path differs from aid to follow it or entry to Paradise. — Ṭabrisī compares the guidance of Thamūd at 41:17 and his discussion at 2:26; Ibn ʿAṭiyya adds that a wrongdoer can repent. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI]

## 2:258/q59 Why is the final denial of guidance so concise?  [لَا يَهْدِى · rhetoric]
- 2:258/q59/p1 Its short general form applies beyond Nimrod and conveys more meaning. — Al-Biqāʿī cites al-Imām for the rhetorical force of brevity. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 2:258/q60 Who are 'the wrongdoing people' in this verse?  [ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · referent]
turns on: Specific unbelief versus the broader pattern of injustice.
- 2:258/q60/p1 It points first to Nimrod or disbelievers who deny God's power. — Muqātil identifies Nimrod; Ibn al-Jawzī equates wrongdoers with unbelievers; al-Biqāʿī includes wrongful killing and sparing. [8 notes: MUQATIL; MAWARDI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(مقاتل); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 2:258/q60/p2 It applies generally to those who turn from truth toward falsehood and put things out of place. — Māwardī gives the broader sense; al-Ṭabarī calls such denial self-wronging; Ṭabāṭabāʾī defines injustice by deviation from proper action. [5 notes: TAB-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES(Muhammad 'Abduh)]

## 2:258/q61 What does this debate establish about religious argument?  [* · law]
turns on: The status, conditions, and epistemic implications of religious disputation.
- 2:258/q61/p1 Reasoned disputation and adducing proof are permissible, and may be required to call others to truth. — Qurtubī cites 2:111, 10:68, 21:52, 11:32-35, 3:61 and 3:66, plus Prophetic and Companion debates; Nasafī says prophets cannot perform forbidden debate; Jishumī and Ṭabrisī use it against blind imitation. [17 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ITQAN; ALUSI-FULL(الكيا); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 2:258/q61/p2 The argument validates inference and shows knowledge of God is acquired rather than innate necessity. — Jishumī and Ṭabrisī say a debate over a necessary truth would be pointless; it refutes blind imitation. Ṭabāṭabāʾī separately says innate disposition points people to a maker. [11 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; ITQAN; TABATABAI]
- 2:258/q61/p3 Debate should seek God's approval and accept the truth that appears. — Al-Muzanī states this condition; another view says usefulness also depends on comparable religion, reason, understanding and fairness. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(المزني,قوم)]

## 2:258/q62 How does this episode relate to the preceding verse about divine guardianship?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the episode illustrates both sides of 2:257 or chiefly the disbeliever's side.
- 2:258/q62/p1 It illustrates God guiding and supporting Abraham, His ally, and leaving the unbelieving ally of false powers confounded. — Jishumī, Ṭabrisī, Abū Ḥayyān, Biqāʿī, Ibn ʿĀshūr, Elmalılı and Ṭabāṭabāʾī connect it to 2:257; Abū Ḥayyān compares God's party in 5:56 and 58:22. [16 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 2:258/q62/p2 It primarily evidences the preceding claim that unbelievers' guardians are false powers; later stories display God's guardianship of believers. — Ālūsī reports this alternative sequencing. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]

## 2:258/q63 How do the three adjacent stories fit together?  [* · links]
turns on: The thematic and syntactic relation among 2:258–260.
- 2:258/q63/p1 They successively display the Creator's power, resurrection, and guidance through argument or witnessed signs. — Rāzī says the first proves the Creator and the second resurrection; Biqāʿī relates 2:243 and the ruined town; Ṭabāṭabāʾī allows three examples or three grades of guidance. [9 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ELMALILI]
- 2:258/q63/p2 The Nimrod and passerby accounts are coordinated through 'have you seen someone like', with the comparison understood. — Al-Farrāʾ, Thaʿlabī, Qurtubī, Burhān, and Bursevī use 2:259 to explain the continuation; some Basrans make kāf extra. [6 notes: FARRA; THALABI(بعض نحاة البصرة); QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); BURHAN; BURSEVI]
- 2:258/q63/p3 The varying dialogue forms stimulate attention and allow each story's distinct contextual effect. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī notes the succession of rhetorical patterns; Abū Ḥayyān says repeated qāla clauses can omit conjunction when meaning itself connects them. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURHAN; TABATABAI]

## 2:258/q64 What connection does the verse have with Abraham's other Qur'anic arguments?  [* · links]
turns on: Which Abraham passages are used as parallels.
- 2:258/q64/p1 God had given Abraham this argument against his people, as 6:83 says. — Al-Ṭabarī links the debate to 6:83; Abū Ḥayyān's and Qurtubī's discussions place it among Abraham's disputes. [2 notes: TAB-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 2:258/q64/p2 The episode's proof of divine unity and creation connects to Abraham's account of the sun in 6:78. — Study Quran notes the other rising-sun context; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says Abraham used a less abstract proof here than his own earlier insight at 6:79. [2 notes: TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]

## 2:258/q65 What does the verse imply about God's support and the believer's conduct before a tyrant?  [* · interpretation]
- 2:258/q65/p1 A believer should answer a false divine claim clearly and rely on God; Abraham's composure and victory demonstrate divine favor. — Elmalılı contrasts Abraham with people who tremble before tyrants and calls the concise debate a model; Ālūsī says the opening consoles Muhammad by recalling God's aid. [6 notes: ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 2:258/q66 Why are the three stories ordered with this one first?  [* · rhetoric]
- 2:258/q66/p1 The Nimrod story is immediately paired with its referent, foregrounds an astonishing self-contained encounter, and leaves later detailed narratives together. — Ālūsī gives these reasons for the order. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:258/q67 What does the account show about Qur'anic dialectic?  [* · rhetoric]
- 2:258/q67/p1 The Qur'an uses premises, conclusions, counterargument, and transitions when an opponent evades a proof. — Itqān points to Abraham's debate with Nimrod and his people; a subsequent entry calls the shift to sunrise intiqāl. [2 notes: ITQAN]

## 2:258/q68 What ancillary grammatical point does al-Farrāʾ make about kam and kaʾayyin?  [* · grammar]
- 2:258/q68/p1 Kam and kaʾayyin are dialectal equivalents; an indefinite after kam can be accusative, genitive, or nominative under different analyses. — Al-Farrāʾ cites Ubayy's reading of kaʾayyin, poetry for number explanation or implied min, and Imruʾ al-Qays for an implied preceding verb; indefinites normally do not precede their governing verbs. [3 notes: FARRA]

## 2:258/q69 What ancillary point does al-Farrāʾ make about replies to questions?  [* · rhetoric]
- 2:258/q69/p1 A response can supply the answer after a question without repeating its opening wording. — Al-Farrāʾ cites questions about ownership of earth and rule of heavens at 23:84–87. [1 notes: FARRA]

## 2:258/q70 What does Wahb's ancillary account say of David and Saul?  [* · reports]
- 2:258/q70/p1 David killed Goliath, delivered 300 enemy foreskins to marry Saul's daughter, and later spared Saul despite persecution; Samuel, not Saul, received revelation. — Wahb says David took Saul's vessel, beard hairs, and clothing fringe as proof he could have killed him; Saul later plotted again. [3 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(وهب بن منبه)]

## 2:258/q71 What ancillary morphology is given for ḥāniya?  [* · grammar]
- 2:258/q71/p1 Ḥāna is shortened from ḥāniya on the fāʿila pattern, like bāla from bāliya. — Ibn Jinnī also cites al-Kisāʾī's derivation of āya from āyiya. [1 notes: IBNJINNI-MUHTASAB]

## 2:258/q72 How does an ancillary poetic metaphor differ from an omitted comparison?  [* · rhetoric]
- 2:258/q72/p1 The she-camel metaphor imagines qualities fully embodied and gains force from expansive hyperbole. — ʿAbd al-Qāhir rejects reducing it to an omitted mithl before moon, willow, amber, and gazelle, or treating the full paraphrase as the poem's actual composition. [3 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL; JURJANI-DALAIL+]

## 2:258/q73 What ancillary principle governs word order and unity of expression?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether thought's grammatical relations or imagined inner wording determines expression.
- 2:258/q73/p1 Arrangement and unity arise from grammatical and intelligible relations among meanings, not from a bare ordering or fusion of words. — ʿAbd al-Qāhir analyzes 'qifā nabki' by governing and coordination and Bashshār's line by subject, attached phrase, predication and qualification; meter is outside the argument. [4 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL]
- 2:258/q73/p2 Mentally picturing inner speech does not prove that thought consists of words. — ʿAbd al-Qāhir compares mentally picturing a seen object with actually seeing it. [1 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL]

## 2:258/q74 What ancillary distinction does al-Rāzī draw about God's speech and the recited Qur'an?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether created sounds are God's eternal attribute itself or signify that attribute.
- 2:258/q74/p1 The eternal speech is an attribute of God; successive recited letters and sounds are created expressions signifying it. — Rāzī says Qur'anic miracle, sūras, verses, eloquence, and interpretation concern the expressed letters; customary oaths follow common speech usage. Denying recited Qur'an as God's speech opposes mass-transmitted religion, but identifying human sounds with the attribute would put it in a human body. [9 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 2:258/q74/p2 The Ḥashwiyya identify sounds heard from humans with God's own speech. — Rāzī rejects this and compares it to extending the Christian claim about the divine Word in Jesus to every speaking person. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(الحشوية); RAZI-FULL-]
- 2:258/q74/p3 Two other arguments for an uncreated audible expression fail: one would make everything eternal and another is too obscure to overcome evident facts. — Rāzī gives these replies in his excursus. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 2:258/q75 What ancillary data does Nashr give about reading transmission?  [* · readings]
turns on: The transmitted basis and scale of the reading tradition.
- 2:258/q75/p1 Recitation is received through transmitted routes, preserving both rare and sound readings. — Nashr says predecessors treated recitation as transmitted Sunnah; the exceptions criticized were Ibn Shunbudh for departing from the ʿUthmānī codex and Ibn Miqsam for accepting unattested readings merely fitting the script.
