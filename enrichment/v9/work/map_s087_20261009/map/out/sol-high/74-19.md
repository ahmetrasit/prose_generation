# 74:19: 10 questions

## 74:19/q01 What does فَقُتِلَ mean here?  [فَقُتِلَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether qutila is read as a conventional imprecation, an Arabic gloss for punishment or defeat, a literal destruction metaphor, or the consequence of his own act.
- 74:19/q01/p1 It invokes a curse on al-Walid, condemning his calculation. — Muqatil ties the curse to his calling Muhammad's message sorcery. Al-Farra cites قاتلهم الله (9:30) and قتل الإنسان ما أكفره (80:17); al-Zajjaj and al-Wahidi cite 51:10, and al-Wahidi also cites 9:30. Al-Damghani and Ibn al-Jawzi describe qatl as an imprecation. Most exegetes, says Ibn Atiyya, condemn his judgment and deem him deserving. Ibn Ashur says the formula originally wishes a killer upon him and an end to his wretched life, becoming a conventional exclamation of amazement or pity; Biqai says the passive invocation declares certain ruin, curse, and expulsion in this life. Bursawi compares قاتلهم الله in 63:4. Alusi notes that such an idiom may mark an enviable extreme; the contextual force is treated under the question about كيف. [26 notes: MUQATIL; FARRA; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS(الفراء); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; DAMGHANI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(أكثر المفسرين); TABRISI(صاحب النظم); IBNJAWZI-NUZHA; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN; NAHHAS-]
- 74:19/q01/p2 It means that he was punished or tormented. — Al-Zuhri's gloss is punishment, explicitly in the form of a curse in al-Qurtubi. Al-Wahidi's short commentary and al-Tabrisi join punishment to cursing; al-Jubba'i says he deserved punishment. Study Quran includes punished as a gloss. [7 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL(الزهري); TABRISI(الجبائي); QURTUBI-FULL(الزهري); STUDYQURAN]
- 74:19/q01/p3 It means destroyed, since a killed person is destroyed. — Al-Nahhas argues from Arabic usage against al-Farra's curse gloss. Ibn Ashur retains the original wish for his death. Asad says the literal wording is 'he has been killed' or 'may he be killed,' but a literal rendering is meaningless in context; Study Quran notes the literal sense 'slain' despite al-Walid's being alive. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN; NAHHAS+]
- 74:19/q01/p4 It means he was overcome or subdued. — Some interpreters cited by al-Qurtubi reason that anything subdued may be called maqtul; Abu Hayyan also reports this possible meaning. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(بعض أهل التأويل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 74:19/q01/p5 It signifies spiritual death or exclusion from divine grace while al-Walid was alive. — Asad attributes the grace reading to many commentators, including al-Tabari. Study Quran infers spiritual death from the application of 'slain' to the still-living al-Walid. [2 notes: ASAD-NOTES(many commentators (Tabari among them)); STUDYQURAN]
- 74:19/q01/p6 It describes self-destruction brought on by his own judgment and attitude. — Asad prefers this rendering and traces the self-destruction through his effort to disprove the messages, search for arguments, frown, and arrogant turning away. Study Quran reports that he determined his own fate by judging the Prophet's nature; Biqai says his thinking ruined others by barring faith and deprived himself of beneficial life in both worlds. [4 notes: ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN(Bg); BIQAI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES+]
- 74:19/q01/p7 The curse is as severe as being killed. — Al-Tabrisi reports this comparison as an alternative explanation. [1 notes: TABRISI(قيل)]

## 74:19/q02 How does فَقُتِلَ connect to the preceding statement?  [فَقُتِلَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the preceding clause is complete and the curse interjected, or كيف qualifies the prior calculating.
- 74:19/q02/p1 It begins an interjected sentence of condemnation after his devising, while the fa still connects its meaning. — The arrangement commentator says the preceding sentence is complete and the new sentence rebukes and marvels. Ibn Ashur places the exclamation between devising and looking, prompted by conduct warranting anger; he distinguishes syntactic interruption from semantic connection by fa and rejects an objection to interjections beginning with fa, citing 16:43–44. [3 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(صاحب النظم); IBNASHUR-FULL+]
- 74:19/q02/p2 It attaches to the preceding calculation: he is cursed however he calculates. — The arrangement commentator compares 'I will kill him however he acts'; al-Tabrisi gives the corresponding comparison 'he will be struck however he acts.' [2 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(صاحب النظم); TABRISI(صاحب النظم)]

## 74:19/q03 What does كَيْفَ express in this verse?  [كَيْفَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether كيف is a literal manner question or an exclamation, and, if exclamatory, whether it condemns, commends skill, praises an earlier judgment, or speaks ironically.
- 74:19/q03/p1 It exclaims in astonishment and reproach at an irrational, invalid calculation. — Al-Wahidi calls it astonishment; al-Baghawi adds rejection and reproach. Abu Hayyan says no rational person could make such an estimate, and likens the idiom to a curse when Arabs marvel at something exceptional. Al-Qurtubi compares 'How did you do this?' and connects 25:9. Biqai says even the manner of such an invalid estimate is objectionable. Al-Nasafi calls it amazement at plotting. Ibn Ashur says the verdict contradicted al-Walid's earlier denial that the Quran was magic, citing 74:24. [10 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(صاحب النظم); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ناس); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNASHUR-FULL+]
- 74:19/q03/p2 It marvels at how effectively he found the accusation Quraysh wanted. — Al-Kashshaf, al-Burhan, Bursawi, and Alusi compare the idiom cursing someone for astonishing bravery; Bursawi nonetheless calls the charge of magic feeble and absurd. Alusi compares 'May God fight them, how are they turned away?' and says this reading is favored, though for a different reason. Al-Razi says no stronger doubt against Muhammad could have been devised; al-Baydawi says he reached the utmost possible accusation. [9 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURHAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 74:19/q03/p3 Its apparent praise is sarcastic, exposing the weakness of his accusation or mocking Quraysh's admiration. — Al-Kashshaf gives ironic praise or quotation of Quraysh's taunt; al-Razi calls his proposal extremely feeble and worthless. Abu Hayyan reports ironic praise and praise for satisfying Quraysh, but calls the repeated crowd-mockery interpretation far-fetched. Bursawi says it quotes Quraysh's curse and mocks their self-admiration; Elmalili says their praise is echoed contemptuously. Alusi reports sarcastic praise and the curse idiom for an envied extreme. Ibn Ashur reports al-Zamakhshari's envy-based metonym but rejects it, favoring simple amazement and condemnation. [9 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(غير مسمى); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL(الزمخشري); IBNASHUR-FULL-]
- 74:19/q03/p4 It can express approving amazement at his initial sound denial that the Quran was poetry, soothsaying, or madness. — Ibn Atiyya says Arabs use apparent curses approvingly; he cites the Prophet's remark about Abu Jandal and Abd al-Malik's قاتل الله كثيرا. Abu Hayyan also reports praise of al-Walid's original admiration. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(غير مسمى)]
- 74:19/q03/p5 It literally asks in what manner al-Walid calculated his claim. — Al-Qurtubi reports this from another group without further argument. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قوم)]

## 74:19/q04 What did قَدَّرَ involve?  [قَدَّرَ · interpretation]
turns on: The content and aim assigned to his calculation: an accusation of magic, persuasive delivery of his opinion, or dismissal of the warning as eloquence.
- 74:19/q04/p1 He measured and rehearsed what hostile claim about the Quran would satisfy Quraysh, eventually choosing magic. — Bursawi defines taqdir as measuring, arranging, and preparing an attack. Al-Tabrisi says poetry would be rejected by Arabs and soothsaying fails because the Quran resembles no soothsayer; al-Walid chose sorcery attributed to earlier magicians. Tabatabai lists poetry, soothsaying, madness, and fables; magic could be said to divide families and allies. The Farra and Jishumi accounts likewise say he rejected madman, poet, and soothsayer before selecting magician; Jishumi adds the claim that the Quran separates families. Muqatil and al-Tabari tie the calculation to branding the message sorcery. [7 notes: MUQATIL; FARRA; TAB-FULL; JISHUMI; TABRISI; BURSEVI; TABATABAI]
- 74:19/q04/p2 He carefully weighed how to deliver an opinion so listeners would find it credible. — Islahi says his words were calculated to take root with listeners; the repeated curse condemns his self-important confidence, likened to treating a pebble as a pearl. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 74:19/q04/p3 He misjudged the Quran as the Prophet's stirring eloquence and ignored its warning. — Islahi says the arrogant leader made and delivered a disdainful excuse, despite the Quran's statements being realities he would soon encounter. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 74:19/q05 Who is the man condemned in this verse?  [فَقُتِلَ قَدَّرَ · referent]
- 74:19/q05/p1 He is al-Walid ibn al-Mughira, the Quraysh leader called al-Wahid. — Jishumi, citing Ibn Amir, identifies the wealthy Makhzumi with sons as al-Wahid among his people. Qatada's report says he was created alone from his mother's womb, connecting 74:11 to the passage through 74:30. Al-Dahhak calls the solitary one al-Walid, whom Muhammad invited to Islam; he delayed, frowned, turned away, called the message magic, and was assigned Saqar. The narrative reports of Ikrima, Ibn Abbas, Ibn Zayd, Qatada, and later sources also name al-Walid. [13 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(ابن زيد,ابن عباس,الضحاك,عكرمة,قتادة); JISHUMI; IBNKATHIR-FULL(قتادة); BURSEVI(قريش); ALUSI-FULL(الوليد بن المغيرة,محيي السنة)]

## 74:19/q06 What encounter and pressure preceded al-Walid's accusation?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports differ especially over the recited passage and the alleged origin of the magic; they also supply different details of the invitation, public debate, and pressure.
- 74:19/q06/p1 He admired the Quran's remarkable speech but under Quraysh's and Abu Jahl's pressure chose to call it magic. — Ikrima says Muhammad's recitation moved him before Abu Jahl pressed him; Ibn Abbas says he initially called it God's speech, neither poetry, magic, nor madness, and Abu Jahl shamed him for fear of conversion. Ibn Zayd says he heard Muhammad in prayer, rejected poetry and soothsaying, then suggested transmitted magic. Qatada says he praised sweetness, beauty, and superiority despite concluding magic. Al-Farra situates the decision in Meccan leaders' debate over what to tell pilgrims. Bursawi reports al-Walid urging a unified answer because the pilgrimage approached, Quraysh accusing him of apostasy after his praise, and Abu Jahl accusing him of seeking food scraps; al-Walid denied needing such food. Alusi's report likewise says Abu Jahl pressed him after he praised its incomparable, fruitful speech. [12 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL(ابن زيد,ابن عباس,عكرمة,قتادة); JISHUMI; IBNKATHIR-FULL(قتادة); BURSEVI(أبو جهل,الوليد بن المغيرة,قريش); ALUSI-FULL(الوليد بن المغيرة)]
- 74:19/q06/p2 He heard al-Sajda, or according to other commentaries the opening of Ha Mim al-Mumin, before praising the speech. — Bursawi reports these alternative recitation identifications, mentioning 41:1 and 40:1. Al-Walid told Banu Makhzum the speech was neither human nor jinn; his image likened its sweet, fresh upper part to fruit and its lower part to abundant flow. Bursawi explains this as a verdant tree with strong roots, fruitful upper branches, and ample water; Kashifi's Persian version puts complete felicity in the fruit and virtues and wisdom in the roots. [4 notes: BURSEVI(الكاشفي,الوليد بن المغيرة)]
- 74:19/q06/p3 He heard 40:1–3 and later alleged magic learned from Musaylima and Babylonians. — Alusi transmits Muhyi al-Sunna: al-Walid praised the recitation's sweetness and power; Abu Jahl provoked him to deny it. This specifies sources of magic absent from the more general 'transmitted from earlier people' report. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(محيي السنة)]
- 74:19/q06/p4 He was invited to Islam, delayed and considered, then frowned, turned away, and called the message magic. — Al-Dahhak's account joins his response to the 'solitary one' of 74:11 and his later consignment to Saqar, with 74:22 and 74:24. Al-Tabari's authorial summary likewise moves from consideration to a pinched brow and grimace. [2 notes: TAB-FULL(الضحاك)]

## 74:19/q07 Why is the curse repeated after this verse?  [فَقُتِلَ كَيْفَ قَدَّرَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the repeated words confirm or escalate denunciation, denote multiple punishments, condemn two aspects of his conduct, or echo the crowd's speech.
- 74:19/q07/p1 The repetition strengthens and confirms the curse or denunciation. — Al-Mawardi says he was cursed repeatedly for calculating magic rather than poetry or soothsaying. Al-Burhan defends repetition as eloquent Arabic used to clarify an obscure matter, indicate nearness, and intensify a curse; repeated wording can stand for an oath or intense supplication, and serves the Quran's challenge in Arabic. It cites paired curses here and at 75:34–35, and pairs at 102:6–7, 78:4–5, 3:78, and 9:69; its chief purpose is confirmation, as the saying 'speech becomes established when repeated' puts it. The author links recurrent admonitions, promises, and threats to varied dispositions and enduring desires and cites 54:17. Study Quran reports emphasis; Ibn Kathir calls the doubled words an imprecation. [9 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(أكثر المفسرين); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURHAN; STUDYQURAN(Bg); BURHAN+]
- 74:19/q07/p2 The second denunciation is more emphatic than the first because of ثم. — Al-Burhan reports al-Zamakhshari's reading of the second thumma in the repeated curse. [1 notes: BURHAN(الزمخشري)]
- 74:19/q07/p3 It indicates repeated punishment, possibly perishing or death in both worlds. — Al-Mawardi says he was punished again and again. Study Quran reports punishment, perishing, or death in this life and the next; Biqai speaks of losing beneficial life in both worlds through his thought. [3 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; STUDYQURAN; BIQAI-FULL]
- 74:19/q07/p4 It condemns both the hideous opinion and the foolish, self-important manner of reaching it. — Islahi says he imagined a pebble to be a pearl; the doubled curse falls before the verdict. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 74:19/q07/p5 It echoes Quraysh's own praise or taunt to mock their admiration. — Al-Kashshaf reports this as an option, and Bursawi and Elmalili take the repeated curse as a contemptuous quotation. Abu Hayyan finds the repetition of the crowd's mockery far-fetched. [3 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(غير مسمى)-]

## 74:19/q08 How is the term for repetition analyzed?  [* · grammar]
turns on: The word's morphological pattern and whether its alif replaces a ya.
- 74:19/q08/p1 Takrar is an irregular verbal noun on tafal with open ta, distinct from tafil. — Al-Burhan defines it as repeating or restoring something and prefers Sibawayh's morphological analysis. [2 notes: BURHAN; BURHAN(سيبويه)+]
- 74:19/q08/p2 Takrar is a verbal noun of faala whose alif replaces the ya of tafil. — Al-Burhan attributes this analysis to the Kufans and prefers Sibawayh's alternative. [1 notes: BURHAN(الكوفيون); BURHAN(سيبويه)-]

## 74:19/q09 What rhetorical principles do the cited examples give for analyzing emphasis and word order?  [* · rhetoric]
- 74:19/q09/p1 Scope and placement determine the specific meaning; an analyst must explain what is newly salient, not merely say a word was fronted for emphasis. — Al-Jurjani contrasts لم يأتني القوم كلهم, where the negation covers the whole group and some may have come, with كلهم لم يأتني, which denies each person's coming. He says the distinction depends on whether كل is inside the negation, not merely whether the verb governs it; separable ما and the poetic lines ما كل ما يتمنى المرء يدركه and ما كل رأي الفتى يدعو إلى رشد prove the point, while fronted كل outside negation denies every instance. In another example, fronting an unlikely killer highlights the surprising agent, rather than the victim. He says generic claims of emphasis miss eloquence and inimitability, as does attention only to sound details such as أأنذرتهم, imala, and الصراط/الزراط, citing 2:6. [5 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL]

## 74:19/q10 What broader lesson is drawn from this episode and its surrounding warning?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the application focuses on every hearer's responsibility or the specific harm caused by his calculation.
- 74:19/q10/p1 The warning leaves its hearers responsible for their response; al-Walid's posture applies to later rejecters too. — Islahi says 'this' in the surrounding passage denotes Quranic verses warning Quraysh of the Hereafter: the truth reached them in its ultimate form and left no excuse. Each person then chooses the path to the Lord's mercy or stays in error and meets the result. Kuranyolu says 74:18–25 condemns people who adopt al-Walid's unbelieving stance. Islahi adds that the leader dismissed as stirring eloquence statements whose realities he would soon meet. [4 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 74:19/q10/p2 His false reasoning ruined his own life and diverted others from faith. — Biqai says his thought blocked others from faith, brought lasting destruction, and deprived him of beneficial life in both worlds. Asad portrays the hostile deliberation as the cause of self-destruction. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES]
