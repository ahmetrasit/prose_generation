# 90:10: 15 questions

## 90:10/q01 What does hadaynāhu mean?  [وَهَدَيْنَٰهُ · meaning]
- 90:10/q01/p1 It means showing someone a route or making it known, rather than compelling him to take it. — Ibn ʿĀshūr defines literal hidāyah as showing the route to an intended destination. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah explicitly excludes compulsion. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ traces hudā through a protruding stone preventing a stumble, daylight disclosing the way, and a leading camel or guide; religious usage adds inspiration or enabling grace. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA(ابن مسعود); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن مسعود وابن عباس والناس); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI]

## 90:10/q02 By what means did God guide the human being?  [وَهَدَيْنَٰهُ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the guidance is explained chiefly as disclosed routes, innate discernment, or reason and the Islamic summons; the accounts can overlap.
- 90:10/q02/p1 God disclosed the moral routes through guidance, including messengers and revealed instruction. — Ibn Zayd and al-Ṭabarī compare 76:3; al-Qurṭubī specifies messengers. Al-Ālūsī says the gift consists in clarity about salvation and ruin even if one does not choose good. Elmalılı says guidance encourages right and warns against wrong. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī compares inspiration in 91:7–8; Iṣlāḥī adds 91:7–10. [11 notes: MUJAHID(ابن عباس); TAB(ابن زيد); TAB-FULL(ابن زيد); QURTUBI(ابن عباس وابن مسعود); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس، ابن مسعود); ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; TABATABAI]
- 90:10/q02/p2 God gives innate moral discernment through reason and the senses, enabling recognition of benefit, harm, good, and evil. — Biqāʿī says reason makes people responsible; original nature recognizes good and evil despite desire, illustrated by sayings about shame and the heart's unease at sin. Qāshānī names sight, inquiry and speech as means of learning. Quṭb calls dual moral capacity part of creation's wisdom. Ibn ʿĀshūr makes this discernment a basis of civilization and knowledge. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ stresses sense perception and innate discrimination; Iṣlāḥī says wrongdoing can arise through emotion despite awareness. [16 notes: QASHANI; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; TABATABAI]
- 90:10/q02/p3 Guidance can be reason's direction to examine evidence for God's existence and unity, and Islam's call toward salvation. — Ibn ʿĀshūr offers rational reflection on divine unity as an alternative nuance and also describes receiving Islam's call as a difficult journey. He connects it to the contested question of accountability for shirk without a messenger. [6 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 90:10/q03 Why is guidance expressed in the past tense here?  [وَهَدَيْنَٰهُ · rhetoric]
- 90:10/q03/p1 It marks reason and moral guidance as a prior, stable gift, after the ongoing provision of bodily faculties. — Biqāʿī contrasts the continuing preservation of eyes, tongue and lips with reason first bestowed and then directed; its hidden, settled character prepares a person for responsibility. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reads past and future references as encompassing the human course from creation to return, comparing the first revelation's rebuke of arrogance in 96:6–8. [4 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BINTSHATI]

## 90:10/q04 How is al-najdayn construed after hadaynāhu?  [وَهَدَيْنَٰهُ ٱلنَّجْدَيْنِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether an unexpressed preposition governs the expression or it functions adverbially.
- 90:10/q04/p1 The second object has an omitted ilā before it. — Al-Naḥḥās assigns this to Basran grammar and says Sībawayh cites poetry as a parallel. Al-Samīn says this ellipsis is needed especially if the referent is the two breasts. [2 notes: NAHHAS; SAMIN-DURR]
- 90:10/q04/p2 Al-najdayn functions as a locative or adverbial expression. — Al-Naḥḥās attributes the adverbial analysis to Kufans, comparing amāma and quddāma; al-Samīn allows a locative analysis. [2 notes: NAHHAS; SAMIN-DURR]

## 90:10/q05 What is the Arabic sense of najd?  [ٱلنَّجْدَيْنِ · meaning]
turns on: The lexical range of elevation, road, neck, and swelling breast; application to the verse is a separate question.
- 90:10/q05/p1 It denotes raised ground, a high neck or ridge, and hence a conspicuous elevated road. — Qutrub and many lexicographers contrast the high region of Najd with low Tihāma; al-Qurṭubī gives plural nujūd. Imruʾ al-Qays's line about Najd Kabkab and al-Aṣmaʿī's anjud verse attest elevated routes; al-Thaʿlabī also cites poetry. Elmalılı names Hijaz Najd, Arid Najd and another Najd near Mahra in Yemen, and extensions to high rank, difficult attainment, courage, grief, overcoming, and sweating. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ adds tall camels and a visible guide. Corpus Coranicum considers the Imruʾ al-Qays line likely authentic and notes the contrast with lowland. Ibn ʿĀshūr places najd below a mountain and mentions a route or hollow. [42 notes: FARRA; MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB; ZAJJAJ; THALABI(ابن عباس); MAWARDI(قطرب); MAWARDI-FULL(قطرب); WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(علي والحسن والفراء); RAZI(أهل العربية); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI(امرؤ القيس); QURTUBI-FULL(امرؤ القيس); BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN(امرؤ القيس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI(المنذري); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT(لسان العرب)]
- 90:10/q05/p2 Najd may mean a raised neck. — Abū Ḥayyān gives its plural as nujūd; al-Samīn also records the raised-neck sense. This is a lexical nuance beside the elevated-road sense. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]
- 90:10/q05/p3 Najd can denote a breast or udder because it swells outward. — Elmalılı cites an Arab oath by a mother's breasts and compares a Turkish oath by the milk one nursed on; al-Ālūsī also cites the Arab oath formula. [2 notes: ELMALILI; ALUSI]

## 90:10/q06 What are the two najds in this verse?  [ٱلنَّجْدَيْنِ · referent]

- 90:10/q06/p1 They are the two moral routes of good and evil, also described as guidance and error, truth and falsehood, obedience and disobedience. — Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Masʿūd, ʿAlī, Mujāhid and many others transmit this; al-Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī, al-Rāzī, Abū Ḥayyān and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ call it the majority account. Al-Rāghib extends the pair to truth/falsehood in belief, truth/lies in speech, and good/bad deeds. Al-Nasafī says the routes lead to Paradise and Hell. ## 90:10/q07 Why are both ways called elevated paths?  [ٱلنَّجْدَيْنِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether elevation chiefly marks visibility, the effort involved in either route, or a figurative naming of evil alongside good.
- 90:10/q07/p1 Their clarity makes both routes as visible to reason as high roads are to sight. — Al-Zajjāj, al-Wāḥidī, al-Rāzī, Ibn al-Shaykh through Bursawī and Corpus Coranicum emphasize conspicuousness; Corpus Coranicum says height recedes behind clarity and najd is broader than the next verse's ʿaqaba. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ notes the otherwise unique Qurʾānic word stresses visibility. [7 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI(ابن الشيخ); BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 90:10/q07/p2 Both routes require effort; good ascends through hardship and evil entails hardship or hard consequences. — Al-Ṭabrisī says avoiding evil itself can bring elevation and honor. Biqāʿī says either choice raises humans above animals, requires climbing, and can be made easy or difficult by divine aid; good is ringed with hardship though appealing in name, and evil can attract desire despite its difficulty. Elmalılı says action on either route requires movement, courage, patience and endurance against the body's preference for rest; one ends in safety and the other ruin. Ibn ʿĀshūr links the good route's difficulty and evil's consequences with the next verse's steep pass. Al-Sāmarrāʾī connects the word with human toil and the hard obstacle, comparing 76:3, 80:20, 1:6 and 5:16. [12 notes: TABRISI; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT; ELMALILI+]
- 90:10/q07/p3 Good is truly elevated; evil is called elevated by predominance, imagined ascent, or a conventional pairing. — Bursawī and al-Ālūsī say evil actually descends from innate purity toward misery; imagination can nevertheless conceive ascent toward any goal. Elmalılı calls evil a low abyss and explains the paired name by good's predominance. Al-Ṭabrisī cites Arab paired-name usage such as al-qamarān for sun and moon, with al-Farazdaq's poetry as evidence. [5 notes: TABRISI; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 90:10/q08 What sounds and recitation rules are noted at al-najdayn?  [ٱلنَّجْدَيْنِ · readings]
turns on: Whether qalqala requires an actual pause and how neighboring sound categories are defined; no variant text is given.
- 90:10/q08/p1 The jīm of al-najdayn has medial qalqala, a forceful audible release of a still consonant. — Ibn al-Jazarī cites Shurayḥ and al-Mubarrad for the medial jīm; final qalqala at a pause is clearer. Al-Khalīl defines qalqala and laqlaqa as forceful sound. Ibn al-Jazarī rejects restricting qalqala to a pause: earlier waqf meant sukūn, and qalqala is neither a vowel nor a movement. [4 notes: NASHR(الخليل)]
- 90:10/q08/p2 The adjacent technical discussion gives different rankings and derivations of long vowels, as well as consonant sound classes. — Ibn al-Jazarī reports the majority's ranking of alif as strongest for madd against Ibn al-Faḥḥām's wāw, yāʾ, alif order; the majority derive fatḥa, ḍamma and kasra from alif, wāw and yāʾ. Authorities disagree whether letters precede vowels, vowels precede letters, or neither derives from the other, with some favoring the third. He defines hidden letters as hāʾ and three madd letters, the two līn letters as sākin wāw/yāʾ after fatḥ, inḥirāf as lām and rāʾ against the Basran lām-only account, and ghunna as nūn and mīm connected to the nasal cavity. These are nearby phonetic observations, with no separate variant reading of the verse stated. [8 notes: NASHR(ابن الفحام,الجمهور)]

## 90:10/q09 What does the verse imply about human moral capacity and divine agency?  [* · theology]
turns on: How acquired human choice, innate disposition, divine creation of acts, and accountability without messengers fit together.
- 90:10/q09/p1 Humans can recognize and pursue either path and are answerable for their choices, while God creates their acts and gives aid. — Biqāʿī distinguishes acquired capacity from autonomous independence, cites 37:96 and 76:3, and says divine guidance makes humans accountable. Quṭb calls dual capacity a wise feature of creation. Elmalılı says people begin helpless, then must thank God by choosing good and avoiding harm to self and others according to ability. Iṣlāḥī says wrongdoing can result from emotions overpowering existing knowledge. [9 notes: JISHUMI; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 90:10/q09/p2 Evil is not an inborn preference or fixed essence; secondary causes and training shape which capacity develops. — Elmalılı rejects the poet's claim that injustice is innate and virtue accidental. The prophetic warning that evil should not be dearer than good supports equal receptivity. He attributes Adam's lapse to Satanic seduction, and the evil-commanding soul to ignorance, corrupt upbringing or habit; training can refine or corrupt character. Natural love of received benefit differs from loving good for its own sake, and material laziness can deter both good and evil action. [7 notes: ELMALILI]
- 90:10/q09/p3 Reason's guidance toward God's unity can ground accountability for shirk or atheism even without a messenger, under one contested theological view. — Ibn ʿĀshūr states this implication of reflection on existence and oneness while noting disagreement between Ashʿarites and Māturīdīs/Muʿtazilites; he does not resolve the dispute here. [2 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 90:10/q10 How does this gift answer the surrounding human boasts?  [* · links]
turns on: Which aspect of the passage is emphasized: the reply to arrogance, the gifts preparing responsibility, or its literary arrangement.
- 90:10/q10/p1 The created faculties and guidance show God's power and knowledge, refuting the arrogant person's claim to escape judgment. — Al-Rāzī points to the boast in 90:5–7 and al-Ḥasan's question about who can reckon with someone who squandered wealth: the creator of eyes, tongue, rational heart and articulate speech can destroy what He made and knows secrets. Evident favors leave no excuse for disbelief or wealth spent taunting God and His supporters. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says the God who taught the paths is still more deserving of knowledge. Jishumī says the bodily marvels make unbelief the person's own doing. [7 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI(الحسن); RAZI-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT]
- 90:10/q10/p2 The gift sequence equips a person with perception, speech and moral direction for responsibility, which the following steep path tests. — Muqātil lists eyes, tongue, lips and the two moral paths. Ibn ʿĀshūr says senses acquire knowledge, speech communicates it and guidance tests action. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ calls the responsibility a difficult trust. Corpus Coranicum says the second triplet's rhyme shift underlines contrast with self-importance, while 90:11–16 judges failure to take the steep path. Al-Sāmarrāʾī connects toil, the ascent, freeing and feeding to patience, and the later mention of mercy to those duties. [7 notes: MUQATIL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT]
- 90:10/q10/p3 The passage is formally arranged as triplets of polemical questions and continues the sūra's opening account of the human being. — Corpus Coranicum describes the second stanza as two triplets united by third-person questions and rhyme, recalling 90:4. Jishumī connects the chapter to al-Fajr's promise, warning and two groups, saying al-Balad explains how success is attained and reinforces its oath. [3 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM; JISHUMI]

## 90:10/q11 Which other verses clarify the two paths?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the comparison emphasizes the guided way in 76:3 or the moral pair in 91:7–10.
- 90:10/q11/p1 Qurʾān 76:2–3 parallels this verse by describing created, tested humans shown a way on which they may be grateful or ungrateful. — Al-Ṭabarī invokes 76:2–3 to prefer the moral-path interpretation; al-Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Ālūsī, Elmalılı, Ibn ʿĀshūr, and Corpus Coranicum likewise cite the passage. Ibn Kathīr emphasizes the shared gifts of hearing and sight. Al-Sāmarrāʾī also contrasts other Qurʾānic path words in 80:20, 1:6 and 5:16. [23 notes: TUSTARI; TAB(ابن زيد); TAB-FULL(ابن زيد); THALABI(أكثر المفسرين); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس، عبد الله، علي، مجاهد، مقاتل، الكلبي); BAGHAWI(أكثر المفسرين); BAGHAWI-FULL(أكثر المفسرين); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ALUSI(الإمام); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI(ابن زيد); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; CORPUSCORANICUM; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT; STUDYQURAN(Q, Ṭ, Ṭs)]
- 90:10/q11/p2 Qurʾān 91:7–10 spells out the pair as inspiration of wickedness and piety, followed by purification or corruption. — Elmalılı says the passage explains al-Balad's two routes and the command to good and prohibition of evil. Quṭb uses both passages for an account of the psyche; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, Corpus Coranicum and Iṣlāḥī make the same moral pairing. [6 notes: ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; TABATABAI; CORPUSCORANICUM; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 90:10/q12 What sūra virtue report accompanies this material?  [* · sciences]
- 90:10/q12/p1 A report promises safety from God's anger on Resurrection Day to one who recites Sūrat al-Balad. — Jishumī transmits it through Ubayy b. Kaʿb as a report from the Prophet; the note gives no grading. [1 notes: JISHUMI(أبي بن كعب)]

## 90:10/q13 Does the extract from al-Rāzī's full text add a gloss on this verse?  [* · reports]
- 90:10/q13/p1 This particular extract quotes the verses about eyes, tongue, lips and the two paths without commentary. — The separate al-Rāzī notes and following extract do give explanation, but this segment itself contains only verse text. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 90:10/q14 What inward paths are discerned in the verse?  [* · inward]
- 90:10/q14/p1 The spirit and heart each have a path, overcome respectively by ego and passion. — Bursawī gives this as an inward reading. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 90:10/q15 What response does God's guidance invite?  [* · interpretation]
- 90:10/q15/p1 It calls for gratitude for the means God provided to live rightly. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the verse embeds this gratitude in the account of guidance and the ability to live well. [1 notes: IBNASHUR]
