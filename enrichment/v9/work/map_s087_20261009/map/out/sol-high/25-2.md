# 25:2: 27 questions

## 25:2/q01 How is the opening relative pronoun attached to the previous verse?  [ٱلَّذِى · grammar]
turns on: Whether the relative depends on its predecessor or begins an independent laudatory construction; al-Ṭībī's proposed substitute is disputed.
- 25:2/q01/p1 It is a nominative adjective of the preceding relative. — Al-Ṭabarī makes both relatives nominative under tabāraka; al-Samīn says the material after nazzala remains within the first relative clause, so attachment is uninterrupted. [6 notes: TAB-FULL; NAHHAS; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]
- 25:2/q01/p2 It is an explanatory apposition or substitute for the preceding relative. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says repetition moves from gratitude for revelation to divine oneness. Al-Ṭībī prefers a nominative substitute because the new clause explains a relative unknown to adversaries; al-Ālūsī calls that reasoning far-fetched. [8 notes: NAHHAS; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الطيبي)+]
- 25:2/q01/p3 It is a predicate of an omitted subject. — The implied subject is 'He'; this yields a nominative independent predication, as al-Nasafī, al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī list. [3 notes: NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]
- 25:2/q01/p4 It is set off as praise in nominative or accusative case. — Al-Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī permit either case for praise; al-Samīn singles out the accusative construction. [5 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]

## 25:2/q02 What does mulk over heaven and earth mean?  [مُلْكُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the expression is explained as sovereign power itself or through its technical distinction from ownership.
- 25:2/q02/p1 Sovereign rule and disposal over all heaven, earth, and their contents. — Al-Ṭabarī specifies prevailing commands, judgments and rulings; Bursevī and al-Rāghib define mulk as command and prohibition among subjects. Al-Ālūsī and Elmalılı add creating, destroying, giving life and death. Al-Kāshifī grounds God's right to rule in His creation. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī describes dependence on one able to dispose of a thing, whether by substance or sovereign command; the lām expresses exclusivity. Abū Ḥayyān links ownership in 24:64 to this added rule and subjection; Study Quran points to 67:1 and 36:83. [19 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(جبريل); THALABI; QUSHAYRI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; QASHANI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI(الكاشفي); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]
- 25:2/q02/p2 Mulk is rule over subjects, distinguished from the wider milk of ownership. — Al-Rāghib says every mulk is milk, but not conversely; another distinction reserves milk for owning substance and mulk for other rule. [2 notes: TABATABAI(الراغب)]

## 25:2/q03 What follows from God's sole dominion?  [لَهُۥ مُلْكُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ · interpretation]
turns on: The notes draw different consequences from sole dominion: obedience, universal revelation, and creaturely dependence.
- 25:2/q03/p1 All creatures depend on and must obey the sole owner and ruler. — Al-Ṭabarī requires obedience and worship of God alone because other worshipped things are His creations and possessions. Al-Rāzī says all depend on God for origin, persistence, essence and existence; their dependence points to His existence and explains why dominion precedes other attributes. Al-Qurṭubī says the ownership statement magnifies Him; Ibn Kathīr links exclusive life, death and the command 'Be' to 7:158. Islahi says rejection of the king's Qurʾānic directive invites unavoidable retribution. [8 notes: TAB-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 25:2/q03/p2 The reach of God's kingdom makes a universal messenger and message fitting. — Al-Biqāʿī reasons that no one within God's heavens and earth is outside His right to send a messenger; no supposed child can defy that messenger, and no supposed partner can claim another jurisdiction. [3 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 25:2/q03/p3 All creation bears contingency and witnesses to its own lack of independent being. — Al-Qāshānī takes the universal dominion of existence as evidence that all produced things depend on God. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 25:2/q04 What inward meaning is drawn from the kingdom?  [مُلْكُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ · inward]
turns on: The two transmitted inward applications emphasize different paths to a share in kingship.
- 25:2/q04/p1 Obedience and preference for God give a person a share in the kingdom. — Ibn ʿAṭāʾ, as transmitted by al-Sulamī, connects devotion to participation in the kingdom. [1 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء)]
- 25:2/q04/p2 Occupation with divine sovereignty makes one sovereign; occupation with kingship gains kingship and kingdom. — Al-Naṣrābādhī, as transmitted by al-Sulamī, distinguishes the fruits of these two orientations. [1 notes: SULAMI(النصرآباذي)]

## 25:2/q05 Whose claim does 'He did not take a child' deny?  [وَلَمْ يَتَّخِذْ وَلَدًا · referent]
turns on: The identified opponents and whether 'taking' points to adoption or all offspring differ.
- 25:2/q05/p1 It denies claims that Ezra, Jesus, or angels are God's children. — Muqātil, Ibn ʿAbbās and others identify Jews' ʿUzayr, Christians' Messiah, and Arab pagans' angelic daughters; Ibn Abī Ḥātim's ʿIkrima also lists Sabeans worshipping angels, Magians worshipping sun and moon, and idolaters worshipping idols among those addressed. Al-Ṭabarī calls attribution of a child a lie. Study Quran cites 2:116 and 6:101–102. [14 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(عكرمة,محمد بن كعب القرظي); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس، مقاتل); IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; STUDYQURAN]
- 25:2/q05/p2 It specifically answers Christian attribution of a son. — Al-Bayḍāwī singles out the Christians; al-Rāzī says an eternally worshipped God cannot have a successor. [2 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; RAZI-FULL]
- 25:2/q05/p3 It also rejects claims of divine offspring or adoption generally. — Abū Ḥayyān names Quraysh, Christians, and Jews; Elmalılı explicitly includes biological offspring and adopted children. Al-Ālūsī says no one has been assigned child status, though he also reports a general denial of offspring. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 25:2/q06 What does taking a child mean here?  [ٱتَّخِذْ وَلَدًا · meaning]
turns on: Whether ittakhadha highlights adoption or conveys the general impossibility of offspring.
- 25:2/q06/p1 It means adopting or assigning someone the status of a child. — Al-Ālūsī explains the wording as assignment of child status; Abū Ḥayyān says God did not adopt anyone. Bursevī cites al-Mufradāt deriving ittakhadha from akhadha and notes walad can be singular or plural, young or old, male or female. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; BURSEVI(المفردات)]
- 25:2/q06/p2 It denies having or begetting any child. — Abū Ḥayyān reports an alternative like 'He did not beget,' because procreation is impossible for God; Elmalılı includes biological offspring as well as adopted children. Al-Burhān contrasts this contextual wording of honor with mā ittakhadha llāhu min walad, a timeless universal denial. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; BURHAN]

## 25:2/q07 Why can God have no child?  [وَلَمْ يَتَّخِذْ وَلَدًا · theology]
turns on: The grounds offered are God's eternal sufficiency, nonbodily nature, and universal creation.
- 25:2/q07/p1 An eternal, all-powerful sovereign needs no child for help or inheritance. — Jishumī, al-Rāzī, Bursevī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reason that God does not die or need an heir; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī adds that a limited owner may need help in management, whereas God has total power. Islahi says He needs no helper. Al-Biqāʿī adds that no child could arrogantly resist His messenger. [6 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BIQAI-FULL]
- 25:2/q07/p2 God's nonbodily, intrinsic power rules out a child. — Jishumī says God is no body and is powerful, knowing and living in Himself, not by a distinct power; His ability to create heaven and earth excludes taking a child. [1 notes: JISHUMI]
- 25:2/q07/p3 Because every other being is His creation and possession, none can be His child. — Al-Ālūsī reports Irshād al-ʿAql al-Salīm making universal creation the ground of both denials; al-Ṭībī compares 6:101. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(إرشاد العقل السليم,الطيبي)]

## 25:2/q08 How is the child-denial joined and placed?  [وَلَمْ يَتَّخِذْ وَلَدًا · rhetoric]
turns on: The clause's coordination, position among four attributes, and antithesis each explain its form.
- 25:2/q08/p1 It is an independent denial, strategically placed between ownership and partner-denial. — Bursevī says it is coordinated with the preceding nominal clause. Al-Ālūsī says its placement establishes independent force rather than letting it seem merely a continuation of ownership. Elmalılı coordinates it with the partner-denial: adoption would lead to partnership. Ibn ʿĀshūr says four attributes frame two contested claims with two facts the audience accepted; he cites 23:86–87 and says wise action is never futile. [6 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 25:2/q08/p2 Its wording contrasts with the polytheists' taking other gods. — Ibn ʿĀshūr describes a rhetorical antithesis between God's not taking a child and the polytheists taking deities in 25:3. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 25:2/q09 Whose claims does the denial of a partner confront?  [وَلَمْ يَكُن لَّهُۥ شَرِيكٌ فِى ٱلْمُلْكِ · referent]
turns on: The sources identify several historical opponents and specify the kind of rivalry or delegation rejected.
- 25:2/q09/p1 It rejects pagan partners, including angels or idols and the talbiya's subordinate partner. — Muqātil identifies Arabs' angelic partners; al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Abī Ḥātim recount a pilgrimage formula allowing a partner owned by God. Ibn ʿAṭiyya attributes this to Quraysh; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says pagans could acknowledge universal ownership yet imagine delegated, lesser lords. Al-Wāḥidī, al-Qurṭubī and Bursevī connect the denial to idolaters. [11 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(محمد بن كعب القرظي); WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; TABATABAI(المشركون,الوثنيون)]
- 25:2/q09/p2 It expressly rejects dualist or Magian claims of multiple divine rulers. — Jishumī includes Christians, dualists and Magians in the paired denial. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, al-Ālūsī and Elmalılı single out dualists; the last two say exclusive ownership already implies no partner but explicit denial answers two or three gods. [5 notes: JISHUMI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 25:2/q09/p3 It excludes any rival or co-owner of sovereignty, whatever the claimant. — Al-Kalbī denies a rival in greatness; Jishumī denies one able to thwart God's will. Al-Rāzī says this cuts off fear and hope directed to stars or idols. Bursevī cites a Mathnawī couplet mocking a claim of partnership; al-Qushayrī extends uniqueness to essence, works, reality and attributes. [10 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); JISHUMI; QUSHAYRI; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 25:2/q10 Why does God's kingdom admit no partner?  [شَرِيكٌ فِى ٱلْمُلْكِ · theology]
turns on: The debate concerns whether sole ownership by itself rules out delegated lords, or whether universal creation supplies the decisive premise.
- 25:2/q10/p1 His unlimited, universal rule leaves no domain or need for a co-ruler. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says partnership would answer a limit in one's own rule, which God has none; Bursevī excludes a competitor or helper in creation. Elmalılı argues creatures cannot exceed their created bounds, whereas God's power is boundless; al-Biqāʿī says no partner could have a command or messenger outside God's realm. [6 notes: JISHUMI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; TABATABAI]
- 25:2/q10/p2 Creation and determination entail direct governance, with no delegated divine affairs. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says bare ownership could coexist in pagan thought with delegated lords; because God creates and determines everything, He governs every affair. Prior and concurrent causes work within the order He determines. [5 notes: TABATABAI]
- 25:2/q10/p3 Every alleged partner is itself created and owned by God. — Al-Ṭabarī says every object of worship is God's property; al-Ālūsī reports this as the explicit explanation of both denials, and al-Ṭībī invokes 6:101. [4 notes: TAB-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(إرشاد العقل السليم,الطيبي)]

## 25:2/q11 What is meant by 'everything' that God created?  [كُلَّ شَىْءٍ · referent]
turns on: Whether the universal requires a supplied restriction; both readings exclude God and eternal attributes.
- 25:2/q11/p1 All things capable of being described as created, including creatures and their actions. — Al-Wāḥidī, Jishumī, al-Baghawī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya and others qualify the universal by 'created'; al-Nasafī says the adjacent verb itself limits 'thing'. Abū Ḥayyān includes entities and acts and says ordinary universals need no explicit exception for the speaker: 'I honored every man' excludes the speaker. Ibn ʿĀshūr includes greatest and smallest existents; al-Biqāʿī includes alleged offspring and partners. [14 notes: THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 25:2/q11/p2 It requires an implicit qualification, 'everything whose creation is valid,' excluding God and eternal attributes. — Abū Ḥayyān reports the proposal, then argues ordinary universal language already excludes the speaker, so no omitted words are needed. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]

## 25:2/q12 What does 'created' mean here?  [وَخَلَقَ · meaning]
turns on: How khalq relates to the later measuring clause, especially whether 'creation' itself signifies measure.
- 25:2/q12/p1 God brought each existent into being, from nonexistence. — Ibn ʿĀshūr includes great and small; Elmalılı stresses that God received, acquired or begot nothing from another. Bursevī describes particular materials, forms, powers and effects; al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī add proportionate materials and shapes. Al-Samīn takes bringing into being as distinct from subsequent exact determination. [10 notes: RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 25:2/q12/p2 Khalq means forming or proportioning according to measure. — Al-Biqāʿī explicitly glosses the verb as bringing into being with due measure and proportion; al-Zajjāj, as preferred by al-Ālūsī, distinguishes this proportioning from subsequent preparation for purpose. Al-Qāḍī treats this sense as limited to bodies rather than accidents. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج)]
- 25:2/q12/p3 Khalq here includes bringing into being and preparing a thing for its proper function. — Al-Samīn says this leaves fa-qaddarahu to add exact determination and avoids repetition; al-Rāzī similarly denies mere repeated estimation. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR; RAZI-FULL]

## 25:2/q13 What does universal creation prove against rival creators?  [وَخَلَقَ كُلَّ شَىْءٍ · theology]
turns on: Whether the stress falls on excluding rival originators or on the governed limits of creatures.
- 25:2/q13/p1 God alone originates everything; light, darkness, Satan, and other beings are no second creator. — Al-Nasafī names Magian and dualist light and darkness, Yazdān and Ahriman; al-Qurṭubī adds Satan and any creaturely power to originate. Al-Ālūsī applies universality against dualism and a Muʿtazilite account of acts. Al-Ṭabarī says all worshipped beings are creatures. [4 notes: TAB-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 25:2/q13/p2 Creation's fixed limits show creatures are governed and cannot become gods. — Al-Biqāʿī says a thing cannot change kind or cross its created limit and creatures cannot fully explain that constraint; Elmalılı says God's power alone has no limit. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 25:2/q14 Does 'He created everything' include human acts?  [وَخَلَقَ كُلَّ شَىْءٍ · theology]
turns on: Whether acts fall under 'everything', how scriptural references to human creators are understood, and whether creation as apportionment can apply to acts.
- 25:2/q14/p1 Yes: acts are things, so their origination falls under God's creation and decree. — Al-Rāzī cites 39:62 and 35:3 against countertexts naming servants creators and says the clause answers those who claim to create their own acts. Al-Nasafī calls it his clearest proof against Muʿtazila; Abū Ḥayyān explicitly includes actions. Al-Rāzī argues that a completed power and motive either necessitates the act through God or else produces an uncaused possibility or regress; involuntary error despite pursuit of truth also points to pervasive decree. [9 notes: RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL+; RAZI-FULL+; RAZI-FULL(القاضي)-]
- 25:2/q14/p2 No: scripture names the servant a creator, so this universal cannot mean God creates human acts. — Al-Qāḍī cites 5:110 and 23:14, including 'best of creators'; al-Rāzī counters with 39:62 and 35:3. Al-Qāḍī also restricts creation-as-apportionment to bodies, not accidents; al-Rāzī replies that such restriction would make the universal ascribe creation to things inaptly. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL(القاضي); RAZI-FULL-]

## 25:2/q15 Does universal creation include evil?  [وَخَلَقَ كُلَّ شَىْءٍ · theology]
turns on: Whether God's praise of creation excludes evil or concerns the display of power in creating it.
- 25:2/q15/p1 Yes; creating evil can display God's universal power even when evil itself is not praiseworthy. — Al-Rāzī answers that origination from nonexistence and extinguishing beings manifest power. He counters al-Qāḍī's appeal to praise and to precise, wise apportionment as limited to good. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL; RAZI-FULL(القاضي)-]
- 25:2/q15/p2 No; divine praise for creation and precise apportionment rules out evil as its object. — Al-Qāḍī gives two grounds: God praises Himself for creation, and only good and wise things merit praise for exact proportioning. Al-Rāzī responds by locating praise in the demonstration of power. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL(القاضي); RAZI-FULL-]

## 25:2/q16 What does the verse imply about the Qur'an being created?  [وَخَلَقَ كُلَّ شَىْءٍ · theology]
- 25:2/q16/p1 The Qur'an is created because it falls among created, measured things. — Jishumī states the inference; al-Nasafī adds that an agent possessing all its attributes cannot itself be an object acted upon, supporting both calling God a thing and calling the Qurʾān created. [2 notes: JISHUMI; NASAFI-FULL]

## 25:2/q17 What does qaddara or taqdīr mean as a word?  [فَقَدَّرَهُۥ تَقْدِيرًا · meaning]
turns on: Whether taqdīr describes apportioning by an agent or a thing's settled measure.
- 25:2/q17/p1 To apportion, measure, determine, or ordain a thing. — Al-Dāmghānī includes lifespans and provisions; Jishumī offers doing a thing according to measure. Study Quran gives knowing and bestowing a measure, estimation and ordainment, and compares qadar, allotted measure, with 54:47–49, 6:60, 17:13–14 and 17:30, 65:3, 68:1, 91:8. [4 notes: DAMGHANI; JISHUMI; STUDYQURAN(IK and Q)]
- 25:2/q17/p2 To establish a thing stably at a measure. — Jishumī transmits this distinct lexical gloss as another account of taqdīr. [1 notes: JISHUMI(قيل)]

## 25:2/q18 What did God determine for each created thing?  [فَقَدَّرَهُۥ تَقْدِيرًا · interpretation]
turns on: The explanations emphasize fitted function, allotted term and decree, guidance, divine knowledge, cosmic constraints, or hidden stages; some overlap in individual sources.
- 25:2/q18/p1 Its fitting form, capacity, properties, and function, in wisdom and without defect. — Muqātil says each is made as it ought; al-Zajjāj and Abū Isḥāq emphasize sustenance and establishment. Qatāda adds sound nature and benefit; al-Kalbī form and endpoint. Al-Baghawī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Nasafī, Bursevī, al-Ālūsī and Ibn ʿĀshūr stress precise preparation without flaw; humans are fitted for perception, religious and worldly duties, crafts and judgment. Mujāhid says animals learn what benefits them and human and animal forms differ. Al-Qāshānī reads each aptitude as capacity for some attributes and perfections. Asad compares 87:2–3; Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 54:49 and 13:17. Abū Ḥayyān relays Ibn ʿAṭiyya's place, time, quantity, benefit and precision. [37 notes: MUQATIL; ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(آخرون,أبو إسحاق,الكلبي); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(قيل); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; QASHANI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية,الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(قتادة,مجاهد); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 25:2/q18/p2 Each thing's fixed lifespan, provision, endpoint, and unfolding decrees. — Ibn ʿAbbās says decrees run through Resurrection and beyond; al-Kalbī names form, endpoint and lifespan, Abū Isḥāq livelihood. Al-Baghawī and Ibn al-Jawzī report allotted lifespan and sustenance; al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī name duration until its known term. Al-Qurṭubī includes the hereafter; Yahyā reports ʿAlī illustrating all-encompassing measure with a fingertip-to-tongue gesture. Study Quran connects this to predestination of every phase of creaturely existence. [15 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(علي); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,ابن عباس,الكلبي); BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN(IK and Q); DAMGHANI]
- 25:2/q18/p3 God guided each creature toward what suits it. — Jishumī reports this as another account of the divine determining; Mujāhid's animal report says God taught animals what benefits them. [2 notes: JISHUMI(قيل); DURR-FULL(مجاهد)]
- 25:2/q18/p4 God's measure is knowledge of each creature's whole existence, without conjecture. — Al-Rāzī says divine knowledge and truthful disclosure cannot fail, whereas human estimation can; divine command and will can therefore diverge. Study Quran relays his distinction between human conjecture and God's eternal unchanging knowledge. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(al-Rāzī)]
- 25:2/q18/p5 Each thing's bounds and relations are set within an ordered cosmos. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says determination precedes and accompanies particular existence and relationships, citing 15:21, 54:49, 87:2–3, 57:22 and 64:11; prior and concurrent causes determine effects within God's order. Islahi points to lifespans, clouds, winds, sun and moon, with 36:38 and 15:21; Kuranyolu says no power can violate the appointed laws. Elmalılı stresses bounds and all decreed circumstances. [6 notes: TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ELMALILI]
- 25:2/q18/p6 God alone knows the precise successive stages and hidden measures of creation. — Al-Ḥusayn, transmitted by al-Sulamī, gives variant sequences, one from light through soul, spirit, form, names, qualities, time and measures, another from time through power, substance, sound and spirit; the sequences vary, while their hidden precision belongs to God. [1 notes: SULAMI(الحسين)]

## 25:2/q19 Why say 'created' and then 'measured'?  [وَخَلَقَ كُلَّ شَىْءٍ فَقَدَّرَهُۥ تَقْدِيرًا · rhetoric]
turns on: How to avoid or interpret the apparent repetition between khalq and taqdīr.
- 25:2/q19/p1 Creation is bringing into being; the following verb adds its exact measure and fitness. — Al-Rāzī and al-Samīn reject repeated estimation; al-Bayḍāwī and Abū Ḥayyān report creation as production and subsequent measurement of that production. Al-Ālūsī offers this as one resolution; Ibn ʿĀshūr says measurement excludes chance and shows knowledge and will. [7 notes: RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 25:2/q19/p2 Creation is proportioning; the following verb adds preparation for the intended purpose. — Al-Ālūsī gives al-Zajjāj's preferred distinction and says a mere inversion of the wording cannot cure repetition without such distinct senses. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج)+]
- 25:2/q19/p3 The measured act of creation is restated to emphasize perfect proportion. — Al-Zamakhsharī, as reported by Abū Ḥayyān, says wise creation occurs according to measure without disparity; al-Bayḍāwī reports measured production prevents unevenness. Abū Ḥayyān notes that if creation itself means measuring, repetition remains a problem. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]

## 25:2/q20 What does the final cognate accusative add?  [تَقْدِيرًا · grammar]
- 25:2/q20/p1 It reinforces that God's measuring is complete and precise. — Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies the verbal noun as a cognate accusative intensifying the kind of measurement; Study Quran notes qaddara followed by its cognate verbal noun. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]

## 25:2/q21 What does the measure imply about divine power, knowledge, and oneness?  [فَقَدَّرَهُۥ تَقْدِيرًا · theology]
- 25:2/q21/p1 Precise creation manifests omniscience, omnipotence, deliberate will, and sole lordship. — Study Quran links taqdīr to created order and divine knowledge and power. Ibn ʿĀshūr says flawless fit entails will and knowledge. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī argues that absolute ownership, creation, determination and administration form a chain proving sole lordship; Islahi calls bounded lifespans and celestial and weather patterns evidence of oneness. Al-Rāzī's account of pervasive decree includes human error and motive. [9 notes: STUDYQURAN; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; RAZI-FULL]

## 25:2/q22 How do the attributes of verse 2 relate to the revelation in verse 1 and the idols in verse 3?  [* · links]
turns on: The sources trace the sequence either from revelation to refutation of idols, from creation back to ownership, or through accepted premises around contested denials.
- 25:2/q22/p1 They identify the revealer as sole sovereign and expose the following idols' lack of divinity. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya says after divine attributes the passage attacks Quraysh's gods in 25:3, which lack them. Al-Biqāʿī says the sequence elaborates revealer, Furqān and recipient from 25:1; Abū Ḥayyān connects 24:64's ownership with this added kingship. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the repeated relative shifts from gratitude for revelation to oneness. Islahi says the verse moves from beneficence in revelation to power and unity; Kuranyolu reads a response to Meccan claims and all denials of unity. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 25:2/q22/p2 Creation explains sole ownership and the denial of child and partner. — Al-Ālūsī transmits Irshād al-ʿAql al-Salīm's explanation of the prior denials, al-Ṭībī's prelude of ownership and 6:101 analogy, and an intensification of the next verse's condemnation. Elmalılı calls the waw explanatory and resumptive: universal origination answers how God acquired and governs so vast a realm. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says ownership, universal creation and direct determination answer the pagan proposal of delegated lords; he declines to discuss other interpretations of this and the previous verse as unhelpful. [8 notes: ALUSI-FULL(إرشاد العقل السليم,الطيبي); ELMALILI; TABATABAI]
- 25:2/q22/p3 Accepted ownership and creation frame disputed claims of child and partner. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says polytheists conceded God owned heaven and earth and created all things, citing 23:86–87; putting these around the two denials gives the disputed claims a familiar premise and conclusion, while wise action cannot be futile. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 25:2/q22/p4 The verse joins revelation to the same majestic creative order seen in nature. — Asad's introduction cites 25:2 alongside 25:30, 45, 54, 61 and 62, and says the Prophet's complaint about neglect of the Qurʾān remains especially relevant. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES]

## 25:2/q23 What occasion-like report illustrates God's formation and decree?  [وَخَلَقَ كُلَّ شَىْءٍ فَقَدَّرَهُۥ تَقْدِيرًا · reports]

## 25:2/q26 How is Sūrat al-Furqān's opening situated in its larger theme?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes highlight either the chapter's argumentative mission or its revelation-and-nature theme.
- 25:2/q26/p1 The opening defends revelation and prophethood while answering denials of oneness and the Hereafter. — Islahi says the chapter's core is prophethood: it answers Quraysh and allies on Muhammad and the Qurʾān, promises believers reward after trial, and warns persistent deniers. The first nine verses oppose claims of fabrication and conspiracy; opponent propaganda aims to turn people from the Prophet and Qurʾān. Islahi also places the chapter amid a larger sequence about warning, migration, war, monotheism, prophethood and the Hereafter; he says al-Aḥzāb supplements its chapter as al-Nūr does the prior chapter. [6 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 25:2/q26/p2 The chapter's cited natural signs place revelation within divine creative order. — Asad links revelation and nature and notes the Prophet's complaint about abandoned Qurʾān as pertinent today. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES]
