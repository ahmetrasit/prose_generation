# 105:5: 19 questions

## 105:5/q01 What does the initial fa indicate?  [فَجَعَلَهُمْ · grammar]
- 105:5/q01/p1 It marks the swift, immediate result of the birds' stone throwing. — Elmalılı calls it both causal and sequential. [1 notes: ELMALILI]

## 105:5/q02 How is fa-jaʿalahum read, and who is its agent?  [فَجَعَلَهُمْ · readings]
turns on: The reported verb forms and whether God or the birds are grammatical subject.
- 105:5/q02/p1 He made them: God is the agent of the destruction. — Ibn Jinnī says the alternative 'left them' can carry this same result. Biqāʿī says the devastation was God's creative act, beyond the stones' ordinary effect. [3 notes: IBNJINNI-MUHTASAB; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL]
- 105:5/q02/p2 He left them like eaten chaff. — Abū al-Malīḥ or Abū al-Khalīj al-Hudhalī is named for fatarakahum; Ibn Jinnī says this stresses their remaining in the ruined state. [3 notes: IBNJINNI-MUHTASAB(أبو المليح الهذلي); IBNATIYYA(أبو الخليج الهذلي); IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبو الخليج الهذلي)]
- 105:5/q02/p3 The birds made them like eaten chaff. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports fajaʿalat-hum, with still tāʾ and fatḥa on lām, taking the feminine birds as subject. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA(بعضهم)]

## 105:5/q03 How does the kāf of comparison work?  [كَعَصْفٍۢ · grammar]
- 105:5/q03/p1 It introduces the comparison, even alongside an expressed 'like' or mithl. — Sībawayh cites Hamīd al-Arqaṭ's rajaz for kāf after mithl. Al-Qurṭubī parallels 42:11, where kāf accompanies mithl. Al-Naḥḥās calls the kāf phrase the second object of jaʿala. [4 notes: SIBAWAYH(حميد الأرقط); NAHHAS; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL]

## 105:5/q04 What is ʿaṣf?  [عَصْفٍۢ · meaning]

Al-ʿAlqama's verse supports ʿuṣayfa as foliage. Elmalılı calls green foliage the basic sense, later drying into straw, and translates it Turkish hasıl; al-Ḥasan remembers barley shoots called ʿaṣīf in Medina. Al-Majāz also gives ʿaṣīfa; al-Qurṭubī lists singulars ʿaṣfa, ʿaṣāfa, ʿaṣīfa. Al-ʿAṣf can be crop or vegetable foliage. ## 105:5/q05 What state does 'eaten chaff' compare the army to?  [كَعَصْفٍۢ مَّأْكُولٍۭ · interpretation]

Ibn Zayd identifies crop and vegetable leaves passing through livestock. Al-Ālūsī says al-Kinānī's excrement provoking Abraha makes this resemblance apt. ## 105:5/q06 Why call the remains 'eaten' instead of naming their bodily end?  [مَّأْكُولٍۭ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the expression alludes to the eventual excrement or is a tense device.
- 105:5/q06/p1 It decorously alludes to excreted, scattered refuse. — Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Biqāʿī, Elmalılı and others compare Qur'an 5:75, where Jesus and Mary are said to eat food. Bursevī compares 21:8. Al-Ālūsī says the blunt term would be indecorous; al-Burhān says eaten leaves eventually become dung. Islahi says the enemy is named by its eventual fate. [13 notes: KASHSHAF; RAZI(مقاتل وقتادة وعطاء عن ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL(مقاتل وقتادة وعطاء عن ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN; BURHAN; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI(غير واحد); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI(Mukātil, Katâde ve Atâ); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 105:5/q06/p2 It casts a past consumption as present by replacing 'animals ate it'. — Al-Ālūsī calls this explanation strained. [0 notes: ALUSI-]

## 105:5/q07 How is maʾkūl read?  [مَّأْكُولٍۭ · readings]
turns on: Whether the hamza retains the passive participle's still vowel or assimilates to the mīm's fatḥa.
- 105:5/q07/p1 With a still hamza, the regular passive participle form. — Abū Ḥayyān attributes this to the majority. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN(الجمهور); ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 105:5/q07/p2 With fatḥa on the hamza by assimilation to the preceding mīm. — Abū al-Dardāʾ is named through Ibn Khālawayh. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī call it anomalous and compare maḥmūm, whose ḥāʾ follows mīm's vowel. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN(أبو الدرداء); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI(أبو الدرداء); ALUSI-FULL(أبو الدرداء)]

## 105:5/q08 What happened to the elephant army in the attack and retreat?  [فَجَعَلَهُمْ كَعَصْفٍۢ مَّأْكُولٍۭ · reports]
turns on: Reports differ on how many were struck or survived, whether a flood followed, and whether Abraha led the army.
- 105:5/q08/p1 Birds and stones destroyed most of the army; some wounded survivors and Abraha died on the return. — Al-Farrāʾ says each stone entered a head and left the anus, killing all. Qatāda reports white sea birds and Abraha's flesh falling away; Ibn Kathīr says most died, all survivors were wounded, and Abraha reached Sanʿāʾ before his chest split. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the retreat to Yemen brought deaths in stages and Abraha's fingers decayed. Poems of Abū Qays and Abū al-Ṣalt mention flight, mutilated elephant, broken legs and al-Mughammas; Rūʾba recalls the birds and stones. A Hudhayl scout reports darkness and wind; ʿUbayd describes swallows, three spotted stones each, and wind; Ibn ʿAbbās describes itching and flesh falling away. Ibn Masʿūd says wind strengthened stones and all struck died, with one Kinda survivor whose poem names al-Mughammas and a cloud. Ibn ʿAbbās says another account had the army at al-Ṣafāḥ and the elephant turn away; Qatāda describes the elephant kneeling and the destroyed church as motive. The commander and a small remnant returned but later died in al-Qurṭubī's report. [28 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL(قتادة); QURTUBI(ابن مسعود,رؤبة بن العجاج,رجل من كندة); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس,ابن مسعود); IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNKATHIR(أبو الصلت بن أبي ربيعة,أبو قيس بن الأسلت الأنصاري المري); IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو الصلت بن أبي ربيعة الثقفي,أبو قيس بن الأسلت الأنصاري المري); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,عبيد بن عمير الليثي,عثمان بن المغيرة بن الأخنس,عثمان بن عفان,قتادة); ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 105:5/q08/p2 A flood swept the struck army into the sea. — Ibn ʿAbbās first says stones produced lesions and the first smallpox; al-Zajjāj reports the same flood ending. [2 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(ابن عباس); ZAJJAJ]
- 105:5/q08/p3 Birds hit only those God willed, leaving a commander and small remnant to return and die later. — Al-Qurṭubī leaves the uncertain count to God; the retreating survivors described the punishment. [3 notes: QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 105:5/q08/p4 Abraha stayed behind; Shamir b. Mafṣūd led 20,000 men, whom birds killed overnight. — Abū Nuʿaym preferred this account, though Ibn Kathīr calls it very unusual. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR(أبو نعيم)+]
- 105:5/q08/p5 Some particular handlers survived blind and crippled. — ʿĀʾisha says she saw the elephant's keeper and driver begging in Mecca. [2 notes: DURR(عائشة); DURR-FULL(عائشة)]
- 105:5/q08/p6 The aftermath included Yaksum and Masrūq ruling, then Persian-backed restoration of Himyarite rule. — Ibn Kathīr says Sayf b. Dhī Yazan sought Persian aid and Arab delegations congratulated him. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL]

## 105:5/q09 What physical mechanism or illness accompanied the stones?  [فَجَعَلَهُمْ كَعَصْفٍۢ مَّأْكُولٍۭ · reports]
turns on: The reports give piercing stones, lesions and wind; later explanations debate a disease or fire behind the devastation.
- 105:5/q09/p1 The stones pierced bodies, sometimes with wind strengthening them, and produced wounds, flesh loss, or smallpox. — Ibn ʿAbbās calls the lesions the first smallpox. Al-Biqāʿī describes hot stones piercing head to anus and leaving black cavities; Ibn Masʿūd and ʿUbayd say wind strengthened or accompanied the stones. Abū Ṣāliḥ reports red-striped, named stones at Umm Hānī's house; ʿUthmān reports a scout's eyewitness wind and darkness. Elmalılı accepts both external wounds and an immediate internal smallpox in ʿIkrima's report and argues bird-hurled stones are unusual but possible. Islahi distinguishes human throwing from God's stone-hurling wind. [17 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(ابن عباس); FARRA; QURTUBI(ابن مسعود); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس,ابن مسعود); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(أبو صالح,ابن عباس,عبيد بن عمير الليثي,عثمان بن المغيرة بن الأخنس,عثمان بن عفان); ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 105:5/q09/p2 The disaster may have been smallpox or typhus, or another disease not identifiable from the reports. — Asad proposes smallpox or typhus. Elmalılı allows smallpox, measles, scarlet fever, plague or an unknown affliction and says only observation or experience could identify it. Qutb rejects the need to reinterpret the image as smallpox or measles. [2 notes: ASAD-NOTES; ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL-]
- 105:5/q09/p3 Fire carried by wind from Arab fires may have burned the Kaʿba or church in the incident. — Al-Ālūsī speculates that this background suits the hollow-chaff image; his full summary mentions fire and the church. [2 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 105:5/q10 When did the expedition occur relative to Muhammad's birth?  [* · reports]
turns on: The transmitted chronological intervals differ.
- 105:5/q10/p1 It occurred in the year of his birth, perhaps on its very day. — Ibn Kathīr calls birth that year the widely held view. Ibn Abzā, Qays b. Makhrama and Muḥammad b. Jubayr report it; the last dates ʿUkāẓ fifteen years later, Kaʿba rebuilding twenty-five years later and prophethood forty years later. Asad and Study Quran give 570 CE. [10 notes: MAWARDI(آخرون); IBNKATHIR-FULL; DURR(ابن أبزى,قيس بن مخرمة,محمد بن جبير بن مطعم); DURR-FULL(ابن أبزى,قيس بن مخرمة,محمد بن جبير بن مطعم); ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN]
- 105:5/q10/p2 It preceded his birth by twenty-three years. — A report from Ibn ʿAbbās states this interval. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 105:5/q10/p3 It preceded his birth by forty years. — Muqātil places the army's destruction forty years before the birth, at the sanctuary boundary before entry. [1 notes: MUQATIL]

## 105:5/q11 What occasion and outcome did the expedition have for Mecca and Quraysh?  [* · reports]
turns on: One Ibn ʿAbbās report names frightening Meccans; most accounts describe demolition of the House and pilgrimage rivalry.
- 105:5/q11/p1 Abraha sought to destroy the Kaʿba and redirect pilgrimage; God preserved the sanctuary and Quraysh. — Ibn Qutayba says the invaders sought the Kaʿba's stones for a rival sanctuary in Yemen. Qatāda says a destroyed or desecrated Yemeni church motivated the march; Asad and Study Quran describe Abrahah's cathedral at Sanʿāʾ. Ibn Isḥāq says Arabs thereafter esteemed Quraysh as God's people and the Prophet used the rescue to recall their political continuity, security and provision in Sūrat Quraysh. Al-Qurṭubī calls it a divine favor. Kuran Yolu says Meccans fled to the mountains unharmed; Muqātil says the invaders never entered the sanctuary. Al-Ālūsī finds punishment by stones apt for would-be demolishers, and sees a dung parallel if al-Kinānī's excrement provoked them. [15 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(قتادة); QURTUBI(ابن إسحاق); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن إسحاق); ABUHAYYAN(ابن إسحاق); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن إسحاق); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,قتادة); ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 105:5/q11/p2 Abraha primarily meant to frighten the Meccans. — An Ibn ʿAbbās report gives this motive; it also says ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib prayed and the birds struck while the elephant bellowed. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]

## 105:5/q12 What was the religious purpose or lesson of the rescue?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the event served a contemporary prophet, anticipated Muhammad, answered prayer, or chiefly demonstrated divine protection; the al-Ḥajjāj objection tests the portent account.
- 105:5/q12/p1 It was a portent preparing Muhammad's prophethood and protecting the House he would honor. — Al-Māwardī reports his birth in its year or day. Al-Rāzī argues that al-Ḥajjāj's later attack on the Kaʿba without comparable punishment does not undermine the earlier event: the irhāṣ was needed before the Prophet, while decisive proofs later established him. Ibn Kathīr calls it a portent for the final unlettered prophet. [4 notes: MAWARDI(آخرون); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; RAZI(بعضهم)-]
- 105:5/q12/p2 It was a miracle for a prophet alive then, possibly Khālid ibn Sinān. — Al-Māwardī transmits this as another explanation of whom the miracle served. [1 notes: MAWARDI(قوم)]
- 105:5/q12/p3 It was an answer to Abraham's prayer. — Abū Ḥayyān reports this view without further argument. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN(قيل)]
- 105:5/q12/p4 It showed God's sole power to defend His House, despite the pagan custodians and the army's strength. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the idols could not protect the House. Al-Biqāʿī says ordinary projectiles could not cause such destruction without God's creative act. Qutb says God let polytheists be defeated so they could claim no credit, kept the holy land free of People of the Book control, and showed an army trusting elephants could be overcome by birds. Kuran Yolu calls it a miracle and notes Meccans could not resist. Corpus Coranicum stresses divine intervention in human affairs, unlike a conception of inactive pre-Islamic Allah; its author says rescue and sanctuary protection dominate over an explicit charge of unbelief. Qutb applies the hope of divine protection to Medina and holy sites in his era. [14 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; QUTB-ZILAL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; CORPUSCORANICUM; ELMALILI]
- 105:5/q12/p5 The rescue should move Quraysh and Arabs to Islam; their enduring strength depends on it. — Qutb rebukes pride in sanctuary service and idolatry. He says Arabs lacked power before Islam, citing foreign rule in Yemen and the north and division in central Arabia; Islam gave them a world role, while ethnic and tribal banners brought subjugation. Ribʿī b. ʿĀmir's statement at Yazdegerd's court describes liberation from human worship, worldly narrowness and oppressive faiths. Qutb compares idea-less conquerors to Tatars and western barbarians, absorbed by those they conquer. [8 notes: QUTB-ZILAL(ربعي بن عامر); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن إسحاق)]
- 105:5/q12/p6 It implies the Elephant account differed from the usual story or had another motive than honoring the Kaʿba. — The objection cites al-Ḥajjāj's destruction of the Kaʿba without similar intervention; al-Rāzī replies with the pre-prophetic portent explanation. [1 notes: RAZI(بعضهم); RAZI-; RAZI-FULL-]

## 105:5/q14 How does this verse connect to Sūrat Quraysh?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the next sūra merely recalls the favor or its opening lām depends grammatically on the Elephant passage; al-Ṭabarī invokes the completeness and separation of each sūra.
- 105:5/q14/p1 The two sūras have a verbal or thematic link, with the Elephant rescue favoring Quraysh's security. — Al-Burhān and al-Itqān point to the closing 'eaten straw' followed by 'for Quraysh.' Ibn Isḥāq says Quraysh's security and provision in 106:1–4 were preserved by the rescue. Al-Zamakhsharī compares a separate poem-line whose meaning depends on its predecessor. [4 notes: BURHAN; ITQAN; IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن إسحاق); IBNASHUR-FULL(الزمخشري)]
- 105:5/q14/p2 Quraysh's opening lām attaches syntactically to the Elephant ending, and the two may be recited as one. — A Basran grammarian reads the lām as 'for' or 'toward' Quraysh; al-Akhfash compares 28:8. Al-Farrāʾ and Ibn Isḥāq are reported for the attachment; al-Qurṭubī attributes it to Mujāhid and an Ibn Jubayr report from Ibn ʿAbbās. Ubayy's codex joins the sūras, and Sufyān b. ʿUyayna knew an imam who recited them continuously. Some authorities cited by Asad regard the next sūra as continuation. [8 notes: TAB-FULL(بعض نحويي البصرة); IBNATIYYA(أبي بن كعب,سفيان بن عيينة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبي بن كعب); ITQAN(الأخفش); IBNASHUR-FULL(الفراء,مجاهد); ASAD-NOTES(some authorities); TAB-FULL-]
- 105:5/q14/p3 The sūras are separately complete, so the lām cannot be attached syntactically to this verse. — Al-Ṭabarī says attachment would leave the Elephant passage incomplete and contradict consensus that the two are separate complete sūras. [1 notes: TAB-FULL+]

## 105:5/q17 How does the episode relate to other scriptural or punishment narratives?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes propose different kinds of literary relationship: biblical simile, narrative genre, or Quranic diction.
- 105:5/q17/p1 The destroyed army resembles the wicked withering like grass in Psalms 37 and 58. — Neuwirth cites Psalms 37:1–2 and 58:8 and calls the account biblically inflected. [2 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Neuwirth)]
- 105:5/q17/p2 The sūra is an early punishment legend whose language shaped later such narratives. — Horovitz calls it the earliest example; Corpus Coranicum says its familiar Meccan legend may have established the genre, though unlike later stories it lacks an explicit accusation of denial or rebellion and focuses on rescue.
