# 43:23: 20 questions

## 43:23/q01 How does 'likewise' connect these earlier peoples to Quraysh?  [وَكَذَٰلِكَ · links]
- 43:23/q01/p1 Earlier peoples answered their warners with the same appeal to ancestral religion as Quraysh, a recurring rejection that consoles Muhammad and exposes inherited imitation as an old error. — Al-Tabari, al-Zajjaj, al-Wahidi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Ibn Kathir, and al-Tabataba'i compare earlier deniers with the Meccans; al-Tabari also cites 34:34. Al-Tha'labi and al-Baghawi say this happened in every town. Ibn 'Atiyya, al-Qurtubi reporting Muqatil, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, al-Biqa'i, and Ibn 'Ashur identify consolation of Muhammad; al-Qurtubi cites 41:43, and Ibn 'Ashur cites 43:22 and 51:53. Ibn Kathir cites 51:52–53 for similar hearts. Al-Biqa'i says the final Prophet must be consoled through previous communities and nearer predecessors console more strongly. Al-Burhan calls the generalization about warners an explanatory tadhyil. Al-Bursawi and al-Alusi read the clause as resuming the preceding exposure of imitation without proof; al-Alusi adds that the forefathers too had no considered proof. Ibn 'Ashur calls the comparison parenthetical and attributes the shared outlook to transgression. Al-Durr reports Qatada's application to Quraysh. Kuranyolu names Abraham's unbelieving people as an example of communities inheriting polytheism and obstructing prophets. [26 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL(مقاتل); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(قتادة); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 43:23/q02 How does the opening waw connect the sentence?  [وَكَذَٰلِكَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the comparison is read as direct coordination or a parenthetical interruption.
- 43:23/q02/p1 It coordinates the sentence with what precedes. — Ibn 'Ashur says even an interrupting waw is formally coordinated to the preceding sentence. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 43:23/q02/p2 It marks a parenthetical interruption. — Ibn 'Ashur allows this analysis while observing that its waw remains formally coordinative. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 43:23/q03 Why does the comparison particle come before the quoted reply?  [وَكَذَٰلِكَ · rhetoric]
- 43:23/q03/p1 Its early placement stresses the likeness and builds anticipation for the elites' reply. — Ibn 'Ashur says the comparison precedes قال مترفوها for this effect. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 43:23/q04 Why specify 'before you' here?  [مِن قَبْلِكَ · rhetoric]
- 43:23/q04/p1 It limits the comparison to earlier warners and gives Muhammad examples from the past for consolation. — Al-Biqa'i says Muhammad is the final prophet, so he has no later community for comparison; closer earlier examples console more. He contrasts 34:34, where مِن قبلك is omitted to generalize because Quraysh had not previously been mentioned. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 43:23/q05 What is the significance of saying 'in a town'?  [فِى قَرْيَةٍۢ · interpretation]
- 43:23/q05/p1 Even town inhabitants, whom one expects to be closer to reason and sound judgment, resisted warnings that challenged their desires. — Al-Biqa'i relates this to the emphatic negation in the sentence. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 43:23/q06 Who are the warners and what do they warn of?  [نَّذِيرٍ · referent]
- 43:23/q06/p1 They are prophets or messengers warning of God's punishment. — Al-Nasafi identifies a nadhir as a prophet; al-Bursawi says the messengers warned of divine punishment. [2 notes: NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI]

## 43:23/q07 How does the negation and exception construction work?  [مَآ إِلَّا · grammar]
- 43:23/q07/p1 The exception clause describes the circumstance: no town received a warner except while its elites made this claim. — Ibn 'Ashur parses the clause as circumstantial; al-Biqa'i notes the emphatic negation directs attention to resistance to unwelcome warning. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]

## 43:23/q08 Who are the town's mutrafun?  [مُتْرَفُوهَآ · referent]
- 43:23/q08/p1 They are its prosperous and pampered leading people: chiefs, nobles, rulers, and sometimes tyrants; indulgence can also characterize anyone devoted to desires. — Muqatil calls them tyrants and leaders; Qatada calls them chiefs and heads of idolatry in 'Abd al-Razzaq and al-Tabari, and leaders and notables in al-Nahhas. Ibn 'Abbas and Muqatil, as reported by al-Wahidi, add kings and elite; al-Qurtubi says rulers and tyrants. Al-Baghawi combines wealthy people with leaders. Al-Nasafi and Abu Hayyan describe abundance making them insolent; Ibn 'Ashur glosses mutraf as one granted luxury and cites 21:13. Al-Bursawi includes rich and powerful people spoiled by abundance and, by conduct, anyone indulging desires and avoiding duties. Asad points to his note on 11:116 for his rendering from tarafa. [16 notes: MUQATIL; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB-FULL(قتادة); NAHHAS(قتادة); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس ومقاتل); BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES]

## 43:23/q09 Why are the affluent singled out as the opponents of warners?  [مُتْرَفُوهَآ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether their opposition is explained by the effects of luxury on judgment and discipline or by their stake in the existing social order.
- 43:23/q09/p1 Luxury, idleness, and desire make them arrogant and disinclined to investigate truth or bear religious duties. — Al-Tabrisi says they chose luxury over proof. Al-Razi says pleasures and amusement displace the effort to seek truth. Al-Baydawi, al-Bursawi, and al-Alusi say ease and idleness divert them from reflection toward imitation. Al-Nasafi and Abu Hayyan add aversion to religious hardship; al-Biqa'i adds fine food, oppression, stubbornness, and that most people follow elites. Ibn 'Ashur says they took divine bounty for granted and forgot dependence on God, citing 73:11. [10 notes: TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 43:23/q09/p2 Leaders benefit from a corrupt social order and have most to lose if a messenger establishes a just one. — The Study Quran calls such leaders the guilty elsewhere, citing 34:34, 51:52–53, and 6:123. It cites opposition to Moses at 7:103–127, 10:75–88, 11:97–98, 23:46–47, 28:20, and 43:46–47; to Noah at 7:60, 11:27, 11:38, and 23:24–25; to other prophets at 7:66, 7:75, 7:88–90, and 23:33–36; and to Muhammad at 38:6. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 43:23/q10 About which people was the verse said to have been revealed?  [مُتْرَفُوهَآ · reports]
- 43:23/q10/p1 It was revealed about the Quraysh leaders al-Walid ibn al-Mughira, Abu Sufyan, Abu Jahl, and 'Utba and Shayba ibn Rabi'a. — Al-Mawardi, al-Qurtubi, and Abu Hayyan transmit this from Muqatil; their spellings vary trivially. Al-Qurtubi and Abu Hayyan add that the comparison consoles Muhammad; al-Qurtubi cites 41:43. [3 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(مقاتل); QURTUBI-FULL(مقاتل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(مقاتل)]

## 43:23/q11 How literally are the earlier peoples' quoted words reported?  [قَالَ · rhetoric]
- 43:23/q11/p1 They are a paraphrase of differing utterances that shared the same appeal to following their fathers. — Ibn 'Ashur says recounting varied speech by its common meaning is normal in the Quran and Arabic; he cites the parallel at 43:22. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 43:23/q12 What does umma mean in the fathers' claim?  [أُمَّةٍۢ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the inherited umma names a creed or religious way, its intended path, a prayer direction, moral uprightness, a favorable condition, or a nation.
- 43:23/q12/p1 A religion, creed, religious way, practice, or community followed by a people. — Mujahid gives a religious way or community; Ibn Qutayba says one religion and says the word originally named people united by one religion, citing al-Nabigha's dhu umma. Abu 'Ubayda adds an established upright tradition. Al-Mawardi transmits religion from Qatada and 'Atiyya with a line by Qays ibn al-Khatim, and creed from Mujahid and Qutrub. Al-Tabari, Ibn 'Abbas in al-Durr, al-Bursawi, and others give religion; al-Durr cites al-Nabigha for Ibn 'Abbas and in another report. 'Asim contrasts the sense here with a period at 12:45 and a group at 28:23. Al-Zamakhshari derives the way from the root of intending a destination. Elmalili includes a followed community or sect. These formulations vary in whether they stress belief, path, practice, or the people sharing it. [15 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; TAB-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL(قتادة وعطية,مجاهد وقطرب); KASHSHAF-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,النابغة الذبياني,عاصم); BURSEVI; ELMALILI]
- 43:23/q12/p2 It means a path aimed for and followed. — Al-Mawardi reports 'Umar ibn 'Abd al-'Aziz deriving his kasra reading from ammamt al-qawm. Al-Zamakhshari explains the path by analogy with a destination one intends. This overlaps the religious-way sense but gives its path and root nuance. [3 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(عمر بن عبد العزيز); KASHSHAF-FULL]
- 43:23/q12/p3 It means a direction of prayer. — Al-Mawardi reports this from al-Farra'. [1 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(الفراء)]
- 43:23/q12/p4 It means uprightness. — Al-Mawardi reports this from al-Akhfash, who cites al-Nabigha. [1 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(الأخفش)]
- 43:23/q12/p5 The phrase means being in a blessing or good condition. — Al-Zamakhshari reports this alternative without choosing it. [1 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL]
- 43:23/q12/p6 It can mean a nation as such. — Elmalili includes 'nation' alongside community or followed sect. [1 notes: ELMALILI]

## 43:23/q13 How is the word for the fathers' umma read?  [أُمَّةٍۢ · readings]
turns on: The transmitted vowel on the initial hamza and a reported codex substitution.
- 43:23/q13/p1 It is read umma with the initial vowel raised. — Al-Durr reports 'Asim saying the alif is raised here and in the other usages at 12:45 and 28:23, though their meanings differ. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(عاصم)]
- 43:23/q13/p2 It is read imma with kasra on the initial hamza. — Al-Mawardi attributes this to 'Umar ibn 'Abd al-'Aziz, who interprets it as a path from ammamt al-qawm. Al-Zamakhshari also reports the variant and says both forms derive from intending or aiming. [3 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(عمر بن عبد العزيز); KASHSHAF-FULL]
- 43:23/q13/p3 Some codices have milla, 'creed,' in place of umma. — Al-Mawardi reports this codex wording. [1 notes: MAWARDI-FULL]

## 43:23/q14 What does following their fathers' tracks mean?  [ءَاثَٰرِهِم مُّقْتَدُونَ · interpretation]
- 43:23/q14/p1 They imitate their fathers' example, deeds, customs, religious practice, and objects of worship. — Mujahid and Qatada gloss the phrase as following the fathers' deeds or practice, as transmitted by al-Tabari, al-Mawardi, and al-Durr. Al-Tabari adds their method and worship of what the fathers worshipped. Al-Zajjaj glosses muqtadun as imitating; al-Qurtubi reports Qatada calling them followers; al-Biqa'i calls it taking fathers as models and rigid imitation. Al-Bursawi adds customs and works. [11 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); TAB-FULL(قتادة,مجاهد); ZAJJAJ; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL(قتادة); QURTUBI-FULL(قتادة); BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(مجاهد); BURSEVI]

## 43:23/q15 How is 'on their tracks' related to 'following' or 'guided'?  [عَلَىٰٓ ءَاثَٰرِهِم مُّقْتَدُونَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the prepositional phrase modifies the following adjective or serves independently as a predicate.
- 43:23/q15/p1 The prepositional phrase attaches to muqtadun or the parallel reading muhtadun, giving 'following or guided in their footsteps.' — Al-Zajjaj gives this attachment; al-Wahidi reports it from Abu Ishaq. Al-Zamakhshari likewise allows attachment to 'guided.' [3 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); KASHSHAF-FULL]
- 43:23/q15/p2 'On their tracks' is one predicate of 'we are,' with 'following' or 'guided' another predicate. — Al-Zajjaj gives this alternative; al-Wahidi reports it from Abu Ishaq. Al-Zamakhshari allows the following clause to serve as the predicate of inna. [3 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); KASHSHAF-FULL]

## 43:23/q16 Does the ancestral claim provide a sound basis for belief?  [وَجَدْنَآ ءَابَآءَنَا مُّقْتَدُونَ · interpretation]
- 43:23/q16/p1 No; it is blind imitation without independent proof, and inherited belief must be tested by rational or revealed evidence. — Al-Zamakhshari says the opponents have no revealed proof and only cite their fathers. Al-Tabrisi argues that rival inherited beliefs conflict while each group rejects the others, so reason or revelation is needed. Al-Qurtubi says they ignored the Messenger's call; al-Baydawi says earlier elites also lacked considered evidence. Al-Bursawi says the ancestors themselves had no independent foundation; al-Alusi says their predecessors had no considered proof. Islahi requires evidence from revelation, reason, or human nature and warns of earlier deniers' fate. The Study Quran says the disbelievers treat ancestry as sufficient justification; al-Tabari calls it a pretext for false religion. [10 notes: TAB-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]

## 43:23/q17 What broader lesson about worldly comfort and the afterlife is drawn from this conduct?  [* · interpretation]
- 43:23/q17/p1 Attachment to worldly pleasure breeds evil and neglected duties; wisdom seeks God and the afterlife through lasting works. — Al-Razi says worldly life and bodily pleasure underlie evil, whereas love of God and the afterlife underlies good; he quotes a Prophetic saying that love of this world is the root of every sin. Al-Bursawi reports a hadith condemning honor for the affluent over worshippers, selective following of the Quran, and striving for decreed worldly gains rather than earned reward and lasting works. He also reports an aphorism: God guarantees worldly life but commands pursuit of the afterlife, so the wise follow the guided and build the next life with certainty. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL(النبي عليه السلام); BURSEVI(الحديث,قال بعضهم)]

## 43:23/q18 How is the speaker's reply in the following exchange read?  [* · readings]
turns on: Whether the reply is narrated as the warner's speech or commanded to him by God.
- 43:23/q18/p1 Ibn Amir and Hafs read 'he said,' making the warner address the people. — Al-Tabrisi reports the reading and Abu Ali's explanation of its speaker. [2 notes: TABRISI(أبو علي,ابن عامر وحفص والباقون وأبو جعفر)]
- 43:23/q18/p2 The other readers read 'say,' making God instruct the warner through revelation. — Al-Tabrisi reports the reading and Abu Ali's explanation of its speaker. [2 notes: TABRISI(أبو علي,ابن عامر وحفص والباقون وأبو جعفر)]

## 43:23/q19 Is the following reply read 'we brought you' or 'I brought you'?  [* · readings]
turns on: The transmitted first-person subject in the following reply.
- 43:23/q19/p1 Abu Ja'far reads 'we brought you.' — Al-Tabrisi reports Abu Ja'far's reading. [1 notes: TABRISI(ابن عامر وحفص والباقون وأبو جعفر)]
- 43:23/q19/p2 The other readers have 'I brought you.' — Al-Tabrisi reports the other readers' wording. [1 notes: TABRISI(ابن عامر وحفص والباقون وأبو جعفر)]

## 43:23/q20 How does the verse serve the surah's larger theme?  [* · links]
- 43:23/q20/p1 It challenges the Makkan idolaters' use of ancestral tradition as a measure of truth within a unified reply to their worldview and objections. — The Study Quran calls the mistaken use of ancestral tradition a major theme and says the surah answers Makkan objections and forms of disbelief. Islahi says every part relates deeply to the central theme and the whole surah is harmonious from beginning to end. [3 notes: STUDYQURAN; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
