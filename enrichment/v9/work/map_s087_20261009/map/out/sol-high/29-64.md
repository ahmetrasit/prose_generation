# 29:64: 27 questions

## 29:64/q01 Who is especially addressed by the warning about worldly life?  [ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَآ · referent]
- 29:64/q01/p1 The polytheists, and others who deny the afterlife through attachment to this world. — Yahya says the polytheists seek nothing beyond this life and deny the next. Kuranyolu says attachment also explains other deniers; desire can make religious obligations seem burdensome despite reason and conscience. [3 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 29:64/q02 Why does the verse say 'this worldly life'?  [هَٰذِهِ ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَآ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the demonstrative's force is general belittlement or its immediate discourse setting.
- 29:64/q02/p1 The demonstrative and description belittle a fleeting, lowly life. — Baydawi, Nasafi and Alusi say its worth before God is less than a mosquito's wing; Alusi cites Tirmidhi's report from Sahl that otherwise no unbeliever would receive water, and a gnostic's repulsive comparison. Abu Hayyan and Nasafi compare children's brief play. Biqai says calling it the lower life itself points to another life; Tabatabai notes the restriction to play and diversion. [9 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(بعض العارفين,سهل بن سعد); TABATABAI]
- 29:64/q02/p2 The demonstrative fits the immediately preceding mention of worldly life through rain reviving the earth. — Razi contrasts the regret passage, whose preceding scene is the afterlife and so says simply 'worldly life'; he cites 2:164, 6:31 and 3:185. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 29:64/q03 Why is worldly life called diversion and play?  [ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَآ لَهْوٌۭ وَلَعِبٌۭ · interpretation]
turns on: The notes distinguish the general transience of worldly attraction from the scope of pursuits criticized and an illustrative poem.
- 29:64/q03/p1 Its pleasures and apparent possessions are brief, deceptive, and without enduring reality. — Muqatil calls it falsehood. Tabari says pleasures soon end. Wahidi reports Ibn Abbas and Muqatil on delusion and idleness. Baghawi distinguishes enjoyment from frivolity. Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan and Alusi liken it to children who gather, enjoy themselves, then disperse; Qurtubi names fading wealth and rank. Tabatabai calls possessions such as wealth, family, power, servants and supporters imaginary; Kuranyolu rejects reducing life to passing pleasure. Study Quran cites 6:32, 47:36, 3:185 and 57:20, whose withering vegetation pictures its end. Burhan says the contrast illustrates worldly existence's incompleteness. [19 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس ومقاتل); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURHAN; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(Aj)]
- 29:64/q03/p2 The criticism applies to pursuits beyond necessities and what supports obedience; deeds done for God belong to the afterlife. — Ibn Atiyya says rich and poor share breathing, food, clothing and shelter from heat and cold. Qurtubi includes necessities and means of obedience, and says seeking God's reward and pleasure endures, citing 55:27. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 29:64/q03/p3 Worldly striving continually shifts its objects. — Bursevi reports a Persian poem in Kashf al-asrar with images of a lion, wolf, partridge and deer. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 29:64/q04 What does lahw mean here?  [لَهْوٌۭ · meaning]
turns on: Whether lahw names distraction, complete absorption, a concrete instrument, play generally, or falsehood.
- 29:64/q04/p1 A distracting pleasure that turns one from truth, benefit, remembrance, or the afterlife. — Razi contrasts turning away from truth with play's turning toward falsehood; stringed instruments qualify because immediate pleasure distracts, unlike chess or pigeon keeping by convention. Nasafi says the pleasure ends quickly. Biqai includes singing and attractive wealth and women and links forgetfulness and misguidance to 31:6. Tabatabai says fading attractions occupy people away from lasting life. [7 notes: RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 29:64/q04/p2 Absorption in one activity so complete that the other is forgotten. — Razi contrasts this with play, in which someone intends to do the other activity later; some people forget the afterlife entirely. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 29:64/q04/p3 The drum. — Ibn Abi Hatim transmits this gloss from Mujahid. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(مجاهد)]
- 29:64/q04/p4 Play, or every kind of play. — Ibn Abi Hatim transmits Ibn Abbas's gloss 'play' and Mujahid's statement that all play is lahw. [2 notes: IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس,مجاهد)]
- 29:64/q04/p5 Falsehood. — Ibn Abi Hatim transmits this gloss from Mujahid. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(مجاهد)]

## 29:64/q05 What does laʿib mean here?  [لَعِبٌۭ · meaning]
turns on: Razi gives two different analyses of play's relation to diversion alongside the ordinary gloss of frivolous pastime.
- 29:64/q05/p1 Fleeting frivolous activity or pleasure, like a child's game, with no serious lasting end. — Baghawi calls it frivolity. Biqai includes dancing after listening, jest, low or false pastimes, toys or chess, frolicking and treating religion lightly. Tabatabai calls it actions arranged for an imaginary end, like children's play; Baydawi and Nasafi use children's brief gathering and dispersal. [6 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL]
- 29:64/q05/p2 Turning toward falsehood for fleeting pleasure. — Razi pairs this with lahw as turning away from truth. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 29:64/q05/p3 Pursuing one activity while intending to return to a more important one later. — Razi contrasts this with total absorption in lahw; some worldly people defer worship and the afterlife this way. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 29:64/q06 Why does lahw precede laʿib here?  [لَهْوٌۭ وَلَعِبٌۭ · rhetoric]
turns on: The proposed contextual triggers differ: provision, current attraction, contrasting life stages, or the sura's call to strive.
- 29:64/q06/p1 The nearby mention of provision makes distraction by wealth especially relevant. — Samarrai's two works cite 29:62 and 63:9 on wealth and children diverting believers from God's remembrance; he says the reversed order is deliberate and this verse is exceptional among Qur'anic instances. [4 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR-MUH; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 29:64/q06/p2 The immediate setting makes worldly absorption more likely, so its term comes first. — Razi contrasts 6:32, where people in regret are far from absorption; here worldly life's attraction is present. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 29:64/q06/p3 Amusement's longer period, youth, comes before shorter childhood play to suit the contrast with lasting afterlife. — Burhan says the usual order follows childhood play then youthful amusement; 57:20 lists life stages. He also says the order in al-A'raf reflects the Day of Judgment and what has just ended. [3 notes: BURHAN]
- 29:64/q06/p4 Diversion comes first because the sura urges striving and forbids wrong, so escaping distraction blocks evil. — Biqai relates the order to the sura's moral aims. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 29:64/q07 How does this verse follow the preceding context?  [* · links]
- 29:64/q07/p1 It corrects attachment to the world after the preceding discussion of God's provision and passing earthly conditions. — Razi says people acknowledge God as creator and provider yet abandon worship for adornment, so God declares the attraction worthless. Biqai says earlier verses show the world's transience. Kuranyolu locates denial in attachment and desire; Samarrai ties the nearby provision to diversion. [7 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; SAMARRAI-ASRAR-MUH; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 29:64/q08 Why does this verse call the next abode true life instead of merely better, unlike 6:32?  [وَإِنَّ ٱلدَّارَ ٱلْءَاخِرَةَ لَهِىَ ٱلْحَيَوَانُ · rhetoric]
- 29:64/q08/p1 People absorbed in worldly life need the stronger warning that real life belongs to the Hereafter. — Razi says the regret passage at 6:32 only needs to call the afterlife better. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 29:64/q09 Why is 'for those who fear God' absent here, unlike 7:169?  [ٱلدَّارَ ٱلْءَاخِرَةَ لَهِىَ ٱلْحَيَوَانُ · rhetoric]
- 29:64/q09/p1 The afterlife's eternal life is universal, though only the God-fearing find it better. — Razi distinguishes the unrestricted permanence asserted here from the restricted advantage expressed in 7:169. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 29:64/q10 What is the force of the lām and other emphasis in the afterlife clause?  [وَإِنَّ ٱلدَّارَ ٱلْءَاخِرَةَ لَهِىَ ٱلْحَيَوَانُ · rhetoric]
- 29:64/q10/p1 They intensify and restrict true life to the Hereafter. — Majaz and Ibn Jawzi report the lām as emphatic; Ibn Jawzi calls it an extra particle. Tabatabai lists inna, lām, the separation pronoun, and the nominal form against the restriction of worldly life to play and amusement. [3 notes: MAJAZ; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو عبيدة); TABATABAI]

## 29:64/q11 Which abode is 'the Abode of the Hereafter'?  [ٱلدَّارَ ٱلْءَاخِرَةَ · referent]
turns on: Whether the phrase is specified as Paradise, characterized as an enduring abode, or read inwardly as the unseen realm.
- 29:64/q11/p1 Paradise. — Muqatil, Yahya, Ibn Umar through Ibn Abi Hatim, Ibn Jawzi and Study Quran explicitly identify it as Paradise; Study Quran lists 2:95, 6:32, 7:169, 12:109, 16:30, 28:77, 28:83 and 33:29. Wahidi reports al-Kalbi; Bursevi also describes Paradise as the true abode. [7 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; IBNABIHATIM(ابن عمر); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; STUDYQURAN; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); BURSEVI]
- 29:64/q11/p2 The enduring abode of life, identified by its permanent inhabitants. — Wahidi reports al-Zajjaj's 'enduring abode'; Qurtubi and Alusi say its inhabitants do not die. Wahidi says 'possessing life' implies worldly life scarcely counts as life. [4 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 29:64/q11/p3 The realm of spirits and the unseen kingdom, alive in all its parts. — Bursevi reports this inward explanation from al-Ta'wilat al-Najmiyya. [1 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]

## 29:64/q12 What does al-ḥayawān mean when predicated of the Hereafter?  [ٱلْحَيَوَانُ · meaning]
turns on: The word is explained as deathless life, life itself used intensively, an animate being, or a spring name; several authors give more than one sense.
- 29:64/q12/p1 True, enduring life without death or extinction. — Mujahid, Qatada, Ibn Abbas and Dahhak are repeatedly reported; Tabari adds no disappearance or interruption. Ibn Jawzi says Paradise lacks worldly defects. Wahidi's al-Farra and al-Kalbi say no death; Ibn Atiyya approves Mujahid's gloss. Razi calls it a substantial second life and alternatively stresses growth and full perception, citing 10:26 and 86:9. Tabatabai adds freedom from pain, misery, nonsense and sin through faith and good works; Biqai lists glorification, knowledge, ease, intimacy and delight. Bursevi's inward source adds freedom from calamity and illness. [38 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(ابن مجاهد); FARRA; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,قتادة,مجاهد); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس,الضحاك,قتادة,مجاهد); MAWARDI-FULL(الضحاك); WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج,الفراء,الكلبي); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الخليل وسيبويه); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; IBNATIYYA-FULL(مجاهد)+]
- 29:64/q12/p2 Life itself, an intensive predication that makes the afterlife life in its fullest sense. — Abu Ubayda equates hayawan and hayat; Majaz cites al-Ajjaj's poetry and derives both from hayiya. Qatada and Suddi also gloss it 'life.' Wahidi says worldly life's deficiency motivates the expression. Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi and Alusi explain the emphatic identification. Asad calls it the only true life. [18 notes: MAJAZ; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); MAWARDI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيدة وابن قتيبة,قتادة والسدي); JISHUMI(أبو عبيدة); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو عبيدة); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES]
- 29:64/q12/p3 A living being or any animate thing. — Abu Zayd contrasts animate with lifeless matter; Qurtubi notes this use. Baydawi, Abu Hayyan and Alusi say the verbal noun can name a living being. [5 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو زيد); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الخليل وسيبويه); ALUSI-FULL(سيبويه)]
- 29:64/q12/p4 The name of a spring in Paradise. — Qurtubi records this additional use of Hayawan. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]

## 29:64/q13 Is al-ḥayawān a verbal noun or an adjective?  [ٱلْحَيَوَانُ · grammar]
turns on: Abu Ubayda's verbal-noun analysis versus Abu Zayd's adjective and its poetic pattern.
- 29:64/q13/p1 A verbal noun from ḥayiya, synonymous with ḥayāh. — Abu Ubayda and Abu Ali also name al-hayy among verbal nouns; Khalil and Sibawayh compare hayaman. Majaz cites al-Ajjaj's line; Samarrai compares ghathayan, fayadan, dawaran and ghaliyan. Wahidi permits this reading and says it means true life without worldly deficiency. [18 notes: MAJAZ; MAWARDI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيدة وابن قتيبة,أبو علي,قتادة والسدي); JISHUMI(أبو عبيدة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الخليل وسيبويه); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو عبيدة); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الخليل وسيبويه); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(سيبويه); SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 29:64/q13/p2 An adjective meaning living, with the description transferred to the abode's inhabitants. — Abu Zayd gives the animate sense; Wahidi allows an adjectival pattern like samiyan and zafiyan, supported by a poem's zafiyan, and explains that inhabitants never perish. [4 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو زيد)]

## 29:64/q14 How did the wāw in al-ḥayawān arise?  [ٱلْحَيَوَانُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the wāw replaces a root yāʾ or is itself a root letter; Abu Ali disputes the latter's analogy.
- 29:64/q14/p1 A yāʾ changed to wāw in an original form with two yāʾs. — Khalil and Sibawayh say this pattern bars assimilation; Baydawi, Nasafi, Alusi and Qurtubi give an original hayayan or hayayyan. Abu al-Baqa says the change avoids confusion with the dual hayyan and an alif would cause deletion of one of two alifs. Samīn and Wahidi cite the name Haywa; Abu Ali says its final yāʾ became wāw to avoid doubling in names. [10 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو علي,الخليل وسيبويه وأصحابهما); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الخليل وسيبويه); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء,سيبويه وأتباعه); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(سيبويه)]
- 29:64/q14/p2 The wāw is original to the root, and the apparent form is retained. — Abu Uthman calls it a verbal noun without a corresponding verb, comparing fawz. Others appeal to the apparent form and proper name Hayat. Samīn and Alusi observe that hayy does not settle the issue, since wāw becomes yāʾ after kasra, as in shaqiya from shaqwa. Abu Ali rejects Abu Uthman's analogy because the relevant yāʾ-medial, wāw-final pattern does not occur. [4 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عثمان); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو علي)-]

## 29:64/q15 Why use the form al-ḥayawān instead of al-ḥayāh?  [ٱلْحَيَوَانُ · rhetoric]
- 29:64/q15/p1 Its faʿalān form intensifies life through the sense of motion and agitation. — Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Biqai, Bursevi and Alusi connect movement with life and stillness with death; Razi calls the form stronger. Samarrai says it conveys continuous renewal and wakefulness, whereas this life resembles sleep and hayat alone could suggest fluctuation. [8 notes: RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 29:64/q16 Does the predicate require an omitted word 'life'?  [ٱلدَّارَ ٱلْءَاخِرَةَ لَهِىَ ٱلْحَيَوَانُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether a matching noun must be supplied or the emphatic direct construction stands.
- 29:64/q16/p1 No; direct predication is intentionally emphatic. — Samin prefers the unexpanded wording. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR+]
- 29:64/q16/p2 Yes; supply 'life' before the subject to align it with al-ḥayawān. — Samin reports this proposal but prefers the direct expression. [0 notes: SAMIN-DURR-]

## 29:64/q17 Where should recitation pause before the closing clause?  [ٱلْحَيَوَانُ لَوْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْلَمُونَ · grammar]
- 29:64/q17/p1 Pause at al-ḥayawān. — Nasafi says connecting the conditional would wrongly make the afterlife's being life depend on people's knowledge. [1 notes: NASAFI-FULL]

## 29:64/q18 What does 'if they knew' imply?  [لَوْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْلَمُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether law introduces an unreal condition with an implied answer or an optative wish.
- 29:64/q18/p1 If they knew the two abodes' truth, they would choose enduring afterlife over the perishing world. — Yahya says they would know the afterlife is better; Wahidi, Ibn Jawzi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Samin, Ibn Kathir, Bursevi, Alusi and Abu Hayyan infer the unspoken choice. Baghawi says they would recognize what perishes and remains. Qurtubi and Tabatabai say they would recognize the truth of the description. Biqai says they mistake this world for permanent and the next for nonexistent; Bursevi's inward source says knowledge would increase striving. [16 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 29:64/q18/p2 The clause expresses a wish that they knew. — Alusi rejects this optative reading as unlikely. [0 notes: ALUSI-FULL-]

## 29:64/q19 What is the omitted answer to the conditional?  [لَوْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْلَمُونَ · grammar]
- 29:64/q19/p1 They would not prefer fleeting worldly life to the enduring afterlife. — Abu Hayyan explicitly calls the answer implicit; Alusi supplies this sense and notes worldly life is incidental and short. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 29:64/q20 What inward reading is given to worldly and afterlife existence?  [ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَآ ٱلدَّارَ ٱلْءَاخِرَةَ لَهِىَ ٱلْحَيَوَانُ · inward]
- 29:64/q20/p1 The unbeliever is spiritually dead here; faith's light revives, and the unseen realm is wholly alive. — Bursevi reports al-Ta'wilat al-Najmiyya citing 27:80 and 36:70. It says every part of the realm of spirits lives; what is missed of its life here cannot be regained, while Hell's people neither truly live nor attain desired death. [3 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]

## 29:64/q21 What is life in the theological sense?  [ٱلْحَيَوَانُ · theology]
- 29:64/q21/p1 An accident unifying a thing's parts so it can know and exert power, defined by perception. — Jishumi gives this account. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 29:64/q22 What unusual duration is reported for this world?  [ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَآ · reports]
- 29:64/q22/p1 One week of the Hereafter, or seven thousand years; 6,200 had passed, and it ends without a monotheist. — Ibn Abi Hatim reports this from Ibn Abbas. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس)]

## 29:64/q23 What does the report say is alive in Paradise?  [ٱلْحَيَوَانُ · reports]
- 29:64/q23/p1 Everything there, including trees, fruit, rooms, walls, rivers, soil and pebbles. — Bursevi reports a hadith with this list. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 29:64/q24 What practical response does the verse call for?  [* · interpretation]
- 29:64/q24/p1 Seek lasting life through faith, obedience and good deeds rather than being ruled by passing desire. — Kuranyolu calls for the afterlife's happiness and peace, cites 18:46 on enduring deeds making present life meaningful, and 2:201 on faithful obedience and good in both worlds. Tabatabai says true life is acquired through faith and good works; Bursevi says knowing Paradise's value would increase striving. [6 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; TABATABAI; BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]

## 29:64/q25 What does the verse's deliberate arrangement illustrate about Qur'anic expression?  [* · rhetoric]
- 29:64/q25/p1 Its contextual word order exemplifies composition beyond human capacity. — Samarrai says each word's place serves the entire expression; both cited works treat the unusual order of diversion and play as an example. [3 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR-MUH; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 29:64/q26 How does the preceding purpose particle relate to this verse?  [* · links]
- 29:64/q26/p1 The preceding li before yakfuru and yatamattaʿu marks a causal result, 'and thus.' — Asad gives this reading of the earlier clause. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES]

## 29:64/q27 How is the cited form ḥayy analyzed?  [ٱلْحَيَوَانُ · grammar]
- 29:64/q27/p1 As a plural on the fuʿūl pattern, like ʿuṣiyy. — Qurtubi reports this analysis from unnamed others beside Abu Ubayda's explanation of the word. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(غيره)]
