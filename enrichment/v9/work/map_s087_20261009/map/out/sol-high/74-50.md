# 74:50: 11 questions

## 74:50/q01 How is the clause كأنهم construed?  [كَأَنَّهُمْ · grammar]
turns on: Which element of the preceding expression supplies the clause's syntactic attachment, or whether the clause substitutes for muʿriḍīn.
- 74:50/q01/p1 It is a circumstantial clause connected to the implicit subject of the preceding 'turning away'. — Bursevi and Alusi describe a second circumstantial clause linked through overlap with the implicit subject of muʿriḍīn. [2 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 74:50/q01/p2 It is a circumstantial clause from the pronoun in the preceding prepositional phrase. — Abu al-Baqa permits this construction. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء)]
- 74:50/q01/p3 It substitutes for the preceding word muʿriḍīn, 'turning away'. — Abu al-Baqa also permits this alternative. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء)]
- 74:50/q01/p4 It is a nested circumstantial clause from the pronoun in muʿriḍīn. — Al-Samin reports this further parse. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 74:50/q02 Who are the people compared to the asses, and what are they doing?  [كَأَنَّهُمْ · referent]
- 74:50/q02/p1 The rejecting unbelievers flee the Qur'an, its reminder, the Prophet, or the truth as wild asses flee danger. — Al-Tabari calls them polytheists turning from the reminder; Wahidi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Kashshaf, Razi, Bursevi and Baydawi specify the Qur'an or its admonition; Abu Hayyan specifies faith and God's signs; Ibn Kathir says truth; Tabatabai places their recoil after the threat and promise and says they seek individual revelation; Ibn Abbas, as cited by Tabrisi and Razi, compares their response when Muhammad recites or approaches to asses seeing a lion. Islahi says the audience's flight has an underlying motive and the Prophet is to remind while benefit belongs to those made receptive by God's laws. [14 notes: TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; TABATABAI; TABRISI(ابن عباس); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 74:50/q03 What are the حمر in the comparison?  [حُمُرٌۭ · meaning]
- 74:50/q03/p1 They are wild asses; ḥumur is the plural of ḥimār, donkey. — Ibn Abbas is cited by Wahidi, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan and Alusi; Razi cites him for both wild asses and mustanfira as fleeing. Bagawi and Tabatabai explicitly give the singular ḥimār. Alusi and Ibn Ashur stress the wild asses' sudden fear and flight, the latter when they hear a hunter. Bursevi says the plural can denote wild asses here. Several commentators state the wild-asses image without further lexical argument. [16 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس وغيره); BAGHAWI-FULL; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; DURR-FULL(عكرمة)]

## 74:50/q04 How is حمر read?  [حُمُرٌۭ · readings]
turns on: Whether the mīm is vowelled, quiescent, or doubled in the transmitted readings.
- 74:50/q04/p1 The mīm has ḍamma in the common or majority reading. — Abu Hayyan and al-Samin call this the majority or common pronunciation. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR]
- 74:50/q04/p2 The mīm is quiescent, with sukūn. — Ibn Atiyya says the mīm may be still; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin attribute this reading to al-Aʿmash. Ibn Atiyya also records Ibn Masʿud's wording ḥumur nāfira, replacing mustanfira with nāfira. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR]
- 74:50/q04/p3 ʿĀṣim reads ḥumur with a doubled mīm. — Al-Durr reports this together with ʿĀṣim's kasra in mustanfira. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(عاصم)]

## 74:50/q05 How is مستنفرة read and understood?  [مُّسْتَنفِرَةٌۭ · readings]
turns on: The fāʾ vowel determines active versus passive force; interpreters then differ over whether the active ist-form simply equals nafara or intensifies deliberate flight, and whether the next verse favors one reading.
- 74:50/q05/p1 With kasra on fāʾ, the asses themselves flee or take fright; istanfara can equal nafara. — Al-Farra calls kasra common and reports ʿĀṣim and al-Aʿmash; Tabari assigns it generally to Kufa and Basra and some Meccans; Farisi, Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, Ibn Ashur, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Nashr and others list its readers, with differing reports about individuals. Abu Ali al-Farisi and Abu al-Hasan prefer it because the following farrat says the asses themselves fled; al-Samin reports the same argument from some, but says an anecdote cannot invalidate a canonical reading. Ibn Khalawayh objects that fatḥa has no stated agent. Ibn al-Jawzi chooses kasra and cites al-Kisaʾi's poetic mustanfar, with Gharb a place. Wahidi, Razi, Qurtubi, Samīn, Bursevi and others compare nafar/istanfara with ʿajiba/istaʿjaba and sakhira/istaskhara; Farisi also compares these forms. Majaz distinguishes mustanfira, startled, from nāfira, fleeing; Ibn Qutayba cites a poet's fleeing ass for kasra. Al-Farra's cited verse, retold by Tabari, Thaʿlabi, Wahidi, Jishumi, Qurtubi and Abu Hayyan, uses mustanfir of an ass, described variously as frightened or fleeing, and Jishumi says it follows she-asses westward. Abu Sawwar's Bedouin judgment, reported with variants of his name by Ibn Khalawayh, Farisi, Thaʿlabi, Wahidi, Tabrisi, Razi, Abu Hayyan, Samīn and Ibn Ashur, turns on hearing that the asses farrat min qaswara; some accounts say he first understood an agent or lion driving them and then chose the active form. Ibn Masʿud's nāfira variant also expresses flight. [51 notes: FARRA; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(الفراء,قراء الكوفة والبصرة); IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA(أعرابي); FARISI-HUJJA(أبو سوار الغنوي,ابن كثير وعاصم وأبو عمرو وحمزة والكسائي); THALABI(أبو سراب الغنوي,الفراء); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو سوار الغنوي,أبو عبيدة، وابن الأعرابي، والفراء، والزجاج); JISHUMI(الباقون,الفراء); BAGHAWI-FULL(أهل المدينة والشام); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الباقون); TABRISI(أهل المدينة وابن عامر,ابن سلام); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو جعفر ونافع وابن عامر والمفضل عن عاصم,أبو عبيدة وابن قتيبة,الفراء); RAZI-FULL(أبو سوار الغنوي,ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(الباقون,الفراء); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الشاعر,باقي السبعة,محمد بن سلام); SAMIN-DURR(محمد ابن سلام); NASHR(المدنيان وابن عامر); DURR-FULL(عاصم); BURSEVI(الراغب); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL(الجمهور,محمد بن سلام); FARISI-HUJJA(أبو الحسن)+; IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبو علي)+; IBNJAWZI-ZAD+; RAZI-FULL(أبو علي الفارسي)+; SAMIN-DURR(بعضهم)+; TABRISI+; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو علي الفارسي)+; NAHHAS-; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيد)-]
- 74:50/q05/p2 With fatḥa on fāʾ, an external agent frightens or drives the asses to flight; the form is passive. — Al-Farra attributes fatḥa to Hijazis, and Ibn al-Jawzi reports it more common in Arab speech. Farisi, Thaʿlabi, Jishumi, Bagawi, Ibn Atiyya, Tabrisi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, al-Nashr, Ibn Ashur and others give reader lists; Nāfiʿ, Ibn ʿĀmir and Medinese readers recur, while Abu Jaʿfar, al-Ḥasan, Abu Rajaʾ, Ayyub, and al-Mufaḍḍal from ʿĀṣim appear in particular accounts. Nahhas reports al-Kisaʾi's two versions and reader lists differing in detail. Ibn Khalawayh and al-Samin explain the passive participle by an unstated frightening agent, identified as hunter, lion or archer; Ibn Atiyya says the pursuer's terror causes their flight, and Ibn Ashur says the next phrase identifies the cause. Abu ʿUbayd prefers fatḥa because istanfara implies another frightens the animals, a preference also attributed to Abu Hatim. Nahhas calls 'frightened' clearer and says the other form normally seeks an action, though it may figuratively mean fleeing. Wahidi lists istanfara, anfara and naffara as verbs for frightening an animal; Tabrisi transmits al-Zajjaj's poetic frightened asses as evidence for the open form. Qiraat-ER calls it a Pss passive participle. [31 notes: FARRA; NAHHAS(أهل المدينة، الحسن، ابن كثير، عاصم، الأعمش، حمزة، أبو عمرو، الكسائي); IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; FARISI-HUJJA(أبو عبيدة,نافع وابن عامر); WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI(أبو جعفر ونافع وابن عامر); BAGHAWI-FULL(أهل المدينة والشام); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(نافع وابن عامر والمفضل عن عاصم); TABRISI(أبو عبيدة,أهل المدينة وابن عامر,الزجاج); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو جعفر ونافع وابن عامر والمفضل عن عاصم,أبو عبيدة وابن قتيبة,الفراء); RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(نافع وابن عامر والمفضل عن عاصم); SAMIN-DURR; NASHR(المدنيان وابن عامر); DURR-FULL(الحسن وأبو رجاء); BURSEVI(الراغب); IBNASHUR-FULL(نافع وابن عامر وأبو جعفر); QIRAAT-ER(Pss); NAHHAS+; QURTUBI-FULL(نافع وابن عامر)+; THALABI+; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيد)+; IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA-]
- 74:50/q05/p3 The active form intensifies the asses' own flight: they seek, urge, or drive themselves into it. — Kashshaf says they seem to summon and urge flight themselves; al-Samin quotes Zamakhshari's vivid interpretation; Nasafi likewise depicts inward incitement. Jishumi distinguishes nufūr, departure in fearful agitation, from istinfār, seeking flight. Ibn Ashur says sīn and tāʾ intensify alarm and full-speed running. Bursevi sets the seeking-flight analysis beside the synonym analysis; Alusi prefers the intensive analysis over deriving it as simple 'fled'. [7 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; JISHUMI; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري)+]
- 74:50/q05/p4 The readings are both acceptable or converge in describing frightened, fleeing asses. — Tabari accepts both established readings as sound; al-Farra says both forms are ordinary Arabic, supported by poetry. Abu ʿUbayda glosses both readings as frightened, while Ibn Qutayba calls the animals frightened and fleeing without assigning a vocalization. Majaz likewise distinguishes startled mustanfira and fleeing nāfira; Wahidi's concise gloss has startled, frightened wild asses. Tabrisi says the root of flight is uncovering or exposure, illustrated by a horse baring its teeth as it flees. Nahhas grants that the difficult active form can figuratively use istanfara for nafara; al-Samin warns that a Bedouin anecdote cannot cancel a transmitted canonical reading. [9 notes: TAB-FULL; FARRA; MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; FARISI-HUJJA(أبو عبيدة); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; TABRISI; NAHHAS; SAMIN-DURR]

## 74:50/q06 What does the next verse's qaswara suggest the asses flee from?  [* · links]
turns on: The competing lexical identifications of qaswara and the poetry and root arguments cited for them.
- 74:50/q06/p1 A lion. — Ibn Atiyya attributes this to Ibn Abbas, Abu Hurayra and most lexicographers, citing rajaz that calls a feared formidable figure al-qaswara al-riʾbāl. Farisi's Abu ʿUbayda and Tabrisi also give lion; Tabrisi derives qaswara from overpowering. Tabrisi's Ibn Abbas and Razi's Ibn Abbas say the unbelievers' flight from Muhammad or his recitation parallels asses that see a lion. Ibn Ashur says, if lion is intended, Qur'anic threats add terror to their refusal, making a novel composite image. [7 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(أبو عبيدة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس وأبو هريرة وجمهور اللغويين); TABRISI(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 74:50/q06/p2 Archers pursuing the animals. — Ibn Atiyya attributes archers to Ibn Abbas, Abu Musa, Qatada and Ikrima; al-Durr transmits Ikrima's image of wild asses fleeing their archers. Abu ʿUbayda, as cited by Farisi, and Tabrisi also give archers; Tabrisi notes the root 'overpower'. [4 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(أبو عبيدة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس وأبو موسى الأشعري وقتادة وعكرمة); TABRISI; DURR-FULL(عكرمة)]
- 74:50/q06/p3 Hunting men or a hunter. — Ibn Atiyya reports Ibn Jubayr, who also attributes it to Ibn Abbas, for hunting men. Tabatabai offers hunter as an alternative to lion; Ibn Ashur uses the hunter to explain the wild asses' fear and the phrase 'they fled from a hunter'. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن جبير وابن عباس); TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL(الجمهور)]
- 74:50/q06/p4 The tumult of people or strong men. — Ibn Atiyya reports these alternative meanings. Labid's line uses qasāwir for forceful, defiant men answering a call in their gathering; Thaʿlab also links qaswara to qasr, domination and force. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ثعلب,قيل,لبيد)]
- 74:50/q06/p5 Darkness at the start of night. — Ibn Atiyya cites Thaʿlab, who specifies the beginning of night relative to its end; he also offers derivation from qasr, force. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ثعلب)]

## 74:50/q07 What effect does the wild-ass comparison produce?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Which aspect of the same simile is being explained: its rebuke, its vivid speed, or the terror implied by a lion.
- 74:50/q07/p1 It censures the rejecters as foolish, ignorant, and humiliated. — Kashshaf compares their senseless avoidance with the donkey carrying books in 62:5; Ibn Atiyya says donkeys are among the most ignorant animals, and Razi repeats Kashshaf's charge of foolishness. Abu Hayyan says the comparison reproaches and degrades them; Bursevi calls donkey an especially harsh insult and wild asses' flight exceptionally vigorous. KuranYolu calls their response to the Prophet and message abnormally thoughtless, foolish, coarse and indecorous. [6 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL(صاحب الكشاف); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); BURSEVI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 74:50/q07/p2 It makes an abstract refusal palpable through the animals' sudden, exceptionally fast panic. — Kashshaf calls the startled stampede a standard Arab comparison for swift camels and flight from hunters at water. Razi reports that no flight matches wild asses' frantic fear. Ibn Ashur calls this an abstract state rendered as a sensory image; he cites Labid's hunter-pursued mount and the wider poetic motif in the Muʿallaqāt of Tarafa, Labid and al-Harith, rajaz by al-Hajjaj and his son, and Dhu al-Rumma. Alusi stresses sudden flight and intense fear; Abu Hayyan stresses panic; Bursevi stresses exceptionally hard flight. [8 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL(صاحب الكشاف); IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 74:50/q07/p3 If the pursuer is a lion, the image combines rejection with terror of the Qur'an's threats. — Ibn Ashur calls the lion interpretation a novel combination of two representations: recoil from revelation and fear of its menacing warnings. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 74:50/q08 How does this picture fit the surrounding passage?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes connect the verse to different stretches of its surrounding passage.
- 74:50/q08/p1 It portrays the astonishing refusal of the Qur'an's reminder after its general address and warning. — Ibn Ashur connects the passage to the Qur'an as a reminder for humankind in 74:31, saying their persistence in rejection is astonishing. Tabatabai connects it to the preceding threat and promise and their demand for personal revelation despite manifest truth. Islahi says the Prophet should continue reminding and that receptivity follows God's laws. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 74:50/q08/p2 The earlier saved are absorbed in their own fate before asking about others. — Biqai says the day is so preoccupying that the saved do not initially know others' fate. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 74:50/q09 What inward cause is given for their flight from listening?  [* · inward]
- 74:50/q09/p1 They lack a listening ear and an awakened heart capable of receiving the living message. — Bursevi gives a Persian gloss for their refusal to listen and cites Mathnawi verses contrasting their condition with the message and its spiritual secret. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 74:50/q10 What anecdote applies the donkey insult to a departing listener?  [* · reports]
- 74:50/q10/p1 A preacher tells a man seeking his lost donkey to take a listener who rises to leave. — Bursevi relates the joke to illustrate the comparison's humiliating force. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 74:50/q11 What reward is reported for reciting this sūra?  [* · sciences]
- 74:50/q11/p1 Ten good deeds for every person in Mecca who believed in Muhammad or disbelieved in him. — Kashshaf attributes the promise to the Prophet and gives no grading. [1 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(رسول الله)]
