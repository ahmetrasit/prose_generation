# 93:3: 26 questions

## 93:3/q01 What does waddaʿaka mean here?  [وَدَّعَكَ · meaning]
- 93:3/q01/p1 God did not take leave of, abandon, or cut off the Prophet; the farewell image refers especially to continued revelation and favor. — Abū ʿUbayda and al-Mubarrad derive it from farewell at parting; al-Rāghib and al-Ālūsī trace farewell to a traveler's prayer for safety, then leave-taking and leaving generally. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Baḥr call tawdīʿ an emphatic departure; al-Ālūsī records the objection that this would deny only emphatic abandonment and replies that emphasis may attach to the negation or answer the mockers' term (41:46). Al-Mutanabbī's line illustrates tender farewell; critics question whether that metaphor answers the mockers, while al-Ālūsī finds their speech sarcastic. Jishumī calls it a metaphor for revelation, support and favor; Ibn ʿĀshūr compares interrupted speech to a traveler leaving. Elmalılı says literal farewell cannot be ascribed to God. Biqāʿī adds that the nurturing Lord cannot part from his charge, as night cannot bid farewell to day. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ notes Qurʾānic forms at 33:48, 6:98, 11:6 and the sole past-tense use here. ## 93:3/q02 How is waddaʿaka read?  [وَدَّعَكَ · readings]
turns on: Whether the dāl is doubled, and whether the light form is an independent verb or a reduction.
- 93:3/q02/p1 The common reading doubles the dāl, from tawdīʿ, bidding farewell. — The majority is cited by Māwardī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān and others; it conveys emphatic separation and is the verse's written form. [11 notes: MAWARDI(الجمهور); MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA(جمهور الناس); IBNATIYYA-FULL(جمهور القراء); QURTUBI(ابن عباس وابن الزبير); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN(الجمهور وعروة بن الزبير وهشام وأبو حيوة وأبو بحرية وابن أبي عبلة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR; BINTSHATI]
- 93:3/q02/p2 A lighter, undoubled dāl is read as wadaʿaka, meaning that God did not leave the Prophet. — Reported for ʿUrwa, his son Hishām, Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn al-Zubayr, ʿUmar, Anas, Abū al-ʿĀliya, Ibn Yaʿmur, Abū Ḥaywa, Abū Baḥriyya, Ibn Abī ʿAbla and Yaʿqūb; Ibn Jinnī calls it the Prophet's reading. Al-Ālūsī offers reduction of the doubled form or an independent verb, records al-Ṭībī's parallelism and a proposed echo of mockers' unusual wording, but says attested usage suffices. ## 93:3/q03 Was the simple verb wadaʿa current Arabic usage?  [وَدَّعَكَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether grammatical descriptions of disuse exclude attested poetic and Prophetic forms, or describe their rarity.
- 93:3/q03/p1 Eloquent speakers largely replaced the simple verb and its derivatives with taraka, treating some forms as obsolete or rare. — Sībawayh disallows wadaʿa; al-Mubarrad points to weak initial wāw; Naḥḥās mentions its heaviness and related hamza substitutions. Grammarians extend disuse to past, verbal nouns and participles. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites al-Jawharī's suggestion that poetic instances reflect poetic necessity, while Abū Ḥayyān maintains replacement in elevated speech. [13 notes: NAHHAS(سيبويه); QURTUBI(محمد بن يزيد المبرد); QURTUBI-FULL(المبرد محمد بن يزيد); ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI(النحاة); ALUSI-FULL(المغرب); BINTSHATI(أبو حيان,الزمخشري والجوهري)]
- 93:3/q03/p2 The simple verb is attested in Arabic, although uncommon, so its reading needs no invented defense. — Al-Zamakhsharī cites a poem with waddaʿnā; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī cite Abū al-Aswad and another poet, including transitive usage. Al-Ālūsī adduces Prophetic sayings with wadʿ and al-Mustawfī's insistence that actual prose and verse usage outweigh a blanket grammatical ban; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ notes Khifāf's mawḍūʿ. [19 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; QURTUBI; ABUHAYYAN(آخر,أبو الأسود); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI(أبو الأسود الدؤلي,المستوفي,النبي ﷺ,شاعر); ALUSI-FULL(المستوفي,المغرب); BINTSHATI(الزمخشري والجوهري)]

## 93:3/q04 Who is 'your Lord' and what does that title convey?  [رَبُّكَ · referent]
turns on: Whether 'Lord/companion' in the surrounding reports names God or Gabriel; al-Ālūsī explicitly rejects Gabriel.
- 93:3/q04/p1 The Lord is God; the attached 'your' recalls His particular nurturing, care, protection and bond with Muhammad. — Biqāʿī says He created, perfected and nurtured the Prophet; al-Ālūsī describes gradual care toward fitting perfection; Quṭb and al-Sāmarrāʾī hear intimate guardianship. Corpus Coranicum directs readers to 95:8 for the divine title. [7 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 93:3/q04/p2 The Lord or companion could refer to Gabriel. — Some inferred this from reports that Gabriel's visits ceased; al-Ālūsī dismisses it as far-fetched. [0 notes: ALUSI(بعض)-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 93:3/q05 What does qalā mean?  [قَلَىٰ · meaning]
- 93:3/q05/p1 It means hated, detested or intensely rejected the Prophet. — Ibn Zayd identifies al-qālī as a hater; Māwardī cites al-Akhṭal, Qurṭubī cites Thaʿlab's line and Imruʾ al-Qays, and others cite poetry. Qilāʾ denotes hatred; common yaqlī contrasts with Ṭayyiʾ yaqlā, with reported vowel and root variants. Al-Rāghib derives strong aversion from throwing away or roasting, depending on wāw or yāʾ root. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites 26:168 and Ibn Sīda's utmost hatred followed by departure. [37 notes: MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB(ابن زيد); TAB-FULL(ابن عباس، ابن زيد); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); NAHHAS(الضحاك); MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI(امرؤ القيس); QURTUBI-FULL(امرؤ القيس,ثعلب); ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI(الراغب,القاموس); ALUSI-FULL(الراغب); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(القمي); BINTSHATI(الراغب)]

## 93:3/q06 Whom does the unexpressed object of qalā include?  [قَلَىٰ · referent]
turns on: Whether the preceding kāf fixes an implicit 'you' or omission deliberately widens the denial.
- 93:3/q06/p1 It means 'you': God did not hate Muhammad, supplied from the preceding kāf. — The prior direct object makes the addressee plain to al-Ṭabarī, al-Farrāʾ, al-Zajjāj, Ibn ʿĀshūr and most readers of the Study Quran. The reassurance answers mockers' claim about Muhammad. [55 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB(ابن عباس); TAB-FULL(ابن عباس، ابن زيد); ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); NAHHAS(ابن عباس,الضحاك); THALABI; MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج,عطاء ومقاتل); WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو عبيدة); RAZI(الفراء); RAZI-FULL(الفراء); QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR; ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; BINTSHATI(الرازي,الزمخشري,الطبري وأبو حيان); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الفراء); STUDYQURAN(most); BINTSHATI-IJAZ+; BINTSHATI+]
- 93:3/q06/p2 Its open expression extends the denial to the Prophet's companions, followers and all whom he loves. — Rāzī connects this to the claim that one is with whom one loves; Biqāʿī and al-Ālūsī likewise hear breadth. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ rejects this expansion as unsupported by context. [6 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI(الرازي)-]
- 93:3/q06/p3 Without an object, it may say that despising anyone is alien to God's nature. — The Study Quran offers this generalization beside the usual supplied 'thee.' [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(most)]

## 93:3/q07 Why is the object omitted after qalā?  [وَمَا قَلَىٰ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether clarity, cadence, courtesy, or expanded scope explains the ellipsis; several sources combine reasons, while Bint al-Shāṭiʾ contests sound alone and unrestricted scope.
- 93:3/q07/p1 The earlier kāf makes 'you' obvious, so the second object is omitted for concise Arabic expression. — Al-Ṭabarī, al-Farrāʾ and Naḥḥās cite Arabic ellipsis; al-Zajjāj and al-Zamakhsharī compare 33:35, and al-Zamakhsharī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān and Ibn ʿĀshūr compare the later omitted objects in 93:6–8. Burhān rejects Ibn al-Shajarī's analysis as a change to third person. [30 notes: FARRA; TAB; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء); KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI(الفراء); RAZI-FULL(الفراء); QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ITQAN; BURHAN(ابن الشجري); ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; BINTSHATI(الرازي,الزمخشري,الطبري وأبو حيان); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الفراء)]
- 93:3/q07/p2 The omission preserves the verse ending and its sound pattern. — Al-Farrāʾ, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Rāzī, Qurṭubī, Samīn, Burhān, Suyūṭī and others give this reason, sometimes alongside clarity. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ rejects rhyme alone: Qurʾānic endings also serve meaning, and the last verse's thāʾ does not match a forced rhyme. [18 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء); RAZI; RAZI-FULL(الفراء); QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURHAN; ITQAN; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI(الرازي,النيسابوري); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الفخر الرازي,الفراء); BINTSHATI-; BINTSHATI-IJAZ-]
- 93:3/q07/p3 The omission avoids directly attaching the harsh idea of being hated to the honored Prophet. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ contrasts severe qalā with farewell's lingering affection and hoped return; al-Ālūsī, al-Sāmarrāʾī and others call it courtesy. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says naming the Prophet with affectionate farewell while omitting him with hatred honors him twice and teaches respectful speech. [9 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; SAMARRAI-ASRAR-MUH; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; BINTSHATI-IJAZ+; BINTSHATI+]
- 93:3/q07/p4 Leaving the object open broadens the denial to those beloved by the Prophet. — Rāzī, Biqāʿī and al-Ālūsī extend the assurance to companions and followers; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ finds this unsupported by context. [6 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI(الرازي)-]

## 93:3/q08 How does this clause function as the answer to the preceding oath?  [مَا وَدَّعَكَ رَبُّكَ وَمَا قَلَىٰ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the negative reassurance itself answers the oath or merely sets up the next verse's promise.
- 93:3/q08/p1 Both negative clauses answer the oath; a negated oath answer needs no introductory lām. — Al-Ṭabarī and many later exegetes identify the oath response. Ibn ʿĀshūr explains the missing lām; the second coordinated denial shares the syntax. Islahi says the answer states only the reassurance most needed. ʿIṣām al-Dīn proposes treating the denial as circumstantial before 93:4, but al-Ālūsī objects that the paired lāms and oath make that strained. [22 notes: TAB; TAB-FULL; THALABI; WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو عبيدة); QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 93:3/q08/p2 The denial is circumstantial, while the true promise in 93:4 supplies the oath's force. — ʿIṣām al-Dīn argues the better Hereafter proves the Lord would not abandon the Prophet; al-Ālūsī objects that the oath and paired lāms resist this paraphrase. [0 notes: ALUSI-FULL(عصام الدين)-]

## 93:3/q09 What reassurance does the verse give about the pause in revelation?  [مَا وَدَّعَكَ رَبُّكَ وَمَا قَلَىٰ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the point is direct consolation, a pattern of testing, or evidence of revelation's external source; these are compatible explanations.
- 93:3/q09/p1 God has neither left nor hated the Prophet; the interruption of revelation does not end His choice, love, care or future favor. — Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn Zayd gloss both denials; Zajjāj, Qushayrī, Jishumī, Ṭabrisī and Ibn ʿĀshūr apply them to continued revelation. Thaʿlabī, Wāḥidī, Baghawī and Nasafī say God does not abandon one He chose or hate one He loved. Rāzī says a delay could mean only missing a preferable thing, never hatred, and serves as a meaningful honor when enemies allege it. Elmalılı distinguishes merciful respite from punitive abandonment; no human escapes divine governance (75:36). Quṭb sees a direct answer to a hurtful allegation; the Study Quran describes the ensuing favors and duties. [46 notes: TAB(ابن عباس); TAB-FULL(ابن عباس، ابن زيد); ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); NAHHAS(ابن عباس); SULAMI(الواسطي); THALABI; QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج,عطاء ومقاتل); WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو عبيدة); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); ITQAN; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 93:3/q09/p2 The Prophet's hardship and delay are part of divine training: alternating ease and difficulty test gratitude and patience, without divine indifference. — Iṣlāḥī reads day and night as necessary phases of growth; opposition and scant resources train the soul. He holds that the Prophet could worry his own mistakes angered God (94:2–3), and that revelation's timing follows wisdom; this reassurance addresses his private concern. [6 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 93:3/q09/p3 The pause proves the Qurʾān came from God, for the Prophet could not have withheld words he authored when he needed them. — Rāzī draws this inference from the painful interruption. [2 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL]

## 93:3/q10 Who was addressed by the singular 'you'?  [وَدَّعَكَ · referent]
turns on: Whether singular second person marks Muhammad specifically or an exemplary generic addressee.
- 93:3/q10/p1 Muhammad is addressed, and his experience can exemplify divine care for listeners generally. — Corpus Coranicum prefers Muhammad while noting the general conduct rules and comparisons with 94:1–4 and 108:3. It adds that personal motifs need not be literal biographical records or mirror his private emotions. [2 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM; CORPUSCORANICUM+]
- 93:3/q10/p2 The singular address may be generic, like the singular in the Ten Commandments. — Rippin proposes a nonindividual addressee; Corpus Coranicum considers Muhammad more probable. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Rippin)]

## 93:3/q11 How long was revelation interrupted?  [مَا وَدَّعَكَ · reports]
turns on: Different transmissions count from uncertain starting points and sometimes refer to distinct interruptions; al-Ālūsī notes that the exact beginning is hard to fix.
- 93:3/q11/p1 The interruption lasted one or two nights. — Jundub's illness and missed night worship are transmitted with this count by Ibn Abī Ḥātim, Ibn Kathīr, Suyūṭī and others; versions vary between one and two. ## 93:3/q16 Could the Prophet have believed that God had rejected him?  [مَا وَدَّعَكَ رَبُّكَ وَمَا قَلَىٰ · theology]

## 93:3/q17 How do the oaths by morning and night connect to this reassurance?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the light/night pair directly models interrupted revelation, rest before renewal, divine lordship, or alternating trials; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ argues for the first.
- 93:3/q17/p1 Day followed by still night makes a pause in revelation intelligible without suggesting God has abandoned earth or the Prophet. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ prefers a sensory analogy: recurring light and quiet darkness prepare the unseen interruption, and the mockers cannot infer rejection. Ṭabāṭabāʾī pairs light and dark with revelation's arrival and pause; Elmalılı reads night as respite and promised renewal. Ibn al-Zubayr and Biqāʿī see the forenoon oath as fitting consolation. Corpus Coranicum notes Psalm 22:25 as a comparable denial of abandonment in affliction. [10 notes: BIQAI(الأستاذ أبو جعفر ابن الزبير); ELMALILI; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI(فخر الدين الرازي ونظام الدين النيسابوري); BINTSHATI-IJAZ; CORPUSCORANICUM; BINTSHATI+]
- 93:3/q17/p2 Morning represents life-giving first revelation; the still night gives rest before renewed work. — Muḥammad ʿAbduh explains the Prophet's distress and relief through renewed revelation. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ objects that making night principally rest weakens the direct sensory analogy. [2 notes: BINTSHATI(الشيخ محمد عبده); ELMALILI; BINTSHATI-]
- 93:3/q17/p3 The day-night pattern shows God's lordship, wisdom and mercy in alternating revelation and respite. — Ibn al-Qayyim matches bright forenoon after darkness to resumed revelation; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ objects that debate over lordship is not the reported issue, which is abandonment. [1 notes: BINTSHATI(ابن قيم الجوزية); BINTSHATI-]
- 93:3/q17/p4 The alternation represents necessary phases of human training: ease and hardship, gratitude and patience. — Iṣlāḥī treats day and night as complementary proof; hardship in the mission therefore does not signal divine indifference. His discourse proceeds through promise, remembered help and duties. [4 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 93:3/q17/p5 Light and darkness can image guidance and error, or Islam's rise and later estrangement. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ allows the sensory contrast to clarify unseen guidance and truth but excludes esoteric identifications of morning and night with the Prophet's face and hair, relatives, knowledge and forgiveness, or historical estrangement. [1 notes: BINTSHATI; BINTSHATI-]
- 93:3/q17/p6 The oath's relevance is insufficiently explained by commentators who merely identify its answer. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ faults al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī and Abū Ḥayyān for giving no clear connection or comparison with explicit denials of oaths. [0 notes: BINTSHATI-]

- 93:3/q19/p2 Sūra 93 and Sūra 94 belong closely together in rehearsing God's favors and turning the Prophet toward action. — The Study Quran reports some considered them one sūra because Companions recited them in one prayer cycle without basmalah; it distinguishes Sūra 93's external needs and Sūra 94's spiritual relief, both recalling blessings before commands. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 93:3/q20 Where does this revelation fall in the sūra's chronology?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Different transmitted revelation orders and whether one counts the earlier and later pauses separately.
- 93:3/q20/p1 This is a Meccan sūra commonly placed eleventh, after an early pause and roughly eight intervening revelations. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says its Meccan setting is undisputed and gives the common eleventh placement after al-Fajr. Ibn ʿĀshūr distinguishes a first pause before al-Muddaththir or al-Muzzammil, after two or three revelations, from a second pause behind this verse; he makes al-Ḍuḥā eleventh, though calls it the third descent in one reconstruction. He says the earlier possible forty-day pause escaped idolaters' notice. [7 notes: IBNASHUR(قيل); IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI]
- 93:3/q20/p2 Early Meccan rankings also place it fourth or seventh; it may be the first complete sūra revealed. — The Study Quran records fourth and seventh rankings and notes that others instead award the first-whole-sūra distinction to al-Fātiḥa. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 93:3/q21 What inward meaning is drawn from the denial of abandonment?  [* · inward]
turns on: Whether the pause figures stable divine nearness, a pedagogical spiritual veil, or should be read only through the oath's sensory analogy.
- 93:3/q21/p1 God did not veil the Prophet from nearness by sending him to people, nor neglect him after choosing him; the nurturing Lord does not abandon His charge. — Ibn ʿAṭāʾ, al-Wāsiṭī and Bursawī apply the reassurance to enduring spiritual nearness and divine nurture. [3 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء,الواسطي); BURSEVI]
- 93:3/q21/p2 The alternation of light and darkness is a temporary veil after disclosure that heightens the beloved's longing before final unveiling. — Qāshānī says God did not leave the Prophet in light with love but veiled Essence, nor reject him in darkness amid the cosmos without love. The interval after witnessing unity dissolves ego and enriches later savor; a report has Muhammad seeking direct sight on mountains until the veil lifts. [4 notes: QASHANI]
- 93:3/q21/p3 Esoteric identifications of the oath's light and night with the Prophet's features, relatives, knowledge or Islam's later fortunes should be excluded. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ rejects such readings for missing the sensory pattern of the oath and reported interruption. [0 notes: BINTSHATI-]

## 93:3/q22 What broader relations between divine nearness and distance does the passage suggest?  [* · theology]
- 93:3/q22/p1 Four ranks appear: obedient believers are near and loved, unbelievers distant and hated, sinful believers combine nearness and distance, and unbelievers' worship is outwardly near but rejected. — Biqāʿī distinguishes love, obedience, sin, rejection and outward worship in the paired denial of abandonment and hatred. [2 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL]

## 93:3/q23 What practice was attached to the descent of this sūra?  [* · law]
- 93:3/q23/p1 The Prophet proclaimed takbīr when the sūra came; takbīr became a practice for this and following sūras. — Biqāʿī states both the report and its ritual consequence. [2 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL]

## 93:3/q24 What can historical reports establish about the verse's setting?  [* · reports]
- 93:3/q24/p1 Occasion reports are contextual clues around the revelation, not necessarily strict causal explanations or records of private emotion. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ notes early uncertainty and divergent accounts. Corpus Coranicum treats personal episodes as literary topoi that need not correspond exactly to biography or Muhammad's emotional state, comparing 108:3 and 111:2. [2 notes: BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 93:3/q25 How should the sūra's verse endings be described and explained?  [وَمَا قَلَىٰ · rhetoric]
- 93:3/q25/p1 They are fawāṣil whose rhythm must be read with semantic need, not treated as forced sound matching or soothsayer-style sajʿ. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ rejects a rhyme-only account of the missing kāf; the last verse ends in thāʾ without an attempted substitution, and Qurʾānic endings serve meaning as well as cadence. [4 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ; BINTSHATI]

## 93:3/q26 What is sworn by in the opening oath before this assurance?  [* · links]
- 93:3/q26/p1 The forenoon and the light God placed in it. — Ibn Kathīr identifies the subject of the preceding oath in explaining the reassurance. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR]
