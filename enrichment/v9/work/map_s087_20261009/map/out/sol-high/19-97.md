# 19:97: 22 questions

## 19:97/q01 How does the opening fa connect this verse to its context?  [فَإِنَّمَا · links]
turns on: Whether the fa explains the duty to convey the revelation or branches from the preceding judgment and promise.
- 19:97/q01/p1 It follows a direction to convey the revelation and states how its good news and warning can be delivered. — Al-Kashshaf calls this the sura's concluding charge; al-Alusi connects the fa to delivery of the revealed sura; Ibn Ashur says it may explain the duty to speak despite the polytheists' anger and their offer to follow if criticism of their gods and ancestors ceased; Bursevi also places it in the context of conveying revelation. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 19:97/q01/p2 It follows the judgment and promise in 19:94–96. — Ibn Ashur relates the resulting warning to the obstinate and the good news to believers. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 19:97/q02 What does the pronoun in yassarnahu refer to, and why is its antecedent unspoken?  [يَسَّرْنَٰهُ · referent]
- 19:97/q02/p1 It refers to the Qur'an, understood without an explicit preceding name. — Ibn Atiyya compares the unspoken antecedent to the sun in 38:32; Ibn Ashur also cites 38:32 and compares the Qur'an's ease in 54:32. Abu Hayyan, al-Alusi, and Ibn Ashur identify the referent directly. Islahi says the sura's opening account of the prophets' shared message supplies it and that omission conveys the Qur'an's majesty. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 19:97/q03 In what sense did God make the Qur'an easy?  [يَسَّرْنَٰهُ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether facilitation denotes Arabic accessibility, the Prophet's ability to recite, fitness for instruction, or descent from an inaccessible divine reality; Tabatabai weighs context against the recitation view.
- 19:97/q03/p1 He made the Qur'an accessible in clear Arabic, enabling understanding and delivery. — Muqatil calls it clear on Muhammad's tongue; al-Suddi says it was eased in his language, and al-Hasan says people otherwise could neither recite nor understand it. Ibn Qutayba, Wahidi, Ibn al-Jawzi citing him, Abu Muslim in Tabrisi, Qurtubi, and Kur'an Yolu explain revelation in the Prophet's or hearers' language. Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Ibn Kathir, Biqai, Bursevi, and Bint al-Shati stress clear or eloquent Arabic. Ibn Ashur says Arabic's excellence and ease of recitation aid distinction and memory, citing 26:192–95; Asad says divine speech is made accessible through human language and concepts, citing 14:4, 2:97, and 26:193–94. Tabatabai prefers Arabic rendering because it serves both purposes and fits the sura; he invokes 43:2–4 for a prior lofty reality. Study Quran cites 44:58 for the Prophet's and Companions' native Arabic; 54:17,22,32,40 describe ease of remembering. Damghani defines taysir generally as facilitation, including the Qur'an and an easy outcome. [30 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(السدي); IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; DAMGHANI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(أبو مسلم); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; BINTSHATI-IJAZ; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(IK, R, Z,Z); TABATABAI+]
- 19:97/q03/p2 He facilitated the Prophet's recitation and made the Qur'an flow on his tongue. — Tabari explains ease of Muhammad's recitation; al-Jubba'i in Tabrisi says God enabled him to recite. Ibn Ashur compares 54:32. Tabatabai reports a view that revelation singled Muhammad out for divine speech and made it flow on his tongue, calling it profound but less suited to context. Al-Kalbi in Wahidi says it was made easy upon the physical tongue. [5 notes: TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); TABRISI(الجبائي); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(قيل); TABATABAI-]
- 19:97/q03/p3 He equipped the Qur'an with what its task of teaching and reminding requires. — Islahi cites 54:17 and says taysir does not mean intellectual simplicity; the sura's close presents the Qur'an as apt and persuasive for conclusive communication. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 19:97/q03/p4 He made an inaccessible divine reality understandable to the Prophet's heart and recitable in Arabic. — The Ta'wilat Najmiyya, reported by Bursevi, says eternal divine speech cannot be contained in finite created letters; Tabatabai describes descent from an inaccessible reality and a prior state not ordinarily understood, citing 43:2–4. [3 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية); TABATABAI]

## 19:97/q04 What does 'your tongue' denote here?  [بِلِسَانِكَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether lisan names the Arabic language or the physical organ on which recitation was facilitated.
- 19:97/q04/p1 The Prophet's language, Arabic. — Ibn Qutayba, Abu Muslim in Tabrisi, and others relate this to revelation in the audience's understandable language; Ibn Ashur emphasizes Arabic eloquence and memorability; Kur'an Yolu cites 12:2 and each prophet's people's language. Samīn explicitly says lisan means language. Study Quran notes Arabic was native to Muhammad and his Companions. [19 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(السدي); IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(أبو مسلم); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(Z)]
- 19:97/q04/p2 The Prophet's literal tongue, upon which recitation was eased. — Al-Kalbi, as reported by Wahidi, expressly denies that lisan means language in this explanation; Study Quran reports an ease-on-the-tongue wording while also tying it to Arabic revelation. [2 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); STUDYQURAN(IK, R, Z)]

## 19:97/q05 How does the preposition in 'bi-lisanika' work grammatically?  [بِلِسَانِكَ · grammar]
turns on: The preposition's sense and whether it attaches to an understood state, an implied sending down, or the act of making easy.
- 19:97/q05/p1 It locates the revelation in the Prophet's language and may form a circumstantial phrase. — Nasafi calls it a circumstantial phrase. Samīn says it may qualify an omitted participle, meaning the Qur'an was made or sent down in his language. Baydawi says the bāʾ can have the sense 'in'. [3 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]
- 19:97/q05/p2 The bāʾ retains its ordinary force because 'made easy' implies sending down. — Baydawi supplies the implied sense of sending down; al-Alusi also connects the preposition to that sense. [2 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 19:97/q05/p3 The bāʾ means 'upon'. — Al-Alusi lists this interpretation. Samīn reports it as an analysis but objects that it is unnecessary and has no clear meaning. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR-]
- 19:97/q05/p4 The bāʾ conveys either cause or accompaniment. — Ibn Ashur gives these two possible relations without choosing between them. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 19:97/q06 Why was the Qur'an facilitated in the Prophet's tongue?  [يَسَّرْنَٰهُ بِلِسَانِكَ لِتُبَشِّرَ وَتُنذِرَ · interpretation]
- 19:97/q06/p1 So the Prophet can communicate its good news and warning intelligibly and effectively. — Muqatil makes clarity serve monotheists' glad tidings and threats; Wahidi says delivery is eased; Abu Muslim says hearers understand Arabic; Qurtubi points to reflection and ready understanding. Bursevi stresses conveyance; Ibn Ashur calls these the purposes of revelation; Islahi calls it apt for conclusive communication and says the Prophet should warn of worldly and afterlife punishment. Kur'an Yolu says the Arab Prophet can understand and explain it; Tabatabai prefers Arabic rendering for both announced purposes; Asad grounds human expression in limited human comprehension. [15 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(أبو مسلم); QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; TABATABAI+]

## 19:97/q07 What variant is reported for 'litubashshira,' and what difference does it make?  [لِتُبَشِّرَ · readings]
turns on: Presence or absence of the doubled shin and the resulting intensity of the announcement.
- 19:97/q07/p1 Form I tabshura, without the doubled shin, conveys plain informing. — Qiraat ER contrasts it with the written Form II tubashshira, which carries solemn heralding. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER]
- 19:97/q07/p2 Form II tubashshira conveys solemn heralding. — Qiraat ER contrasts its doubled shin with Form I's simpler informing. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER]

## 19:97/q08 To whom are the glad tidings addressed, and what makes them God-fearing?  [ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ · referent]
turns on: Whether the recipients already observe piety, are brought to it by the Qur'an, or are distinguished by graded forms of avoidance.
- 19:97/q08/p1 Believers who obey, respond to revelation, and guard against disobedience. — Yahya calls them God-fearing; Wahidi defines them through obedience to the Prophet; Baghawi and Nasafi call them pious believers, and Ibn Kathir calls them responsive. Tabrisi specifies avoiding shirk and major sins. Biqai says they guard against what angers God, neither suppress truth nor legitimate falsehood, and repent promptly. Bursevi defines piety by obeying Qur'anic commands and prohibitions; al-Alusi reports the same explanation. Ibn Ashur opposes their obedience to the opponents' rebellion. [12 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 19:97/q08/p2 Those whom the Qur'an draws toward piety. — Al-Alusi reports this as an alternative to those already observing its commands and prohibitions. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 19:97/q08/p3 They have three grades: avoiding shirk, avoiding sin, and turning from all besides God. — The Ta'wilat Najmiyya, reported by Bursevi, pairs these respectively with tawhid, obedience, and God. [1 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]

## 19:97/q09 What are the glad tidings, and by what are they delivered?  [لِتُبَشِّرَ بِهِ ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ · interpretation]
- 19:97/q09/p1 Through the Qur'an, the pious are promised Paradise, reward, and other pleasing outcomes. — Yahya says the Qur'an conveys the tidings and names Paradise; Tabari and Ibn Atiyya also name Paradise, while Ibn Atiyya adds lasting bliss and worldly honor. Tabrisi describes pleasing divine promises; Abu Hayyan specifies the reward for piety. Muqatil addresses monotheists, and Qushayri says felicity belongs to those enabled to receive the same speech that warns others. Islahi says receptive believers succeed at Judgment. [10 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; QUSHAYRI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 19:97/q10 What does warning by the Qur'an convey?  [وَتُنذِرَ بِهِۦ · interpretation]
- 19:97/q10/p1 It cautions the defiant about divine threats, the Fire, and punishment in this world and the next. — Muqatil mentions the Qur'an's threats; Yahya says the warning of the Fire is through the Qur'an. Wahidi contrasts those who disobey the Prophet with those who obey. Islahi says refusers face their fate and specifies worldly and afterlife punishment. Bursevi cites al-Qamus: indhar informs, cautions, and frightens about the matter. Qushayri says the forsaken face woe even when addressed by the same speech. [7 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; WAHIDI-BASIT; QUSHAYRI; BURSEVI(القاموس); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 19:97/q11 Who are the 'stubborn people' warned here?  [قَوْمًۭا لُّدًّۭا · referent]
turns on: Whether the phrase singles out its immediate Meccan opponents, applies generally, or covers three specified classes of opponents.
- 19:97/q11/p1 Quraysh or the Meccans who opposed Muhammad. — Yahya cites 43:57–58 for Quraysh's disputatiousness; Tabari, Ibn Atiyya, Ibn Abbas in Tabrisi, Kashshaf, Nasafi, Qatada in Abu Hayyan and Durr, al-Alusi, and Islahi identify them. Islahi traces their rigidity and repeated demands to tribal life and illiteracy; Ibn Ashur says their stubbornness persisted despite knowledge of Muhammad's truth, citing 6:33. Ibn Kathir reports Quraysh among transmitted descriptions, and Tabatabai calls them the Prophet's people. [13 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(يحيى); TAB-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(ابن عباس); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قتادة); IBNKATHIR-FULL(السدي); DURR-FULL(قتادة); ALUSI-FULL(قتادة); TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 19:97/q11/p2 All obstinate opponents of truth who resist the warning. — Baghawi, Wahidi Wajiz, Ibn Kathir, and Biqai use general descriptions; al-Alusi likens them to people whose lack of belief follows contention. Ibn Ashur says 'people' makes obstinacy their collective trait, comparing 2:164 and 10:101. Islahi says refusers face their fate. [7 notes: WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 19:97/q11/p3 They include unbelievers, disputing People of the Book, and innovators or philosophers arguing against truth. — The Ta'wilat Najmiyya, reported by Bursevi, distinguishes fighting for falsehood, disputing over superseded religions, and false argument against truth. [1 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]

## 19:97/q12 What does 'luddan' mean?  [لُّدًّۭا · meaning]
turns on: Whether the word names false disputation itself or traits associated with it: failure to act rightly, evil, deafness, injustice, or aversion to truth. Wahidi and al-Alusi favor the disputation sense as primary.
- 19:97/q12/p1 Fierce, obstinate disputants who argue falsely and reject truth. — Muqatil cites 2:204 and connects it to al-Akhnas ibn Shurayq. Majaz cites Muhalhil's 'aladd dha mighlaq' and Ru'ba's al-alwad/al-aldad; a variant reads 'mighlaq al-hujja'. Mwardi cites two poems of hostile persistent dispute and Ibn Bahr's image of medicine placed at the sides of the mouth. Abu Ubayda, quoted by Tha'labi, Baghawi, and Qurtubi, says an aladd refuses truth and claims falsehood; Ibn Zayd cites 2:204. Tabari calls it intense contentiousness, and Wahidi prefers Qatada's false-argument sense over associated traits. Kashshaf, Nasafi, and Abu Hayyan say they take every side of a dispute. Raghib in al-Alusi derives the image from the sides of the neck. Ibn Atiyya says Arabs once boasted of contentiousness as intelligence and manliness, citing a poem, but genuine aladd contention is false. Biqai connects persistence to contempt and arrogance; Ibn Ashur says shirk mixed this vice with otherwise praised Arab traits. Study Quran reports vain argument and insolence. [54 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(قتادة); MAJAZ; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(ابن زيد,قتادة); ZAJJAJ; MAWARDI-FULL(ابن بحر,الشاعر,قتادة); WAHIDI-BASIT(قتادة); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); THALABI(أبو عبيد,ابن عباس,الضحاك,مقاتل); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(مجاهِد); TABRISI(ابن عباس,قتادة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو عبيدة,ابن عباس,الضحاك); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قتادة); SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL(السدي); BURSEVI; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(الضحاك,قتادة); ALUSI-FULL(الراغب,قتادة); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN(Ṭ); WAHIDI-BASIT+]
- 19:97/q12/p2 They fail to act uprightly or straighten themselves. — Mujahid gives this gloss repeatedly through different transmitters; Tha'labi combines it with unjust conduct. Wahidi treats such glosses as aspects of the false disputation, while al-Alusi likewise calls them associated traits. [8 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); YAHYA-SALLAM(مجاهد); TAB-FULL(مجاهد); THALABI(مجاهد); WAHIDI-BASIT(مجاهد); BAGHAWI-FULL(مجاهد); IBNKATHIR-FULL(مجاهد); DURR-FULL(مجاهد)]
- 19:97/q12/p3 They are wicked, sinful, or evildoing people. — Mujahid is cited for wickedness in Tabari, Mwardi, Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, and Abu Hayyan; Ibn Abbas in Durr says wicked or sinful. Ibn Atiyya narrows the wickedness to contentiousness and says other commentators give evildoer or wrongdoer glosses. Al-Alusi counts such glosses as accompanying traits. [9 notes: TAB-FULL(مجاهد); MAWARDI-FULL(مجاهد); IBNATIYYA-FULL(مجاهِد); QURTUBI-FULL(مجاهد); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(مجاهد); IBNKATHIR-FULL(السدي); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); ALUSI-FULL(قتادة)]
- 19:97/q12/p4 They are deaf to truth, especially in their hearts. — Al-Hasan supplies the deafness gloss; al-Rabi' specifies deaf hearts or the ears of the heart. Wahidi says this deafness explains their failure to understand Muhammad and consequent false argument; Study Quran also reports deafness as an implication. Ibn Kathir and al-Alusi include it among transmitted descriptions. [14 notes: TAB-FULL(الحسن); THALABI(الحسن,الربيع); WAHIDI-BASIT(الحسن,الربيع); BAGHAWI-FULL(الحسن); QURTUBI-FULL(الحسن,الربيع); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحسن); IBNKATHIR-FULL(السدي); DURR-FULL(الحسن); ALUSI-FULL(قتادة); STUDYQURAN(Ṭ)]
- 19:97/q12/p5 They are unjust or oppressive people. — Ibn Abbas calls them unjust in Tabari and Abu Hayyan; Ibn Zayd calls al-aladd oppressive and cites 2:204. Tha'labi and Baghawi transmit Mujahid's combined unjust-and-not-upright gloss. Qurtubi says these meanings are close; al-Alusi treats injustice as an associated trait. [8 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن زيد,ابن عباس); THALABI(مجاهد); BAGHAWI-FULL(مجاهد); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن زيد); ALUSI-FULL(قتادة)]
- 19:97/q12/p6 They turn away from truth or are crooked toward it. — Abu Salih gives the turning-away gloss; al-Suddi in Ibn Kathir calls them crooked and turned from truth. Ibn Kathir lists related reports, and al-Alusi attributes the crookedness gloss to Abu Salih. [4 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو صالح); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو صالح); IBNKATHIR-FULL(السدي); ALUSI-FULL(قتادة)]

## 19:97/q13 What is the form and syntactic role of 'luddan'?  [قَوْمًۭا لُّدًّۭا · grammar]
- 19:97/q13/p1 It is an adjective modifying qawman and the plural of aladd, an intensely contentious person. — Jishumi specifies its adjectival role. Ibn Qutayba, Zajjaj, Nahhas, Baghawi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Qurtubi, Samīn, Bursevi, Ibn Ashur, and Tabatabai give the singular. Zajjaj and Nahhas compare summ from asamm; Samīn compares humr from ahmar. Majaz gives poetic forms al-alwad and al-aldad; Jishumi records aludd as a plural, while Nasafi writes the singular aladd in its discussion. [14 notes: MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 19:97/q14 Why are the obstinate singled out for warning and paired with the God-fearing?  [ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ وَتُنذِرَ بِهِۦ قَوْمًۭا لُّدًّۭا · rhetoric]
- 19:97/q14/p1 Their opposition needs a pointed warning; less contentious people yield more readily once proof appears. — Ibn al-Anbari in Wahidi says others follow after proof and the uncontentious submit quickly; Qurtubi similarly says less stubborn people are easier to guide. Ibn Atiyya says Scripture answers their proud contentiousness with the destruction of stronger disputants, and Ibn Ashur reads the pairing as obedience against rebellion and stubborn disbelief despite recognizing truth in 6:33. He says 'people' makes the trait collective, comparing 2:164 and 10:101. [5 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن الأنباري); QURTUBI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 19:97/q15 What Prophetic report bears on fierce disputants?  [لُّدًّۭا · reports]
turns on: The reports address related but distinct vices: combative argument and the pride that sustains rejection.
- 19:97/q15/p1 The intensely disputatious adversary is among the people most hated by God or by the Prophet. — Aisha's report in Abd al-Razzaq says the Prophet most disliked such a man; Tha'labi, Ibn Atiyya, Bursevi, and Ibn Ashur report the formulation that God most hates him. Ibn Ashur calls the report sound; Bursevi applies it to aladd. [6 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(عائشة); THALABI(عائشة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(رسول الله); BURSEVI(النبي); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 19:97/q15/p2 Pride is rejection of truth and contempt for people, not simply enjoyment of good clothing. — Biqai cites Ibn Mas'ud's Prophetic report from Muslim, Abu Dawud, Tirmidhi, and Ibn Majah to explain the obstinacy; he adds that persistent pride risks death in disbelief and exclusion from Paradise. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 19:97/q16 What does the opponents' contentiousness show about the Qur'an's inimitability?  [قَوْمًۭا لُّدًّۭا · interpretation]
- 19:97/q16/p1 Their failure to meet the Qur'anic challenge despite skill and incentive demonstrates inability. — Khattabi says scholars differ over the precise mechanism of inimitability but human inability is plain. Muhammad challenged Arab opponents for twenty years to produce a sura; although they had intelligent minds, skilled orators, poets, and a contentious disposition attested by 43:58, they chose costly fighting and lost lives, kinship, and property. Had a verbal answer been possible, they would have chosen it; he compares dying of thirst beside drinkable water. Bint al-Shati says Qur'anic portrayals cast their polemics as argument rather than a genuine demonstration of ability. [5 notes: KHATTABI; BINTSHATI-IJAZ]

## 19:97/q17 Was this verse revealed during Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf's migration?  [* · reports]
- 19:97/q17/p1 A report associates its revelation with Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf's migration, but Ibn Kathir rejects it. — Ibn Kathir says the whole sura is Meccan, none was revealed after Hijra, and the report's chain is unsound. [0 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL-]

## 19:97/q18 Is the clause ending with 'al-muttaqin' counted as a verse ending?  [ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ · sciences]
- 19:97/q18/p1 It was not counted as an ending because its cadence does not match neighboring endings. — Burhan lists this Maryam clause among such excluded endings, alongside examples at 4:133, 4:172, 43:8, 20:113, and 65:11–12. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 19:97/q19 How does this verse conclude Maryam and connect with neighboring suras?  [* · links]
turns on: These are complementary observations about the sura's ending, the next sura, and the Arabic-letter openings.
- 19:97/q19/p1 It closes Maryam by summarizing the revelation's stories, lessons, promises, warnings, and purpose. — Kashshaf calls it the conclusion's instruction to relay revelation. Ibn Ashur describes a comprehensive ending after varied narratives; Tabatabai pairs it with recollection of earlier peoples' destruction. Islahi says the preceding analysis establishes the sura's coherent structure and treats the close as conclusive communication. Bint al-Shati notes five 'remember in the Book' passages at 19:41,51,54,56 and the closing Qur'an reference, against Rida's exception to Ibn Kathir's observation; she also places nearby suras' prophet stories in an argument for Muhammad's prophethood. [7 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI-IJAZ; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BINTSHATI-IJAZ+]
- 19:97/q19/p2 Maryam's closing statement about the Qur'an's ease and warning is reinforced by Taha's opening. — Al-Alusi notes that both suras open with disconnected letters and cites reports from Ibn Abbas and Jabir ibn Zayd placing Taha after Maryam. Islahi also cites 20:113 in explaining the close's communication and warning. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 19:97/q19/p3 The disconnected letters of nearby suras emphasize that an eloquent Arabic book is made from familiar Arabic letters. — Bint al-Shati surveys openings of one to five letters, observes that the apparent foreign strings become meaningful in eloquent expression, and relates this to the clear Arabic Qur'an of 26:195 and its ease in the Prophet's language. [3 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ]

## 19:97/q20 What reward is reported for reciting Sura Maryam?  [* · sciences]
- 19:97/q20/p1 A report promises ten good deeds for each named prophet and each person who denied or affirmed them, and for those who prayed or did not pray to God. — Kashshaf and Baydawi transmit the report as attributed to the Prophet; their wording varies slightly over whether the prophets or the people associated with them are counted. [2 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(رسول الله); BAYDAWI-FULL]

## 19:97/q21 How does the warning here lead into the destruction mentioned in the next verse?  [وَتُنذِرَ بِهِۦ قَوْمًۭا لُّدًّۭا · links]
turns on: Whether the next verse stresses complete destruction with no surviving sight or sound, or disappearance from human report.
- 19:97/q21/p1 The next verse threatens the obstinate with the disappearance of peoples destroyed before them. — Ibn Atiyya says God rebuts their boast of argumentative strength with former peoples' destruction. Abu Hayyan says 'before them' refers back to the qawman luddan; its interrogative 'do you perceive' has negative force. Ibn Kathir reports Hasan's explanation that none survived to see or be heard, and Tabatabai includes the recall of destruction in the sura's close. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحسن); TABATABAI]
- 19:97/q21/p2 They died and were forgotten, leaving no report about them. — Abu Hayyan reports this alternative explanation of the next verse's question. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 19:97/q22 What readings and meanings of the next verse's sensory words accompany this warning?  [وَتُنذِرَ بِهِۦ قَوْمًۭا لُّدًّۭا · readings]
turns on: Variant forms and derivations in 19:98, plus the breadth of rakz's sensory sense.
- 19:97/q22/p1 The majority read 'hal tuhissu' as the present tense of ahassa. — Abu Hayyan transmits this as the majority reading of the next verse. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور)]
- 19:97/q22/p2 Abu Haywa, Abu Bahriyya, Ibn Abi Abla, and Abu Ja'far read 'tuhassu' with open ta and damma on ha. — Abu Hayyan names these readers and vowels. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو حيوة، أبو بحرية، ابن أبي عبلة، أبو جعفر المدني)]
- 19:97/q22/p3 Another reading derives the verb from hassa, sensing or perceiving. — Abu Hayyan relates the derivation to words for the senses and perceptible things. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 19:97/q22/p4 Hanzala reads 'aw tusmaʿu' passively, 'or is heard'. — Abu Hayyan reports Hanzala's reading of the next verse. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(حنظلة)]
- 19:97/q22/p5 Rakz is a faint sound, an indistinct sound, or sound and perception. — Ibn Qutayba says it is sound that cannot be understood; Ibn Abbas in Abu Hayyan says a faint, quiet sound; Ibn Zayd broadens it to sound or perception. [3 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن زيد,ابن عباس)]
