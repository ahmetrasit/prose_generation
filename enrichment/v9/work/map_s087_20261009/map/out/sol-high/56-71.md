# 56:71: 13 questions

## 56:71/q01 What kind of seeing does the opening question call for?  [أَفَرَءَيْتُمُ · rhetoric]
- 56:71/q01/p1 People should inspect the fire with their eyes and insight and recognize God's power in it. — Al-Biqāʿī calls the kindled fire a particular, unexpected created sign that warrants both sensory inspection and reflection. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 56:71/q02 How is the hamza read in the adjoining fire challenge?  [* · readings]
turns on: Whether the adjacent double-hamza interrogative is lengthened, shortened, softened, deleted, or pronounced with both hamzas; Abū Ḥātim calls the short form weak.
- 56:71/q02/p1 The interrogative āntum has a lengthened hamza or a softened second hamza represented by vowel lengthening. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports lengthening from most readers. QIRAAT-ER describes two Ḥamza variants as replacing or softening the second hamza with alif-madd to ease the double-hamza sequence; it contrasts these with standard Ḥafṣ retaining both hamzas. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الجمهور وأبو عمرو وعيسى وأبو حاتم); QIRAAT-ER(hmz)]
- 56:71/q02/p2 The interrogative āntum is read with a short hamza. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports this from Abū ʿAmr and ʿĪsā; Abū Ḥātim considered it weak. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الجمهور وأبو عمرو وعيسى وأبو حاتم)]
- 56:71/q02/p3 The hamza is completely deleted by tashīl. — QIRAAT-ER reports the Cs reading as an attested canonical form that removes the glottal interruption. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER(Cs)]
- 56:71/q02/p4 Both hamzas are retained. — QIRAAT-ER identifies this as the standard Ḥafṣ form when contrasting it with the Ḥamza reading. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER(hmz)]

## 56:71/q03 Which fire is meant by al-nār?  [ٱلنَّارَ · referent]
turns on: How narrowly nār denotes the kindled phenomenon: ordinary usable fire, fire latent in flint, or the initial spark rather than the resulting flame.
- 56:71/q03/p1 The familiar fire people kindle and draw out of a source. — Muqātil lists wood, stones, and reeds, except al-anab. Al-Thaʿlabī and al-Baghawī mention the firestick; al-Māwardī adds wood, iron, and stone. Al-Qurṭubī mentions striking damp wood. Al-Biqāʿī says the wood may be dry or green and hides the fire before extraction. Elmalılı explains generation by friction and contact; Asad contrasts people's kindling with God's creation of its fuel tree. [10 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ASAD-NOTES]
- 56:71/q03/p2 The fire concealed in flint and struck from it. — Al-Dāmghānī gives this as a sense of nār. [1 notes: DAMGHANI]
- 56:71/q03/p3 Specifically the spark produced by the fire drill, rather than the resulting flame. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the pronoun in the following shajaratihā refers to this fire and denotes the kind of wood used to kindle it. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL+]

## 56:71/q04 What source or tree yields the fire?  [ٱلنَّارَ · referent]
turns on: Whether the following tree points to a special tinder species, any suitable wood, or a figurative identification with the fire's fuel and flames.
- 56:71/q04/p1 A particular kind of tinder tree, especially marḫ and ʿafār, yields it readily. — Al-Rāzī proposes a species such as marakh used with a fire drill. Ibn ʿAṭiyya includes soft woods such as marḫ and ʿafār among firestick materials, alongside stones and stone with iron. Al-Qurṭubī cites the proverb that every tree contains fire but these two abound in it and ignite readily; al-Biqāʿī cites a like saying that they are best. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 56:71/q04/p2 Any suitable wood or fuel tree can supply it. — Al-Rāzī says fire catches on wood more readily than on other materials. Abū Ḥayyān takes the tree as actual wood from which fire is struck and objects to making the tree itself fire. Muqātil also lists wood, stones, and reeds as means of kindling, except al-anab; al-Māwardī includes wood, iron, and stone; al-Biqāʿī allows dry or green wood. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL; MUQATIL; MAWARDI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+]
- 56:71/q04/p3 The tree can denote the sources and fuel of the flames, by which fire cooks things. — Al-Rāzī gives this as a third proposal. Abū Ḥayyān objects that construing the tree itself as fire is an unnecessarily strained metaphor. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]

## 56:71/q05 What does tūrūn mean here?  [تُورُونَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the verb describes visible perception or the action of extracting and igniting fire; Abū Jaʿfar argues from its firestick derivation.
- 56:71/q05/p1 You kindle or strike fire, drawing it out from a firestick or other source. — Ibn Qutayba, Abū ʿUbayda as reported by Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Zajjāj, al-Kashshāf, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Nasafī, al-Samīn, al-Burūsawī, al-Ālūsī, Ibn ʿĀshūr, and al-Majmaʿ as reported by al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī explain extraction or ignition. Al-Zajjāj distinguishes a catching fire drill from awraytu, causing ignition. Al-Kashshāf and al-Nasafī describe rubbing two sticks, upper male and lower female, likened to mating cattle; al-Burūsawī names the upper zand and lower zandah, while Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the upper zand or zinād. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites Dhū al-Rumma's enigmatic description. Al-Majmaʿ says a flint strike that ignites is expressed differently from one that fails, called akba. Abū Jaʿfar, reported by al-Naḥḥās, derives the verb from kindling a firestick and rejects ocular seeing. [20 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; ZAJJAJ; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو عبيدة,ابن قتيبة,الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; NAHHAS(بعض العلماء)+]
- 56:71/q05/p2 It means seeing the fire with the eyes. — Al-Naḥḥās reports this from some scholars but says Abū Jaʿfar rejects it on the firestick derivation. [0 notes: NAHHAS(بعض العلماء)-]

## 56:71/q06 What are the verb's form and related firemaking usages?  [تُورُونَ · grammar]
- 56:71/q06/p1 Tūrūn derives from tawriyūn and belongs to the firestick verb whose transitive form draws out flame. — Al-Samīn states the original form توريون and distinguishes striking a firestick to draw out flame from the intransitive form, in which its fire comes forth. Al-Zajjāj explains awraytu as causing ignition; Abū ʿUbayda, reported by Ibn al-Jawzī, notes that wariytu is more usual; al-Qurṭubī gives warā al-zand yarī for a firestone sparking and mentions another pronunciation. [5 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ZAJJAJ; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو عبيدة); QURTUBI-FULL]

## 56:71/q07 What is omitted from the relative clause 'which you kindle'?  [ٱلَّتِى تُورُونَ · grammar]
- 56:71/q07/p1 Its object return, referring to the fire, is omitted. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says deletion of accusative resumptive pronouns in relative clauses is common; the understood wording is 'which you kindle.' [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 56:71/q08 What is understood in the phrase 'kindle the fire'?  [ٱلنَّارَ تُورُونَ · grammar]
- 56:71/q08/p1 It means 'kindle its tree,' with a genitive complement left unspoken. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the following verse makes the omitted complement clear and that Arabic commonly uses this kind of ellipsis. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 56:71/q09 What figurative resonance does the hidden fire have?  [ٱلنَّارَ تُورُونَ · rhetoric]
- 56:71/q09/p1 Its emergence evokes tawriya: something outward appears while another meaning is intended. — Al-Biqāʿī likens the fire hidden in wood and then manifesting with great power to this rhetorical figure. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 56:71/q10 What does the example of fire teach or recall?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Which implication of the fire example is emphasized: warning of Hell, possibility of resurrection, recognition of God's power and favor, or His lordship and the duty of worship.
- 56:71/q10/p1 It recalls Hellfire and prompts seeking God's protection. — Al-Ṭabrisī reports ʿIkrima, Mujāhid, and Qatāda: seeing earthly fire reminds one of the greater Fire. The prophetic report transmitted by Abū Hurayra, cited by al-Baghawī, compares earthly fire with Hellfire. [2 notes: TABRISI(عكرمة ومجاهد وقتادة); BAGHAWI-FULL(أبو هريرة)]
- 56:71/q10/p2 It makes resurrection or a second creation conceivable. — Al-Ṭabrisī reports the explanation that the One who produces fire from a green tree can create again. Ibn ʿĀshūr says drawing fire from apparently dead sticks shows something can emerge from lifeless wood. [2 notes: TABRISI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 56:71/q10/p3 It displays God's creative power and His gift of useful fire. — Al-Thaʿlabī and Asad contrast people's kindling with God's creation of the source tree. Al-Biqāʿī calls the hidden fire's emergence an unexpected created sign for recognition of divine power. Ibn ʿĀshūr sees a reminder of God's favor; Kuran Yolu links familiar useful fire with reflection on the Creator's will and power and the duty of worship. [5 notes: THALABI; ASAD-NOTES; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 56:71/q10/p4 Alongside crops and water, it establishes God's lordship over the necessities of worldly life. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says creation and allotted death prepare the argument for resurrection and recompense; crops, water, and fire, the three chief necessities, establish lordship as governance rooted in ownership. Kuran Yolu likewise connects creation and familiar resources with the duty of worship. [2 notes: TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 56:71/q11 How does earthly fire compare with Hellfire in the reported saying?  [ٱلنَّارَ · reports]
- 56:71/q11/p1 The fire people kindle is one part in seventy of Hellfire; Hellfire surpasses it by sixty-nine parts. — Al-Baghawī transmits this prophetic saying from Abū Hurayra. [1 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(أبو هريرة)]

## 56:71/q12 How does this fire example fit the neighboring examples?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes explain different features of the sequence: the order of resources, the repeated reminder of favor, and the absence of a removal threat for fire.
- 56:71/q12/p1 It follows the movement from crops and trees to water, completing a sequence of basic resources. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the discourse moves from water to fire as it earlier moved from crops and trees to water; striking trees produces fire and wood fuels it. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī names crops, water, and fire the three chief necessities of worldly life. Kuran Yolu connects the questions about human creation, crops, rain, fresh water from salty seas, and useful fire. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 56:71/q12/p2 It continues the preceding water example's reminder of divine favor. — Ibn ʿĀshūr explicitly compares the fire's favor with the preceding argument about drinking water at 56:68. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 56:71/q12/p3 Its treatment differs from food and water because no threat to remove fire is stated. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī says people can live without fire but cannot live without food or water, explaining why the passage describes fire's condition without a removal threat. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
