# 36:69: 27 questions

## 36:69/q01 Who is meant by 'him' in 'We taught him'?   [عَلَّمْنَٰهُ · referent]
- 36:69/q01/p1 Muhammad, the Messenger. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls this unanimous; al-Suddī, Yaḥyā, Abū Ḥayyān and Iṣlāḥī identify him directly. [6 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; IBNABIHATIM(السدي); IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; DURR-FULL(السدي); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 36:69/q02 What does God’s not teaching him poetry deny?  [وَمَا عَلَّمْنَٰهُ ٱلشِّعْرَ · interpretation]
turns on: The scope of teaching: poetic craft or authorship, the Qur’an’s form, or a stronger denial of poetic facility.
- 36:69/q02/p1 He was not made a poet and did not acquire the craft or compose poetry; quoting an existing line remains possible. — Al-Naḥḥās calls this the apparent wording; Abū Isḥāq and al-Zajjāj distinguish recitation from composition. Al-Qāḍī doubts inability to memorize. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ says the denial implies inability to compose, not mere abstention. [16 notes: TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق); JISHUMI(القاضي); MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; QURTUBI-FULL(الزجاج والنحاس)+]
- 36:69/q02/p2 God did not teach poetry through the Qur’an: what he brought is no poem. — Al-Kashshāf and al-Ālūsī distinguish the taught Qur’an from metered, rhymed, imaginative poetry; al-Ālūsī says this also implies he is no poet. Al-Bayḍāwī contrasts form and meaning. [10 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; BURSEVI(الشريف الجرجاني); IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 36:69/q02/p3 He was protected from poetry and had no natural facility for its meter or memorization. — Qatāda says God protected him; Ibn Kathīr says he did not master, enjoy or retain regular meter. Al-Shaʿbī says he alone among ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib’s children did not recite poetry; al-Biqāʿī argues talent would not arise after his long youth without verse. [11 notes: IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(الشعبي); BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(قتادة); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(IK, Q)]
- 36:69/q02/p4 God withheld the basic act of teaching, without an intensified denial. — Al-Burhān compares form II with 73:4, where tartīlā indicates abundant measured recitation; abundance is impossible in this negation. [2 notes: BURHAN]

## 36:69/q03 Why does the verse single out poetry?  [ٱلشِّعْرَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the charge concerns authorship, poetic form, imaginative effect, or older beliefs about poets.
- 36:69/q03/p1 It answers the accusation that Muhammad was a poet and the Qur’an poetry. — Muqātil names ʿUqba and companions; al-Kalbī and Muqātil say Meccan polytheists also said sorcerer and liar. Al-Rāzī says poetry alone is specified because the Qur’an, unlike reports of unseen or miracles, was the direct challenge (2:23). Kur’an Yolu notes the cited accusations call him a poet, not always the Book poetry. [29 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي ومقاتل); KASHSHAF-FULL(عقبة بن أبي معيط); IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(المفسرون); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL(مشركو قريش); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI(الراغب,بعض المحصلين); ALUSI-FULL(القائلون من الكفار); ELMALILI; ASAD-NOTES(Muhammad's opponents); BINTSHATI-IJAZ; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî); STUDYQURAN]
- 36:69/q03/p2 The accusation meant deceptive or imaginative speech, rather than a claim about exact meter. — Al-Bāqillānī suggests subtle verbal craft or philosophers’ metaphorical use of poet for sages, preferring these to a literal charge made by people ignorant of meter; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ stresses poetry’s effect; investigators cited by Bursavī say poet meant liar. Kur’an Yolu traces the old association of poet and unseen influence. [11 notes: BAQILLANI; BURSEVI(الحكماء القدماء,بعض المحصلين); BINTSHATI-IJAZ; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; BAQILLANI+; BAQILLANI-]
- 36:69/q03/p3 A literal metrical accusation was made by people ignorant of poetic meters. — Al-Bāqillānī calls it least plausible; Bursavī reports al-Rāghib’s account that some unbelievers heard meter and rhyme. [1 notes: BURSEVI(الراغب); BAQILLANI-]
- 36:69/q03/p4 The verse is a divine denial of a poet’s speech, parallel to other Qur’anic denials. — Al-Bāqillānī cites 26:224 and 69:41; al-Ṭabarī says thoughtful hearers recognize revelation, not poetry or soothsaying; Anīs tested the Prophet’s words against meters, while ʿUtba and perceptive Quraysh denied that the Qur’an matched poetry. [7 notes: TAB-FULL; BAQILLANI; QURTUBI-FULL(أنيس,عتبة بن ربيعة,قريش); MUSLIM(ابن عباس); STUDYQURAN]

## 36:69/q04 What is shiʿr or poetry in this discussion?  [ٱلشِّعْرَ · meaning]
turns on: Definition by craft and intention, meter alone, or imaginative premises.
- 36:69/q04/p1 In ordinary usage, poetry is deliberately metered and usually rhymed speech. — Al-Rāghib traces shiʿr to precise knowing; al-Qāmūs notes a broader knowledge sense but conventional verse won the name. Al-Rāzī makes intent to meter decisive; incomplete rajaz and one isolated line are disputed. [10 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI(الراغب,القاموس,بعضهم); QURTUBI-FULL; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(أكثر أهل صناعة العربية,الباقلاني,العلماء)]
- 36:69/q04/p2 In philosophers’ usage, poetry is discourse built on imaginative, possibly false premises, whether metered or not. — Bursavī reports ancient philosophers and distinguishes logical from conventional poetry; al-Sharīf al-Jurjānī notes the appeal of imagined premises and the maxim that the best poetry is most false. [4 notes: BURSEVI(الحكماء القدماء,الشريف الجرجاني,بعضهم)]
- 36:69/q04/p3 Rajaz, especially shortened forms, does not count as poetry. — Al-Khalīl is variously reported to exclude truncated, half, and sometimes four-foot rajaz; some define the full form by six mustafʿilun feet. Al-Qurṭubī records disagreement over paused bāʾs; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports this defense. [8 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; KASHSHAF-FULL(الخليل); QURTUBI-FULL(الخليل,قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الخليل,قوم); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(بعضهم)]
- 36:69/q04/p4 Rajaz or metered speech cannot simply be denied the name poetry. — Al-Naḥḥās and al-Qurṭubī reject denying observable Arab meters, citing al-Khalīl’s transmitted verses; the exact case endings can change a scan. [2 notes: NAHHAS; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 36:69/q04/p5 Poetry can contain wisdom and good speech; its meter alone does not make it blameworthy. — Al-Jurjānī cites the maxim that good poetry is good and bad poetry bad, even reported as Prophetic; he praises diction, metaphor, allusion and meaning. Al-Rāzī says meaning is the heart of wording, and cites the saying that some poetry contains wisdom. [11 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL(العلماء); RAZI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبي بن كعب وبريدة بن الحصيب وعبد الله بن عباس,أمية بن أبي الصلت,صحابي)]

## 36:69/q05 What does 'poetry is not fitting for him' mean?  [وَمَا يَنۢبَغِى لَهُۥٓ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether yanbaghī concerns facility, moral fitness, actual composition, or the Qur’an as antecedent.
- 36:69/q05/p1 Poetry neither comes easily to him nor suits his prophetic role. — Abū Isḥāq glosses yanbaghī as 'be easy'; Ibn ʿAbbās says he could not keep a line in meter; al-Rāzī reports both 'not natural' and 'not easy' variants. Al-Biqāʿī adds not sought, proper, or possible; al-Rāghib compares cloth made subject to fire. [24 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; MUQATIL; NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,ابن عباس,المفسرون); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; MAWARDI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL(الخليل); RAZI-FULL(آخرون,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,قوم); QURTUBI-FULL(الخليل بن أحمد,الزجاج); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(الراغب); STUDYQURAN(IK, Q)]
- 36:69/q05/p2 He is not to be a poet or compose poetry, although reciting a line is possible. — Al-Zajjāj and al-Naḥḥās say no-poet does not mean never quoting verse; the Prophet’s own disclaimer follows Abū Bakr’s corrections. [9 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق); QURTUBI-FULL(الزجاج والنحاس,قيل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الرسول صلى الله عليه وسلم); BAGHAWI-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 36:69/q05/p3 Poetry cannot fit a truth-telling messenger because meter, invention, and exaggeration can distort meaning. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya contrasts poets’ imaginings with truths and proofs; al-Rāzī says a lawgiver starts with meaning whereas a poet starts with diction; Iṣlāḥī cites 26:224–227 on poets’ contradictions, failure to practice claims and emotional scope, with rare exceptions. [31 notes: QUSHAYRI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(حسان بن ثابت); BURHAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية); BURSEVI(الشريف الجرجاني); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عطية)+; RAZI-FULL+]
- 36:69/q05/p4 Its unsuitability is a matter of prophetic dignity and truth, without denying natural ability. — Bursavī argues ability need not yield composition and rebuts mixing a logical definition of poetry with a metrical reading of yanbaghī. Some literary writers say he could compose but refrained; Bursavī reports their position then says it conflicts with the verse. Al-Ālūsī also rejects this claimed ability. [7 notes: BURSEVI(البغوي,بعض الأدباء); ALUSI-FULL(بعض الناس); TABATABAI; BURSEVI+]
- 36:69/q05/p5 It is unfitting for the Qur’an, rather than for Muhammad, to be poetry. — The antecedent is supplied by context despite not being named, says Ibn ʿAṭiyya; al-Ālūsī calls this remote because the preceding pronoun naturally points to Muhammad. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 36:69/q06 To whom does 'for him' refer?  [لَهُۥٓ · referent]
turns on: Which contextual antecedent the pronoun takes.
- 36:69/q06/p1 To Muhammad. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya allows it; Abū Ḥayyān prefers it and al-Ālūsī rejects the other reading as remote. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+]
- 36:69/q06/p2 To the Qur’an, understood from context. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya says proximity supplies the unnamed Qur’an; al-Bayḍāwī and al-Samīn record it, whereas Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī object. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 36:69/q07 How is yanbaghī derived?  [يَنۢبَغِى · meaning]
- 36:69/q07/p1 It comes from seeking or attaining: what is sought and made easy is said to yanbaghī. — Al-Wāḥidī and Bursavī give ṭalab and the idiom 'I sought it and it came about'; al-Rāghib analogizes fire's fitness to burn cloth. [3 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; BURSEVI(الراغب)]

## 36:69/q08 Is a different vocalization of yanbaghī transmitted?  [يَنۢبَغِى · readings]
- 36:69/q08/p1 A reading has short vowels on both bāʾs and no lengthening of the first tāʾ. — Al-Bayḍāwī reports the form alongside discussion of whether the pronoun refers to Muhammad or the Qur’an. [1 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL]

## 36:69/q09 Why was poetic skill withheld from Muhammad?  [وَمَا عَلَّمْنَٰهُ ٱلشِّعْرَ وَمَا يَنۢبَغِى لَهُۥٓ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the reason is removing suspicion, protecting prophetic truth and dignity, or a judgment on poetry.
- 36:69/q09/p1 To remove suspicion that his poetic gift produced the Qur’an, making revelation’s proof clearer. — Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī and others compare his inability to write (29:48); Ibn al-Ḥājib says composing would invite doubt. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Ālūsī reply that his eloquent prose could invite the same doubt, while the miracle itself removes it. [11 notes: THALABI; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(قوم); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قوم); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن الحاجب); STUDYQURAN; IBNATIYYA-FULL-; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية)-; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 36:69/q09/p2 To raise him above poetry’s imagined embellishment and keep prophetic speech directed to truth. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the Qur’an’s arrangement and divine knowledge distinguish it; al-Ālūsī prefers this over the suspicion argument. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ says the exclusion is elevation, not deficiency; al-Biqāʿī contrasts serious prophetic meaning and rhyme-driven distortion. [22 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية); BIQAI-FULL(حسان بن ثابت); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عطية)+]
- 36:69/q09/p3 The verse necessarily disparages poetry itself. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya explicitly holds this; Mālik advises against frequent recitation, while Abū Ḥayyān reports a view calling poetry inferior. Al-Jurjānī and Ibn al-ʿArabī reject inference that poetry or eloquence is inherently defective. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(مالك); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قوم); JURJANI-DALAIL-; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن العربي)-; BINTSHATI-IJAZ-]
- 36:69/q09/p4 Denying poetry to him does not denigrate poetry as such. — Ibn al-ʿArabī compares his not writing; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says the distinction protects recognition of Qur’anic miracle. Al-Maʾmūn calls lack of poetry a virtue for the Prophet but a deficiency for another man. ʿUmar’s inquiry and Labīd’s cessation after al-Baqara are reported. [15 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(ابن العربي,المأمون,عمر بن الخطاب); BINTSHATI-IJAZ; JURJANI-DALAIL(العلماء); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(أبو هريرة,الخليل)]
- 36:69/q09/p5 God's choice shows that He teaches the Prophet what He wills and withholds what He wills. — Al-Rāzī draws the theological inference directly. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 36:69/q10 What can be inferred from the Prophet's encounters with poetic lines?  [ٱلشِّعْرَ · reports]
turns on: The reported line, attribution, meter, correction, and chain differ across transmissions.
- 36:69/q10/p1 He sometimes quoted existing poetry with words or meter altered, then disclaimed being a poet. — ʿĀʾisha says it was his most disliked speech; al-Ḥasan’s Islam-and-gray-hair line and Ṭarafa’s unexpected-news line are recurring examples, with Abū Bakr or ʿUmar correcting him. Ibn Kathīr cites al-Tirmidhī’s ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ grading for a Ṭarafa report and al-Bukhārī’s correction of another wording. [48 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(عائشة); ABDURRAZZAQ(عائشة); TAB-FULL(عائشة); IBNABIHATIM(الحسن البصري,عائشة); THALABI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,عائشة); WAHIDI-BASIT(العباس بن مرداس,طرفة,عبد بني الحسحاس); JISHUMI(القاضي,عائشة); BAGHAWI-FULL(الحسن,رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم,عائشة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن بن أبي الحسن,عائشة); TABRISI(الحسن,عائشة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو بكر); RAZI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); QURTUBI-FULL(الحسن بن أبي الحسن,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس,الحسن البصري,عائشة,قتادة); DURR-FULL(أبو بكر,ابن عباس,عائشة); BURSEVI(إنسان العيون); ALUSI-FULL(الحسن,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,عائشة); TABATABAI(أبو بكر عن النبي صلى الله عليه وآله وسلم,الحسن وعائشة)]
- 36:69/q10/p2 He quoted or voiced correctly metered lines on rare occasions without becoming a poet. — Al-Qurṭubī names a line of Ibn Rawāḥa; Ibn Kathīr notes authentic Trench work-song reports. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ preserve the fact of quotation alongside the denial of poetic authorship. [9 notes: ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS; QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); TABATABAI(الحسن وعائشة)]
- 36:69/q10/p3 The poet behind the attempted line was al-Aʿshā. — Yaḥyā prefers this identification. [1 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM+]
- 36:69/q10/p4 The poet behind the attempted line was Ṭarafa. — Several ʿĀʾisha versions and the report of an invocation against Ṭarafa name him; he corrected the words but called the meanings equivalent. Other versions call the poet a man of Banū Qays. [16 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(أبان العطار,بعضهم); ABDURRAZZAQ(عائشة); TAB-FULL(عائشة); THALABI(عائشة); WAHIDI-BASIT(طرفة); BAGHAWI-FULL(عائشة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(عائشة); QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس,عائشة,قتادة); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,عائشة); ALUSI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,عائشة)]
- 36:69/q10/p5 The quoted line was by ʿAbd Allāh ibn Rawāḥa. — Al-Baghawī attributes a line about unexpected news to him; Ibn Kathīr cites Trench work-songs; Abū Ḥayyān says its meter need not imply poetic craft. [5 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(عائشة); QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 36:69/q10/p6 The al-ʿAbbās ibn Mirdās spoil-division line was quoted with names reversed or reordered. — Ibn Hishām records the poem, a variant line, and the figurative 'cut his tongue' payment; Abū Bakr or al-ʿAbbās corrects al-Aqraʿ and ʿUyayna, with the Prophet saying the sense is the same. Al-Suhaylī sees the new order as honoring loyal al-Aqraʿ over later apostate ʿUyayna. [11 notes: IBNHISHAM(أبو بكر الصديق,ابن إسحاق,عباس بن مرداس,يونس النحوي); WAHIDI-BASIT(العباس بن مرداس); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو بكر); QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNKATHIR-FULL(السهيلي,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); DURR-FULL(أبو بكر); ALUSI-FULL(عبد الرحمن بن أبي الزناد)]
- 36:69/q10/p7 A report says he completed or composed one hopeful poetic line. — Al-Mizzī objects that its transmitters are unknown; al-Durr says the chain has an unknown narrator and ʿĀʾisha proposed a nonscanning inflection. Al-Ālūsī also marks an unknown narrator. [3 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(عائشة); DURR-FULL(عائشة); ALUSI-FULL(عائشة)]
- 36:69/q10/p8 He began a line among the Badr dead and Abū Bakr completed it. — Al-Umāwī’s report is cited by Ibn Kathīr. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 36:69/q10/p9 He identified a most poetic speaker by quoting a line beginning 'Do you not see me ... at night?' — Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ālūsī give the question; Abū Ḥayyān includes this and the booty line. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 36:69/q10/p10 He said he did not care if he used an antidote, wore an amulet, or composed poetry. — Ibn Kathīr says Abū Dāwūd alone has the report; al-Durr also records it. [2 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(عبد الله بن عمرو); DURR-FULL(رسول الله)]
- 36:69/q10/p11 A rare report condemns composing a line after night prayer. — Ibn Kathīr specifies composing, not merely reciting. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(شداد بن أوس)]

## 36:69/q11 Do accidentally metered Qur’anic or Prophetic phrases count as poetry?  [ٱلشِّعْرَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether poetry requires intent, minimum length, or a particular audible scansion.
- 36:69/q11/p1 No: poetry requires deliberate meter or poetic craft; natural speech may scan by accident. — Examples include 3:92, 18:29, 34:13, 61:13; the Prophet’s Ḥunayn 'I am the Prophet' and injured-finger sayings, and ordinary market speech. Al-Qurṭubī says the exact pronunciation is unknown; al-Nāsafī gives possible inflections, while the bloodied-finger line scans only with a particular tāʾ vowel. [39 notes: NAHHAS; WAHIDI-BASIT; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن العربي,الأخفش,الخليل,النحاس,قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI(إنسان العيون,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,بعضهم); ALUSI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); TABATABAI(مجمع البيان); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(أكثر أهل صناعة العربية,الباقلاني,العلماء,بعضهم)]
- 36:69/q11/p2 The Ḥunayn line should be acknowledged as poetry or deliberate verse, making rejection of the report easier. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ finds denials of its meter or deliberateness less persuasive than rejecting the report. [1 notes: TABATABAI(مجمع البيان)]
- 36:69/q11/p3 A short rhymed line or two alone does not make a poet. — Al-Bāqillānī is cited for a two-line minimum, and some require consistent meter or rhyme; al-Qurṭubī says even conceding poetry, rare quotation cannot make him learned in it. [3 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(أكثر أهل صناعة العربية,العلماء); QURTUBI-FULL]
- 36:69/q11/p4 Its poetic status depends on actual pronunciation and inflection. — Al-Naḥḥās and al-Qurṭubī report claims that nominative or genitive endings break the 'I am the Prophet' meter; Ibn al-ʿArabī suggests those endings, but al-Qurṭubī notes pronunciation is not known. For the finger line, a kasra gives sarīʿ while a still tāʾ may not. [9 notes: NAHHAS; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن العربي,النحاس,قيل); NASAFI-FULL]

## 36:69/q12 How did the Prophet regard poetry as speech?  [ٱلشِّعْرَ · reports]
turns on: Whether reports speak of all poetry or distinguish its content and amount.
- 36:69/q12/p1 He disliked poetry, especially excess or false, obscene, and satirical verse. — ʿĀʾisha repeats that it was his most disliked speech; Abū Hurayra's pus report is given with a strong chain by Ibn Kathīr, and al-Ālūsī construes it as excess. Bursavī distinguishes objectionable kinds. [15 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(عائشة); TAB-FULL(عائشة); IBNABIHATIM(عائشة); THALABI(عائشة); BAGHAWI-FULL(عائشة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(عائشة); QURTUBI-FULL(الحسن بن أبي الحسن); IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو هريرة,عائشة); DURR-FULL(عائشة); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(أبو هريرة,عائشة)]
- 36:69/q12/p2 He valued wise and morally useful poetry and supported Muslim poets. — Al-Khalīl says some poetry was dear to him; he encouraged a Companion through 100 lines by Umayya, praised wisdom in poetry, and promised Ḥassān support by the Holy Spirit and a mosque pulpit. Al-Ālūsī reconciles these reports by kinds of verse. [10 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(الخليل); QURTUBI-FULL(الخليل بن أحمد); TABRISI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبي بن كعب وبريدة بن الحصيب وعبد الله بن عباس,أمية بن أبي الصلت,صحابي); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الخليل)]
- 36:69/q12/p3 A poet's wise words may coexist with a disbelieving heart. — The Prophet said Umayya’s poetry believed while his heart did not, according to Ibn Kathīr. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(أمية بن أبي الصلت)]

## 36:69/q13 What do the first denials imply about the final positive clause?  [إِنْ هُوَ إِلَّا ذِكْرٌۭ وَقُرْءَانٌۭ مُّبِينٌۭ · rhetoric]
- 36:69/q13/p1 The exclusive positive statement strengthens the denial: it is revelation, reminder and Qur’an, not poetry. — Al-Jurjānī compares 53:3–4; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ calls the restriction qaṣr al-qalb. Al-Burhān notes that a tightly explanatory or emphatic clause need not be joined by a conjunction. [3 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL; BURHAN; TABATABAI]
- 36:69/q13/p2 Inna here has the sense of negative ma, forming 'it is only ...'. — Yaḥyā explicitly glosses the construction this way. [1 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM]

## 36:69/q14 What does the pronoun 'it' refer to?  [هُوَ · referent]
turns on: Whether the antecedent is the delivered Qur’an, the Messenger, or the act of teaching.
- 36:69/q14/p1 The Qur’an or what was taught and delivered. — Muqātil, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Nāsafī and al-Qurṭubī identify the revelation, which is clarified by the following nouns. [6 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]
- 36:69/q14/p2 Muhammad himself is the reminder sent to people. — Al-Ṭabarī explicitly identifies Muhammad as dhikr and the associated message as Qur’an; Qatāda instead identifies the phrase with this Qur’an. [1 notes: TAB-FULL]
- 36:69/q14/p3 The knowledge implied by 'We taught him'. — Al-Samīn says the earlier verb supplies knowledge as an antecedent. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 36:69/q15 What is dhikr here?  [ذِكْرٌۭ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether dhikr names the message or the Messenger.
- 36:69/q15/p1 A reminder, reflection, and admonition from God for people and jinn. — Yaḥyā includes believers among jinn and humans; al-Ḥasan says it recalls Paradise, while another report says it concerns God. Sahl stresses understanding and obedience. Iṣlāḥī says it recalls innate truths, the history of guidance and consequences of rejecting messengers; Study Quran notes earlier scriptures also called Reminder. [29 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(الحسن,بعضهم); TUSTARI; SULAMI(سهل); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(قتادة); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 36:69/q15/p2 The Messenger Muhammad is himself the reminder sent to people. — Al-Ṭabarī makes this identification, alongside the Qur’an as the message. [1 notes: TAB-FULL]

## 36:69/q16 What is meant by 'a clear Qur’an'?  [وَقُرْءَانٌۭ مُّبِينٌۭ · interpretation]
- 36:69/q16/p1 A manifest divine recitation or book that clarifies truth and supplies guidance, duties and rulings. — Ibn ʿAbbās lists obligations, penalties and rulings; al-Ṭabrisī adds proofs and earlier nations. Asad takes mubīn as clear itself and truth-disclosing. Iṣlāḥī stresses reasoned guidance for all life; Kur’an Yolu says it invites thought rather than riddling. [26 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 36:69/q16/p2 A heavenly book recited in worship, whose recitation and practice bring success. — Al-Kashshāf, al-Nāsafī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Biqāʿī and Kur’an Yolu stress prayer recitation and practice; al-Zajjāj and Bursavī call its enduring miracle a prophetic proof. [12 notes: ZAJJAJ; KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(كشف الأسرار); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 36:69/q16/p3 The word Qur’an means a gathering of parts as well as recitation. — Al-Ṭabrisī says the two names dhikr and Qur’an carry distinct benefits: rulings and proofs versus gathered parts. [1 notes: TABRISI]

## 36:69/q17 How is Qurʾān read and derived here?  [قُرْءَانٌۭ · readings]
- 36:69/q17/p1 A hamza-less qurān is linked to q-r-y, 'to gather', rather than q-r-ʾ, 'to read'. — The reading source says the emphasis shifts toward compilation. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER(hmz)]

## 36:69/q18 What does 'and' contribute between reminder and Qur’an?  [وَقُرْءَانٌۭ · grammar]
- 36:69/q18/p1 It joins distinct benefits of admonition and a recited comprehensive book. — Al-Ṭabrisī says the labels are not redundant; Bursavī says the Qur’an includes rulings beyond admonition. Asad compares 15:1 and calls the Qur’an integral to revelation. [3 notes: TABRISI; BURSEVI; ASAD-NOTES]

## 36:69/q19 How does the verse connect to the sūra and nearby verses?  [* · links]
- 36:69/q19/p1 It returns to the opening assertion of Muhammad's mission and the revealed, wise Qur’an. — Al-Rāzī says monotheism and resurrection preceded the third principle, prophethood; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ, Asad, Iṣlāḥī and Study Quran see a return to the opening revelation theme. The next verses specify who benefits and move to signs calling for gratitude. [10 notes: RAZI-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; BINTSHATI-IJAZ; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 36:69/q19/p2 The Qur’an and providential blessings give the same clear message, yet deniers evade it by calling the Book poetry. — Iṣlāḥī relates the closing passage to its later signs, moral responsiveness and reassurance to the Prophet. [3 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 36:69/q20 What is the inward reading of not teaching poetry?  [وَمَا عَلَّمْنَٰهُ ٱلشِّعْرَ · inward]
- 36:69/q20/p1 God did not address Muhammad in coded, ambiguous speech or leave him ignorant of hidden intended meanings. — Ibn ʿArabī treats poetry as riddling and double meaning; Bursavī asks how this treats disconnected opening letters and suggests he may not see them as reserved ambiguity. [2 notes: BURSEVI(الشيخ الأكبر)]
- 36:69/q20/p2 All skills and states ultimately come from divine teaching, through humans or directly. — Al-Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya cites 2:31 and David’s armor in 21:80; Bursavī adds craft by insight, Mathnawī lines on revelation and reason, and Cain learning burial from the crow in 5:31. [5 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]

## 36:69/q21 Does the verse's denial extend to other prophets?  [وَمَا عَلَّمْنَٰهُ ٱلشِّعْرَ · theology]
turns on: Whether the verse's pronoun and rationale establish a general rule or a particular honor.
- 36:69/q21/p1 All prophets were barred from poetry. — Al-Ālūsī reports that the verse gives no apparent reason to restrict the denial to Muhammad. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 36:69/q21/p2 Muhammad alone lacked poetic ability as an added honor; others may have ability without composing. — Al-Ālūsī reports a possibly authentic account of Adam composing verses after his son's death as support. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(آدم عليه السلام,بعضهم,قائلون)]

## 36:69/q22 What do unrelated grammatical examples in the collected notes establish?  [* · grammar]
- 36:69/q22/p1 Diminutives normally do not restore deleted roots, though reported irregular forms occur. — Sībawayh gives miyyit and huwayr; Yūnus reports huwayʾir and Abū ʿAmr’s hamzated forms; the rule also controls other diminutives, with irregular forms not generalized. [5 notes: SIBAWAYH(أبو عمرو,يونس)]
- 36:69/q22/p2 Four- and five-radical words preserve original consonants; defective roots follow specific lengthening and weak-letter changes. — Sībawayh distinguishes jaʿfar and safarjal, rejects false augment analyses, and gives qawwal, buwiʿ, changed wāw/yāʾ, heavy hypothetical yawm forms, hamzated plurals and long-vowel patterns. [17 notes: SIBAWAYH]
- 36:69/q22/p3 Nisba from two-radical nouns restores a lost final radical, with reported dialect and analogy exceptions. — Sībawayh cites abawī, akhawī, ḥamawī; al-Khalīl supports akhawī and calls Yūnus’s ukhtī nonanalogical. Other variants use wāw or hāʾ roots, supported by plurals and a poem. [5 notes: SIBAWAYH(الخليل,يونس)]
- 36:69/q22/p4 Vocatives normally drop feminine hāʾ, with pause and dialectal exceptions. — Sībawayh reports yā Salama, pause restoration, meter-licensed omission, and limits on deleting earlier letters. [5 notes: SIBAWAYH]
- 36:69/q22/p5 The negative particle lā cannot govern a definite noun, while coordination changes the case analysis. — Sībawayh compares rabb and gives lā rajula laka wa akhāh. [2 notes: SIBAWAYH]

## 36:69/q23 What do other cited reports and reading discussions add?  [* · reports]
- 36:69/q23/p1 The Buḥayrā account describes signs noticed during Muhammad's youthful Syria journey. — Ibn Isḥāq reports the monk's inherited Christian knowledge, cloud, bending tree, and insistence that the boy join the caravan meal. [3 notes: IBNHISHAM(ابن إسحاق)]
- 36:69/q23/p2 A literary anecdote and poetry examples illuminate claims about character, age, and craft. — Al-Qurṭubī recounts an ill layman asking for a physician; Ibn Kathīr cites an unnamed al-Ḥamāsa fragment. Al-Biqāʿī cites Banū Qurayʿ verse on wealth, poverty, muruwwa, and innate character. [5 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 36:69/q23/p3 Canonical seven-reader selection is editorial, not the seven revealed aḥruf or an exhaustive test of sound readings. — Ibn al-Jazarī says writings still use weak readings; early imams criticized Ibn Mujāhid’s seven; al-Mahdawī says narrowing to seven then two transmitters misled the public, sometimes excluding stronger readings. [7 notes: NASHR(أحمد بن عمار المهدوي,الأئمة المتقدمون)]
- 36:69/q23/p4 The neighboring warning verb has a second-person tāʾ reading and a third-person yāʾ reading. — Al-Thaʿlabī gives Medinan, Syrian and Basran support for tāʾ except Abū ʿAmr; al-Jishumī names Abū Jaʿfar, Nāfiʿ, Ibn ʿĀmir and Yaʿqūb. The yāʾ can refer to Qur’an, Book or Prophet. [2 notes: THALABI; JISHUMI(أبو جعفر، نافع، ابن عامر، يعقوب)]
- 36:69/q23/p5 A report says Abū Dharr’s brother found Muhammad commanding noble morals and speaking words unlike poetry. — Muslim transmits Ibn ʿAbbās’s account. [1 notes: MUSLIM(ابن عباس)]

## 36:69/q24 How do these sources distinguish Qur’anic speech from poetry?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the contrast is in form, priority of meaning, or source.
- 36:69/q24/p1 The Qur’an is unlike poetry in form and in its relation of sound to meaning. — Elmalılı says its arrangement has neither poetic meter nor rhyme. Asad says every sound and position serves the intended meaning. Abū Isḥāq says denying poetic teaching does not forbid the Prophet from quoting a line; the Qur’an differs from created speech and Arab meters. [3 notes: ELMALILI; ASAD-NOTES; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق)]
- 36:69/q24/p2 The Qur’an is the revealed reminder, whereas poetry may be associated with satanic prompting. — Qatāda identifies the phrase with this Qur’an. Al-Nāsafī contrasts it with satanic poetry, while Bursavī says God taught knowledge of former and later peoples but not Satan's poetry, which Satan called his own Qur’an. [3 notes: TAB-FULL(قتادة); NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI]

## 36:69/q25 Why might the Prophet not retain poetry?  [وَمَا عَلَّمْنَٰهُ ٱلشِّعْرَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether it arose from his attention or God's diversion.
- 36:69/q25/p1 He did not attend to memorizing it. — Al-Jishumī offers this as one explanation of altered quotations. [1 notes: JISHUMI]
- 36:69/q25/p2 God diverted him from it. — Al-Jishumī offers divine diversion as another explanation. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 36:69/q26 Could Muhammad evaluate poetry despite not composing it?  [ٱلشِّعْرَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether ability to judge verse implies ability to compose it.
- 36:69/q26/p1 He could distinguish good from bad verse, perhaps metered from unmetered speech. — Bursavī reports al-Baghawī's distinction and suggests that 'good and bad' may mean discerning meter. [1 notes: BURSEVI]
- 36:69/q26/p2 Claims that he could compose poetry were used to undermine the verse. — Yanbūʿ al-Ḥayāh calls those advancing this claim outwardly Muslim heretics. [1 notes: BURSEVI(ينبوع الحياة)]

## 36:69/q27 How was Muhammad's wise speech formed?  [وَمَا عَلَّمْنَٰهُ ٱلشِّعْرَ · interpretation]
- 36:69/q27/p1 His wisdom and concise noble expression came through inspiration and Gabriel's instruction. — Al-Biqāʿī adds that he chose the easier option except where sin or severing kinship was involved. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
