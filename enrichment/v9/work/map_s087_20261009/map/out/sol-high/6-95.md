# 6:95: 26 questions

## 6:95/q01 Why is the opening assertion emphatic and nominal?  [إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ فَالِقُ ٱلْحَبِّ وَٱلنَّوَىٰ · rhetoric]
- 6:95/q01/p1 It affirms God's enduring creative attribute and presses a neglected inference. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says polytheists accepted these acts but ignored their implication for resurrection, so inna addresses a state like doubt; the nominal form conveys an enduring attribute of act or power. He says the sentence is not formally restrictive. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 6:95/q02 What does fāliq mean here?  [فَالِقُ ٱلْحَبِّ وَٱلنَّوَىٰ · meaning]
turns on: Whether falq is creation, the planted seed opening into new growth, an existing fissure, or separation from the parent plant.
- 6:95/q02/p1 God splits grain and fruit stones so plants and trees emerge. — Qatāda, al-Suddī, Ibn Zayd and many others describe shoots, ears, palms, and trees emerging. Al-Zajjāj stresses dry seed yielding tender green growth. Al-Ṭabarī prefers this because the next clause explains the split; Ibn ʿAṭiyya finds it comprehensive; Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī call it the apparent or majority reading. Rāzī describes shoots rising and roots descending in moist soil. Elmalılı details pressure and cracks. ## 6:95/q03 What lexical range supports the word fāliq?  [فَالِقُ · meaning]
- 6:95/q03/p1 Its root is splitting or separating parts; related senses name things split or openings. — Ṭabrisī cites a low place as though split from earth; Samīn lists a hollow between rises, Moses' sea, morning, rivers (27:61), a split thing, a marvel, and gaps among mountains or camel humps (113:1). Abū Ḥayyān, Bursevī, Elmalılı, Ibn ʿĀshūr and al-Rāghib give the core sense. [7 notes: TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الراغب); BURSEVI; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 6:95/q04 How is fāliq al-ḥabb constructed grammatically?  [فَالِقُ ٱلْحَبِّ · grammar]
- 6:95/q04/p1 It is an active participle in annexation to the grain. — Jishumī says annexation drops nunation and makes al-ḥabb genitive. Samīn says genuine annexation can refer to completed action, supported by ʿAbdallāh's past tense reading; alternatively non-genuine annexation narrates a present or future act, with al-ḥabb genitive in form but accusative in syntactic position. With nunation Jishumī makes it accusative object. [3 notes: JISHUMI; SAMIN-DURR]

## 6:95/q05 Is fāliq read as a participle or a past verb?  [فَالِقُ · readings]
turns on: Whether the divine action is worded as a participial attribute or a completed finite act.
- 6:95/q05/p1 The familiar wording has the active participle fāliq. — Samīn discusses its annexation and its past or ongoing sense. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR]
- 6:95/q05/p2 ʿAbdallāh reads falaqa as a past tense verb. — Abū Ḥayyān reports the reading; Samīn uses it to support taking the annexation of the familiar participle as genuine. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(عبد الله); SAMIN-DURR(عبد الله)]

## 6:95/q06 What does al-ḥabb denote?  [ٱلْحَبِّ · meaning]
- 6:95/q06/p1 It is the collective or plural for grains and seeds, especially those without fruit stones. — Al-Ṭabarī, Jishumī, Ṭabrisī, Elmalılı and Ibn ʿĀshūr give ḥabba as the singular; Muqātil and others list wheat, barley and corn, and al-Kalbī contrasts them with pitted fruit. Elmalılı calls them herb seeds. [15 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; THALABI(الضحاك); WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي,مقاتل); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(مجاهد، أبو مالك); RAZI-FULL(الأكثرون); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 6:95/q07 What does al-nawā denote?  [ٱلنَّوَىٰ · meaning]
turns on: Whether nawā names the familiar fruit stone or the germ within a seed.
- 6:95/q07/p1 It denotes the stones or kernels of dates and other fruit. — Muqātil names peaches, jujubes, apricots, grapes, pears; other lists add figs. Al-Layth gives nawāt as singular and a verb for stone formation. Samīn notes collective form, distance as another sense, hardened date pit, and final radical yāʾ. Ālūsī distinguishes common unqualified date use from broader fruit pit use and records variant gender and plurals. [21 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; THALABI(الضحاك); WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي,الليث,مقاتل); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL(الأكثرون); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 6:95/q07/p2 The word more aptly denotes the plant germ inside a grain. — Elmalılı reasons that both ḥabb and nawā are plants and compares this germ with the kernel of a date or grape. [1 notes: ELMALILI]

## 6:95/q08 What does splitting seeds show about God's action?  [فَالِقُ ٱلْحَبِّ وَٱلنَّوَىٰ · interpretation]
- 6:95/q08/p1 The coordinated emergence and design of plants reveal God's power, wisdom, provision and singular governance. — Rāzī argues opposite upward and downward growth cannot follow one natural tendency; fragile roots penetrate dense soil, while walnuts, citrons, grapes, leaf veins, fruit arrangements and animal-specific effects show a choosing designer. Biqāʿī relays these examples. Elmalılı notes plant anatomy and diverse structures; Iṣlāḥī notes seed potential and coordinated earth, sky, winds, clouds and seasons; Ālūsī calls it perfect power. Jishumī calls grain and pits signs of provision and fruit. Rāzī traces plant service through animals to human knowledge, love and worship (51:56); leaf contemplation recalls uncountable favors (14:34). [34 notes: JISHUMI(قيل); RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(الإمام الرازي); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; TABATABAI]
- 6:95/q08/p2 The seed's return from decay is evidence for resurrection. — Al-Zajjāj says observed creation establishes ability to raise the dead; Abū Ḥayyān connects the kernel sign with the preceding resurrection discussion (6:94); Iṣlāḥī compares seeds rising from earth after decay with human burial and resurrection. [7 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 6:95/q09 What inward meanings are given to the grain and stone?  [ٱلْحَبِّ وَٱلنَّوَىٰ · inward]
turns on: Whether the seed image maps to inner moral growth or to believers' and disbelievers' primordial clay.
- 6:95/q09/p1 They symbolize contents or capacities of the heart and soul. — Ibn ʿAṭāʾ says the heart's seed yields sincerity and ostentation; another interpretation says God reveals sincerity and hypocrisy. Qāshānī says spirit light splits the heart's seed into knowledge and gnosis and heart light splits the soul's kernel into virtues. Qāshānī also says God changes people's states, so they should not turn from Him. [4 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء); MAWARDI-FULL(بعض أصحاب الغوامض); QASHANI]
- 6:95/q09/p2 Grain and kernel symbolize mixed clay of believers and disbelievers. — A report from Abū ʿAbdallāh makes grain believers' loved clay and kernel disbelievers' clay distanced from good; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī explicitly labels this inward rather than apparent meaning and defers the clay traditions. [2 notes: TABATABAI(أبو عبد الله)]

## 6:95/q10 What are the living and dead brought out of one another?  [يُخْرِجُ ٱلْحَىَّ مِنَ ٱلْمَيِّتِ وَمُخْرِجُ ٱلْمَيِّتِ مِنَ ٱلْحَىِّ · referent]

## 6:95/q11 Are plants literally living in this wording?  [ٱلْحَىَّ ٱلْمَيِّتِ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether 'life' is taken strictly as sentient or animate life or extended to vegetation.
- 6:95/q11/p1 Growth may be called life and dry or inert matter death by broad usage or metaphor. — Al-Ṭabarī and al-Zajjāj cite Arab usage of living upright vegetation; Nasafī invokes revived earth (30:50). Bursevī says literal life requires sensation and voluntary motion, so growth is metaphorical. Ālūsī says applying literal life and death to plants requires a general metaphor or combined senses. [8 notes: TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; TABRISI(الزجاج); RAZI-FULL(الزجاج); NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام)]
- 6:95/q11/p2 Strictly, living means possessing life, so plants are not living. — Rāzī draws the literal boundary; this favors the human and bird examples. Jishumī explicitly ranks person and bird interpretations higher because they are literal; Ṭabrisī calls the semen example soundest. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; JISHUMI; TABRISI(الحسن وقتادة وابن زيد)]

## 6:95/q12 What reported incidents apply the life-from-death phrase to people?  [يُخْرِجُ ٱلْحَىَّ مِنَ ٱلْمَيِّتِ · reports]
turns on: Which woman and episode the reported application concerns.
- 6:95/q12/p1 The Prophet applied it to Khālida bint al-Aswad, a righteous woman with an unbelieving father. — Ibn Abī Ḥātim transmits a visit in which the Prophet asks who she is and praises God as the one bringing living from dead; another version supplies her character and father's unbelief. [2 notes: IBNABIHATIM(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 6:95/q12/p2 The Prophet recited it for Umm Kulthūm bint ʿUqba after her migration. — Ibn ʿĀshūr records that she migrated to Medina, refused return to the polytheists, and received this figurative application. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(النبي)]

## 6:95/q13 How is the clause 'He brings the living from the dead' parsed?  [يُخْرِجُ ٱلْحَىَّ مِنَ ٱلْمَيِّتِ · grammar]
- 6:95/q13/p1 It explains the seed splitting, either independently or as another predicate of inna. — Kashshāf, Rāzī, Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān and Bayḍāwī say sprouting explains how splitting brings life from dead matter; Samīn supplies the independent and second-predicate parses. Ibn ʿĀshūr favors the unjoined clarifying clause and allows a broader second predicate; Ibn Kathīr says the explanation precedes the next coordinated phrase. [10 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); IBNKATHIR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL+]

## 6:95/q14 Why is the living mentioned before the dead?  [ٱلْحَىَّ ٱلْمَيِّتِ · rhetoric]
- 6:95/q14/p1 The order honors the living. — Ibn al-Ṣāʾigh lists living before dead among examples of precedence for honor, alongside men before women, free before enslaved, and hearing before sight; Ibn ʿAṭiyya transmits al-Naqqāsh on the latter pair. [1 notes: ITQAN(ابن الصائغ)]

## 6:95/q15 Why use a verb for bringing out the living and a participle for the dead?  [يُخْرِجُ ٱلْحَىَّ وَمُخْرِجُ ٱلْمَيِّتِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the contrast privileges life's renewal over death's stability or jointly conveys recurrence and permanence.
- 6:95/q15/p1 The verb depicts renewed action and the more striking, mobile production of life; the participle conveys stable production of death. — ʿAbd al-Qāhir compares recurring divine provision (35:3) with a dog's fixed posture (18:18). Rāzī and the Imam say life is nobler and gets more verbal attention; Biqāʿī calls the participle settled fact. Ibn al-Munīr adds vivid present tense and temporal priority, while Iṣlāḥī stresses God's decision over unavoidable death. Samarrai links movement with life and stillness with death; ʿAlī's examples include 22:63 and 2:15. [15 notes: RAZI-FULL(عبد القاهر الجرجاني); BIQAI-FULL; ITQAN(فخر الدين الرازي); ALUSI-FULL(ابن المنير,الإمام,عبد القاهر); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; SAMARRAI-ASRAR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 6:95/q15/p2 Both forms together mark the processes as recurring, constant and intrinsic, with neither privileged. — Ibn ʿĀshūr reads the imperfect as repeated deliberate action rather than coincidence and the participle as permanence; he declines to make one process superior. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 6:95/q16 How is al-mayyit read?  [ٱلْمَيِّتِ · readings]
turns on: Whether yāʾ is geminated or light in the two occurrences.
- 6:95/q16/p1 Nafiʿ, Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī and Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim double the yāʾ in both occurrences. — Rāzī records this distribution; Nashr says Abū Jaʿfar doubles yāʾ across the listed parallels, including 'the living from the dead and the dead from the living.' [2 notes: RAZI-FULL(نافع، وحمزة، والكسائي، وحفص عن عاصم); NASHR(أبو جعفر)]
- 6:95/q16/p2 Other readers lighten both occurrences to mayt. — Rāzī says the light form holds consistently for this form elsewhere; Qiraat Er says mayt may suggest recent or less permanent death and is repeated here for internal consistency. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL(نافع، وحمزة، والكسائي، وحفص عن عاصم); QIRAAT-ER]

## 6:95/q17 What does 'bringing the dead from the living' coordinate with?  [وَمُخْرِجُ ٱلْمَيِّتِ مِنَ ٱلْحَىِّ · grammar]
turns on: Whether syntax follows the matching participles or the paired meanings of life and death.
- 6:95/q17/p1 The participle coordinates with fāliq, while the intervening finite clause explains seed splitting. — Kashshāf and its followers align two participial predicates; Zarkashī endorses this because bringing dead from living does not explain seed splitting. Abū Ḥayyān notes noun and verb may still coordinate semantically; Samīn cites al-Zamakhsharī and poetry with mixed forms. [13 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); IBNKATHIR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL(الزمخشري); BURHAN(الزمخشري)+]
- 6:95/q17/p2 It coordinates with the preceding verbal clause as the paired, completing opposite. — Ibn Mālik treats the noun as verbal in sense, and Samīn gives this alternative, citing poems permitting noun and verb coordination. Ibn ʿĀshūr prefers the semantic pairing and 6:96 analogy; Ibn al-Munīr says the frequent paired present clauses make a syntactic break unlikely. Burhān sets out coordination by overt wording, syntactic position, and supposition. [10 notes: BURHAN(ابن مالك); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(ابن المنير); IBNASHUR-FULL+]

## 6:95/q18 What follows from God producing opposite outcomes?  [يُخْرِجُ ٱلْحَىَّ مِنَ ٱلْمَيِّتِ وَمُخْرِجُ ٱلْمَيِّتِ مِنَ ٱلْحَىِّ · theology]
turns on: Whether diverse outcomes imply a freely acting divine governor and how created causes participate in that governance.
- 6:95/q18/p1 Opposite and varied effects point to a wise, freely acting God rather than an independent uniform nature. — Rāzī uses opposed plant growth, diverse fruit qualities and a medicinal counteraction between poison and snakebite; Biqāʿī and Elmalılı likewise reject uniform natural causes. Ibn ʿĀshūr says a free agent can produce opposites; Biqāʿī says fixed intrinsic properties could not. Iṣlāḥī argues a rival god should be able to suspend the universal life-death law; uninterrupted matter or blind force would not explain change. [19 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(الإمام الرازي); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 6:95/q18/p2 Created causes really act, but continuously depend on God's determination and guidance. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the Qur'an attributes real effects to creatures and presumes regular causation (7:101), while tracing all changing capacities, actions and ends to God (13:16; 39:62; 25:2; 20:50; 87:2-3). He rejects a withdrawn creator, occasionalism, suspended causality in miracles, and origin-only dependence. Higher and proximate causes form a hierarchy: cosmic order and ongoing change attest continual divine bestowal (14:10; 28:88; 25:3; 17:20). [20 notes: TABATABAI(هؤلاء); TABATABAI(جم غفير من أهل الكلام)-]
- 6:95/q18/p3 Natural effects are mere divine habit without real causal connection. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports this occasionalist objection through fire and heat, then rejects it as undermining the causal law used to establish God and scripture. [1 notes: TABATABAI; TABATABAI-]
- 6:95/q18/p4 Once God originates things, they persist through independent natural causes. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports and rejects a God who withdraws after creation and theologians who deny the need for divine sustenance; he says the latter view came through an Israelite idea and would deny badāʾ and abrogation. [3 notes: TABATABAI(جم غفير من أهل الكلام); TABATABAI-]
- 6:95/q18/p5 The whole integrated universe has one governor. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī points to mutually serving plants, animals, humans and celestial systems; Iṣlāḥī says each seed requires coordinated cosmic conditions and excludes separate gods in different domains. [6 notes: TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 6:95/q19 Who is 'that' in 'That is Allah'?  [ذَٰلِكُمُ ٱللَّهُ · referent]
- 6:95/q19/p1 It identifies the sole agent of the described creative acts as God, alone entitled to worship. — Muqātil, al-Ṭabarī, Ibn ʿAbbās and others stress no partner acts with Him. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the demonstrative emphatically identifies Him by His great name and rebukes obtuseness; Bursevī notes majesty. Iṣlāḥī observes that Arabs conceded God created but still associated partners; al-Māturīdī relates provisions to God as humanity is related to one soul (4:1). [21 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL(بعضهم); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الماتريدي); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]

## 6:95/q20 How is 'That is Allah' parsed?  [ذَٰلِكُمُ ٱللَّهُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the identification is complete on its face or read with an implied predicate.
- 6:95/q20/p1 It is a subject and predicate, an independent identifying assertion. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it a warning; Qurṭubī states the subject-predicate parse. Ibn ʿĀshūr makes the following question parenthetical. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 6:95/q20/p2 The clause may carry an omitted predicate supplied by God's lordship. — Iṣlāḥī reads it with 6:102 and supplies 'your Lord'; he also allows the complete reading, 'all this is God's benevolence.' [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 6:95/q21 What does annā mean in the question?  [فَأَنَّىٰ · meaning]
turns on: Whether annā expresses manner or source/direction.
- 6:95/q21/p1 It asks 'how' they can be turned away. — Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Ḥasan gloss it as 'how'; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it an incredulous rejection. [6 notes: IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس,الحسن); MAWARDI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; DURR-FULL(الحسن); BIQAI-FULL]
- 6:95/q21/p2 It asks 'from where' or 'in what direction' they are turned. — Al-Zajjāj asks from where they turn from truth; Ibn ʿĀshūr takes it as 'from where,' indicating no valid direction away from divine oneness. [3 notes: ZAJJAJ; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 6:95/q22 What does tuʾfakūn mean?  [تُؤْفَكُونَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether ifk is described as turning aside or as the falsehood that results.
- 6:95/q22/p1 It means being turned, diverted or inverted away from truth. — Jishumī explains ifk from deviation and inverted places; Bursevī calls it turning something aside. Ibn ʿĀshūr defines afk as turning from a place or act and says the passive leaves several causes possible: Satan, leaders and personal desire. Abū Muslim calls the passive an Arabic idiom. Al-Suddī specifies minds turned from recognizing these signs. [22 notes: ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(الحسن); NAHHAS; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(أبو مسلم); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; DURR-FULL(السدي)]
- 6:95/q22/p2 It can be glossed as lying or being deceived. — Ibn ʿAbbās glosses it 'you lie'; al-Durr's report of him gives 'be deceived or lie.' [2 notes: IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]

## 6:95/q23 What does the closing rebuke challenge?  [فَأَنَّىٰ تُؤْفَكُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the rebuke chiefly exposes idolatry or denial of resurrection.
- 6:95/q23/p1 It rebukes turning from God's oneness and worship to idols or partners after manifest signs. — Al-Ṭabarī says crops and fruit come from the one God rather than powerless, deaf and blind idols. Jishumī reports Abū ʿAlī's 'how make idols gods'; Ṭabrisī stresses worship of the benefactor. Bursevī addresses Quraysh in the Meccan sura; Iṣlāḥī says they initially see truth but turn aside. [26 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; NAHHAS; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(أبو علي,أبو مسلم); KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL(بعضهم); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 6:95/q23/p2 It also challenges denial of resurrection despite proof from life emerging from death. — Al-Aṣamm gives this as the closing question; al-Zajjāj, Nasafī and Rāzī say first generation of life from semen or seed establishes capacity to raise bodies from dust. [8 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج); JISHUMI(الأصم); RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 6:95/q24 Does the passive rebuke imply that God does not create human deviation?  [تُؤْفَكُونَ · theology]
turns on: Whether moral blame requires human acts to lie outside divine creation.
- 6:95/q24/p1 Human deviation cannot be created by God if the rebuke is to be fitting. — Al-Ṣāḥib ibn ʿAbbād argues that asking how people turn away would be unfitting if God created their turning. Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports a wider argument that divine creation of ugly acts would defeat choice, commands and blame. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL(الصاحب بن عباد); ALUSI-FULL(الصاحب بن عباد); TABATABAI(جمع من الباحثين)]
- 6:95/q24/p2 Divine creation of acts can coexist with human responsibility and the rebuke. — Rāzī replies that uncaused choice among equal alternatives is chance, while a determining motive ultimately returns to God. Ṭabāṭabāʾī distinguishes chosen acts from involuntary states, created reality from conventional moral labels: sin and obedience are judged in social or legal relation, while every created existent is good as existent (32:7); commands and accountability remain. He rejects dualism. [10 notes: RAZI-FULL; TABATABAI; TABATABAI(جمع من الباحثين)-]

## 6:95/q25 How does this verse connect with surrounding and parallel passages?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the link is the immediate sequence of signs or a parallel life-death formulation elsewhere.
- 6:95/q25/p1 It resumes the argument for oneness through visible creation and leads into the following cosmic signs. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya sees continuity with the preceding God-idol contrast; Rāzī and Ālūsī say discourse returns from unity and prophecy to proofs of power, knowledge and wisdom. Biqāʿī links Abraham's argument and 6:95–96 to 6:14 and 6:100; Elmalılı and Iṣlāḥī connect split dawn with split seed. Iṣlāḥī sees a move from small to large signs; Samarrai's tafṣīl discussion tracks seeds, dawn, sun, moon and stars. [17 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; JISHUMI; TABRISI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; STUDYQURAN; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 6:95/q25/p2 It echoes other life-from-death verses and their grammatical settings. — Ibn al-Jawzī, Abū Ḥayyān and Ṭabāṭabāʾī refer back to 3:27; Abū Ḥayyān says its two preceding finite clauses suit verbal coordination. Kashshāf and Nasafī invoke 30:50 on revived earth. Samarrai says the event-rich Āl ʿImrān uses verbs while al-Anʿām foregrounds attributes with nouns. Iṣlāḥī references 6:37, 6:102 and 56:86–87; Asad points to his note on 5:90. [12 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURHAN; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ASAD-NOTES; SAMARRAI-ASRAR]

## 6:95/q26 What other reports or cited technical examples accompany discussions of the verse?  [* · reports]
turns on: These are distinct ancillary excerpts rather than rival interpretations of the verse.
- 6:95/q26/p1 A report uses the grain-splitting oath in a statement about love of ʿAlī. — Qurṭubī cites Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim: ʿAlī swears by the One who split grain and created the soul that the Prophet covenanted only a believer loves him and only a hypocrite hates him. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(علي)]
- 6:95/q26/p2 A literary excerpt juxtaposes poetic pairs on gifts, friendship, love, loyalty, scale, generosity and agency. — ʿAbd al-Qāhir compares Umayya and Abū Tammām on gifts; Jarīr and Abū Nuwās on friends and enemies; Kathīr and Abū Tammām on first love; al-Mutanabbī and Abū Tammām on fidelity; al-Buḥturī and al-Mutanabbī on streams and sea, innate generosity and the cutting hand; and al-Buḥturī and Abū Tammām on liberality. The supplied notes state no specific tie from these pairs to 6:95. [8 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL]
- 6:95/q26/p3 Technical recitation excerpts discuss articulation points and assimilation in other cited phrases. — Nashr locates throat, uvular, mid-tongue, tongue-edge and tongue-tip letters, recording disputes over ghayn/khāʾ, shīn/jīm/yāʾ and ḍād. Its assimilation examples concern ʿukūf, thāʾ, jīm in 'akhraja shaṭʾahu' with both clear and assimilated transmissions, ḥāʾ in 'zuḥziḥa ʿan', and other phrases. These notes do not state a variant of this verse's words except the separate al-mayyit note.
