# 2:132: 38 questions

## 2:132/q01 What does enjoining by waṣṣā or tawṣiya mean?  [وَوَصَّىٰ · meaning]
- 2:132/q01/p1 It is beneficial admonition or a covenant urging a person toward obedience or pious action. — Abū Ḥayyān defines waṣiyya as an admonitory instruction; Jishumī calls it a command to obey. Bayḍāwī and Ālūsī derive its sense from joining the recommender's act to the recipient's, opposed to separation; Ālūsī allows speech or sign and times other than death. Ibn ʿĀshūr says it is stronger than an ordinary order because loss is feared, especially at death or travel; he cites the Prophet's farewell-like counsels to al-ʿIrbāḍ and Muʿādh, and the counsel against anger. Bursevī says it may benefit religious or worldly life. Ibn Abī Ḥātim reports Ibn ʿAbbās that Abraham counseled adherence to God's religion, Islam; Rāzī says the designation 'testament' is more emphatic than an order because concern for religion intensifies as death approaches. [9 notes: JISHUMI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL]

## 2:132/q02 How is وَوَصَّىٰ read, and do its forms differ in force?  [وَوَصَّىٰ · readings]
turns on: Whether the two Arabic verb forms merely alternate or the doubled form carries intensity, repetition, or a religious use.
- 2:132/q02/p1 Nāfiʿ and Ibn ʿĀmir read awṣā with alif; others read geminated waṣṣā; both convey enjoining, while some give waṣṣā greater force or repetition. — Al-Farrāʾ, Thaʿlabī, Baghawī, Rāzī, and others associate awṣā with Medinan/Syrian codices; Abu Jaʿfar and Ibn ʿAbbās are also named. Ibn Mujāhid and al-Fārisī specify readers. Al-Fārisī and Ṭabrisī support Form II by tawṣiya and Form IV by 4:11–12 and waṣiyya; the root denotes joining plant/house parts. Zajjāj, Māwardī, Ibn al-Jawzī and others say gemination intensifies or repeats counsel. Qirāʾāt-ER describes awṣā as formal bequeathing. Thaʿlabī and Qurṭubī report codex variants; Abū Ḥayyān relays Thaʿlab's twelve further Medinan/Iraqi differences. Kashshāf also locates wa-awṣā in the codices of the Hijaz and Syria. [35 notes: FARRA; IBNMUJAHID(نافع، ابن عامر); IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; FARISI-HUJJA; THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; JISHUMI(أبو جعفر ونافع وابن عامر); ZAJJAJ; MAWARDI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(نافع وابن عامر); TABRISI(أهل المدينة والشام,الباقون); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL(نافع وابن عامر); NASAFI-FULL(شامي,مدني); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ثعلب,نافع وابن عامر); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(نافع وابن عامر); ELMALILI; QIRAAT-ER; BIQAI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL]
- 2:132/q02/p2 The two forms are synonymous, with no necessary contrast in frequency or force. — Al-Farrāʾ says both are sound and common; Naḥḥās rejects a forced multiplicity because it makes the geminated reading less apt, comparing karramnā/akramnā. Jishumī, Ṭabrisī, Qurṭubī, Baghawī and Ālūsī treat them as equivalent; Baghawī compares anzala/nazzala. Sāmarrāʾī proposes a separate tendency: waṣṣā for religion and awṣā for worldly inheritance, while 19:31 is a religious exception; he compares karrama/akrama. [10 notes: FARRA; JISHUMI; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(نافع وابن عامر); SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; SAMARRAI-ASRAR; NAHHAS+]

## 2:132/q03 What does the feminine pronoun in بِهَآ refer to?  [بِهَآ · referent]
turns on: Which feminine antecedent the context supplies: the explicit religion, the nearer declaration, or an implied feminine notion.
- 2:132/q03/p1 It refers to Abraham's previously mentioned religion or way, the milla. — Zajjāj cites the surrounding mention of Abraham's way at 2:130. Jishumī prefers the explicit antecedent as broad enough to include the other suggestions; the Qāḍī, Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī likewise prefer its explicitness and breadth. Rāzī's Qāḍī says it encompasses all that secures afterlife success; Ālūsī links it to prophets transmitting true religion. [21 notes: ZAJJAJ; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(الزجاج); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; ALUSI-FULL+; JISHUMI(أبو علي والزجاج)+; QURTUBI-FULL+; RAZI-FULL(القاضي)+]
- 2:132/q03/p2 It refers to Abraham's declaration 'I have submitted to the Lord of the worlds,' understood as a feminine word or sentence. — Zamakhsharī cites 2:131 and 43:28, where it is called an enduring word; Ibn ʿAṭiyya prefers this nearer antecedent. Samin corrects an attribution by noting Zamakhsharī also gave it. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the declaration condenses the religion's meaning. [17 notes: THALABI; JISHUMI(أبو القاسم وبريدة); KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(الزجاج); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية)+; IBNATIYYA-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR(ابن عطية)+; QURTUBI-FULL-]
- 2:132/q03/p3 It refers to the confession of God's oneness, 'There is no god but God.' — Ibn ʿAbbās and Muqātil call this the word of sincerity; Ṭabrisī connects it to the enduring word of 43:28. Thaʿlabī and Baghawī report the same identification. [8 notes: THALABI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(ابن عباس ومقاتل); BAGHAWI-FULL(الكلبي ومقاتل); TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); SAMIN-DURR; QASHANI]
- 2:132/q03/p4 It refers to the testament or command itself, including the later instruction to die Muslim. — ʿIkrima and Zajjāj identify the matter of counsel; Abū ʿUbayda, Abū Ḥayyān, Samin and Biqāʿī allow the testament or the subsequent charge as an implied feminine antecedent. [6 notes: THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(عكرمة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL]
- 2:132/q03/p5 It refers to implicit obedience or Abraham's chosen quality. — Al-Mufaḍḍal supplies obedience and adduces Arabic idiom and poetry, including 53:33; Abū Ḥayyān and Samin list obedience, and Biqāʿī allows the chosen quality. [4 notes: THALABI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL]

## 2:132/q04 Why does Abraham address his sons?  [بَنِيهِ · rhetoric]
- 2:132/q04/p1 A father's special care and his sons' readiness make them fitting recipients; addressing all of them shows the religion's priority. — Jishumī and Ṭabrisī say he also invited everyone; Rāzī says no son was excluded and no other bequest competed with religion. Abū Ḥayyān says the sons already followed Islam, so the counsel seeks their afterlife safety, is unrestricted in time and place, and ends with a strong warning. Bursevī says a sound leader's family helps make the community sound; he and Rāzī note the urgency of a father’s care. [9 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 2:132/q04/p2 He passed on a precious religion to successors so they would not deviate from it. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says people of truth guard hard-won teaching through succession; Biqāʿī notes that descendants transmitted the testament in turn, especially Jacob. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 2:132/q05 How many sons of Abraham are reported, and who were they?  [بَنِيهِ · reports]
turns on: Different transmitted genealogical counts and lists.
- 2:132/q05/p1 Four sons are counted, including Ishmael, Isaac, Midian and Mada'in/Medan. — Muqātil counts four in the opening note; Ibn al-Jawzī names them, and Elmalılı repeats the four-name list as one report. [4 notes: MUQATIL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(مقاتل); BAYDAWI-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 2:132/q05/p2 Eight sons are counted: Ishmael, Isaac, and six by Qantura/Keturah. — Thaʿlabī, Baghawī, Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, Bursevī and Ibn ʿĀshūr name the mothers Hagar, Sarah, and Qantura. Lists of six vary (Midian, Midan, Zamran and other forms); Qurṭubī says Qantura married Abraham after Sarah's death. Ibn ʿĀshūr says only Midian's line receives much Torah detail, including Moses' refuge and Jethro/Reuel; Abū Ḥayyān says only Ishmael and Isaac left enduring lines. [9 notes: THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 2:132/q05/p3 Other counts are fourteen, twelve, or twenty-four sons. — Bayḍāwī lists fourteen beside four and eight; Ālūsī attributes a twelve-son list to al-Itqān, while Elmalılı reports twenty-four. Al-Itqān names the twelve as Ismail, Ishaq, Madyan, Zamran, Sarh, Nafsh, Nafshan, Umaym, Kaysan, Surah, Lutan, and Nafish. [4 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ITQAN]

## 2:132/q06 What background is given about Ishmael and Isaac?  [بَنِيهِ · reports]
turns on: Different age and sacrifice reports in the historical background.
- 2:132/q06/p1 Ishmael was Hagar's son, older than Isaac, and was taken to Mecca as an infant. — Qurṭubī prefers infancy over reported ages two and fourteen; historians cited by Abū Ḥayyān say he was fourteen years older than Isaac and eighty-nine when Abraham died. Qurṭubī gives Ishmael's death at 137 or 130, Isaac's at 180, and says Isaac was buried beside Abraham; Abū Ḥayyān concurs on the latter. Qurṭubī lists Isaac's descendants as Romans, Greeks, Armenians and Israelites. [5 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(المؤرخون); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 2:132/q06/p2 Reports put Ishmael at two or fourteen when brought to Mecca. — Qurṭubī transmits both reported ages and separately favors infancy. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قول)]
- 2:132/q06/p3 The son offered for sacrifice was Ishmael. — Qurṭubī reports this identification while favoring the Isaac identification. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قول)]
- 2:132/q06/p4 The son offered for sacrifice was Isaac. — Qurṭubī calls this the more correct identification and says he will discuss it under al-Ṣāffāt. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قول)+]

- 2:132/q07/p1 Ibrāhāmu is a canonical pronunciation with long ā in the final syllable. — Qirāʾāt-ER says the adaptation affects pronunciation and lineage resonance rather than syntax; Ibn Khālawayh notes Arabic adaptation of foreign names. [2 notes: QIRAAT-ER; IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA]

## 2:132/q08 How does وَيَعْقُوبُ attach to the sentence, and who receives the counsel?  [وَيَعْقُوبُ · grammar]
turns on: The case of Yaʿqūb and whether coordination, ellipsis, or object attachment governs him.
- 2:132/q08/p1 Nominative Jacob is coordinated with Abraham: each charged his own sons. — Al-Ṭabarī rejects making the two patriarchs' charges different; Ibn ʿAbbās and Qatāda report the shared testament. Ṭabrisī and Samin prefer coordination because it needs no ellipsis; Naḥḥās explicitly parses both as nominative subjects. Ibn Kathīr cites the next account at 2:133 for Jacob's own charge. [33 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,قتادة); NAHHAS; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(عمرو بن فائد الأسواري); TABRISI(ابن عباس وقتادة); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; QASHANI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); SAMIN-DURR+; TABRISI+]
- 2:132/q08/p2 Nominative Jacob begins a new clause with an understood 'he charged' or 'he said'; his own sons then hear the address. — Akhfash prefers this analysis because Abraham's earlier clause already implies speech. Jishumī, Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān and Samin report it; Samin notes that on this parse the quoted 'my sons' belongs to Jacob. [8 notes: JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL(عمرو بن فائد الأسواري); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; AKHFASH+; TAB-FULL-; TABRISI-]
- 2:132/q08/p3 Accusative Jacob is coordinated with 'his sons' and was among those Abraham enjoined, as a grandson. — Akhfash offers object coordination; ʿAmr b. Fāʾid and Ismāʿīl b. ʿAbd Allāh are named as readers. Abū Ḥayyān says 'sons' can include a grandson. Qushayrī objects that Jacob was neither a son nor alive to hear it; Ibn Kathīr disputes the chronological objection using 11:71, 29:27 and 21:72. [14 notes: AKHFASH; THALABI(ابن مجاهد); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(عمرو بن فائد الأسواري); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(عمرو بن فائد الأسواري وإسماعيل بن عبد الله المكي); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(إسماعيل بن عبد الله المكي والضرير وعمرو بن فائد الأسواري); SAMIN-DURR(إسماعيل بن عبد الله وعمرو بن فائد); IBNKATHIR-FULL(القشيري,بعض السلف); ALUSI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(القشيري)-]
- 2:132/q08/p4 Jacob is an implicit agent of the enjoining. — Ālūsī records this analysis only to call it remote. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:132/q09 How is the name Yaʿqūb explained?  [وَيَعْقُوبُ · meaning]
turns on: Whether an Arabic birth/descendant etymology can apply to a foreign proper name.
- 2:132/q09/p1 He was named for following his twin Esau out of the womb while holding Esau's heel. — Ibn ʿAbbās is repeatedly cited. Bursevī adds a womb dialogue: Esau demanded to emerge first; Jacob yielded for their mother's safety, and their dispositions later diverged. Qurṭubī and Ālūsī doubt the etymology or report; Samin argues a foreign proper name cannot be derived as though Arabic. [7 notes: THALABI(ابن عباس); JISHUMI(ابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن عباس); TABRISI(ابن عباس); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(قول)-; SAMIN-DURR-]
- 2:132/q09/p2 The name relates to Jacob's many descendants. — Thaʿlabī and Baghawī report this alternative; Samin rejects Arabic derivations of the foreign name. [2 notes: THALABI(ابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR-]
- 2:132/q09/p3 It is an indeclinable foreign proper name, though yaʿqūb also names a partridge in Arabic. — Samin argues that a masculine name derived from the Arabic bird would decline; Qurṭubī says the foreign name only coincides with the bird name. [3 notes: SAMIN-DURR; QURTUBI-FULL(قول); SAMIN-DURR+]

## 2:132/q10 What is reported about Jacob, his sons, and their lives?  [وَيَعْقُوبُ بَنِيهِ · reports]
turns on: The biographical reports and the chronology relevant to Jacob hearing Abraham's counsel.
- 2:132/q10/p1 Jacob instructed twelve sons, the tribal ancestors, and later died in Egypt with burial in Canaan beside Isaac. — Sources list the twelve sons by name. Qurṭubī and Bursevī give Jacob 147 years and Joseph's transfer of his body; Ibn ʿĀshūr names Rebekah as his mother, Israel as his other name and Machpelah as the burial site. Bursevī adds that Esau and Jacob died the same day and were buried together. [12 notes: THALABI; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 2:132/q10/p2 Jacob gathered his sons after seeing Egyptians worship idols, fire, and cattle. — Al-Kalbī reports this as the setting for his question about what they would worship after him; Bursevī repeats the Egyptian idolatry background. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الكلبي); BURSEVI]
- 2:132/q10/p3 Jacob lived in Abraham's lifetime. — Ibn Kathīr cites the promise of Isaac and Jacob at 11:71 and grants of Jacob in 29:27 and 21:72; he also discusses Jerusalem's building and a forty-year interval from the Sacred Mosque, rejecting Ibn Ḥibbān's chronology. Ibn ʿĀshūr agrees. [4 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(القشيري); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 2:132/q10/p4 Jacob was born only after Abraham died. — Qushayrī, as quoted by Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr, uses this against the accusative reading; Ibn Kathīr says this requires sound proof. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(القشيري); IBNKATHIR-FULL(القشيري)-]

## 2:132/q11 How does يَٰبَنِىَّ introduce the testament, and what is its reading?  [يَٰبَنِىَّ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the speech after the testament requires an implied saying verb or is governed by the speech sense of enjoining; a variant supplies an.
- 2:132/q11/p1 An implied verb of saying introduces the quoted address. — Basran grammarians require 'he said' because a full sentence cannot normally be a direct object; Akhfash and Naḥḥās offer this. If Jacob is accusative the implied verb is singular, while nominative allows dual, according to Ālūsī. Farra, Ṭabarī, Thaʿlabī, Jishumī and others compare Qur'anic speech constructions, including 4:11 and 33:29. [18 notes: AKHFASH; FARRA; TAB-FULL(بعض أهل العربية); NAHHAS; THALABI; JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(البصريون); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(البصريون)]
- 2:132/q11/p2 No separate saying verb is needed because enjoining itself is a speech act. — Kufans connect the quotation directly to waṣṣā; al-Ṭabarī treats omitted an as semantic syntax, illustrated by 4:11 and poetry. Al-Farrāʾ rejects the claim that an was intended and elided: such speech clauses may appear with or without it. [11 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL(بعض أهل العربية); THALABI; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(البصريون); ALUSI-FULL(البصريون)]
- 2:132/q11/p3 Ubayy, Ibn Masʿūd and al-Ḍaḥḥāk read 'an yā banī' with an explicit explanatory particle. — Al-Farrāʾ, Thaʿlabī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, Samin and Ālūsī cite the reading. Samin says it supports an explanatory clause rather than a verbal noun; those denying explanatory an call it redundant. Ālūsī notes that it can remove Basrans' need for an implied verb. [9 notes: FARRA; THALABI; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبي وعبد الله والضحاك); SAMIN-DURR(ابن مسعود); ALUSI-FULL(ابن مسعود)]

## 2:132/q12 What is the vocative and possessive form of يَٰبَنِىَّ?  [يَٰبَنِىَّ · grammar]

- 2:132/q14/p1 It starts the quoted speech conveyed by waṣṣā/awṣā, either directly or after an implicit 'he said.' — Zajjāj, Abū Isḥāq and Ibn ʿAṭiyya explain the kasra by reported speech. ## 2:132/q15 What does ٱصْطَفَىٰ mean, and how was the religion chosen?  [ٱصْطَفَىٰ · meaning]
turns on: Whether choice is explained through the word's sense, proofs clarifying the religion, or divine enablement.
- 2:132/q15/p1 It means choosing or selecting the purest religion for them. — Majāz derives it from ṣafwa, purity; Qurṭubī cites a rajaz line for the form, and Samin traces its alif through yāʾ from a root waw. Jishumī explains selection either as purifying and distinguishing it through proofs or simply as choosing Islam. [10 notes: MAJAZ; JISHUMI; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 2:132/q15/p2 God's choosing includes establishing proofs, calling them, and enabling their acceptance. — Rāzī adds clear proofs and protection from other religions; Kashshāf, Nasafī and Ālūsī say God enabled them to follow the finest faith. Biqāʿī calls this mercy that spares a search; Qushayrī and Abū Ḥayyān say the announcement encourages obedience and hope of divine aid. [7 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:132/q16 What does لَكُمُ contribute?  [لَكُمُ · grammar]
- 2:132/q16/p1 It means 'for your sake,' without implying the religion is pure only for these addressees. — Samin glosses lakum as li-ajlikum; Ālūsī says Islam is inherently the purest religion, rather than only pure for them. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:132/q17 Which religion is meant by ٱلدِّينَ?  [ٱلدِّينَ · referent]
turns on: The known historical religion of Islam and an inward account of its divine unity.
- 2:132/q17/p1 It is the known religion of Islam, the upright monotheist way, selected over other religions. — Ṭabarī says the definite article marks the religion familiar from ancestral counsel. Wāḥidī, Jishumī, Ṭabrisī, Qurṭubī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Samin take the article as referential, rather than generic. Wāḥidī calls it ḥanīfiyya; Qushayrī says legal practices may vary while monotheist foundation stays one. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites 3:19 for inherited Islam; Study Quran distinguishes Islam as Muhammad's proper name and a primordial universal orientation, citing 3:19, 3:85, 5:3 and 6:125. [28 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 2:132/q17/p2 In an inward reading, it is the divine religion of unity with God, beyond a separate self. — Qāshānī reads the enjoined word as unity and says God's religion and essence are one. [2 notes: QASHANI]

## 2:132/q18 What does the prohibition فَلَا تَمُوتُنَّ require?  [فَلَا تَمُوتُنَّ · interpretation]
turns on: How a command expressed at the involuntary event of death applies to a person's controllable religious state.
- 2:132/q18/p1 Remain in Islam at every moment, so death finds one Muslim. — Ṭabarī, Zajjāj and many others say death is uncontrollable and its time unknown; the command therefore addresses the lifelong state. Wāḥidī and Jishumī compare 'do not let me see you here.' Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān call it concise admonition that also warns of sudden death. Rāzī says delay may leave one outside Islam at death; Abū Ḥayyān says avoid acts that lead to that end. Kashshāf and Bayḍāwī compare 'die a martyr' and prayer conditioned on humility; the state determines the honor or worth of death. [37 notes: TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(طاوس); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(الزجاج); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; MUQATIL]
- 2:132/q18/p2 The needed state is inward faith alone when bodily deeds are no longer possible at death. — Ālūsī reports this proposal, based on a Prophetic supplication for living Muslims and faithful dead, but finds it unpersuasive. [0 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)-]
- 2:132/q18/p3 An inward reading sees natural death of ignorance replaced by death to self and life through God until bodily death. — Qāshānī explains the injunction as remaining alive through God. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 2:132/q19 What forms and usages of māt are cited?  [تَمُوتُنَّ · meaning]
- 2:132/q19/p1 Arabic uses mawta, mayta and mamāta, with related expressions for lifelessness and death. — Wāḥidī transmits al-Farrāʾ's forms and usages for barren land, mass death, selling livestock as against other goods, and a person with a lifeless heart. [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء)]

## 2:132/q20 How is فَلَا تَمُوتُنَّ formed grammatically?  [فَلَا تَمُوتُنَّ · grammar]
- 2:132/q20/p1 It is a prohibitive jussive with emphatic heavy nūn; a wāw is lost at the meeting of two sukūns. — Naḥḥās and Qurṭubī give the jussive and deletion; Samin derives it from tamūtūnanna, with the indicative nūn dropped and the ḍamma left as a trace. [3 notes: NAHHAS; QURTUBI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]

## 2:132/q21 Why is the death command worded this way?  [فَلَا تَمُوتُنَّ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the wording is a direct prohibition of a state at death or a metaphorical negation of the person.
- 2:132/q21/p1 It makes lifelong constancy urgent by presenting death's uncertain arrival and the disgrace of dying outside Islam. — Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān stress brevity and unknown timing; Kashshāf, Bayḍāwī and Samin say the wording judges death outside faith as devoid of good. Al-Fāḍil al-Yamanī calls it metaphorically negating the person to negate a state, but Ālūsī objects that negating the person would negate all attributes. [9 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]
- 2:132/q21/p2 The wording metaphorically negates the person to negate one particular state. — Al-Fāḍil al-Yamanī proposes this analysis; Ālūsī objects that negating a person negates all attributes, rather than just one state. [0 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الفاضل اليمني)-]

## 2:132/q22 What does مُسْلِمُونَ mean here?  [مُّسْلِمُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether Muslim describes faith and obedience generally, inward entrustment, or especially good expectation of God at death.
- 2:132/q22/p1 Believers who submit obediently to God and remain sincere monotheists. — Muqātil explains 'submit' as sincerity. Thaʿlabī, Jishumī, Baghawī and Qurṭubī transmit variants 'believers,' 'sincere,' or 'entrusting themselves to God'; Biqāʿī says wholly surrendered. Wāḥidī reports Ibn ʿAbbās's uncompromising rejection of associating anything with God, even under torture. [10 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI]
- 2:132/q22/p2 It means entrusting oneself to God's command at all times. — Tustarī says continuous surrender and entrustment distinguish those who follow divine command from those left to reasoning; the wording admits a particular or general sense. [4 notes: TUSTARI; THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 2:132/q22/p3 It includes having a good opinion or hope of one's Lord at death. — Al-Fuḍayl b. ʿIyāḍ gives this gloss. Baghawī cites Jābir's report that the Prophet said three days before death that none should die without good opinion of God; Qurṭubī includes good hope among glosses. [6 notes: THALABI(الفضيل ابن عياض); BAGHAWI-FULL(الفضيل بن عياض,جابر بن عبد الله); QURTUBI-FULL; DURR-FULL(فضيل بن عياض); BURSEVI]

## 2:132/q23 What is the grammatical function of وَأَنتُم مُّسْلِمُونَ?  [وَأَنتُم مُّسْلِمُونَ · grammar]
- 2:132/q23/p1 It is a nominal subject-predicate clause in the circumstantial accusative, within an exception of states. — Naḥḥās, Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Qurṭubī state the circumstantial analysis; Samin calls it a total exception of states governed by the preceding verb. [4 notes: NAHHAS; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]

## 2:132/q24 What does the verse teach about final states and human action?  [* · theology]
- 2:132/q24/p1 Steadfast practice and intention seek a Muslim end; deeds are judged by their endings. — Ibn Kathīr says people generally die and rise in the condition they followed, and God eases good intentions; he reconciles a sound final-destiny hadith by versions saying contrary deeds only appeared so to people and cites 92:5–10. Abū Ḥayyān explicitly cites the judgment of deeds by endings. Tustarī says piety is required according to capacity under 3:102 and is no license to abandon it. [4 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; TUSTARI]
- 2:132/q24/p2 The verse indicates the prophets' faithful descendants and divine selection are fitting to protected prophets. — Jishumī says Abraham accepted truth when clear, resisted habit and desire, and was chosen for obeying well; the verse indicates prophetic protection from sin and that descendants followed Islam. [4 notes: JISHUMI]

## 2:132/q25 What inward or spiritual lessons are drawn from the testament?  [* · inward]
- 2:132/q25/p1 Surrender calls for entrusting oneself to God, dying to self, and inward piety that transforms outward life. — Sulamī links Abraham's charge to his unhesitating submission during his son's sacrifice until ransom. Tustarī reports that reforming the inward reforms the outward; Godfearing people become mercy for wrongdoers, and 49:13 promises honor, nearness, Paradise and success through piety. Qāshānī reads Islam as unity and life through God. [7 notes: SULAMI(قيل); TUSTARI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,سهل); QASHANI]

## 2:132/q26 What counsel for parents and dependents follows from the verse?  [* · law]
turns on: Whether the testament belongs specifically to the deathbed or also to other moments of feared loss.
- 2:132/q26/p1 People approaching death should counsel dependents to keep religion, obedience and piety. — Ṭabrisī states the lesson. Al-Ḥasan says Jacob had no bequest but piety, implying deathbed testament. Ibn ʿĀshūr says counsel is given when loss is feared, especially death or departure. Abū Ḥayyān cites the Prophet's challenge to a father who gifted only one child and wanted equal dutifulness. [5 notes: TABRISI; JISHUMI(الحسن); IBNASHUR-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(رسول الله عن النعمان بن بشير)]
- 2:132/q26/p2 A testament may also be delivered before another feared loss of religious teaching. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the fathers may have spoken at death, as 2:133 suggests, or at another time. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 2:132/q27 What occasion or challenge is reported for the verse?  [* · reports]
- 2:132/q27/p1 It answered Jews who claimed Jacob enjoined Judaism on his sons at death. — Bayḍāwī transmits this report; Biqāʿī links the following verses to refuting Jewish and Christian claims that the patriarchs followed their later religions. [2 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]

## 2:132/q28 How does this verse connect with its surrounding passage?  [* · links]
- 2:132/q28/p1 It continues the account of Abraham's submission and care for descendants, then presents the religion transmitted through Jacob against later scriptural claims. — Jishumī says the sequence moves from concern and prayers for descendants' welfare to religion. Biqāʿī places the testament after Abraham's leadership and before refutation of later Jewish and Christian identifications. Abū Ḥayyān says the prophets' call continues and praises the discourse's coherence, comparing the challenge at 17:88. Elmalılı says it clarifies Israel's brothers and Islam's value; Study Quran traces submission from 2:128 onward. [13 notes: JISHUMI; BIQAI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ELMALILI; STUDYQURAN; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 2:132/q28/p2 The 2:133 account of Jacob's own address supports understanding him as another testator. — Ibn Kathīr explicitly adduces the next verse, and Ibn ʿĀshūr cites it as evidence for a possible deathbed setting. [2 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 2:132/q29 What other reports about Abraham's era accompany the discussion?  [* · reports]
- 2:132/q29/p1 Chronologies and genealogies are reported with differing figures and textual lineages. — Thaʿlabī transmits a prophetic report of eight thousand prophets, half from Israel. Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān date Abraham's death about 2,600 years before Muhammad, while noting a Jewish chronology 400 years shorter. Biqāʿī gives the Torah lineage through Terah and Noah and notes Luke's extra Cainan. [4 notes: THALABI(رسول الله); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(المؤرخون); BIQAI-FULL]
- 2:132/q29/p2 The Torah account traces Abraham's migration, covenants, children and descendants. — Biqāʿī reports Terah leaving Ur for Haran, God's call to Abram at seventy-five, travel to Canaan and Egypt, Lot's separation and rescue, a star-numbered progeny and promised land, the four-hundred-year enslavement prediction, Hagar and Ishmael, Abraham's renaming and covenant at ninety-nine, circumcision, Sarah and Isaac, Ishmael's blessing and twelve princes, and Hagar's later wilderness ordeal. He gives ages eighty-six at Ishmael's birth, thirteen at his circumcision, and day eight for Isaac's. He reconciles a Torah exile account with the sound report of nursing Ishmael at the House by possible miraculous travel. He and earlier scholars read the promises about blessing through Abraham and Ishmael's hand as prophecies of Muhammad. [16 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 2:132/q30 What does the name ٱللَّهَ involve in lexical analysis?  [ٱللَّهَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the divine name is derived and what function al- has.
- 2:132/q30/p1 The article replaces a deleted hamza from ilāh. — Al-Itqān reports Sībawayh and al-Fārisī: the article's fixed form and hamza's cut pronunciation support the analysis. [1 notes: ITQAN(سيبويه)]
- 2:132/q30/p2 The article marks glorifying definiteness added to a form from lāh. — Al-Itqān reports this alternative. [1 notes: ITQAN(آخرون)]
- 2:132/q30/p3 The al- is inseparable from the name and is not definiteness. — Al-Itqān attributes this to a group. [1 notes: ITQAN(قوم)]
- 2:132/q30/p4 The form grows from written hāʾ, possessive lām, and exaltatory al-. — Al-Itqān attributes this derivation to some. [1 notes: ITQAN(بعضهم)]
- 2:132/q30/p5 The name is indivisible and has no prior derivation. — Al-Itqān attributes this to al-Khalīl and others. [1 notes: ITQAN(الخليل)]

## 2:132/q31 How are feminine and diminutive forms illustrated beside the address 'my sons'?  [يَٰبَنِىَّ · grammar]
- 2:132/q31/p1 Sībawayh drops an added feminine tāʾ in diminutives and substitutes original feminine hāʾ; some Arabic usage varies. — He exemplifies bint → bunayya and ukht → ukhayya; other Arabs say hunayha for hunt and hunayya for hun, with hāʾ substituting for yāʾ as in dhih. [2 notes: SIBAWAYH(العرب)]

## 2:132/q32 What do comparisons of smooth and subtle expression illustrate in the accompanying material?  [* · rhetoric]
- 2:132/q32/p1 Smooth wording is clear and easy on the tongue; a subtle comparison asks the reader to infer a deeper relation. — Jurjānī distinguishes ordinary praise of transparent, familiar diction from a refined ring image: Kaʿb described al-Muhallab's sons as day guardians and night cavalry, united like a ring without a discernible end. The image needs discernment beyond immediate hearing. [5 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR(كعب الاشقري)]

## 2:132/q33 What broader remarks on Qur'anic letters accompany the material?  [* · sciences]
- 2:132/q33/p1 Common opening letters can represent the whole and carry articulatory significance. — Al-Burhān says alif, lām, mīm are common and span deep throat, tongue tip and closed lips; the testimony of faith similarly entails other duties. It also contrasts tāʾ's five articulatory qualities with sīn and hāʾ. [4 notes: BURHAN]

## 2:132/q34 How do the accompanying recitation discussions distinguish stopping and pausing?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether a brief sakt requires specific transmission or can clarify an ending more generally.
- 2:132/q34/p1 Verse fragments should not be left unfinished; qaṭʿ ends recitation at verse end, waqf pauses for breath and resumption, and sakt interrupts briefly without breath. — Al-Nashr and al-Itqān transmit Ibn Abī al-Hudhayl's report that predecessors disliked abandoning part of a verse; al-Jazarī and al-Suyūṭī infer the Companions. Waqf may occur at verse ends or inside verses, never inside a word or joined spelling; a basmala accompanies a new sura. Earlier scholars used the terms interchangeably, later ones distinguished them. Hamza, al-Ashnānī, al-Kisāʾī, Ibn Ghalbūn, Makkī, Ibn Shurayḥ, Qutayba, al-Dānī and al-Jaʿbarī give varying descriptions of sakt's shortness. [19 notes: NASHR(ابن أبي الهذيل); ITQAN(ابن أبي الهذيل,ابن شريح,ابن غلبون,الأشناني,الجعبري,الداني,الكسائي,حمزة,قتيبة,مكي)]
- 2:132/q34/p2 Sakt is allowed only where transmitted recitation supports an intended meaning. — Al-Itqān reports Ibn al-Jazarī's preferred restriction. [1 notes: ITQAN(ابن الجزري)+]
- 2:132/q34/p3 Sakt may be made at verse endings while continuing, to clarify meaning. — Al-Itqān reports this alternative and notes that some understand a transmitted hadith accordingly. [1 notes: ITQAN(قيل)]

## 2:132/q35 What other word-form observations occur in the accompanying material?  [* · grammar]
- 2:132/q35/p1 Tanwīn may replace an added verse-final lengthening letter; structural alteration is another kind of word change. — Al-Itqān cites Ibn Hishām as an alternative to Zamakhsharī's analysis of verse-end tanwīn; it also illustrates structural alteration with Ṭūr Sīnīn and an original Sīnā. [2 notes: ITQAN(ابن هشام)]

## 2:132/q36 What levels of Islam and righteousness are distinguished in the accompanying discussion?  [* · theology]
- 2:132/q36/p1 Islam and faith have levels; Abraham's request for righteousness may concern a higher rank. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī says earlier reports describe the first level. He cites 21:72, 2:130, 29:27 and 16:122 for Abraham's request to join predecessors and God's answer in the Hereafter; he suggests the higher company could be Muhammad and his purified family, citing 7:196. [3 notes: TABATABAI]

## 2:132/q37 How is modern kalām and science discussed in material attached to the verse?  [* · theology]
- 2:132/q37/p1 Renewed kalām should preserve ethical roots while updating its methods to address contemporary science and culture. — Y. Khūlī says Islamic identity can resist cultural loss; classical kalām proved creed and routed natural science through ontology and origination proofs, while new kalām starts from accepted creed and aims at historical presence. He favors connection with the old alongside an epistemological break, invoking Bachelard and Ḥasan Ḥanafī, and explains revolution as effective progress. He argues methods endure better than changing scientific claims, so nature belongs in epistemology and philosophy of science rather than ideology; modern science uses creative hypotheses, tests and revisions rather than pure induction. He compares Newtonian determinism with relativity and quantum observation, and cites Heisenberg, Bohr and Margolis on science as a human dialogue/adventure. [26 notes: YKHULI-TAJDID(باشلار,حسن حنفي,مارجوليس,نيلز بور,هيزينبرج); YKHULI-TAJDID+]

## 2:132/q38 How is mīm before bā pronounced in recitation?  [إِبْرَٰهِۦمُ بَنِيهِ · sciences]
- 2:132/q38/p1 Mīm before bā is hidden with nasalization after a vowel but pronounced clearly after sukūn. — Ibn al-Jazarī says earlier scholars wrongly classified the first pronunciation as idghām; it is ikhfāʾ. [1 notes: ITQAN(ابن الجزري)]
