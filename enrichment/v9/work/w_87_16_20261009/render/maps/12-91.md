# 12:91: 21 questions

## 12:91/q01 What does the oath تالله express?  [تَٱللَّهِ · rhetoric]
- 12:91/q01/p1 The brothers swear by God in a form that venerates Him; the oath tāʾ is restricted to Allah’s name. — Al-Samarraʾi counts five Qurʾanic uses, three in Joseph (12:73, 12:85, 12:91) and two in al-Naḥl (16:56, 16:63). He derives tāʾ from wāw by substitution and contrasts wāw oaths by dawn, forenoon, night, and sun. Al-Qushayri says their oath reverses their earlier denial of Joseph’s merit: one who truly witnesses does not deny. [3 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; QUSHAYRI]

## 12:91/q02 How does لقد mark the brothers’ oath?  [لَقَدْ ءَاثَرَكَ · grammar]
- 12:91/q02/p1 The lām in لقد introduces the response to the oath. — Al-Suyuti contrasts this oath-response lām with other lām uses illustrated by 21:57, 48:25, and 2:251. [1 notes: ITQAN]

## 12:91/q03 What does آثرك mean?  [ءَاثَرَكَ · meaning]
- 12:91/q03/p1 It means God chose or preferred Joseph over others, favoring him with gifts and special standing. — Muqatil compares the choosing in 20:72. Al-Nahhas gives the active participle muʾthir and verbal noun īthār. Al-Asmaʿi calls a specially favored person athīr; al-Layth adds special virtue and connection. Jishumi relates īthār to choice and the lasting good trace behind athar, āthār, and maʾthara. Ibn ʿAtiyya and Abu Hayyan extend it to every kind of gift. Al-Samin links it to following a trace of honor, cites a poet’s ātharaka Allāhu bihi īthārāka, and explains the hadith about athara as monopolizing goods; istaʾthara is reserving something for oneself, and God’s istaʾthara bi-fulān figuratively means choosing him. Al-Tabatabaʾi glosses it as choosing or preferring. [14 notes: MUQATIL; IBNABIHATIM(ابن إسحاق); NAHHAS; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس,الأصمعي,الليث); JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL(الأصمعي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الشاعر); TABATABAI]

## 12:91/q04 In what respect did God prefer Joseph over his brothers?  [ءَاثَرَكَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the confession names a particular gift or acknowledges Joseph’s whole range of virtues; al-Alusi favors the preceding piety-and-patience context.
- 12:91/q04/p1 In a wide range of gifts and virtues, including knowledge, character, beauty, judgment, and worldly rule. — The lists vary: al-Tabari names knowledge, forbearance, virtue; the commentators cited by al-Wahidi add intelligence, beauty, kingship; al-Wahidi al-Wajiz adds intellect and excellence; Jishumi lists knowledge, judgment, kingship; al-Tabrisi adds patience and reason; Ibn al-Jawzi’s fourth report includes knowledge, intelligence, beauty, and other virtues; al-Razi adds reason and kingship; al-Qurtubi lists knowledge, forbearance, judgment, intellect, kingship; al-Nasafi includes piety and patience; al-Biqaʿi includes rule and piety; Bursevi adds perfection, status, wealth; al-Ghunyān as cited by al-Alusi includes beneficence, patience under harm, and power. These are variations of a broad account. [12 notes: TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(صاحب الغنيان)]
- 12:91/q04/p2 In Joseph’s forbearance, gentleness, and forgiveness toward them. — Qatada says they encountered a forbearing man after Joseph identified himself; al-Dahhak’s and Abu Sulayman’s reports name patience or forbearance and forgiveness. The al-Durr report says Joseph did not expose or rebuke them. Al-Alusi also reports this account from Sulayman al-Dimashqi. [7 notes: TAB-FULL(قتادة); IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو سليمان الدمشقي,أبو صالح عن ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو سليمان الدمشقي); DURR-FULL(قتادة); ALUSI-FULL(سليمان الدمشقي)]
- 12:91/q04/p3 In his piety, patience, and good conduct. — Al-Zamakhshari, reported by Abu Hayyan, connects this to the preceding verse. Al-Alusi prefers the piety-and-patience account among the reports he surveys. Al-Qushayri says Joseph chose hardship for God over bodily pleasure, after which God favored him. [3 notes: QUSHAYRI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL+]
- 12:91/q04/p4 In kingship. — Al-Dahhak transmits this from Ibn ʿAbbas. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الضحاك عن ابن عباس)]
- 12:91/q04/p5 In kingship, patience, and knowledge. — Abu Hayyan transmits this combined list from Ibn ʿAbbas; al-Alusi also mentions it among other reports. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 12:91/q04/p6 In his good appearance and complete conduct. — Al-Baydawi gives this focused explanation. [1 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL]

## 12:91/q05 How does God’s preference relate to the brothers’ resentment of their father’s preference?  [ءَاثَرَكَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا · links]
- 12:91/q05/p1 Their resentment began with their father’s preference, but God’s preference exposed their error and affirmed Joseph’s merit. — Islahi makes the brothers’ present admission answer their earlier resentment. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 12:91/q06 What does Joseph’s superiority imply about his brothers’ prophetic status?  [ءَاثَرَكَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا · theology]
- 12:91/q06/p1 Some infer that the brothers were not prophets. — Al-Razi reports the argument that prophethood exceeds worldly office, so if the brothers shared prophethood their confession of Joseph’s superiority would not fit. Jishumi also reports a view that the brothers were adults but not prophets. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; JISHUMI]

## 12:91/q07 What does خاطئين mean here?  [لَخَٰطِـِٔينَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether khaṭiʾ marks intentional culpability in contrast to akhṭaʾ, or can serve as an equivalent word for error, as cited poetry suggests.
- 12:91/q07/p1 It means culpable, deliberate sinners or wrongdoers. — Al-Thaʿlabi cites Umayya’s line for sin; Ibn ʿAbbas says they intentionally missed truth, and every sinner leaves its path for doubt and disobedience, as al-Qurtubi also reports. Al-Mawardi says the sinful sense implies culpability. Jishumi explains khaṭīʾa as moving something from its proper course, distinguishing intentional khaṭiʾa from akhṭaʾa; al-Baghawi, Ibn ʿAtiyya, al-Razi, Abu Hayyan, al-Alusi, and Ibn al-Anbari likewise contrast deliberate sin with inadvertent error or a jurist’s mistaken judgment. Ibn ʿAtiyya cites Umayya’s poetic example. Al-Wahidi refers to 2:81 and 2:286 for the lexical distinction. Al-Nasafi says their conduct lacked piety and patience; al-Biqaʿi calls them intrinsically wrongdoers. [23 notes: THALABI(أمية بن الأكسر,ابن عباس); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس وغيره); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن الأنباري,ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 12:91/q07/p2 It can mean mistaken or in error, with khaṭiʾ and akhṭaʾ or khāṭiʾ and mukhṭiʾ treated as equivalent. — Abu ʿUbayda says the forms are equivalent and quotes Imruʾ al-Qays’s khaṭiʾna and Umayya’s khaṭiʾa and ḥāba as evidence. Al-Tabari also cites Umayya but distinguishes the verb forms. Al-Mawardi allows mistaken as an alternative. Ibn al-Anbari and al-Zajjaj are reported by al-Wahidi; al-Zajjaj chooses this sense on the poetic evidence. Al-Tabatabaʾi says both personal forms denote one who errs. Al-Nahhas simply glosses it as committing error. [8 notes: MAJAZ(أمية بن الأسكر,امرؤ القيس); TAB-FULL; NAHHAS; MAWARDI-FULL; TABATABAI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن الأنباري والزجاج)+]

## 12:91/q08 What wrongdoing do the brothers confess?  [وَإِن كُنَّا لَخَٰطِـِٔينَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether their apology concerns the original acts against Joseph or the concealment that continued once they were adults.
- 12:91/q08/p1 Their mistreatment of Joseph, including separating him from his family, throwing him into the well, and selling him. — Al-Tabari names separation from father and brother and further mistreatment; al-Razi reports the well, sale, and separation as the majority account. Ibn Ishaq and others identify their conduct toward Joseph generally. Muqatil calls it wrongdoing, and several sources join it to their admission of divine preference. Al-Razi rejects confining the confession to later concealment: Jacob would hardly have sent unaccompanied prepubescent boys. [15 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(ابن إسحاق); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 12:91/q08/p2 Their later concealment of Joseph’s survival from Jacob. — Abu ʿAli says they began acting in childhood but continued concealing the affair in adulthood. Al-Jubbaʾi says they did not apologize for prepuberty acts; they failed to correct the wolf story after learning Joseph lived. Al-Mawardi likewise locates adult culpability in continued concealment. [4 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI(أبو علي); RAZI-FULL(أبو علي الجبائي); RAZI-FULL-]
- 12:91/q08/p3 Wrongdoing against one another. — Jishumi reports this as one of the glosses beside sinners in general. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 12:91/q09 How could the brothers be culpable or repent if their first acts occurred in childhood?  [وَإِن كُنَّا لَخَٰطِـِٔينَ · interpretation]
turns on: Their age at the first wrongdoing and whether later concealment or renewed repentance supplies the ground for their confession.
- 12:91/q09/p1 They became culpable by continuing to conceal the matter into adulthood. — Al-Mawardi and Abu ʿAli say the acts began when they were young, but concealment continued after maturity. Jishumi prefers this account. Al-Jubbaʾi specifies failure to tell Jacob that the wolf had not eaten Joseph. [4 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI(أبو علي); RAZI-FULL(أبو علي الجبائي); JISHUMI+; RAZI-FULL-]
- 12:91/q09/p2 They had to seek forgiveness after reaching puberty. — Jishumi reports this explanation without naming its advocate. [1 notes: JISHUMI]
- 12:91/q09/p3 Repentance for childhood acts was commendable even if not obligatory. — Jishumi says Joseph prayed for such repentance. Al-Razi says an apology can be fitting even when not required and people may renew repentance after forgiveness. [2 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL]
- 12:91/q09/p4 They were already adults when they acted, though not prophets. — Jishumi reports this further view. Al-Razi argues Jacob would hardly send unaccompanied boys before puberty, rejecting al-Jubbaʾi’s childhood premise. [2 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL]

## 12:91/q10 What does the brothers’ confession accomplish?  [قَالُوا۟ وَإِن كُنَّا لَخَٰطِـِٔينَ · interpretation]
turns on: The sources emphasize different effects of the same admission: an appeal to Joseph and a reversal of the brothers’ earlier judgment.
- 12:91/q10/p1 It apologizes, admits guilt, and indirectly asks Joseph for pardon or kindness. — Al-Suddi places the apology after Joseph identifies himself in 12:90. Ibn ʿAtiyya calls it a plea for reconciliation; al-Qurtubi finds an implicit request for pardon. Abu Hayyan says the confession seeks kindness. Al-Alusi adds an implicit signal of repentance, and al-Tabrisi says the admission shows remorse rather than persistence. Kuranyolu says Joseph forgave them. [7 notes: TAB-FULL(السدي); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 12:91/q10/p2 It reverses their earlier denial of Joseph’s worth and openly recognizes God’s preference. — Al-Qushayri says true witnessing prevents denial. Islahi relates this reversal to their resentment of Jacob’s preference. [2 notes: QUSHAYRI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 12:91/q11 What does إن convey in their confession?  [وَإِن كُنَّا · grammar]
turns on: Al-Farraʾ allows either a concessive or a restrictive sense.
- 12:91/q11/p1 It can mean ‘although we were sinners.’ — Al-Farraʾ supplies this as one of two senses reported by Ibn al-Jawzi. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD]
- 12:91/q11/p2 It can mean ‘we were only sinners.’ — Al-Farraʾ supplies this second sense in Ibn al-Jawzi’s report. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD]

## 12:91/q12 How is وإن كنا لخاطئين constructed and emphasized?  [وَإِن كُنَّا لَخَٰطِـِٔينَ · grammar]
- 12:91/q12/p1 The conjunction gives their state; إن is lightened, خاطئين is kāna’s accusative predicate, and the lām reinforces the assertion. — Jishumi identifies the accusative predicate. Abu ʿUbayda says the added fatḥa-lām intensifies and confirms. Al-Suyuti describes the non-governing lām of initiation shifting from sentence-initial position after inna to avoid two adjacent emphatic markers, and says it can make an imperfect present in sense. Al-Alusi calls the wāw circumstantial, supplies an implicit pronoun of shaʾn for lightened inna, and identifies the shifted emphatic lām. Al-Biqaʿi says the shortened in heightens the urgency of their apology. [5 notes: MAJAZ; JISHUMI; BIQAI-FULL; ITQAN; ALUSI-FULL]

## 12:91/q13 Why use خاطئين instead of مخطئين?  [لَخَٰطِـِٔينَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Ibn al-Anbari gives a semantic explanation and a sound-pattern explanation for the form.
- 12:91/q13/p1 It expresses culpable sin rather than merely missing the right course. — Ibn al-Anbari contrasts the two forms; several lexicographers and commentators likewise distinguish deliberate khaṭiʾ from inadvertent akhṭaʾ. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن الأنباري)]
- 12:91/q13/p2 It may have been chosen to match the verse endings. — Ibn al-Anbari offers this as another explanation. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن الأنباري)]

## 12:91/q14 How is the hamza in آثرك pronounced?  [ءَاثَرَكَ · readings]
- 12:91/q14/p1 It is lightened rather than fully realized. — Al-Qurtubi states this while explaining the verb and Joseph’s gifts. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]

## 12:91/q15 How may خاطئين be pronounced?  [لَخَٰطِـِٔينَ · readings]
- 12:91/q15/p1 A dialectal or Qurʾanic variant simplifies and drops its hamza without changing the meaning. — Qiraat-ER reports the pronunciation variant. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER]

## 12:91/q16 Why choose آثرك الله rather than فضلّك?  [ءَاثَرَكَ ٱللَّهُ · rhetoric]
- 12:91/q16/p1 It is lighter diction suited to eloquent word choice. — Al-Barizi, reported by al-Suyuti, pairs ātharaka Allāh over faḍḍalaka with āmana over ṣaddaqa, atā over aʿṭā, and other examples. His wider account says eloquence selects each fitting word from the full range of meanings and expressions, which are present in God’s knowledge, making the Qurʾan the finest speech. [2 notes: ITQAN(البارزي)]

## 12:91/q17 What happens when the brothers recognize Joseph?  [قَالُوا۟ · reports]
turns on: The reports supply different details of the recognition scene.
- 12:91/q17/p1 They apologize after he identifies himself and recognize his forbearance. — Al-Suddi connects the apology to Joseph’s identification in 12:90. Qatada says they then encountered a forbearing man; Ibn Abi Hatim reports that they saw God had favored him. Kuranyolu connects Joseph’s self-identification and recall of what they did to him and Benjamin with the promise of 12:15 that he would tell them when they failed to recognize him. [4 notes: TAB-FULL(السدي,قتادة); IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 12:91/q17/p2 They approach the throne to fall at his feet, but he descends and embraces them. — Bursevi reports this sequence after their recognition. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 12:91/q18 How does Joseph respond to his brothers’ wrongdoing?  [* · interpretation]
- 12:91/q18/p1 He forgives them without exposing or rebuking them; ‘no reproach’ is praised as beautiful forgiveness. — Qatada says he did not expose or rebuke them. Kuranyolu says he tells them he forgives them. The Study Quran reports that reproach may also refer to physical punishment. Al-Razi says people may renew repentance after forgiveness. [4 notes: DURR-FULL(قتادة); RAZI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(Kl)]

## 12:91/q19 How does their wording to Joseph differ from their request to Jacob?  [وَإِن كُنَّا لَخَٰطِـِٔينَ · links]
- 12:91/q19/p1 To Joseph they imply guilt without naming the offense or explicitly requesting forgiveness; to Jacob they request forgiveness and confess sins with emphatic إنّا. — Al-Samarraʾi compares the light in here with their address to Jacob in 12:98. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 12:91/q20 What does the brothers’ outcome teach about God’s direction of human affairs?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes draw different lessons from the same reversal: irresistible divine gift, patient trust, hidden providence, and the consequences of wrongdoing.
- 12:91/q20/p1 Envy cannot prevent a blessing God decrees. — Kuranyolu cites the Prophet’s prayer that none can withhold what God grants or grant what He withholds. It also cites Ziya Pasha’s couplet in which divine power makes tyrants finally admit God’s preference for another. [3 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Resûlullah,Ziyâ Paşa)]
- 12:91/q20/p2 The story teaches patience, measured action, and trust as difficult events unfold gradually under divine wisdom. — Al-Biqaʿi links this to 12:110 and says ordered causes test both obedience and disobedience. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 12:91/q20/p3 God directs human affairs beyond people’s grasp; what they hate may benefit them and what they love may harm them. — Asad contrasts the Qurʾanic story with Genesis’ romantic account and cites 2:216. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES]
- 12:91/q20/p4 Worldly outcomes reveal consequences of wrongdoing: Joseph receives kingship while his brothers stand before him as humbled petitioners. — Al-Nasafi contrasts Joseph’s kingship with their abasement. [1 notes: NASAFI-FULL]

## 12:91/q21 How was Joseph’s forgiveness later echoed?  [* · links]
- 12:91/q21/p1 At the conquest of Mecca, Muhammad cited Joseph’s forgiveness and freed Quraysh after they acknowledged his noble standing. — The Study Quran reports the Prophet’s action and calls Joseph’s no-reproach response a beautiful form of forgiveness. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN(Kl,Prophet Muhammad)]
