# 79:38: 21 questions

## 79:38/q01 What does āthara mean here?  [وَءَاثَرَ · meaning]
- 79:38/q01/p1 To prefer or choose one thing over another, here worldly life over the Hereafter. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says īthār applies when the conditions of both choices cannot be combined. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites Joseph 12:91, al-Aʿlā 87:16, and Ṭā Hā 20:72 for preference, and al-Ḥashr 59:9 for generously preferring others to oneself; she adds that choosing may imply judging a thing best and most lasting. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI]

## 79:38/q02 What does the root of āthara evoke here?  [وَءَاثَرَ · rhetoric]
- 79:38/q02/p1 It evokes a remaining trace or legacy, suggesting what a person chooses to retain or leave behind. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says athar is a lasting sign, an effect is a durable trace, and āthār are remains of earlier people. She distinguishes īthār, choosing what seems best and lasting, from athara, keeping chosen things for oneself. [3 notes: BINTSHATI]

## 79:38/q03 How are the two terms of the preference expressed?  [وَءَاثَرَ · grammar]
- 79:38/q03/p1 The preferred thing is the direct object; its counterpart takes ʿalā and is omitted here because the Hereafter is understood. — Ibn ʿĀshūr cites Joseph 12:91 for the construction. The familiar contrast between this life and the Hereafter supplies the omitted term; conversely, al-Ḥashr 59:9 omits the preferred item when the counterpart is what matters, meaning that the poor are preferred over oneself. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 79:38/q04 What does worldly life denote here?  [ٱلْحَيَوٰةَ ٱلدُّنْيَا · referent]
- 79:38/q04/p1 The particular benefits and sensory pleasures of earthly life. — Ibn ʿĀshūr understands an implied 'bliss of worldly life': its pleasures are particular to this life and are not shared with the Hereafter. Qāshānī contrasts sensory pleasure with real life. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; QASHANI]

## 79:38/q05 What does preferring worldly life involve?  [وَءَاثَرَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةَ ٱلدُّنْيَا · interpretation]
- 79:38/q05/p1 Choosing present worldly pleasures and desires over the Hereafter, religion, and preparation for lasting life. — Tustarī and Sahl, as reported by Sulamī, stress pursuit of desires and passions; Nasafī likewise says following desire. Ibn al-Jawzī states the contrast with the afterlife. Bayḍāwī and Biqāʿī add immersion and failure to worship or discipline the soul; Biqāʿī compares fixation on the visible present to animal behavior. Ālūsī contrasts fleeting pleasures with everlasting life and names faith and obedience as neglected preparation. Ibn Kathīr adds preference over religion. Qāshānī calls it choosing sensory pleasure over real life. Ibn ʿĀshūr says it entails rejection of the Hereafter's bliss; Ṭabāṭabāʾī links it to desire and rejection of the afterlife. Kuran Yolu describes living for the moment without looking beyond the transient world. Rāzī diagnoses corrupt action, also reported by Study Quran. Jishumī specifies work for this life while heedless of the Hereafter. [17 notes: TUSTARI; SULAMI(سهل); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; QASHANI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(al-Rāzī)]

## 79:38/q06 Why do people prefer worldly life?  [وَءَاثَرَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةَ ٱلدُّنْيَا · interpretation]
turns on: The notes identify desire, denial of the afterlife, fixation on what is immediate, and protection of authority as motives; the last concerns leaders specifically.
- 79:38/q06/p1 They seek to satisfy the lower self and its desires. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says divine instruction alone identifies the relevant interests and includes shirk, rejection of messengers, aggression, arrogance, and related vices. Ṭabāṭabāʾī connects worldly preference to following the self's desires. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 79:38/q06/p2 They deny the afterlife and turn away from it. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the preference follows denial of the afterlife. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says transgression entails its rejection; Abū ʿUthmān, as reported by Sulamī, defines the adjacent transgression as turning from the afterlife toward this life. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABATABAI; SULAMI(أبو عثمان)]
- 79:38/q06/p3 They attend to immediate, visible life and overlook the unseen future. — Biqāʿī likens this to an animal limited to present things. Kuran Yolu says such people usually live for the moment and do not look beyond the transient world. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 79:38/q06/p4 Leaders resent following the Messenger and losing their authority despite recognizing his truth. — Ibn ʿĀshūr gives this motive specifically for leaders. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 79:38/q07 How does transgression alongside preference characterize a person?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes characterize the paired fault through disbelief, orientation away from the afterlife, loss of human balance, or the combination of intellectual and practical corruption.
- 79:38/q07/p1 It is disbelief that crosses human limits and violates God's rights. — Sahl, reported by Sulamī, defines transgression as denying God's rights and rejecting His blessings. Ibn ʿAṭiyya describes exceeding human limits through disbelief. Wahidī and Baghawī identify the transgressor who prefers the world as a disbeliever. Ibn Kathīr says the rebel defies God. [5 notes: SULAMI(سهل); WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 79:38/q07/p2 It is turning away from the afterlife toward this life. — Abū ʿUthmān, reported by Sulamī, defines transgression this way; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says it entails denial of the afterlife, worldly preference, and pursuit of desire. [2 notes: SULAMI(أبو عثمان); TABATABAI]
- 79:38/q07/p3 It is an impulsive or animal-like departure from proper human balance. — Qāshānī says transgressors move toward animal or predatory ranks. Kuran Yolu contrasts ṭughyān with ḥilm in al-Ṭūr 52:33 and associates it with impulsive, unthinking, stubborn unbelief among pre-Islamic people. [2 notes: QASHANI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 79:38/q07/p4 It joins corrupt understanding to corrupt action at their greatest degree. — Rāzī says worldly preference corrupts the practical faculty and, paired with transgression, reaches maximal corruption; Study Quran reports his account as intellectual and practical corruption. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(al-Rāzī)]

## 79:38/q08 How do transgression and worldly preference relate to the contrasting pair?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: The notes give causal order and reciprocal contrast as two features of the arrangement.
- 79:38/q08/p1 Transgression comes first because it is a major cause of worldly preference. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the order follows natural causal order. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 79:38/q08/p2 Each side of the contrast implies the opposite of the other side's second trait. — Biqāʿī says transgression implies the opposite of restraint, and restraint implies rejection of worldly preference. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says fear restrains worldly attachment and forbids following desire, the opposite of the transgressor's preference. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 79:38/q09 Who is characterized by this preference in the passage?  [* · referent]
turns on: Ṭabāṭabāʾī limits the classification to exemplary classes and reserves other cases for God's judgment.
- 79:38/q09/p1 The transgressing unbelievers, contrasted with faithful and pious people. — Wahidī calls the person who both transgresses and prefers this life a disbeliever; Baghawī does likewise. Rāzī's maximally corrupt person is an unbeliever. Ṭabāṭabāʾī identifies the two exemplary classes as unbelieving wrongdoers and the faithful, pious. [4 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL; RAZI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 79:38/q09/p2 The two exemplary classes leave the weak and people of mixed deeds unassigned. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī leaves their fate to God, who may forgive or accept intercession. [1 notes: TABATABAI]

## 79:38/q10 What fate follows this preference with transgression?  [* · interpretation]
- 79:38/q10/p1 Hell becomes the person's home; maximal corruption brings everlasting punishment. — Baghawī calls Hell his home, and Qāshānī says it becomes the transgressor's home. Ibn Kathīr adds food of zaqqūm and drink of boiling water. Rāzī specifies eternal punishment for the unbeliever in whom transgression and practical corruption reach their maximum; Study Quran reports the same diagnosis. [5 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL; QASHANI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; RAZI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(al-Rāzī)]

## 79:38/q11 What moral lesson is drawn from preferring worldly life?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes draw censure of worldly preference, a diagnosis of sin, and an inward standard of preferring God.
- 79:38/q11/p1 Preferring the world while heedless of the Hereafter is blameworthy; choosing the Hereafter is praiseworthy. — Jishumī states both sides and adds that work for this life under heedlessness is blameworthy. Study Quran cites 3:185 and 57:20 for its delusive enjoyment and 87:17, 6:32, 9:38, and 14:3 for the superior, more enduring Hereafter and censure of worldly preference. [3 notes: JISHUMI; STUDYQURAN]
- 79:38/q11/p2 Love of the world is the root of sins and manifests corruption in action. — Rāzī cites a Prophetic saying that worldly love is the root of every sin; Study Quran cites a hadith saying it is the source of all error and reports Rāzī's practical-corruption reading. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL(النبي); STUDYQURAN(al-Rāzī)]
- 79:38/q11/p3 One should prefer God over self, spirit, this world, and even the afterlife. — Tustarī says everyone else is ignorant. [1 notes: TUSTARI]

## 79:38/q12 What signs show detachment from worldly life?  [* · inward]
turns on: These are three signs in Tustarī's account, each with a distinct focus.
- 79:38/q12/p1 Calamities, even losing one's child or oneself, become light. — Tustarī gives this as a sign. Muslim b. Yasār says grief for his dead son distracted him from grief over him and asks God to assign the reward of the loss to his son. [2 notes: TUSTARI(مسلم بن يسار)]
- 79:38/q12/p2 Worldly pleasures, including one's own life, carry little weight. — Tustarī gives this as a second sign. [1 notes: TUSTARI]
- 79:38/q12/p3 Nothing is nearer to the person than God. — Tustarī gives this as a third sign. ʿĀmir b. ʿAbd al-Qays says he saw God nearer than himself to anything he looked at. [2 notes: TUSTARI(عامر بن عبد القيس)]

## 79:38/q13 About whom were the verses said to be revealed?  [* · reports]
- 79:38/q13/p1 Al-Naḍr and his father al-Ḥārith. — Wahidī reports this from Muqātil and al-Kalbī. [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل، الكلبي)]

## 79:38/q14 How does this verse fit the passage and sūra?  [* · links]
- 79:38/q14/p1 It identifies the wretched class after the Resurrection is announced, contrasting its fate with that of the blessed. — Rāzī says the general Resurrection account gives way to a division of morally responsible people, beginning with the wretched. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says Hell's people come first because the address targets polytheists; transgression and worldly preference define them, fear and restraint the other class. Kuran Yolu says the brief verses summarize attitudes leading to immense loss or salvation. Biqāʿī says the sūra establishes resurrection, gathering, and final fate after the preceding sūras' exposition, with oaths marking a decisive climax. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; BIQAI-FULL]

## 79:38/q15 What image conveys investigation in the associated figurative note?  [* · rhetoric]
- 79:38/q15/p1 A figure follows a scent with its muzzle, as if using a rock-breaking axe, to investigate. — Ibn Qutayba gives the sniffing and axe image. [1 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL]

## 79:38/q16 How can silent things be said to speak?  [* · rhetoric]
- 79:38/q16/p1 Their visible signs convey information or admonition metaphorically without vocal speech. — Ibn Qutayba distinguishes metaphorical statements about a leaning wall or animal from actual speaking, which requires utterance. An anonymous poem makes graves and fading tongues admonish through signs; al-Kumayt's earth reports a man's canals, trees, and traces; ʿAwf b. al-Kharaʿ's ruined house indicates its history like a whisper. Ibn Qutayba says the sages' silent speaker is workmanship pointing to a maker and director, and says such metaphorical actions do not take an emphatic verbal noun such as a wall intensely wanting to fall or a tree forcefully speaking. [6 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL(الشاعر,الكميت,عوف بن الخرع)]

## 79:38/q17 How do meaning and form contribute to speech's merit?  [* · rhetoric]
- 79:38/q17/p1 Some speech has intrinsic worth across forms, while other speech depends on crafted form. — Jurjānī compares intrinsic worth to gold and says form-dependent speech loses value when its form is stripped away. He frames his inquiry as tracing how meanings converge and diverge and how they relate to reason and borrowed status; he begins with simile, representation, and metaphor because most or all beauties of speech branch from them. [3 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR]

## 79:38/q18 Why examine grammar when reading the Qurʾān?  [* · grammar]
- 79:38/q18/p1 Serious grammatical inquiry unlocks meanings and purposes and explains regular linguistic conventions. — Jurjānī says parsing supplies a standard for soundness and merit; neglect loses an available gain. He sets aside contrived drills on rare word weights and hypothetical names. His examples of proper inquiry are the changes in weak wāw, yāʾ, and alif; dual and sound-plural inflection and the accusative matching the genitive; the nūn substituting for vowel and tanwīn or vowel alone; and reasoned secondary causes of diptote declension, in which two causes or a repeated cause make a noun secondary in two respects, like a verb relative to a noun. [7 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL]

## 79:38/q19 How can an explicit noun be used in place of a pronoun?  [* · rhetoric]
- 79:38/q19/p1 A context calling for a pronoun may instead use an explicit noun. — Burhān refers to an earlier treatment under emphasis. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 79:38/q20 What does a pronoun after a construct normally refer to?  [* · grammar]
- 79:38/q20/p1 It refers to the head noun, the topic, rather than the genitive complement. — Burhān qualifies the general rule that a pronoun refers to the nearest preceding noun. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 79:38/q21 How are Qurʾānic verse endings related to sajʿ and meaning?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether sajʿ applies to the Qurʾān, whether verse endings differ from it, and whether sound may control wording or meaning.
- 79:38/q21/p1 The Qurʾān has no sajʿ. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports that some Islamic theological schools denied sajʿ categorically. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الفرق الإسلامية)]
- 79:38/q21/p2 Sajʿ occurs in the Qurʾān and is part of its miraculous expression. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports that other theological schools affirmed this; most rhetoricians find no real difference between sajʿ and verse endings and explain the terminological distinction as reluctance to use a word associated with soothsayers. [2 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(أكثر البلاغيين,الفرق الإسلامية)]
- 79:38/q21/p3 Qurʾānic sajʿ is sought for its own sake and can alter order or word choice for matching endings. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ attributes this view to al-Farrāʾ. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الفراء); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن سنان الخفاجي وابن الأثير)-]
- 79:38/q21/p4 The meanings of Qurʾānic verse endings are not subordinate to their wording. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports this denial from Ibn Sinān al-Khafājī and Ibn al-Athīr. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن سنان الخفاجي وابن الأثير)]
- 79:38/q21/p5 Qurʾānic verse endings are distinct from sajʿ. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ attributes the distinction to al-Bāqillānī and al-Rummānī; most rhetoricians regard it as a terminological reluctance tied to soothsayers. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(القاضي الباقلاني وعلي بن عيسى الرماني); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(أكثر البلاغيين)-]
