# 53:5: 17 questions

## 53:5/q01 Whom or what does the object pronoun in 'taught him' denote, and what other object is understood?  [عَلَّمَهُۥ · grammar]
turns on: The antecedent of the hāʾ and which of the verb's two objects is left implicit; Ibn Atiyya, Razi, and Samin favor Muhammad, while Razi reports a majority for revelation.
- 53:5/q01/p1 It denotes Muhammad; the Qur'an or revelation is the unexpressed other object. — Zajjaj says Gabriel taught the Prophet. Ibn Atiyya says this is likelier than a Qur'an antecedent; Razi points to the earlier 'companion' and describes Gabriel's intellectual and practical powers. Abu Hayyan and Samin call revelation the omitted second object, as do Alusi and the alternative reported by Ibn Ashur; Ibn Ashur says this rebuts the human-teacher claim of 16:103. Elmalili says either object analysis yields the same sense. Bursevi specifies that Gabriel brought, recited, and explained scripture, or conveyed inspiration to Muhammad's heart (26:193-194). [11 notes: ZAJJAJ; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; BURSEVI; IBNATIYYA-FULL+; RAZI-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 53:5/q01/p2 It denotes the Qur'an or revelation; Muhammad is the unexpressed recipient. — Razi attributes this to most commentators and allows scripture or inspiration (26:193). Ibn Atiyya and Samin report it as possible; Abu Hayyan says the Messenger is omitted as first object. Ibn Ashur links the antecedent to huwa in 53:4 and identifies the omitted recipient with 'your companion' in 53:2. Elmalili says the alternative construction has the same sense. Tabatabai allows the Qur'an as revelation or revelation generally; Alusi reports Abu Hayyan's alternative. [8 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL(أكثر المفسرين); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 53:5/q02 How does the verb 'taught' work here?  [عَلَّمَهُۥ · grammar]
turns on: These are complementary accounts of the verb's syntax and force, not rival object readings.
- 53:5/q02/p1 It takes two objects, one of which is omitted. — Ibn Ashur derives doubled ʿallama from single-object ʿalima and therefore assigns it two objects. Abu Hayyan, Samin, Alusi, Elmalili, Ibn Ashur, and Tabatabai identify the possible omitted recipient or revelation according to the pronoun's antecedent; Tabatabai says the second object is omitted in each construction. [9 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 53:5/q02/p2 The doubled form conveys sustained, repeated teaching. — Samarrai contrasts ʿallamahu with aʿlamahu. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 53:5/q03 Who is 'the one mighty in powers' who taught?  [شَدِيدُ ٱلْقُوَىٰ · referent]
turns on: Whether the surrounding angelic descriptions and vision language point to Gabriel, or God's own teaching in 55:1-2 is the immediate sense; Qashani gives a spiritual designation.
- 53:5/q03/p1 Gabriel, God's angel and messenger of revelation. — Muqatil calls him powerful in every respect; Farra connects dhu mirra in the next verse. Mawardi asserts consensus, Qurtubi a majority, and Ibn Ashur unanimity among exegetes; Ibn Abbas, Qatada, and al-Rabi are repeatedly cited. Ibn Atiyya says the following descriptions fit Gabriel and argues from 53:13's 'another descent' and the absence of a report of seeing God before the Night Journey, while allowing heart-sight of God; Abu Hayyan calls the God reading far-fetched. Samin finds Gabriel most apparent; Ibn Kathir compares 81:19-21, Tabatabai 81:20. Baydawi and Elmalili call Gabriel the intermediary of miracles; Aisha's report names him. Kuranyolu favors him from the flow and description; Samarrai says the wording identifies Gabriel as the revealer beyond ordinary ilham. Biqai links his extraordinary capacity to the unlettered Prophet's instruction. Farra, Ibn Qutayba, Zajjaj, and others identify him directly. [36 notes: MUQATIL; FARRA; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(الربيع); NAHHAS(قتادة); MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس والمفسرون); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(قتادة والربيع وابن عباس); TABRISI(ابن عباس والربيع وقتادة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL(الجمهور); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس، قتادة، الربيع); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس وقتادة والربيع); ELMALILI(Beyzâvî,Hazret-i Âişe); IBNASHUR-FULL(المفسرون); TABATABAI(المفسرون); ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; STUDYQURAN(most commentators); IBNATIYYA-FULL+; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR+; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 53:5/q03/p2 God is the mighty teacher. — Al-Hasan is cited by Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, and Abu Hayyan; Elmalili cites a report from Ibn Abbas; Tabatabai and Study Quran report the view. Samin adduces 55:1-2 as its support. Abu Hayyan finds it far-fetched, and Ibn Atiyya argues the continuation and 53:13 favor Gabriel, while allowing a vision of God by the heart. [7 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن); QURTUBI-FULL(الحسن); SAMIN-DURR; ELMALILI(İbn Abbas); TABATABAI(قيل); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(other commentators); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحسن)-; IBNATIYYA-FULL-; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR-]
- 53:5/q03/p3 The Spirit of Holiness, a spirit powerful over the levels beneath it, taught him. — Qashani describes its power to subdue and affect lower levels; he does not name it Gabriel in this note. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 53:5/q04 What does 'mighty' say about the teacher's power?  [شَدِيدُ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the phrase emphasizes intrinsic faculties or the means at the teacher's disposal; the notes offer no explicit argument against either gloss.
- 53:5/q04/p1 His capacities are exceptionally strong, including the ability to execute divine commands and preserve revelation. — Muqatil says powerful in every respect; Tabrisī says Gabriel is powerful in himself and his created form. Razi describes strong intellectual and practical faculties and specifically perception, memory, and integrity, comparing the strong and trustworthy messenger of 81:20-21. Nishaburi gives the intellectual and practical reading. Ibn Ashur includes great intellectual and physical works and bringing revelation to messengers. Biqai says he can carry out all God's commands. Samarrai joins strength with knowledge, judgment, and precision for ascent, protection of the Prophet, and preservation of revelation, citing the need for sultan in 55:33. [8 notes: MUQATIL; TABRISI(ابن عباس والربيع وقتادة); RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI(Nîsâbûrî); IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 53:5/q04/p2 He is strong in means or resources. — Elmalili attributes this gloss to Tabari. [1 notes: ELMALILI(Taberî)]

## 53:5/q05 How is Gabriel's strength illustrated in transmitted accounts?  [شَدِيدُ ٱلْقُوَىٰ · reports]
- 53:5/q05/p1 Accounts point to his overwhelming acts and extraordinarily swift movement. — Al-Kalbi in Tha'labi and Qurtubi says Gabriel lifted Lot's towns from black water on his wing and overturned them; Qurtubi says he carried them to heaven. Baydawi, Nasafi, Bursevi, and Alusi repeat the Lot account with variants of subterranean or dark water and raising to heaven. Al-Kalbi says a wing stroke threw Iblis to a distant Indian mountain; Qurtubi places the encounter during Iblis's speech to Jesus, while Bursevi says the blast sent him from the Holy Land to India. Al-Kalbi also cites Gabriel's cry killing Thamud; Baydawi, Nasafi, Bursevi, and Alusi repeat it, with Baydawi saying they lay collapsed by morning. Al-Kalbi describes rapid descents to prophets and ascents to heaven; Qurtubi says faster than a glance, and Alusi says faster than a blink, exceeding sunlight's speed. [13 notes: THALABI(الكلبي); QURTUBI-FULL(الكلبي); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 53:5/q06 What does 'al-quwā' mean?  [ٱلْقُوَىٰ · meaning]
turns on: The notes distinguish inherent strength or ability from means or resources, without presenting a direct dispute.
- 53:5/q06/p1 Strength, power, or capacity. — Majaz treats it as a collective for strength; Jishumi and Tabrisī gloss quwwa as ability rooted in strength. Ibn Qutayba traces its image to rope strands, one quwwa being a strand, as reported also by Ibn Jawzi. Bagḥawi and Ibn Atiyya call it plural of quwwa; Tabrisī and Bursevi compare mirra's image of a tightly twisted cord, with Bursevi citing Raghib and dictionary senses of physical strength, sound judgment, firmness, and strong rope. Razi and Nishaburi apply it to intellectual and practical powers. [11 notes: MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(ابن عباس والربيع وقتادة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة); RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI(الراغب); ELMALILI(Nîsâbûrî)]
- 53:5/q06/p2 Means or resources. — Elmalili cites Tabari's gloss of 'strong in powers' as strong in means or resources. [1 notes: ELMALILI(Taberî)]

## 53:5/q07 What is the form of 'al-quwā'?  [ٱلْقُوَىٰ · grammar]
turns on: Whether its form is explained as a plural of quwwa or as a collective expression.
- 53:5/q07/p1 It is the plural of quwwa. — Akhfash compares dialectal plural pairs ḥabwa/ḥuban and rashwa/rashan; Ibn Qutayba and Ibn Jawzi use a rope strand as the singular image. Bagḥawi, Ibn Atiyya, and Tabrisī state the plural analysis. [6 notes: AKHFASH; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(ابن عباس والربيع وقتادة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة)]
- 53:5/q07/p2 It functions as a collective word for strength. — Majaz calls al-quwā a collective term. [1 notes: MAJAZ]

## 53:5/q08 What variant reading of 'al-quwā' is reported?  [ٱلْقُوَىٰ · readings]
- 53:5/q08/p1 The qāf is read with kasra. — Nahhas attributes the reading to al-Farra, who grounds it in similarity between the patterns fiʿla and fuʿla. [1 notes: NAHHAS(الفراء)]

## 53:5/q09 How is the phrase 'shadīd al-quwā' constructed grammatically?  [شَدِيدُ ٱلْقُوَىٰ · grammar]
- 53:5/q09/p1 Shadīd is an intensive adjective annexed to its agent in a non-genuine construct; an implied 'angel' may be supplied. — Nasafi calls the genitive a construction of an intensive adjective with its agent, not true possession. Samin calls it non-genuine annexation. Bursevi compares ḥasan al-wajh and supplies 'an angel mighty in strength, Gabriel.' Ibn Ashur supplies 'angel' from the following angelic traits. [4 notes: NASAFI-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 53:5/q10 Why describe the teacher as mighty in powers?  [شَدِيدُ ٱلْقُوَىٰ · rhetoric]
turns on: The notes give complementary rhetorical purposes: student praise, rebuttal and assurance, and consolation.
- 53:5/q10/p1 Praise of the teacher highlights the distinction and reliable instruction of Muhammad, his student. — Razi says praising Gabriel also praises Muhammad rather than merely naming the teacher; Nishaburi as cited by Elmalili and some exegetes as cited by Kuranyolu make the same point. Biqai says the extraordinary teacher makes the unlettered Prophet's instruction remarkable. Islahi portrays an affectionate teacher approaching and bending over his pupil so the Prophet fully grasped the teaching rather than hearing vague distant words. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI(Nîsâbûrî); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 53:5/q10/p2 It rebuts the claim that a weak human taught Muhammad, and establishes the revelation's dependable source. — Razi cites the opponents' claim that Muhammad heard ancient tales on a Syria trip, and compares Gabriel's perception, memory, and integrity with 81:20-21. Nishaburi contrasts humans' weakness and limited knowledge, citing 16:103, 4:28, and 17:85. Kuranyolu cites the unbelievers' human-teacher claim of 16:103. Samarrai says the wording identifies Gabriel rather than ordinary ilham such as that to Moses' mother, the bee, or humans and jinn. Islahi says 53:1-18 rejects soothsaying and fortune-telling by tracing the Qur'an to God's trusted angel. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL; ELMALILI(Nîsâbûrî); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 53:5/q10/p3 The description consoles Muhammad by showing that Gabriel can bear divine communication. — Razi adds that God may teach first through an intermediary and then directly, citing 'He taught you what you did not know' in 4:113. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 53:5/q11 How does Gabriel's teaching relate to God's teaching?  [عَلَّمَهُۥ شَدِيدُ ٱلْقُوَىٰ · interpretation]
- 53:5/q11/p1 God is the ultimate teacher; Gabriel conveys the revelation by God's permission. — Elmalili cites 55:1-2 for God's teaching, 2:97, 16:102, 26:193, and 42:51 for Gabriel as messenger to Muhammad's heart. Kuranyolu cites the Merciful teaching the Qur'an (55:1-2) and communication through a messenger (42:51, 2:97, 16:102, 26:193). Samarrai says God remains the ultimate teacher while Gabriel identifies the specific revealer. Razi allows a sequence of instruction through an intermediary and then directly (4:113). [5 notes: ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; RAZI-FULL]

## 53:5/q12 How does Gabriel convey scripture or inspiration to the Prophet?  [عَلَّمَهُۥ · interpretation]
turns on: The notes describe outward delivery and inward reception of revelation without presenting them as mutually exclusive.
- 53:5/q12/p1 For scripture he brings, recites, and explains it; for inspiration he conveys it to the Prophet's heart. — Bursevi distinguishes the two senses of waḥy and cites 26:193-194. Razi's reported revelation-object reading likewise allows scripture or inspiration (26:193). Elmalili says Gabriel conveyed by God's permission to Muhammad's heart, citing 2:97, 16:102, 26:193, and 42:51. [3 notes: BURSEVI; RAZI-FULL(أكثر المفسرين); ELMALILI]
- 53:5/q12/p2 Heart revelation is protected from error when the heart is aware of God's regard. — Bursevi reports the Sufis' account of God's regard for the heart and a saying that a believer sees by God's light, guarding against error and forgetfulness; his citation of Rumi distinguishes the Qur'an from astrology, geomancy, and dreams as divine revelation. [3 notes: BURSEVI(صوفيان)]

## 53:5/q13 What place does this clause have in the surrounding passage?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes address different points of the wider passage: revelation's origin and the limits of sensory or pagan conceptions.
- 53:5/q13/p1 It begins an explanation of how revelation reached Muhammad and counters claims that he invented or imagined it. — Ibn Ashur calls it a new explanatory passage. Study Quran says the opening answers the accusation of invention in 52:33 and notes the opening's disputed meaning and rhetorical power. Islahi says verses 1-18 establish the recited Qur'an as revelation through a trusted angel, reject soothsaying and fortune-telling, affirm the verses as true and inevitable, and deny that Muhammad's revelation experiences and Gabriel are illusions. Rumi as cited by Bursevi distinguishes divine revelation from astrology, geomancy, and dreams. [6 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BURSEVI]
- 53:5/q13/p2 Its vivid mysterious scene directs faith toward what the senses cannot reach and warns against overstraining reason. — Kuranyolu also says the passage condemns pagan efforts to make divinity concrete through material or spiritual intermediaries and to grant godhood to beings besides God. [2 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 53:5/q14 Whom did Muhammad see in the opening vision?  [* · referent]
turns on: Whether the descriptions and 53:13's 'another descent' identify Gabriel, or the passage describes a vision of God at the Night Journey; the mode of seeing God also varies.
- 53:5/q14/p1 He saw Gabriel in his true form, unlike Gabriel's usual human appearance. — Ibn Atiyya favors Gabriel throughout these descriptions: 53:13 calls another descent, implying an earlier one, and no report places a sight of God before the Night Journey. Study Quran attributes the two-Gabriel-visions reading to many commentators. Ibn Kathir transmits reports of Gabriel's vast winged form and of Gabriel's created form rising dark from the east. [4 notes: STUDYQURAN(many commentators); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس,عبد الله); IBNATIYYA-FULL+]
- 53:5/q14/p2 He saw God during the Night Journey and Ascension, by the heart or by eye and heart. — Study Quran reports the competing interpretation; Ibn Atiyya allows sight of God by the heart while arguing that no report puts a sight of God before the Night Journey. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(many others); IBNATIYYA-FULL-]

## 53:5/q15 What distinct reports describe a sight of Gabriel near this passage?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports describe different vision scenes and details; the Anas account also has explicit transmission and wording objections.
- 53:5/q15/p1 Anas reports that Gabriel struck the Prophet's shoulders and they rose from a tree to the horizons before a heavenly light, pearl-and-ruby veil, and revelation appeared. — Ibn Kathir transmits Anas's account; Ibn Kathir calls it extraordinary, with objectionable wording, and suggests it may have been a dream. Al-Bazzar says al-Harith ibn Ubayd alone carried it, which frames the later reliability objections. [2 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو بكر البزار,أنس بن مالك); IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو حاتم الرازي,أحمد بن حنبل,ابن حبان,يحيى بن معين)-]
- 53:5/q15/p2 Abdullah reports seeing Gabriel with six hundred horizon-filling wings shedding pearls, rubies, and other marvels. — Ibn Kathir says Ahmad alone transmits this version. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(عبد الله)]
- 53:5/q15/p3 Ibn Abbas reports that a dark mass rose from the east when the Prophet asked to see Gabriel in his created form; he fainted and Gabriel revived him. — Ibn Kathir says Ahmad alone transmits this version; Gabriel wiped the Prophet's mouth after reviving him. [2 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(أحمد بن حنبل,ابن عباس)]

## 53:5/q16 How is the Anas vision report assessed?  [* · reports]
- 53:5/q16/p1 Al-Harith ibn Ubayd was the sole transmitter, a known Basran, but the report has serious reliability objections. — Al-Bazzar identifies al-Harith as sole transmitter. Yahya ibn Ma'in calls him unreliable; Ahmad calls his hadith troubled; Abu Hatim says his material may be written but is not proof; Ibn Hibban says his many errors preclude relying on him alone. Ibn Kathir calls this one of his unusual reports, objects to wording and extraordinary details, and suggests a dream. [6 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو بكر البزار,أبو حاتم الرازي,أحمد بن حنبل,ابن حبان,يحيى بن معين)]

## 53:5/q17 How are the endings of the surrounding verses read?  [* · readings]
turns on: Which readers or transmissions use plain fatḥa, intermediate pronunciation, or imāla at particular endings.
- 53:5/q17/p1 Ibn Kathir, Asim, and Ibn Amir use fatḥa at all verse endings. — Ibn Atiyya transmits these assignments. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن كثير وعاصم وابن عامر ونافع وأبو عمرو وحمزة والكسائي)]
- 53:5/q17/p2 Asim through Abu Bakr uses imāla on raʾā. — Ibn Atiyya transmits this exception. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن كثير وعاصم وابن عامر ونافع وأبو عمرو وحمزة والكسائي)]
- 53:5/q17/p3 Nafi and Abu Amr use an intermediate form. — Ibn Atiyya transmits this assignment. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن كثير وعاصم وابن عامر ونافع وأبو عمرو وحمزة والكسائي)]
- 53:5/q17/p4 Hamza and al-Kisai use imāla throughout. — Ibn Atiyya transmits this assignment. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن كثير وعاصم وابن عامر ونافع وأبو عمرو وحمزة والكسائي)]
- 53:5/q17/p5 Abu Amr through Ubayd uses imāla for al-aʿlā and tadallā. — Ibn Atiyya transmits this exception. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن كثير وعاصم وابن عامر ونافع وأبو عمرو وحمزة والكسائي)]
