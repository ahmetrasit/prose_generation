## THE CURRENT MAP
12:18/q01 Why use 'came' here? [وَجَآءُو · rhetoric]
  12:18/q01/p1 It describes bringing a concrete item. — reasons: Suyuti contrasts jāʾa for concrete things with atā for meanings and times, citing God's command.
12:18/q02 What did the brothers do to Joseph's shirt? [عَلَىٰ قَمِيصِهِۦ · reports] (turns on: Reports differ in details of its preparation and presentation.)
  12:18/q02/p1 They stripped it, stained it with slaughtered animal blood, and brought it intact as evidence. — reasons: Muqatil says they stripped it after casting him in the well; Study Quran says before. Ibn Kathir says the blood reinforced their plot. Genesis is cited for the special robe.
  12:18/q02/p2 The bloodstained shirt was fabricated evidence. — reasons: Tabatabai notes scant blood and the intact shirt gave it little force.
12:18/q03 Whose blood was put on the shirt? [بِدَمٍۢ كَذِبٍۢ · reports] (turns on: The reports name different animals.)
  12:18/q03/p1 A lamb, sheep, or kid goat. — reasons: Mujahid, Ibn Abbas, Suddi and many later sources report slaughtered young sheep or goat.
  12:18/q03/p2 A deer or gazelle. — reasons: Attributed especially to Qatada.
  12:18/q03/p3 A young camel calf. — reasons: An alternative in Wahidi.
12:18/q04 How did the shirt expose the brothers' story? [عَلَىٰ قَمِيصِهِۦ · reports] (turns on: Versions add different clues and episodes.)
  12:18/q04/p1 A wolf would have torn the garment or left marks. — reasons: Jacob found the shirt intact and ironically called the wolf gentle, merciful, or patient. Samarrai notes that 'ate' avoids the implication of torn clothing in 'preyed on'.
  12:18/q04/p2 When robbers were blamed, Jacob asked why they left the useful shirt. — reasons: Their incompatible accounts heightened suspicion, according to Wahidi, Tabarsi, Razi, Qurtubi and Alusi.
  12:18/q04/p3 The brothers presented a bloodied wolf, which spoke and denied eating Joseph. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says its mouth was smeared; Baghawi says it came to visit kin. Qurtubi's unsourced story includes Judah averting an attack on Jacob and the wolf seeking a brother.
12:18/q05 What three signs are attached to Joseph's shirt? [عَلَىٰ قَمِيصِهِۦ · links] (turns on: Whether these signs involve one garment or several.)
  12:18/q05/p1 False blood, a tear from behind proving innocence, and restoration of Jacob's sight. — reasons: Sha'bi, Shafi'i and many sources connect 12:18 with 12:25 and 12:93/96. Samarrai frames the shirts as marking grief's beginning and end and three stages of Joseph's story.
  12:18/q05/p2 The three signs concern different shirts. — reasons: Qurtubi rejects Mawardi's formulation and notes a variant identification of the torn garment with the herald's.
12:18/q06 How is 'on his shirt' grammatically attached? [عَلَىٰ قَمِيصِهِۦ · grammar] (turns on: Dispute concerns adverbial sense and advance of a circumstance before its genitive.)
  12:18/q06/p1 It is a circumstance of the blood placed before its noun. — reasons: Abu al-Baqa restores the order 'blood on his shirt'; Ibn Ashur agrees. Al-Samin cites grammarians and poetry allowing the advance; Abu Hayyan finds its sense suitable.
  12:18/q06/p2 It means 'above the shirt' as an adverb. — reasons: Zamakhshari rules out a fronted circumstance from a genitive. Abu Hayyan and al-Samin object that arriving people cannot be above a shirt. Alusi prefers blood placed above it.
  12:18/q06/p3 It attaches to 'they came'. — reasons: Al-Hawfi offers this; al-Samin and Abu Hayyan object that arrival cannot literally be on a shirt.
  12:18/q06/p4 They arrived possessing the shirt, the blood being circumstantial. — reasons: Alusi reports this minor alternative.
12:18/q07 What does 'false blood' mean? [بِدَمٍۢ كَذِبٍۢ · meaning] (turns on: The mechanisms are attributed lie, emphatic source, passive sense or ellipsis.)
  12:18/q07/p1 It was actual blood falsely claimed to be Joseph's. — reasons: The lie was the brothers', not the blood's. Akhfash and Ibn Qutayba compare inanimate attributions and 2:16; Nahhas compares 'ask the village' (12:82). Burhan notes literal lying is speech.
  12:18/q07/p2 The verbal noun 'lie' intensively describes the blood as falsehood itself. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya prefers this, rejecting Tabari's poetic evidence; Kashshaf calls it hyperbole; Tabatabai treats the blood as actively lying. Wahidi cites 2:16, 67:30, 68:6.
  12:18/q07/p3 'Lie' is a verbal noun with passive sense: falsified blood. — reasons: Farra cites ma'qul, majlud and poetry; Abu Tharwan uses makdhuba. Tabari's Kufan analysis agrees. Tabrisi says Sibawayh rejects the analysis and supplies ellipsis.
  12:18/q07/p4 An omitted noun such as 'possessing a lie' explains the attribution. — reasons: The Basran grammarian and Nahhas compare other figurative attribution of a property to a noun.
12:18/q08 How is the word for 'false' read? [بِدَمٍۢ كَذِبٍۢ · readings] (turns on: Variants change consonant or case.)
  12:18/q08/p1 The common reading has dotted dhāl, kadhib. — reasons: Jishumi explicitly contrasts this with Aisha's dāl reading.
  12:18/q08/p2 An undotted dāl gives kadb/kadib. — reasons: Attributed to Aisha, Hasan, Ibn Abbas and Abu al-Aliya; glosses vary among fresh, murky, dry and discolored. Ibn Jinni derives a patterned mark from white nail spots. Ibn Atiyya calls it weak.
  12:18/q08/p3 The accusative kadhaban is read. — reasons: Farra allows adverbial 'came falsely'; others parse a circumstance of brothers or blood, or a causal object. Burhan compares 100:1; Alusi notes an indefinite circumstantial head is disfavored.
12:18/q09 What does 'rather' reject? [قَالَ بَلْ · interpretation]
  12:18/q09/p1 Jacob denies the wolf account and charges them with another deed. — reasons: Al-Samin supplies 'the wolf did not eat him'; Wahidi, Razi, Ibn Ashur and Tabatabai explain the replacement accusation.
12:18/q10 What does sawwalat mean? [سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنفُسُكُمْ · meaning] (turns on: Sources offer adornment, command, ease and desire etymologies.)
  12:18/q10/p1 Their souls made their deed or account attractive and acceptable. — reasons: The predominant explanation; Study Quran says they persuaded themselves it was not grave. Islahi includes satanic embellishment. Biqai surveys related desires and deception.
  12:18/q10/p2 Their souls commanded or prompted them. — reasons: Ibn Abbas is transmitted for this gloss.
  12:18/q10/p3 They made the offense easy for one another. — reasons: Zamakhshari derives from laxity; Abu Muslim and Jubba'i say mutual easing led to killing. Alusi calls that derivation less likely.
  12:18/q10/p4 Taswil forms a desired wish and makes the wished-for thing beautiful. — reasons: Azhari links it to sawl, a wish or demand, and softened hamza. Raghib describes the desired object painted as attractive.
12:18/q11 What matter did their souls devise? [أَمْرًۭا · referent] (turns on: Its indefinite form leaves the deed unspecified.)
  12:18/q11/p1 An ugly act against Joseph, not the wolf attack. — reasons: Tabari says an act concerning Joseph; Ibn Ashur allows killing, sale, or banishment and says indefiniteness sounds ominous. Tabatabai says Jacob accuses without naming it.
  12:18/q11/p2 Another story or plan to cover what happened. — reasons: Thalabi calls it a plan; Baghawi and Ibn al-Jawzi stress the embellished account.
12:18/q12 How did Jacob recognize the lie? [قَالَ بَلْ · interpretation] (turns on: Sources offer inference from signs, revelation, promise or intuition.)
  12:18/q12/p1 He inferred it from intact shirt, envy, or incompatible claims. — reasons: Jacob knew the brothers' envy; a wolf should leave marks and robbers would take a shirt. Qushayri says his heart sensed the truth.
  12:18/q12/p2 God disclosed it by revelation or an earlier promise that Joseph would survive. — reasons: Mawardi and Tabarsi list revelation; Razi cites God's choice of Joseph in 12:6; Tabatabai's report invokes the truthful dream.
  12:18/q12/p3 A sound intuition perceived it. — reasons: Mawardi and Tabarsi allow intuition; Qushayri generalizes insight of truthful hearts.
12:18/q13 How is 'beautiful patience' parsed and read? [فَصَبْرٌۭ جَمِيلٌۭ · grammar] (turns on: Nominal ellipsis and accusative command or verbal noun are disputed.)
  12:18/q13/p1 Nominative: 'my affair or settled practice is beautiful patience'. — reasons: Sibawayh compares raised Arabic answers; Khalil and Zajjaj prefer this. Poetry shows the nominative; a topic may be omitted as in 'Who are you? Zayd'.
  12:18/q13/p2 Nominative: 'my patience is beautiful' or 'it is beautiful patience'. — reasons: Qutrub supplies 'my patience'; Farra supplies 'it'; Wahidi lists 'what I believe' and similar understood words.
  12:18/q13/p3 Nominative subject: 'beautiful patience is best'. — reasons: A suppressed predicate is supplied. Islahi prefers this analysis. Ibn Ashur's cited poem illustrates patience as more beautiful than no recourse.
  12:18/q13/p4 Nominative phrasing exhorts lasting patience. — reasons: Ibn Ashur prefers exhortation and says nominative suggests firmness and duration, citing 11:69 and 2:45. Samarrai contrasts the accusative as temporary. Baghawi also reports Jacob choosing this patience.
  12:18/q13/p5 Accusative fa-sabran jamilan is an implied self-command. — reasons: Attributed to Ubayy, Ibn Masud, Ashhab, Uqayli, Isa ibn Umar, Kisai and codices of Anas and Abu Salih. Sibawayh's restriction of accusative expressions to requests supports self-address; poetry parallels it. Tabarsi explains a cited lament poem by its horses mourning the dead, reins drooping in grief.
  12:18/q13/p6 Accusative is an implied verbal noun: 'I will endure beautifully'. — reasons: Nahhas and Thalabi offer this. Sibawayh, Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and al-Samin say a declarative reading is weak unless construed as self-address.
  12:18/q13/p7 A heard form addresses someone named Jamil. — reasons: Abu al-Hasan al-Athram reports ṣabran jamīl as a vocative.
