 inward meaning is given to the fire and Moses' hopes? [* · inward] (turns on: Different inward readings identify the fire with the Holy Spirit, divine manifestation, or spiritual awakening.)
  20:10/q25/p1 The fire is the Holy Spirit illuminating souls, and the family are Moses' faculties. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī says the faculties remain still as sensory activity quiets; the firebrand is a luminous benefit to them, while finding a guide is gaining knowledge and gnosis leading to truth.
  20:10/q25/p2 God appeared in the desired form of fire to draw Moses toward Him. — reasons: Bursevī transmits some Sufis' explanation that another form might have made Moses turn away; his poem says Moses did not yet recognize the divine manifestation in light and speech.
  20:10/q25/p3 The fire symbolizes rising spiritual certainty. — reasons: The Study Quran adds the mountain of God, Horeb, from Exodus; Asad treats the lost road as a possible symbol of spiritual need and the fire as a burning-bush allegory.
20:10/q26 How did Moses recognize the divine call at the fire? [* · theology] (turns on: Inference from the character of the speech versus direct divinely given recognition.)
  20:10/q26/p1 He inferred it from the speech's ordered structure and composition. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī says this recognition came when he tried to take a flame.
  20:10/q26/p2 God made His speech known through special inspiration without inference. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī reports this alternative.
20:10/q27 Why did Moses remove his sandals, according to related reports? [* · reports] (turns on: The reports appeal to the sandals' material, the valley's blessing, or symbolic humility.)
  20:10/q27/p1 They were made from donkey hide. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī transmits Ibn Masʿūd's report from the Prophet specifying a dead donkey and a variant specifying untanned hide; al-Ḥasan contrasts this with Muhammad's prayer in sandals.
  20:10/q27/p2 His bare feet could touch and receive the blessed ground. — reasons: ʿIkrima and Mujāhid call it twice sanctified; another explanation singles out his heels.
  20:10/q27/p3 Barefootedness expresses humility or detachment from family. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī reports these explanations, adding that shoes in dreams signify a wife.
  20:10/q27/p4 Removing sandals was associated with the sacred valley. — reasons: Abū al-Aḥwaṣ reports Ibn Masʿūd joking to Abū Mūsā, who had removed sandals before prayer, 'Are you in the sacred valley?'
20:10/q28 How are other nearby words and the preceding question explained? [* · meaning] (turns on: These are separate contextual observations included in the collected notes.)
  20:10/q28/p1 The preceding hal keeps its ordinary confirmatory interrogative sense. — reasons: Al-Samīn also reports proposed readings as qad or as negation.
  20:10/q28/p2 Akhfaytu can mean concealed or revealed, while khafaytu means revealed only. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī records the distinction and also glosses radā and taraddā as destruction.
  20:10/q28/p3 Saʿy means swift movement or action, and radā and ardā concern perishing and destroying. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān cites Durayd ibn al-Ṣimma's poetry for ardā.
  20:10/q28/p4 The king's dream in Joseph was true inspiration, not the confused dreams claimed by his court. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites 12:43–44 in her wider discussion of distinctive Qurʾānic diction.
20:10/q29 What view of divine istiwāʾ does the source favor in the preceding context? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether istiwāʾ is explained as power or as a distinct act or attribute.)
  20:10/q29/p1 It means divine power. — reasons: Al-Jishumī prefers Abū ʿAlī's explanation and calls the Kullābī account of a separate act or attribute named istiwāʾ unintelligible.
  20:10/q29/p2 It is an act or attribute called istiwāʾ. — reasons: Al-Jishumī reports the Kullābī account solely to dispute its intelligibility.
