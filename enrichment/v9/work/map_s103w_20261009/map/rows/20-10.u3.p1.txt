has seen a fire? [إِنِّىٓ ءَانَسْتُ نَارًۭا · rhetoric]
  20:10/q10/p1 He reports certain perception to reassure his family while leaving the hoped-for benefits uncertain. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī contrasts certain sight with merely hoped-for fire and guidance; al-Ālūsī says inna confirms the sight even though the family expressed no doubt. Ṭabāṭabāʾ takes the singular emphatic report to imply the others did not perceive the fire. Ibn ʿĀshūr says inna marks the importance of this welcome news to the family in the darkness.
20:10/q11 How is the first-person yāʾ of 'innī' read? [إِنِّىٓ ءَانَسْتُ · readings] (turns on: Whether the possessive yāʾ is opened in connection or kept quiescent.)
  20:10/q11/p1 It is read open, innīya. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī names the two Medinese, Ibn Kathīr and Abū ʿAmr among five yāʾs in this passage; Qirāʾāt-Er says the change affects prosody without changing meaning. Ibn Mujāhid reports that Nāfiʿ opens the listed first-person yāʾs except in walī fīhā and akhī ushdud; Abū ʿAmr excepts walī fīhā and ḥashartanī aʿmā; Ibn Kathīr excepts walī fīhā, yassir lī amrī, lidhikrī in, ʿalā ʿaynī idh, and biraʾsī innī. Each opens the remaining listed yāʾs.
  20:10/q11/p2 It is read quiescent, innī. — reasons: Qirāʾāt-Er presents the quiescent form as the contrasting reading. Ibn Mujāhid reports that Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī, Ibn ʿĀmir, and ʿĀṣim through Abū Bakr keep all the listed first-person yāʾs quiescent.
20:10/q12 Why does Moses say 'perhaps'? [لَّعَلِّىٓ · rhetoric]
  20:10/q12/p1 A firebrand or guide was hoped for, not guaranteed. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says Moses would not promise what he could not guarantee, contrasting a claim that Abraham lied for a good purpose. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī and al-Ālūsī distinguish the uncertain outcome from the certain sight.
  20:10/q12/p2 The hope concerns finding a guide; Moses was certain he could take a flame. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says taking a flame is certain, while meeting and accompanying someone who knows the route remains possible.
20:10/q13 How is the yāʾ of 'laʿallī' read? [لَّعَلِّىٓ ءَاتِيكُم · readings] (turns on: Opening or quiescence of the first-person yāʾ.)
  20:10/q13/p1 It is read with fatḥ, laʿalliya. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī names Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr, Abū ʿAmr, Ibn ʿĀmir and Abū Jaʿfar across six occurrences; Qirāʾāt-Er says meaning is unchanged. Ibn Mujāhid says Nāfiʿ, Abū ʿAmr, and Ibn Kathīr open the listed first-person yāʾs apart from their individually named exceptions; laʿallī is not among those exceptions.
  20:10/q13/p2 It is read quiescent, laʿallī. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī names the Kufans and Yaʿqūb; Qirāʾāt-Er contrasts it with open yāʾ. Ibn Mujāhid reports that Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī, Ibn ʿĀmir, and ʿĀṣim through Abū Bakr keep all listed first-person yāʾs quiescent.
20:10/q14 What is the 'qabas' Moses hopes to bring? [بِقَبَسٍ · meaning] (turns on: Whether qabas is specifically a flaming brand, as al-Ālūsī insists, or may also be called an ember.)
  20:10/q14/p1 A flame or burning brand taken from a larger fire, carried on a stick or wick for warmth. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ, al-Ṭabarī and al-Zajjāj specify the tip of a stick or reed, and al-Zajjāj allows a wick; al-Samīn calls it a passive-sense verbal noun, a glowing end taken up. Al-Ālūsī equates it with the jadwah of 28:29 and distinguishes it from an ember. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ contrasts its guiding warmth with hostile meteors and notes iqtabasa at 57:13; a report from al-Bāqir says the family needed warmth. Ibn ʿĀshūr describes a flame taken from a burning source, such as a firebrand, and infers darkness and no means of kindling their own fire.
  20:10/q14/p2 An ember or piece of fire to carry back. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Nasafī allow an ember, and Ibn Kathīr cites 28:29 for a glowing brand and warmth; Iṣlāḥī likewise speaks of an ember against cold. Ibn ʿĀshūr explicitly includes an ember or coal among what can be taken as a qabas.
20:10/q15 How do the related verbs for taking or giving fire and knowledge differ? [بِقَبَسٍ · meaning] (turns on: Whether usage assigns separate domains to the simple and prefixed forms or allows both in both domains.)
  20:10/q15/p1 The simple form concerns fire; the prefixed form concerns seeking or imparting knowledge. — reasons: Abū Zayd distinguishes bringing fire from seeking it; al-Mubarrad and al-Samīn distinguish giving fire from giving knowledge. These reports differ in their precise formulation.
  20:10/q15/p2 Both forms can be used for fire or knowledge. — reasons: Al-Kisāʾī allows both; al-Mubarrad says their underlying sense is one because both fire and knowledge illuminate.
20:10/q16 How is 'from it' attached grammatically? [مِّنْهَا بِقَبَسٍ · grammar] (turns on: Direct verbal attachment or an omitted circumstance describing the brand.)
  20:10/q16/p1 It attaches directly to the verb 'bring'. — reasons: Al-Samīn and Abū al-Baqāʾ, as cited by al-Ālūsī, permit this attachment.
  20:10/q16/p2 It is an omitted circumstantial qualification of the firebrand. — reasons: Al-Samīn and Abū al-Baqāʾ supply the omitted circumstance.
20:10/q17 Do the two hopes joined by 'or' exclude one another? [أَوْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the alternatives are merely listed as possibilities or explicitly compatible.)
  20:10/q17/p1 They are alternative hoped-for outcomes but need not exclude obtaining both. — reasons: Bursevī explicitly allows both; al-Ālūsī says aw prevents exclusion and presents two anticipated possibilities.
  20:10/q17/p2 They express two possible outcomes, neither yet certain. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī treats aw in the hope clause as the expression of two anticipated possibilities.
  20:10/q17/p3 The alternatives are a certain flame and the possible added benefit of a guide. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr calls aw a choice and distinguishes the certainty of taking a flame from the possibility of finding and accompanying someone who knows the route.
20:10/q18 What does 'upon the fire' mean? [عَلَى ٱلنَّارِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether ʿalā retains spatial elevation, stands for another local preposition, or describes an implicit guide.)
  20:10/q18/p1 On or above a nearby elevated place by the fire. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says people around a fire stand on overlooking ground; Abū Ḥayyān keeps the ordinary elevation sense, and al-Bayḍāwī and al-Nasafī allow elevated ground. Al-Ālūsī says elevation becomes an idiom for a nearby place, citing Sībawayh's account of bi as contact with a nearby place. Ibn ʿĀshūr interprets ʿalā as figurative spatial nearness to someone tending the fire for light or warmth, and cites a verse by al-Aʿshā for this use; al-Aʿshā's usage is also separately reported.
  20:10/q18/p2 It means 'at,' 'with,' or 'by' the fire. — reasons: Ibn al-Anbārī offers these prepositional senses; al-Ālūsī says substitution is unnecessary.
  20:10/q18/p3 It is a circumstance of an implied guide located near the fire. — reasons: Some grammarians move a description before its noun and take the result to mean someone near the fire who could help the family warm itself.
20:10/q19 What guidance did Moses hope to find by the fire? [هُدًۭى · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the family's lost route and need for water call for practical directions, or the phrase suggests religious guidance; the latter is challenged using the parallel at 28:29.)
  20:10/q19/p1 A person or people to guide him along the lost road. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, Qatāda, al-Suddī and others give this sense. Al-Farrāʾ and al-Wāḥidī call hudā a verbal noun in place of hādī; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī allow ellipsis 'one possessing guidance' or metonymy. Al-Suddī reasons people must tend a fire. Al-Ṭabarī permits spoken information about the route; Wahb seeks knowledge from an account. Qatāda transmits Aylah as a place attribution; al-Māwardī reports the family later stayed three days before a shepherd found them and then lived in a village forty years. Qatāda, Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid, as transmitted in al-Durr, identify a person who could guide or point out the road. Ibn ʿĀshūr adds that Moses hoped for someone who knew the route and was traveling in the same direction, so they could accompany one another.
  20:10/q19/p2 A sign or source of direction revealing the route. — reasons: Al-Māwardī and al-Ṭabrisī explicitly allow a sign, and al-Ṭabrisī says hudā names both guidance and what guides. Al-Rāzī gives a source or sign; al-Ṭabarī and Wahb allow route information.
  20:10/q19/p3 A person to lead him to water or to both water and the road. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj is cited for lost water; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī report the water variant; al-Ālūsī's travel account says there was no water. ʿIkrima, as transmitted in al-Durr, specifies a guide to water.
  20:10/q19/p4 Religious guidance. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī lists it as an alternative; Abū Ḥayyān attributes it to Mujāhid and Qatāda but calls it remote. Al-Ālūsī says their minds remained on religion, but rejects the fit with the family's needs and 28:29. Asad sees a possible symbol of Moses' dawning spiritual need. Ibn ʿĀshūr says God inspired Moses to say hudā, anticipating the great guidance he would find at the fire and the benefit he would bring his people.
20:10/q20 How is 'hudā' read at a pause? [هُدًۭى · readings] (turns on: Whether the final alif is merely a pause substitute for tanwīn or can be treated as a lexical ending permitting imāla.)
  20:10/q20/p1 Pause without imāla, preserving the alif replacing tanwīn. — reasons: Al-Samīn calls this better; Abū al-Baqāʾ prefers alif without inclination.
  20:10/q20/p2 Pause with imāla. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports explanations that tanwīn resembles a lexical ending, that the ending remains without tanwīn, or that a pausal form is used in all cases.
20:10/q21 What grammatical role can the clause of hope play? [لَّعَلِّىٓ ءَاتِيكُم أَوْ أَجِدُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the hope clause explains the command, describes Moses, or modifies the fire; al-Ālūsī rejects the last.)
