20:10/q12 Why does Moses say 'perhaps'? [لَّعَلِّىٓ · rhetoric]
  20:10/q12/p1 A firebrand or guide was hoped for, not guaranteed. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says Moses would not promise what he could not guarantee, contrasting a claim that Abraham lied for a good purpose. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī and al-Ālūsī distinguish the uncertain outcome from the certain sight.
20:10/q13 How is the yāʾ of 'laʿallī' read? [لَّعَلِّىٓ ءَاتِيكُم · readings] (turns on: Opening or quiescence of the first-person yāʾ.)
  20:10/q13/p1 It is read with fatḥ, laʿalliya. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī names Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr, Abū ʿAmr, Ibn ʿĀmir and Abū Jaʿfar across six occurrences; Qirāʾāt-Er says meaning is unchanged. Ibn Mujāhid says Nāfiʿ, Abū ʿAmr, and Ibn Kathīr open the listed first-person yāʾs apart from their individually named exceptions; laʿallī is not among those exceptions.
  20:10/q13/p2 It is read quiescent, laʿallī. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī names the Kufans and Yaʿqūb; Qirāʾāt-Er contrasts it with open yāʾ. Ibn Mujāhid reports that Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī, Ibn ʿĀmir, and ʿĀṣim through Abū Bakr keep all listed first-person yāʾs quiescent.
20:10/q14 What is the 'qabas' Moses hopes to bring? [بِقَبَسٍ · meaning] (turns on: Whether qabas is specifically a flaming brand, as al-Ālūsī insists, or may also be called an ember.)
  20:10/q14/p1 A flame or burning brand taken from a larger fire, carried on a stick or wick for warmth. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ, al-Ṭabarī and al-Zajjāj specify the tip of a stick or reed, and al-Zajjāj allows a wick; al-Samīn calls it a passive-sense verbal noun, a glowing end taken up. Al-Ālūsī equates it with the jadwah of 28:29 and distinguishes it from an ember. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ contrasts its guiding warmth with hostile meteors and notes iqtabasa at 57:13; a report from al-Bāqir says the family needed warmth.
  20:10/q14/p2 An ember or piece of fire to carry back. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Nasafī allow an ember, and Ibn Kathīr cites 28:29 for a glowing brand and warmth; Iṣlāḥī likewise speaks of an ember against cold.
20:10/q15 How do the related verbs for taking or giving fire and knowledge differ? [بِقَبَسٍ · meaning] (turns on: Whether usage assigns separate domains to the simple and prefixed forms or allows both in both domains.)
  20:10/q15/p1 The simple form concerns fire; the prefixed form concerns seeking or imparting knowledge. — reasons: Abū Zayd distinguishes bringing fire from seeking it; al-Mubarrad and al-Samīn distinguish giving fire from giving knowledge. These reports differ in their precise formulation.
  20:10/q15/p2 Both forms can be used for fire or knowledge. — reasons: Al-Kisāʾī allows both; al-Mubarrad says their underlying sense is one because both fire and knowledge illuminate.
20:10/q16 How is 'from it' attached grammatically? [مِّنْهَا بِقَبَسٍ · grammar] (turns on: Direct verbal attachment or an omitted circumstance describing the brand.)
  20:10/q16/p1 It attaches directly to the verb 'bring'. — reasons: Al-Samīn and Abū al-Baqāʾ, as cited by al-Ālūsī, permit this attachment.
  20:10/q16/p2 It is an omitted circumstantial qualification of the firebrand. — reasons: Al-Samīn and Abū al-Baqāʾ supply the omitted circumstance.
20:10/q17 Do the two hopes joined by 'or' exclude one another? [أَوْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the alternatives are merely listed as possibilities or explicitly compatible.)
  20:10/q17/p1 They are alternative hoped-for outcomes but need not exclude obtaining both. — reasons: Bursevī explicitly allows both; al-Ālūsī says aw prevents exclusion and presents two anticipated possibilities.
  20:10/q17/p2 They express two possible outcomes, neither yet certain. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī treats aw in the hope clause as the expression of two anticipated possibilities.
20:10/q18 What does 'upon the fire' mean? [عَلَى ٱلنَّارِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether ʿalā retains spatial elevation, stands for another local preposition, or describes an implicit guide.)
  20:10/q18/p1 On or above a nearby elevated place by the fire. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says people around a fire stand on overlooking ground; Abū Ḥayyān keeps the ordinary elevation sense, and al-Bayḍāwī and al-Nasafī allow elevated ground. Al-Ālūsī says elevation becomes an idiom for a nearby place, citing Sībawayh's account of bi as contact with a nearby place.
  20:10/q18/p2 It means 'at,' 'with,' or 'by' the fire. — reasons: Ibn al-Anbārī offers these prepositional senses; al-Ālūsī says substitution is unnecessary.
  20:10/q18/p3 It is a circumstance of an implied guide located near the fire. — reasons: Some grammarians move a description before its noun and take the result to mean someone near the fire who could help the family warm itself.
20:10/q19 What guidance did Moses hope to find by the fire? [هُدًۭى · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the family's lost route and need for water call for practical directions, or the phrase suggests religious guidance; the latter is challenged using the parallel at 28:29.)
  20:10/q19/p1 A person or people to guide him along the lost road. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, Qatāda, al-Suddī and others give this sense. Al-Farrāʾ and al-Wāḥidī call hudā a verbal noun in place of hādī; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī allow ellipsis 'one possessing guidance' or metonymy. Al-Suddī reasons people must tend a fire. Al-Ṭabarī permits spoken information about the route; Wahb seeks knowledge from an account. Qatāda transmits Aylah as a place attribution; al-Māwardī reports the family later stayed three days before a shepherd found them and then lived in a village forty years.
  20:10/q19/p2 A sign or source of direction revealing the route. — reasons: Al-Māwardī and al-Ṭabrisī explicitly allow a sign, and al-Ṭabrisī says hudā names both guidance and what guides. Al-Rāzī gives a source or sign; al-Ṭabarī and Wahb allow route information.
  20:10/q19/p3 A person to lead him to water or to both water and the road. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj is cited for lost water; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī report the water variant; al-Ālūsī's travel account says there was no water.
  20:10/q19/p4 Religious guidance. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī lists it as an alternative; Abū Ḥayyān attributes it to Mujāhid and Qatāda but calls it remote. Al-Ālūsī says their minds remained on religion, but rejects the fit with the family's needs and 28:29. Asad sees a possible symbol of Moses' dawning spiritual need.
20:10/q20 How is 'hudā' read at a pause? [هُدًۭى · readings] (turns on: Whether the final alif is merely a pause substitute for tanwīn or can be treated as a lexical ending permitting imāla.)
  20:10/q20/p1 Pause without imāla, preserving the alif replacing tanwīn. — reasons: Al-Samīn calls this better; Abū al-Baqāʾ prefers alif without inclination.
  20:10/q20/p2 Pause with imāla. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports explanations that tanwīn resembles a lexical ending, that the ending remains without tanwīn, or that a pausal form is used in all cases.
20:10/q21 What grammatical role can the clause of hope play? [لَّعَلِّىٓ ءَاتِيكُم أَوْ أَجِدُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the hope clause explains the command, describes Moses, or modifies the fire; al-Ālūsī rejects the last.)
  20:10/q21/p1 It gives the reason for telling the family to stay. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī lists this as one analysis.
  20:10/q21/p2 It describes Moses' state as he went toward the fire. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī lists a circumstantial analysis.
  20:10/q21/p3 It is an adjective describing the fire. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports a defense from a poetic comparison, but calls the analysis flawed.
20:10/q22 Why repeat 'the fire' instead of using a pronoun? [عَلَى ٱلنَّارِ · rhetoric]
  20:10/q22/p1 Naming the fire explains why someone offering guidance might be found there. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says people commonly gather around a fire; al-Suddī makes the same practical inference.
20:10/q23 How does this telling relate to other tellings of Moses' fire? [* · links]
  20:10/q23/p1 Parallel passages vary their wording while recounting the same episode. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī cites Ṭā Hā, al-Naml and al-Qaṣaṣ as varied expression that broadens the challenge to produce comparable discourse, citing 27:8, 28:29 and 52:34; he says paraphrase reveals the loss of Qurʾānic wording. Al-Burhān notes Ṭā Hā's atāhā against al-Naml's jāʾahā. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ distinguishes Moses' shihāb qabs in 27:7 and 28:29 from hostile meteors in 15:18, 37:10 and 72:9; she records al-Rāghib's bright-flame sense and Arabic applications to stars, snow mountains and white streaks.
  20:10/q23/p2 The parallel passages clarify that Moses sought warmth and a glowing brand. — reasons: Al-Yaḥyā cites 27:7; Ibn Kathīr and others cite 28:29 for darkness, cold and the brand.
20:10/q24 How does the episode serve the surrounding sūra? [* · links] (turns on: The sources emphasize consolation, literary and narrative sequence, or the beginning of the prophetic call.)
  20:10/q24/p1 It consoles Muhammad through Moses' trials and God's support. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says the episode amid Quraysh's persecution promises Muhammad's eventual ascendancy and weakening of unbelievers' schemes, urging endurance in worship and effort. Al-Biqāʿī prefers a coherence reading in which all creation belongs to God and divine aid overcomes difficulty; Moses' rescue, Pharaoh's ruin and Israel's emergence exemplify it.
  20:10/q24/p2 Moses follows mention of Muhammad because God used the apparent fire to draw him from his family. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī says the fire was a means rather than the ultimate aim; al-Bāqillānī praises the exact ordering and smooth transitions between stories.
  20:10/q24/p3 The episode begins Moses' call to prophethood in the sacred valley. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī places the return from Midian at Ṭuwā, where Moses receives prophethood; Kuranyolu calls the light an entry into divine presence.
