# 98:3: 23 questions

## 98:3/q01 What does 'in them' refer to?  [فِيهَا · referent]
turns on: Whether the pages are the recited sheets or celestial tablets.
- 98:3/q01/p1 The purified pages or scrolls recited by Muhammad. — Al-Nasafī identifies the antecedent as the sheets; the written contents are in them. Al-Ālūsī treats the phrase as a modifier of, or circumstance attached to, those pages. [4 notes: NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 98:3/q01/p2 On the inward reading, the pages are tablets of celestial intellects and souls. — Al-Qāshānī says detachment grants access to them and they are free of material and human corruption. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 98:3/q02 How does 'in them are books' attach grammatically?  [فِيهَا كُتُبٌۭ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the whole clause or only its prepositional phrase modifies the pages.
- 98:3/q02/p1 Only the prepositional phrase modifies the pages, and 'books' is its subject. — Al-Samīn calls this the best analysis; al-Ālūsī also records it. [3 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 98:3/q02/p2 The whole clause is a second modifier of the pages. — Al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī give this construction. [3 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 98:3/q02/p3 The whole clause is circumstantial, attached to the pronoun in 'purified'. — Al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī give this construction. [3 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 98:3/q03 What are the 'books' in the pages?  [كُتُبٌۭ · referent]
turns on: Whether 'books' denotes written contents, legal decrees, earlier scriptures, the Qur'an's divisions, or celestial foundations.
- 98:3/q03/p1 They are written verses, teachings, and other contents of the revealed pages. — Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Jarīr call them divine writings free of error; Ibn al-Jawzī says verses distinguish truth from falsehood. Al-Baghawī includes verses and rulings. Al-Biqāʿī says their precious knowledge merits writing. Al-Ālūsī calls them written contents. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī lists writing, medium, words and meanings as possible senses. [24 notes: TAB; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR(ابن جرير); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن جرير); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI]
- 98:3/q03/p2 They are God's upright judgments, duties, decrees, or explicit directives. — Al-Suddī calls them just obligations. Ṣāḥib al-Naẓm, al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī cite 58:21 and the Prophet's promise to judge by God's Book, including stoning although it is not written in the text; al-Qurṭubī also cites poetry using 'write' for decree. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites 2:216; Iṣlāḥī stresses clear sharia directives, not assorted stories. [20 notes: MAWARDI(السدي); MAWARDI-FULL(السدي); WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI(صاحب النظم); QURTUBI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,بعض أهل العلم); QURTUBI-FULL; TABATABAI; BURSEVI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 98:3/q03/p3 They are earlier divine scriptures whose truth the Qur'an contains or confirms. — Ibn ʿĪsā says the Qur'an mentions and confirms them. Al-Ṭabrisī cites 3:3; al-Taysīr says confirmation makes the prophets' books as if contained in the Qur'an. Al-Rāghib says the final scripture gathers their fruit, with earlier laws superseded or completed; Asad says shared ordinances reach their final expression in the Qur'an. [9 notes: MAWARDI(ابن عيسى); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عيسى); TABRISI(قيل); ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BURSEVI(المفردات); ITQAN(الراغب); ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 98:3/q03/p4 They are the Qur'an, called 'books' for its many subjects or kinds of exposition. — Muqātil says the Qur'an contains many different matters. Another view in al-Ṭabrisī makes each kind of knowledge a book. Quṭb compares topical chapters on purification, prayer, decree and resurrection. Al-Qurṭubī says its varied exposition accounts for the plural; Elmalılı identifies its sheets and surahs. [9 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); TABRISI(قيل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(مقاتل); QURTUBI(بعضهم); QURTUBI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; ELMALILI; DAMGHANI]
- 98:3/q03/p5 On the inward reading, they are enduring and just foundations of the true religion. — Al-Qāshānī locates them in the purified celestial tablets. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 98:3/q04 Why can pages contain 'books' if pages themselves are books?  [كُتُبٌۭ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether 'books' means decrees or distinguishable written contents.
- 98:3/q04/p1 'Books' means the rulings or decrees contained in the pages. — Al-Qurṭubī presents the apparent redundancy and resolves it through scriptural, Prophetic and poetic usage for divine judgment, citing 58:21 and the stoning judgment. [4 notes: QURTUBI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,بعض أهل العلم); QURTUBI-FULL]
- 98:3/q04/p2 The pages contain written verses or subjects that are themselves called books. — Al-Baghawī describes the inner contents as written verses and rulings. Muqātil and later commentators explain the plural through the Qur'an's many subjects. [7 notes: MUQATIL; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(مقاتل); QURTUBI(بعضهم); QURTUBI-FULL]

## 98:3/q05 Is any word understood as omitted from 'upright books'?  [كُتُبٌۭ قَيِّمَةٌۭ · grammar]
turns on: Whether an ellipsis is required or the surface wording is complete.
- 98:3/q05/p1 The expression means 'the rulings of upright books,' with 'rulings of' omitted. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya supplies an omitted construct before 'upright books'. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 98:3/q05/p2 'Upright' qualifies an omitted 'religion' or 'law'. — Al-Jishumī reports this analysis and says its feminine ending may agree semantically with milla, citing poetry. [1 notes: JISHUMI]
- 98:3/q05/p3 The direct sense 'upright books' needs no omitted word. — Al-Wāḥidī prefers the commentators' apparent reading because the alternative needs supplementation. [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT+; JISHUMI-]

## 98:3/q06 What does 'upright' mean here?  [قَيِّمَةٌۭ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the root conveys moral straightness or active authority and care.
- 98:3/q06/p1 Straight, sound, true and just, without error, contradiction or crookedness. — Abū ʿUbayda glosses it as standing and just; Ibn Zayd as balanced. Al-Zajjāj says it clearly separates truth from falsehood like a standing proof. Al-Qurṭubī derives it from q-w-m; Ibn ʿĀshūr treats standing ready for useful work as a metaphor for perfection, citing 18:1–2. Ibn Jarīr and Study Quran relate freedom from error to divine origin; al-Biqāʿī stresses freedom from shirk. Iṣlāḥī adds clarity and explicitness. [51 notes: MUQATIL; MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB; TAB-FULL(ابن زيد); NAHHAS(ابن زيد); QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,أبو عبيدة,الكلبي,عطاء,مقاتل); WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF; IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI(الزجاج); QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR(ابن جرير,ابن زيد); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن جرير,ابن زيد); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(IK, Sh,R)]
- 98:3/q06/p2 They stand as sound and conclusive authority over people and their affairs. — Ṣāḥib al-Naẓm derives this from managing affairs properly and compares a standing authority over a people, citing the hadith about a female manager. Al-Rāzī reports the independently standing and conclusive proof sense. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says divine rulings preserve society and its interests, citing 12:40. [4 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(صاحب النظم); RAZI; TABATABAI]

## 98:3/q07 Why is 'upright' feminine or intensive?  [قَيِّمَةٌۭ · grammar]
turns on: Whether feminine agreement follows the collective, an omitted noun, or intensification.
- 98:3/q07/p1 It agrees with 'books' treated as a collective group. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the feminine form describes the collective; Asad's note reports al-Rāzī's aggregate connotation. [2 notes: IBNASHUR; ASAD-NOTES(Razi)]
- 98:3/q07/p2 It is intensive, stressing established justice and guidance. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it an intensive form; al-Jishumī also offers an intensive feminine suffix. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; JISHUMI]
- 98:3/q07/p3 It agrees in sense with an omitted feminine 'religion' or 'law'. — Al-Jishumī reports this semantic agreement with milla and cites a poetic parallel. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 98:3/q08 How can an unlettered Messenger recite written pages?  [فِيهَا كُتُبٌۭ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether recitation is understood as oral delivery of contents or a miracle.
- 98:3/q08/p1 Reciting their contents from memory counts as reciting the pages. — Al-Ṭabrisī says the Prophet did not read from a physical book; al-Rāzī likewise distinguishes recitation of contents from reading the writing. [2 notes: TABRISI(قتادة); RAZI]
- 98:3/q08/p2 His reading without writing may have been miraculous. — Al-Rāzī cites a book attributed to Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq as a possible basis. [1 notes: RAZI]

## 98:3/q09 Who is the messenger associated with these pages?  [* · referent]
turns on: Whether the messenger and pages are earthly proclamation or angelic transmission.
- 98:3/q09/p1 Muhammad is the messenger who recites the purified pages. — Al-Ṭabarī connects the proof to Muhammad's messengership; al-Ālūsī prefers this and cites Ibn ʿAbbās and Muqātil. [5 notes: TAB; TAB-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+; ALUSI(بعض)+]
- 98:3/q09/p2 The messenger is Gabriel, with angelic copies from the Preserved Tablet. — Al-Ālūsī reports this view, then rejects it because making the pages mean revelation is weak. [0 notes: ALUSI(بعض)-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 98:3/q10 How is the messenger the clear proof?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: What bayyina itself denotes.
- 98:3/q10/p1 He is the clarifier of truth if 'proof' denotes the Prophet. — Al-Ālūsī says the identification is direct on this sense of bayyina. [1 notes: ALUSI]
- 98:3/q10/p2 His qualities and other miracles supply the proof. — Al-Ālūsī cites al-Ghazālī and al-Būṣīrī for the Prophet's miraculous qualities. [1 notes: ALUSI]

## 98:3/q11 How do these books relate to the upright religion mentioned later?  [كُتُبٌۭ قَيِّمَةٌۭ · links]
- 98:3/q11/p1 The later 'upright religion' means the upright books of verse 3. — Al-Ālūsī reports al-Ṭalqānī linking the definite expression to these earlier books. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(محمد بن الأشعث الطالقاني)]

## 98:3/q12 How did the groups respond when the proof arrived, and how does this fit their earlier state?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: How 'until' marks a change despite the later division and disbelief.
- 98:3/q12/p1 They had pledged to remain in their religions only until the promised Prophet came, but then divided and broke their pledge. — Al-Kashshāf's solution, favored by al-Rāzī, makes verse 4 expose this reversal; the People of the Book had invoked the Prophet against Arab opponents, then rejected him. Al-Rāzī likens it to a sinner who increases sin after wealth. Elmalılı finds extending this pledge to polytheists awkward. [11 notes: KASHSHAF; BIQAI(أبو جعفر ابن الزبير); ALUSI(الواحدي); ELMALILI(Ebussuud,Fahr-ı Râzî,Sâhib-Keşşâf); ELMALILI-]
- 98:3/q12/p2 Before decisive proof they could remain in an excused inherited state; after it, denial and division became culpable. — Elmalılı prefers this account. He describes an interval of corrupted religions and confusion, cites 5:19 and the rule in 17:15 that punishment follows a messenger, and says the mission makes truth, duty and accountability clear. Iṣlāḥī attributes rejection to conceit rather than real doubt; al-Biqāʿī contrasts informed rejectors with formerly uninformed believers. [9 notes: BIQAI(أبو جعفر ابن الزبير); ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ELMALILI+]
- 98:3/q12/p3 They continued mentioning the coming messenger's virtues until he arrived, then disagreed. — Al-Rāzī's third proposal, reported by Elmalılı, cites 2:89. [1 notes: ELMALILI(Fahr-ı Râzî)]
- 98:3/q12/p4 The group as a whole ceased to be disbelieving when some converted and others did not. — Al-Rāzī's fourth proposal makes the change after 'until' a change in the group's collective description. [1 notes: ELMALILI(Fahr-ı Râzî)]
- 98:3/q12/p5 The mission loosened their inherited certainties, though some stubbornly persisted and divided. — Al-Rāzī's fifth proposal distinguishes infikāk, detachment from what was clung to, from tafarruq, dispersion. [2 notes: ELMALILI(Fahr-ı Râzî)]

## 98:3/q13 How does this sūra connect with the previous sūra and its own later verses?  [* · links]
turns on: Which neighboring theme supplies the link.
- 98:3/q13/p1 It continues al-Qadr's account of revelation by identifying the clarifying scripture and the human response to it. — Ibn al-Zubayr says the Jews anticipated the Book after its revelation night but rejected it; the informed rejectors become the worst creatures, while previously uninformed believers become the best. Elmalılı relates the prior sūra's wisdom to the period before accountability. Iṣlāḥī says rejection came from conceit and demands for a sign were a pretext. [5 notes: BIQAI(أبو جعفر ابن الزبير); ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 98:3/q13/p2 After the upright books, the discourse turns to the people who believed. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya locates this transition at verse 4. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA]
- 98:3/q13/p3 Verse 3's mention of the People of the Book can include those with explicit or implicit shirk. — Elmalılı prefers this reading as best fitting the wording of verse 3. [1 notes: ELMALILI+]

## 98:3/q14 What is reported about the Prophet reciting this sūra to Ubayy?  [* · reports]
turns on: Whether the command names this sūra or the Qur'an generally.
- 98:3/q14/p1 God commanded the Prophet to recite the sūra to Ubayy, naming him; Ubayy wept. — Al-Baghawī transmits Anas's report. Ibn Kathīr conjectures that Ubayy's doubt after a recitation dispute prompted reassurance; he says the Prophet recited to communicate, confirm and warn. Ubayy had questioned Ibn Masʿūd's reading, and a report says Gabriel authorized up to seven modes. [5 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(أنس بن مالك); IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبي بن كعب); DURR-FULL(أبي بن كعب)]
- 98:3/q14/p2 A variant says God commanded the Prophet to recite the Qur'an to Ubayy. — Al-Baghawī attributes this wording to Qatāda. [1 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(قتادة)]

## 98:3/q15 What further wording was reported with Ubayy's recitation?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports preserve different expansions and uncertainty about the disputed wording.
- 98:3/q15/p1 One expanded recitation adds true non-polytheist hanifiyya and a promise that good is not denied. — Al-Durr reports Ubayy's version of the opening and its additions. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(أبي بن كعب)]
- 98:3/q15/p2 Another expanded recitation explains upright religion, deeds, best and worst creatures, and the God-fearing's reward. — Al-Durr reports this as a second Ubayy account. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(أبي بن كعب)]
- 98:3/q15/p3 The disputed two-valley saying says a person with two valleys of gold seeks a third; only dust fills the belly, and God accepts repentance. — Ibn ʿAbbās remembers the Prophet saying it; Ubayy said the Prophet had taught it to him. ʿUmar asked about entering it into the codex; Ubayy did not prohibit this but was unsure whether it was revelation or Prophetic speech. [3 notes: DURR-FULL(أبي بن كعب,ابن عباس)]

## 98:3/q16 What virtue is reported for reciting this sūra?  [* · sciences]
- 98:3/q16/p1 Its reciter will be with the best of creation at the evening resting place on Judgment Day. — Al-Bayḍāwī transmits this as a Prophetic report. [1 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 98:3/q17 How are the purified pages described elsewhere?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the note emphasizes the pages' honor or their Qur'anic identity and purity.
- 98:3/q17/p1 They are honored, exalted and purified. — Study Quran cites 80:13–14 and 85:21–22 for this description. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(Q)]
- 98:3/q17/p2 They are the Qur'an, free from falsehood, lies and slander. — Al-Ṭabrisī reports Qatāda's view; al-Durr reports him praising the Qur'an in the finest terms. [2 notes: TABRISI(قتادة); DURR-FULL(قتادة)]

## 98:3/q18 How were demands for written sheets portrayed?  [* · rhetoric]
- 98:3/q18/p1 The demand for sheets resembling the Torah's stone tablets is treated with biting ridicule. — Iṣlāḥī presents this as the point of the wording. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 98:3/q19 What meaning is given for hanif in the nearby passage?  [* · meaning]
turns on: Whether the root is explained through inclination or uprightness.
- 98:3/q19/p1 It means inclined toward truth. — Al-Ṭabrisī gives this gloss. [1 notes: TABRISI]
- 98:3/q19/p2 It can be derived from uprightness, with 'slanted foot' a hopeful inversion. — Al-Ṭabrisī reports this alternative derivation. [1 notes: TABRISI]

## 98:3/q20 What other senses can kitab have?  [كُتُبٌۭ · meaning]
- 98:3/q20/p1 It may designate the Qur'an, Torah, Gospel or an obligation. — Al-Dāmghānī lists these among ten senses but gives no examples for these four in the excerpt. [1 notes: DAMGHANI]

## 98:3/q21 How can readers recognize Qur'anic inimitability?  [* · theology]
turns on: The reader's command of Arabic eloquence and differences of expert taste.
- 98:3/q21/p1 Non-Arabs infer it from the Arabs' failed challenge. — Al-Bāqillānī says one unable to master Arabic is still less able to match the Qur'an. [1 notes: BAQILLANI]
- 98:3/q21/p2 Arabic speakers lacking mastery of style rely on the same indirect evidence. — Al-Bāqillānī distinguishes language knowledge from mastery of eloquence and forms. [1 notes: BAQILLANI]
- 98:3/q21/p3 Masters of Arabic style directly perceive speech beyond human limits. — Al-Bāqillānī likens them to money changers and cloth merchants discerning qualities others miss; expert tastes can still differ. [2 notes: BAQILLANI]
- 98:3/q21/p4 Experts differ in taste for weighty, flowing, or obscure speech. — Al-Bāqillānī reports preferences for solidity, graceful clarity, and rare diction. [1 notes: BAQILLANI]

## 98:3/q22 What poetic example is cited for judging speech and people?  [* · reports]
- 98:3/q22/p1 ʿUmar praised Zuhayr for praising people only for traits they possessed. — Al-Bāqillānī reports that ʿUmar then addressed ʿAbd Banī al-Ḥaṣḥāṣ after a line about age and Islam restraining a man. [1 notes: BAQILLANI(عمر بن الخطاب)]

## 98:3/q23 How was Majaz al-Qur'an used and assessed by later scholars?  [* · reports]
- 98:3/q23/p1 It remained an influential reference despite criticism, used by many later scholars. — The writer notes a missing Cairo section criticizing Abū ʿUbayda, making its value hard to assess; al-Ṭabarī's objections appear in footnotes. Users named include Ibn Qutayba, al-Bukhārī, al-Ṭabarī, al-Yazīdī, al-Zajjāj, Ibn Durayd, al-Sijistānī, Ibn al-Naḥḥās, al-Azharī, Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī, al-Jawharī, Abū ʿUbayd al-Harawī, Ibn Barrī and Ibn Ḥajar; a detailed study of al-Bukhārī's use is deferred. [11 notes: MAJAZ]
