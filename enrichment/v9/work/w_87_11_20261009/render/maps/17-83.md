# 17:83: 22 questions

## 17:83/q01 Who is the human described here?  [ٱلْإِنسَٰنِ · referent]
turns on: Whether the generic noun is unrestricted, contextually narrowed to unbelievers or Quraysh, or tied to a named occasion; al-Rāzī disputes the al-Walīd report.
- 17:83/q01/p1 Human beings generally, though the fault is strongest in unbelievers and does not include those God protects. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya includes sinners while saying the trait is strongest in unbelievers. Al-Rāzī cites 96:6–7 against restricting the verse to al-Walīd. Ibn Kathīr cites 10:12 and 17:67 and excepts those God protects. Al-Biqāʿī says exceptions are few, citing the prodigal of 10:12 and patient of 11:11. Al-Ālūsī says a generic species term can be true of only some members. Study Quran treats blessing and harm as a test of people generally (21:35). [13 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN; RAZI-FULL+]
- 17:83/q01/p2 Unbelievers or polytheists specifically, despite the general wording. — Muqātil calls him an unbeliever, and Yaḥyā a polytheist. Ibn ʿAṭiyya points to the preceding wrongdoers; al-Ṭabrisī says unbelievers expect no reward for illness. Abū Ḥayyān compares other generic condemnations in 100:6 and 70:19. Kuranyolu points to the ẓālimūn of 17:82. Al-Qurṭubī identifies those whom the Qurʾān increases in loss. [8 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 17:83/q01/p3 Al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra. — Ibn ʿAbbās is cited by al-Wāḥidī, Ibn al-Jawzī, and al-Rāzī; al-Qurṭubī says a report makes this the occasion. Al-Rāzī calls the identification far-fetched and instead applies the verse generally. [6 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; RAZI-FULL-]
- 17:83/q01/p4 The Quraysh opponents who evaded God's Book and Messenger. — Iṣlāḥī takes the general noun as contextually referring to the Quraysh miscreants. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 17:83/q01/p5 ʿUtba ibn Rabīʿa. — Al-Dāmghānī identifies him in the verses about turning away after mercy. [1 notes: DAMGHANI]

## 17:83/q02 What blessing has God given the human?  [أَنْعَمْنَا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the favor is material well-being or revealed guidance.
- 17:83/q02/p1 Worldly ease, health, wealth, provision, rescue, victory, or removal of harm. — Muqātil specifies provision, Yaḥyā ease and health, al-Māwardī health and wealth, Ibn al-Jawzī ample provision and relief, and al-Kashshāf health and prosperity. Al-Ṭabarī frames the favor as rescue from peril at sea after supplication; Ibn Kathīr adds victory and desired things. [11 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 17:83/q02/p2 Guidance offered to the person. — Al-Māwardī's alternative makes the following aversion refusal to listen and accept. [1 notes: MAWARDI-FULL]
- 17:83/q02/p3 The Qurʾān itself. — Al-Nasafī gives this alternative, so the following turning away is from the Qurʾān. [1 notes: NASAFI-FULL]

## 17:83/q03 What does turning away describe?  [أَعْرَضَ · interpretation]
turns on: Which aspect of aversion is foregrounded: neglected devotion, rejected revelation, or pride.
- 17:83/q03/p1 Neglecting God's remembrance, supplication, worship, obedience, and gratitude after favor. — Muqātil, al-Wāḥidī, and al-Baghawī stress ceasing prayer. Al-Ṭabarī says the rescued sea traveler acts as though he never called on God. Al-Rāzī says the face turns aside literally; al-Ṭabrisī says he neglects duties owed for blessing. Al-Ālūsī says he acts self-sufficient. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the omitted object is supplied by context: God, worship, commands, and prohibitions. Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports a saying about a believer turning away and God turning away from him. [19 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(رسول الله); TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 17:83/q03/p2 Rejecting offered guidance, especially listening to and accepting the Qurʾān. — Al-Māwardī makes guidance the blessing. Al-Nasafī offers the Qurʾān as the blessing. Iṣlāḥī says Quraysh evaded the Book and Messenger; Elmalılı says such people heed neither glad tidings nor warnings. [5 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ELMALILI; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 17:83/q03/p3 Proud refusal and self-exaltation. — ʿAṭāʾ glosses the act as arrogance in al-Thaʿlabī; al-Rāzī says prosperity causes rebellion, citing 96:6–7; al-Biqāʿī and Iṣlāḥī also emphasize pride. [4 notes: THALABI(عطاء); RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 17:83/q04 What does نأى بجانبه mean?  [وَنَـَٔا بِجَانِبِهِۦ · meaning]
turns on: Whether naʾy names distance, deliberate shunning, failed gratitude, arrogant posture, or inward refusal; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ disputes the lexical equation of naʾy with buʿd.
- 17:83/q04/p1 He distances his whole person from God, leaving closeness to Him. — Mujāhid is repeatedly quoted as saying he moves far from God; Yaḥyā calls it equivalent to turning away. Abū ʿUbayda says he withdraws to his side. Al-Ṭabarī recalls his earlier prayer in hardship. Ibn Khalawayh gives the root noun al-naʾy; al-Qurṭubī cites al-Nābigha's al-muntaʾā for a remote place. Al-Rāghib, via al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, contrasts showing one's side in aʿraḍa with taking distance in naʾā. Al-Māwardī also reports distancing oneself from the Lord. [19 notes: MUJAHID; YAHYA-SALLAM(مجاهد); MAJAZ; TAB-FULL(مجاهد); IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(مجاهد وابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(مجاهد); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(النابغة); IBNKATHIR-FULL(مجاهد); DURR-FULL(مجاهد); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI(المفردات); BINTSHATI-IJAZ-]
- 17:83/q04/p2 He withdraws from the obligations and gratitude due for God's blessing. — The semanticists cited by al-Wāḥidī support the root sense with 6:26; al-Wāḥidī's Wajīz and Ibn al-Jawzī specify rights owed for blessing. Al-Qurṭubī joins distance from duties with arrogance. [6 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أهل المعاني); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; TABRISI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 17:83/q04/p3 He shuns, refuses, and turns away; Qurʾānic naʾy is more specific than mere spatial distance. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ rejects treating naʾy as synonymous with buʿd, citing contexts in 41:51 and 6:25–26. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ+]
- 17:83/q04/p4 He separates himself in self-admiration and arrogance. — ʿAṭāʾ is cited by al-Wāḥidī and al-Baghawī; al-Māwardī says conceited people shun others. Al-Rāzī explains that the arrogant turn their flanks and backs; al-Bayḍāwī calls the flank a metaphor. Ibn ʿAṭiyya prefers distinguishing the rising nāʾa as arrogant self-separation from naʾā as simple distance. [14 notes: THALABI(عطاء); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(مجاهد وابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL(عطاء); KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNATIYYA-FULL+]
- 17:83/q04/p5 He turns away inwardly from faith. — Al-Dāmghānī says jānib may mean the heart, making the withdrawal inward. [1 notes: DAMGHANI]

## 17:83/q05 What does the mention of his side add?  [بِجَانِبِهِۦ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether jānib is a literal body part, a stand-in for the person, or the heart.
- 17:83/q05/p1 His physical side or flank visibly turns away in recoil, displaying arrogance. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says jānib means flank and bāʾ marks accompaniment; turning it depicts recoil. Al-Rāzī and al-Bayḍāwī appeal to the customary posture of proud people, and al-Ālūsī allows a literal side or back. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 17:83/q05/p2 The side stands for the whole person who withdraws. — Al-Ṭabarī says he turns from God with his own person, and al-Ālūsī explicitly allows metonymy for the person. [2 notes: TAB-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 17:83/q05/p3 The side is the heart, so the withdrawal is from faith inwardly. — This is al-Dāmghānī's explanation of jānib. [1 notes: DAMGHANI]

## 17:83/q06 How does نأى بجانبه relate to أعرض?  [أَعْرَضَ وَنَـَٔا بِجَانِبِهِۦ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the second clause is chiefly emphatic or depicts a further stage of departure.
- 17:83/q06/p1 It reinforces the turning away while adding a stronger picture of withdrawal and recoil. — Yaḥyā says Mujāhid's gloss has the same meaning as turning away. The semanticists quoted by al-Wāḥidī say naʾā adds stronger withdrawal, citing 6:26. Al-Kashshāf and al-Nasafī see an intensification; al-Ālūsī says conjunction can coordinate near-synonyms for force and need not be omitted for emphasis. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the second phrase adds distance and recoil; Iṣlāḥī says hateful evasion becomes physical. [7 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(مجاهد); WAHIDI-BASIT(أهل المعاني); KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 17:83/q06/p2 The first depicts turning the face or side; the second depicts moving away or presenting the back. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya distinguishes turning one's side from departure. Al-Nasafī describes a sequence from face to back, while al-Rāghib as cited by al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī contrasts showing one's side with distancing oneself. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; TABATABAI(المفردات)]

## 17:83/q07 How is نأى read?  [وَنَـَٔا · readings]
turns on: Vowel inclination, whether the hamza precedes the alif, underlying yāʾ, and varied transmission across this verse and Fuṣṣilat/32:51 or 41:51.
- 17:83/q07/p1 Naʾā, with nūn and hamza open and hamza before alif. — Ibn Kathīr, Nāfiʿ, Ḥafṣ, and many Abū ʿAmr transmissions are assigned this form; the majority is repeatedly cited. Ibn Khalawayh says it preserves the faʿala root; Abū ʿAlī says the uninclined form is common Ḥijāzī usage. Al-Rāzī calls it dominant Arabic and notes 41:51; al-Ṭabarī prefers it as more eloquent than the Medinan inversion. [15 notes: IBNMUJAHID; IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; FARISI-HUJJA(أبو عمرو,ابن كثير ونافع,حفص عن عاصم); THALABI(القراء); WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNASHUR-FULL(الجمهور); TAB-FULL+]
- 17:83/q07/p2 Nāʾa, with long alif before the hamza, transmitted especially from Ibn ʿĀmir and Abū Jaʿfar. — Sources differ in attribution: Ibn Dhakwān is specified by al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Samīn, al-Ālūsī, and Ibn ʿĀshūr; al-Ṭabarī calls it Medinan. Related readings recur in Fuṣṣilat/Ḥā Mīm al-Sajda. Its rival derivations are treated in the next question. Al-Ṭabarī finds it valid but less eloquent. [23 notes: TAB-FULL; IBNMUJAHID; NAHHAS(أبو جعفر); IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; FARISI-HUJJA(ابن عامر); THALABI(أبو جعفر وعامر); WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL(حمزة والكسائي وأبو بكر وابن عامر وأبو جعفر); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عامر); TABRISI(أبو علي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(ابن عامر); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عامر); BAYDAWI-FULL(ابن عامر); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(ابن ذكوان); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عامر); IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن ذكوان، أبو جعفر); QIRAAT-ER; TAB-FULL-]
- 17:83/q07/p3 Naʾā with full imāla or kasra-like inclination of both nūn and hamza. — Ibn Khalawayh and Abū ʿAlī derive the inclination from the underlying yāʾ and vowel assimilation, comparing shaʿīr and baʿīr. Transmissions of Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī, Abū Bakr, and others vary; al-Samīn distinguishes full from intermediate imāla and says the parallel in Fuṣṣilat differs for Abū Bakr. [16 notes: IBNMUJAHID; IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; FARISI-HUJJA(أبو بكر عن عاصم,حمزة والكسائي); THALABI(القراء); WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL(حمزة والكسائي وأبو بكر وابن عامر وأبو جعفر); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(حمزة والكسائي); NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الأخوان وأبو بكر عن عاصم والسوسي,خلف)]
- 17:83/q07/p4 Open nūn with kasra or inclination on the hamza only. — Ibn Khalawayh says the nūn remains in its original vowel while hamza approaches the adjacent yāʾ. Abū ʿAlī says the first vowel need not incline merely because a later one does. Khallād and other transmissions are named by al-Wāḥidī, Ibn al-Jawzī, and al-Rāzī; al-Samīn records the parallel dispute in Fuṣṣilat. [11 notes: IBNMUJAHID; IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; FARISI-HUJJA(حمزة والكسائي); THALABI(القراء); WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL(حمزة والكسائي وأبو بكر وابن عامر وأبو جعفر); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(أبو عمرو وعاصم وحمزة والكسائي); SAMIN-DURR(الأخوان وأبو بكر عن عاصم والسوسي)]
- 17:83/q07/p5 Kasra on nūn without hamza imāla, if Abū Bakr's report is taken that way. — Abū ʿAlī relates it to the present yanʾā and compares shahida and abā yaʾbā; he notes Abū Bakr's reading here differs from al-Sajda. Al-Nasafī also reports a kasra reading from ʿAlī. [3 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(أبو بكر عن عاصم); NASAFI-FULL]

## 17:83/q08 How is the reordered ناء form derived and understood?  [وَنَـَٔا · readings]
turns on: Whether the changed letter order is a metathesis of the distance root or a distinct root meaning rising or heaviness.
- 17:83/q08/p1 It is metathesis of naʾā, retaining the sense of moving away. — Al-Ṭabarī calls it valid Arabic but less eloquent; al-Naḥḥās points to naʾaytu and raʾaytu rather than hypothetical naʾtu and raʾtu. Abū ʿAlī and al-Wāḥidī cite rāʾa/raʾā and poetry; Ibn ʿĀshūr says rearrangement eases hamza after consonant before a long vowel. Al-Farrāʾ assigns naʾā to the Ḥijāz and nāʾa to some Hawāzin, Kināna, and most Anṣār, whereas al-Kisāʾī calls them dialects. Al-Samīn prefers avoiding metathesis when another derivation is possible. [16 notes: TAB-FULL; NAHHAS(أبو جعفر); FARISI-HUJJA; THALABI(أبو جعفر وعامر); WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL(حمزة والكسائي وأبو بكر وابن عامر وأبو جعفر); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عامر); TABRISI(أبو علي); RAZI-FULL(ابن عامر); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عامر); BAYDAWI-FULL(ابن عامر); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عامر); IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن ذكوان، أبو جعفر); SAMIN-DURR-]
- 17:83/q08/p2 It comes from nāʾa yanūʾu, rising or standing under a load, hence rising away heavily. — Ibn Khalawayh says the original wāw became alif and cites 28:76. A poem cited by Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn depicts rising while leaning on the left side or the shoulder rising. Al-Thaʿlabī and al-Qurṭubī note nawʾ may mean rising or its opposite, falling or sitting. Ibn ʿAṭiyya prefers the semantic distinction of arrogant rising from simple distance; Qirāʾāt Er describes pulling away with effort. [17 notes: IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; THALABI(أبو جعفر وعامر); BAGHAWI-FULL(حمزة والكسائي وأبو بكر وابن عامر وأبو جعفر); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عامر); RAZI-FULL(ابن عامر); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL(ابن عامر); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(ابن ذكوان); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عامر); QIRAAT-ER; IBNATIYYA-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 17:83/q08/p3 It evokes heaviness or sluggishness toward gratitude. — Ibn ʿĀshūr takes the alternate form as becoming heavy toward gratitude and compares the one clinging to earth in 7:176; al-Ālūsī also mentions haste or sluggishness among possible senses. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عامر)]

## 17:83/q09 How is يؤوسا pronounced when its hamza is eased?  [يَـُٔوسًۭا · readings]
turns on: Whether easing hamza retains a consonant cluster or transfers the vowel to wāw.
- 17:83/q09/p1 The hamza may be dropped while leaving adjacent silent letters, an attested Arabic treatment. — Al-Farrāʾ compares ولا يؤده حفظهما (2:255) and بعذاب بئيس (46:34) and says ordinary speech keeps the hamza-less cluster. [2 notes: FARRA; FARRA+]
- 17:83/q09/p2 Readers transfer the hamza's vowel to wāw, yielding a yūsa-like form. — Al-Farrāʾ reports this practice but calls it heavier and unattested in ordinary speech; al-Kisāʾī transmits يوسا and compares وإذا المودة to الموزة. [2 notes: FARRA; NAHHAS(الكسائي); FARRA-]

## 17:83/q10 What harm touches the person?  [ٱلشَّرُّ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether sharr specifies poverty, illness, the sword, or includes all kinds of adversity.
- 17:83/q10/p1 Poverty or loss of provision. — Muqātil and Qatāda through al-Māwardī specify poverty; al-Kashshāf, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Rāzī, and others include it among hardships. [13 notes: MUQATIL; MAWARDI-FULL(قتادة); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الفلاسفة)]
- 17:83/q10/p2 Illness or loss of health. — Al-Kalbī through al-Māwardī identifies illness; Yaḥyā adds illnesses and hardships. Al-Wāḥidī, al-Kashshāf, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, and others include sickness as one possible adversity. [10 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; MAWARDI-FULL(الكلبي); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL]
- 17:83/q10/p3 Any hardship, distress, affliction, calamity, or financial loss. — Yaḥyā says hardships, al-Thaʿlabī hardship and distress, al-Baghawī harm, and al-Nasafī any severe event. Al-Ṭabrisī and Ibn Kathīr include afflictions; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī stresses even slight harm. [12 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 17:83/q10/p4 The sword. — Al-Māwardī reports it as a further specific possibility. [1 notes: MAWARDI-FULL]

## 17:83/q11 What does يؤوسا mean and how is its form constructed?  [يَـُٔوسًۭا · meaning]
- 17:83/q11/p1 Deeply despairing, with no hope of good, relief, or God's mercy. — Qatāda equates it with losing hope; al-Majāz calls it profound despair, al-Akhfash derives it from yaʾisa, and al-Jishumī equates yaʾs with qunūṭ. Al-Ṭabrisī contrasts believers who hope for relief. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it an intensive form and says kāna marks entrenched despair. Al-Durr transmits Ibn ʿAbbās's gloss as hopelessness. [29 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(قتادة); MAJAZ; AKHFASH; THALABI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]

## 17:83/q12 Why does adversity make the person despair?  [وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ ٱلشَّرُّ كَانَ يَـُٔوسًۭا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether despair follows unbelief, guilt, delayed relief, reliance on causes, former ingratitude, or grief itself.
- 17:83/q12/p1 He lacks faith, trust in God's grace, or hope of recompense. — Yaḥyā says he does not intend to seek recompense; al-Māwardī gives disbelief as one cause. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says unbelievers do not trust God's control; al-Ṭabrisī says they expect no reward for illness. Al-Wāḥidī and al-Qurṭubī identify mistrust of divine grace. [7 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 17:83/q12/p2 He knows his sins merit punishment. — Al-Māwardī offers this as an alternative to despair caused by unbelief. [1 notes: MAWARDI-FULL]
- 17:83/q12/p3 His prayer for relief goes unanswered for a time. — Al-Baghawī reports the view that a distressed person prays and despairs if the response is delayed; believers should continue supplicating. [1 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل)]
- 17:83/q12/p4 Attachment to visible causes blinds him to God's power once those causes fail. — Al-Qāshānī calls this a veil over the all-powerful God; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī explains that ordinary people depend on visible causes in prosperity and see no other source of good when harm removes them. [3 notes: QASHANI; TABATABAI]
- 17:83/q12/p5 His failure to act well during prosperity leaves him expecting no mercy during hardship. — Al-Ālūsī connects despair to earlier neglect of gratitude. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 17:83/q12/p6 He is consumed by grief and constriction rather than using harm to reflect or repent. — Al-Rāzī compares the prosperity-and-loss pattern with 89:15–16 and 70:19–21. Ibn ʿĀshūr says hardship should prompt thought about remedy but he remains angry and does not repent. Kuranyolu describes pain and grief. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 17:83/q13 What moral pattern does the verse describe?  [* · interpretation]
- 17:83/q13/p1 People forget God and become proud in ease, then lose hope in hardship. — Al-Rāzī cites 89:15–16 and 70:19–21 for the two-state pattern; al-Biqāʿī excepts those God preserves from the devil. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls it the socially conditioned ordinary response. Study Quran calls good and evil a moral test (21:35), citing 41:51, 11:10, 39:49, 41:50, 16:53, and 4:79 for pride, and 41:49, 70:20, 10:12, 16:54, and 30:33 for anxiety and forgetfulness. Elmalılı says such people do not heed warning or good news. [17 notes: THALABI; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; QASHANI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]

## 17:83/q14 What response should replace arrogance and despair?  [* · interpretation]
- 17:83/q14/p1 Gratitude to God in ease and patience, hope, and continued prayer in adversity. — Al-Qāshānī says gratitude binds blessing and patience repels calamity. Al-Ṭabrisī contrasts the believer's hope of mercy. Al-Baghawī says prayer should persist even if the answer is delayed. Ibn Kathīr cites Hūd 11:10–11 for the patient exception; Elmalılı, Iṣlāḥī, and Kuranyolu likewise urge gratitude, patience, and prayer. Kuranyolu calls this a religious, psychological, and moral human need. Study Quran cites 22:35, 31:17, and 2:156 for patient endurance and acknowledging return to God. [9 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); TABRISI; QASHANI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]

## 17:83/q15 How does the verse connect with the surrounding Qurʾānic discussion?  [* · links]
turns on: Which nearby wording supplies the connection: the warning of loss, the Qurʾān's healing, or the next verse's account of each person's way.
- 17:83/q15/p1 It explains the Qurʾān's preceding warning that revelation increases wrongdoers in loss. — Al-Jishumī poses the connection as a compositional question. Al-Ṭabrisī cites Noah 71:6 for supplication driving people farther away and alternatively says revelation exposes their hidden malice against the Prophet. Al-Qurṭubī says those increased in loss refuse to reflect on signs; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says their forgetting God in blessing and despair in harm explain the warning. Kuranyolu connects the people to 17:82 and its healing and merciful teaching. [7 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 17:83/q15/p2 The surrounding mention of Qurʾānic healing calls people to recognize sin and seek its cure. — Bursevī says the Qurʾān names sin as disease and repentance as medicine, and diagnosis precedes treatment. He transmits al-Tamīmī's remedies: water from a vessel inscribed with al-Fātiḥa for a sick face, drinking it for trembling, doubts, and palpitations, and rosewater for poor memory, all by God's permission. [2 notes: BURSEVI(الشيخ التميمي)]
- 17:83/q15/p3 The next verse's shākila means one's direction, side, or way. — Abū Ḥayyān transmits Ibn ʿAbbās's gloss, also reported by al-Durr with a gloss of despair. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]

## 17:83/q16 How can despair here accord with passages where harm makes people pray?  [وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ ٱلشَّرُّ كَانَ يَـُٔوسًۭا · links]
turns on: The stage or kind of distress and whether the verse describes ordinary behavior or emergency instinct.
- 17:83/q16/p1 Despair follows experienced harm, while other passages depict prayer at the onset of harm or an ordinary person's different response. — Al-Burhān distinguishes certain experience of harm from eager prayer when a person of little patience first meets it. Bursevī says this description is of some people and does not conflict with 41:51. Ibn ʿĀshūr says 17:83 concerns anger and failure to repent and does not contradict 41:51. [3 notes: BURHAN; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 17:83/q16/p2 Prayer under complete failure of apparent causes is an innate emergency response; despair here is the ordinary cause-bound response. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī compares 17:67 and 10:12, distinguishing the innate turn to God when all causes fail from attachment to causes in social life. [2 notes: TABATABAI]

## 17:83/q17 Why is blessing attributed directly to God while evil merely touches the human?  [أَنْعَمْنَا مَسَّهُ ٱلشَّرُّ · rhetoric]
- 17:83/q17/p1 Good is intended by divine generosity in itself; evil is not directly attributed because it is incidental or relative to its bearer. — Bursevī and al-Ālūsī note the asymmetry. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the universal order makes evil incidental to greater good. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says blessing is from God, not people (17:100), and compares the Qurʾān's refusal to say 'We adorned their evil deeds.' Al-Ālūsī cites the Prophet's supplication that good is in God's hands and evil is not attributed to Him. [6 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(النبي); TABATABAI; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 17:83/q18 What is evil and how does it arise under divine providence?  [ٱلشَّرُّ · theology]
turns on: The nature of evil as privation, limits of contingent matter, whether the whole order has predominant good, and the disputed relation of divine choice to necessity.
- 17:83/q18/p1 True evil is privation of an attainable good; positive harmful things count as evil through the loss they cause. — Philosophers reported by al-Ālūsī distinguish nonbeing such as poverty and ignorance from weather, vice, theft, or pain that obstruct good; fire perfects itself though it harms its victim, and revenge can perfect the irascible faculty. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī quotes Plato's killing example: the evil is the victim's lost life, not the sword's sharpness or the killer's strength. He distinguishes absolute nonexistence from missing a fitting capacity, as in blindness. [7 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الفلاسفة); TABATABAI(أفلاطون)]
- 17:83/q18/p2 Essential limits come from contingency; accidental loss of attainable perfection arises through matter and limited receptivity. — Al-Ālūsī's philosophical account says only God has absolute perfection; a thing's impossible perfections require no further cause, whereas loss of an attainable quality such as philosophy does. Material beings, unlike immaterial creations perfect for their kind, have prime matter and receptivity that permit such privation. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says a plant's lack of animal life follows its essence and is not created, while illness and pain arise in matter. God's decree wills a thing's attainable existence essentially; the accompanying lack follows limited capacity incidentally. Al-Ālūsī reports philosophers locating defects in contingent receptivity rather than divine stinginess. [10 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الفلاسفة); TABATABAI]
- 17:83/q18/p3 Divine wisdom creates pure good and mixtures with more good than evil; evil-dominant or equal mixtures do not occur. — Philosophers reported by al-Ālūsī say pure, dominant, and equally balanced evil are absent; withholding goods of the elemental world to avoid rare harms would cause greater evil and deprive many beings. Al-Ālūsī says a small evil can beautify the whole like a dark beauty mark. He says God brings about what is free of evil or has little evil where absence would be worse, links the principle to 20:50, and points to al-Ishārāt and its commentaries. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī also evaluates five possible balances and reaches pure or predominant good. [7 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الفلاسفة); TABATABAI]
- 17:83/q18/p4 The inquiry presupposes a debate over divine choice, but philosophers can affirm God's choice and explain evil without dualism. — Al-Ālūsī says the analysis assumes a view of divine choice shared by Ashʿarīs, while philosophers relate choice to universal laws and wisdom; he rejects al-Rāzī's charge of pointlessness. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports al-Rāzī's analogy to fire's necessary burning, replies that necessity of the effect cannot compel its cause, and praises al-Ālūsī's fair account. He says philosophers investigate evil to avoid positing two principles, as Magians do. [7 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الفلاسفة); TABATABAI(الإمام,الرازي,صاحب روح المعاني); ALUSI-FULL+; TABATABAI+]
- 17:83/q18/p5 Philosophical inquiry into evil is pointless if God acts by necessity rather than free choice. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī quotes al-Rāzī's criticism comparing necessary divine action to fire burning; al-Ālūsī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reject it as partisan or biased. [2 notes: TABATABAI(الإمام,الرازي); ALUSI-FULL-; TABATABAI-]

## 17:83/q19 How is يؤوسا parsed?  [كَانَ يَـُٔوسًۭا · grammar]
- 17:83/q19/p1 Yaʾūsan is accusative as the predicate of kāna. — Al-Jishumī supplies this grammatical analysis; Ibn ʿĀshūr says kāna also conveys a settled disposition of despair. [2 notes: JISHUMI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 17:83/q20 What inward reading is given to blessing and harm?  [* · inward]
turns on: Whether the inward obstruction is reliance on spiritual blessings or attachment to bodily and visible causes.
- 17:83/q20/p1 Enjoying obedience or relying on blessings can make a person turn from the Giver. — Al-Wāsiṭī through al-Sulamī includes the savor of obedience, settling into it, and reliance on it for protection; he names guidance, faith, gnosis, and divine friendship the greatest blessings. [1 notes: SULAMI(الواسطي)]
- 17:83/q20/p2 Attachment to self and body veils the Real in prosperity and God's power in hardship. — Al-Qāshānī says material effects breed pride, while insight would see God's power in both states and answer with gratitude and patience. [3 notes: QASHANI]

## 17:83/q21 What wordplay is noted in the wider passage?  [* · rhetoric]
- 17:83/q21/p1 Jinās al-iṭlāq can link merely resembling words. — Al-Itqān lists janna/jannatayn, ʿamali/al-qalīn, yuwārī, irtadd, and repeated arḍ and duʿāʾ forms as examples. [1 notes: ITQAN]

## 17:83/q22 Which other verses parallel the two responses to blessing and harm?  [* · links]
- 17:83/q22/p1 Other passages portray prosperity followed by pride and adversity followed by grief or despair, while some name the patient exception. — Asad cites Hūd 11:9–10. Al-Rāzī compares 89:15–16 and 70:19–21; Ibn Kathīr adds Hūd 11:10–11, whose patient righteous are excepted. Study Quran cites 41:51, 11:10, 39:49, 41:50, 16:53, and 4:79 for prosperous pride, and 41:49, 70:20, 10:12, 16:54, and 30:33 for anxious hardship and forgetting God afterward.
