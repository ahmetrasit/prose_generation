# 20:118: 20 questions

## 20:118/q01 What does لك convey in this promise?  [لَكَ · interpretation]
- 20:118/q01/p1 It marks a benefit God undertakes to give Adam. — Al-Biqāʿī says the benefit incumbent on God is Adam's freedom from hunger and being stripped, inwardly and outwardly. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 20:118/q02 How does Ubayy ibn Kaʿb read the two verbs?  [تَجُوعَ تَعْرَىٰ · readings]
- 20:118/q02/p1 He reads them passively: Adam will not be made hungry or left unclothed. — Ibn al-Jawzī reports Ubayy's reading with ḍamma on tāʾ and a long alif. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبي بن كعب)]

## 20:118/q03 How is إن لك ألا تجوع ولا تعرى constructed?  [إِنَّ لَكَ أَلَّا تَجُوعَ وَلَا تَعْرَىٰ · grammar]
- 20:118/q03/p1 The clause about not hungering is the accusative noun or complement of إن, with لك its predicate; تعرى is subjunctive through coordination with تجوع. — Al-Ṭabarī calls the an-clause the accusative complement of inna. Al-Wāḥidī compares the implied an construction to 'you have property.' Al-Samīn and al-Būrsawī identify lak as the predicate and the an-clause as the noun; al-Samīn explains the subjunctive of taʿrā by coordination with tajūʿ. [5 notes: TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI]

## 20:118/q04 How is the following assurance read and coordinated with ألا تجوع?  [أَلَّا تَجُوعَ · readings]
turns on: The hamza vowel of the following innaka or annaka and whether its clause resumes speech or coordinates with the an-clause here.
- 20:118/q04/p1 With fatḥa on wa-annaka, the following clause coordinates with the assurance that Adam will not hunger. — Ibn Khālawayh says the coordination follows meaning rather than surface grammar. Al-Kashshāf and al-Rāzī say wāw can stand for a governing particle other than inna or an, allowing the coordination although one an would not normally precede another. [4 notes: IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL]
- 20:118/q04/p2 With kasra on wa-innaka, the following assurance starts an independent sentence. — Ibn Khālawayh explicitly contrasts independence with coordination; al-Kashshāf and al-Rāzī report both vowel readings. [3 notes: IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL]

## 20:118/q05 What does ألا تجوع promise Adam?  [أَلَّا تَجُوعَ · interpretation]
- 20:118/q05/p1 In the Garden Adam has sufficient, continuing food and never suffers hunger. — Muqātil, al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Ṭabrisī and Asad state the assurance directly. Al-Wāḥidī explains it as satiety; his report from Ibn ʿAbbās limits the promise to Adam's stay in Paradise. Ibn ʿAṭiyya stresses complete, lasting provision, including drink and protection from the sun. Al-Jishumī and al-Kashshāf add that obtaining food needs no effort or worldly earnings. Al-Ālūsī specifies that hunger never reaches Adam at all, rather than being relieved after it occurs. [15 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES]

## 20:118/q06 What place does فيها designate?  [فِيهَا · referent]
- 20:118/q06/p1 It designates the Garden or Paradise in which Adam stays. — Yaḥyā ibn Sallām and al-Nasafī state the referent; Ibn ʿAbbās, as reported by al-Wāḥidī, says the freedom from hunger lasts while Adam remains there. Al-Būrsawī ties it to the availability of the Garden's blessings. [4 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI]

## 20:118/q07 What does ولا تعرى mean for Adam, and how was he covered?  [وَلَا تَعْرَىٰ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether absence of nakedness means supplied covering or absence of felt nakedness, and whether the reports specify the covering as nail-like material or bull hide.
- 20:118/q07/p1 Adam would not be bare skinned or lack clothing, because the Garden provides it. — Muqātil, al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī and Asad give the ordinary provision reading; al-Nasafī and al-Būrsawī emphasize that garments are always available. Al-Wāḥidī lists ʿariya, yaʿrā, ʿuryan, ʿārin, and feminine ʿuryāna or ʿariyya; he cites the Prophet riding Abū Ṭalḥa's horse ʿaryan without a saddle. He reports al-Azharī's restriction: Arabs say ʿarī of a horse and aʿrāʾ of horses, not ʿarī of a man. Abū Ḥayyān defines nakedness as nothing protecting the skin and cites a poet's contrast with clothed girls. Al-Samīn likewise cites an anonymous line for ʿariya yaʿrā; al-Būrsawī calls ʿurāʾ uncovered skin. [16 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الأزهري,النبي); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ASAD-NOTES; ASAD-NOTES-]
- 20:118/q07/p2 Adam and his wife wore a nail-like covering called al-ẓufr. — This is Yaḥyā ibn Sallām's report of their covering. [1 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM]
- 20:118/q07/p3 Adam's clothing was made from the bull's hide. — Al-Wāḥidī reports this from al-Kalbī. [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي)]
- 20:118/q07/p4 The phrase concerns innocent humans' lack of any felt nakedness, even without clothes. — Asad argues this against a merely literal sense and cites 20:121 and 7:22 about awareness of nakedness, alongside the allegory in 7:20. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES+]

## 20:118/q08 How are hunger, nakedness, thirst, and sun exposure paired and ordered?  [أَلَّا تَجُوعَ وَلَا تَعْرَىٰ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the order follows shared inner and outer effects, separates expected counterparts for emphasis, ranks needs by importance, or serves a wrap-and-unfold rhyme pattern; al-Ālūsī disputes two poetic or rhyme arguments.
- 20:118/q08/p1 The pairs link inner and outer afflictions: hunger with nakedness, then thirst with the sun's heat. — Al-Ṭabrisī calls hunger and nakedness inner and outer deprivation, while heat causes thirst. Ibn Kathīr calls hunger inward humiliation and nakedness outward humiliation. Al-Burhān, al-Itqān, al-Ālūsī and Ibn ʿĀshūr develop emptiness or missing bodily protection and inner versus outer heat. Abū Ḥayyān notes shared semantic features between each cross pair. [7 notes: TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURHAN; ITQAN; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 20:118/q08/p2 Separating the expected counterpart terms makes the several blessings, or the contrast between them, more striking. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the pairs disperse customary associations and cites Arabic poetry, including Imruʾ al-Qays on riding and embracing. Al-Ṭabrisī compares Imruʾ al-Qays's scrambled expressions with ʿAbd Yaghūth's more fitting pairs, and also says Arabic can state two expressions before their semantic counterparts. Al-Intiṣāf, reported by al-Ālūsī, calls it qaṭʿ al-naẓīr ʿan al-naẓīr and says separation keeps two blessings from seeming one. Al-Ālūsī reports verses of the first and later Kindī and Sayf al-Dawla's reproach to the latter; he questions whether the Kindī verses truly illustrate separation, since riding and urging horses both evoke bravery and woman and wine both evoke pleasure. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the poetic examples of Imruʾ al-Qays and Abū al-Ṭayyib show a more striking antithesis. [9 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(امرؤ القيس); TABRISI; ALUSI-FULL(الانتصاف,الكندي الآخر,الكندي الأول,سيف الدولة); IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 20:118/q08/p3 The first blessings, satiety and clothing, come first because they are primary; drink and shade complement them. — Al-Ālūsī reports this from al-Kashf, which also notes the contrast of inna laka and innaka; he reports al-Ṭībī that the four are ordered by importance and expand the warning about expulsion. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الطيبي,الكشف)]
- 20:118/q08/p4 The four comforts follow an ordered wrap-and-unfold pattern serving rhyme. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī gives their unmarked order as hunger, thirst, nakedness, sun exposure. Al-Ālūsī objects that rhyme has not been shown to require this exact sequence; another order might also fit. [1 notes: TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 20:118/q08/p5 The pairing can also be illustrated by al-Mutanabbī's line about standing before death. — Al-Burhān cites the anecdote of al-Mutanabbī and Sayf al-Dawla as a related rhetorical illustration. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 20:118/q09 Why are the blessings expressed as the absence of hardships?  [أَلَّا تَجُوعَ وَلَا تَعْرَىٰ · rhetoric]
- 20:118/q09/p1 Naming the hardships warns Adam more forcefully to avoid the cause that would cost him the Garden's blessings. — Al-Kashshāf, al-Rāzī and Ibn ʿĀshūr say the negative wording makes Adam hear forms of misery and avoid their cause. Al-Ālūsī says the feared afflictions are recalled more strongly than positive promises of food, drink, clothing and dwellings; he adds that permission to enjoy the Garden except the tree in 2:35 had already expressed the positive side, and specifies that none of the hardships ever reaches Adam. [6 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 20:118/q10 What human necessities do these assurances identify?  [أَلَّا تَجُوعَ وَلَا تَعْرَىٰ · interpretation]
- 20:118/q10/p1 Food, drink, clothing, and shade or suitable shelter are fundamental human needs, all supplied in the Garden. — Al-Kashshāf says Adam needs no other provider or worldly earnings. Al-Rāzī calls the four pillars of well-being and likewise says no earning is required. Al-Jishumī stresses lack of effort for food, drink and clothing. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the four absences foreshadow human life's work to secure these needs: circumstances of creation indicate defining components of the created being; he compares the Israʾ report in which the Prophet chose milk over wine and was told that wine would have misled his community. Al-Ālūsī's report from al-Kashf makes food and clothing primary, drink and shade complementary. [7 notes: JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الكشف); IBNASHUR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 20:118/q11 What legal measure of maintenance follows from the four necessities?  [أَلَّا تَجُوعَ وَلَا تَعْرَىٰ · law]
- 20:118/q11/p1 Providing the four necessities fulfills required maintenance; extra provision merits reward. — Al-Qurṭubī reasons that food, clothing, drink and shelter sustain life. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]

## 20:118/q12 How does the promise relate to the warning about leaving the Garden?  [* · links]
- 20:118/q12/p1 The Garden's four comforts explain the threatened hardship of expulsion and urge Adam to avoid its cause. — Al-Rāzī says the four freedoms explain the preceding misery. Al-Qurṭubī contrasts Garden provision with toil, hunger, nakedness, thirst and sun after expulsion. Al-Ālūsī says complete comforts make staying essential; his report from al-Ṭībī says repetition of fīhā and negation evokes worldly hardship because the Garden is for delight. Ibn ʿĀshūr says departure removes abundant food, clothing, drink and a fitting climate. Study Quran contrasts Garden ease with the hardship after yielding to Satan, citing 2:36 and 7:24–25. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الطيبي); IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]

## 20:118/q13 Did Adam ask about food, clothing, drink, and shade?  [* · reports]
- 20:118/q13/p1 One report says Adam asked about all four and God answered with these assurances. — Al-Ālūsī reports the suggestion as a reason for singling Adam out, while doubting its authenticity; he also refers to another reason given earlier without specifying it in this note. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]

## 20:118/q14 How could Adam know these hardships before experiencing them?  [* · interpretation]
- 20:118/q14/p1 He knew their realities through God's teaching of all the names. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says Adam had not directly experienced hunger, nakedness, thirst or sun exposure and cites 2:31. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 20:118/q15 What verbal form is used for Adam's hunger?  [تَجُوعَ · grammar]
- 20:118/q15/p1 God's address to Adam uses the imperfect verb. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ notes this form in Ṭā Hā 20:118. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ]

## 20:118/q16 What inward meaning is drawn from Adam's provision?  [* · inward]
turns on: The reported inward readings focus respectively on Adam's enduring human need or on the animal self's enjoyment in Paradise.
- 20:118/q16/p1 Despite Adam's singular creation and honors, he returns to the human condition of needing food and drink. — Al-Sulamī reports Ibn ʿAṭāʾ on what suits human nature, and al-Wāsiṭī on God's creating Adam by His hand, breathing His spirit into him and choosing him before returning him to human measure. [2 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء,الواسطي)]
- 20:118/q16/p2 The abiding Garden, though near God, also gives the animal self a pasture for bodily enjoyment. — Al-Būrsawī reports al-Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya: the self enjoys food, drink, clothing and sexual partners there as in worldly life. [1 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]

## 20:118/q17 What report describes shade in Paradise?  [فِيهَا · reports]
- 20:118/q17/p1 A tree's shade is so vast that a rider cannot cross it in a hundred years; it is the Tree of Eternity. — Al-Durr reports this saying from the Prophet. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 20:118/q18 How does this description connect with the worldly desires and afterlife blessings of 3:14–15?  [* · links]
- 20:118/q18/p1 Six worldly desires are paired with six afterlife blessings, preserving desire and sustenance at the ends and culminating in God's approval. — Al-Burhān says the afterlife order also places spouses appropriately and cites 3:14–15. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 20:118/q19 How does the nakedness here relate to the couple's disobedience?  [وَلَا تَعْرَىٰ · links]
- 20:118/q19/p1 Satan's whisper and their disobedience expose previously hidden nakedness and lead to awareness of it and discussion of clothing. — Study Quran compares the Quran and Bible, citing 20:121 and 7:22, 7:26–27, 7:31–32; Asad also invokes 20:121, 7:22 and the allegory of 7:20 for the loss of innocence. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN; ASAD-NOTES]

## 20:118/q20 How does this telling relate to earlier Quranic accounts of Adam?  [* · links]
- 20:118/q20/p1 This sura's distinctive wording supplies the focus after Adam's creation and the angels' prostration were told in al-Baqara and al-Aʿraf. — Ibn ʿĀshūr explicitly sets aside the already related events to examine what is distinctive here. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
