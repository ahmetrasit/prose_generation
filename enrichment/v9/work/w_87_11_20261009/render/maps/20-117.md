# 20:117: 19 questions

## 20:117/q01 Why does God warn Adam at this point in the story?  [فَقُلْنَا يَٰٓـَٔادَمُ · links]
turns on: Whether the note explains the immediate narrative sequence or the lesson of its retelling.
- 20:117/q01/p1 The warning follows Iblis's refusal to bow and counsels Adam for his protection. — Bursevi says God addressed Adam immediately after the refusal; Alusi says it was solicitous counsel; Tabataba'i says it followed the refusal and benefited Adam. Asad calls it a warning more precisely than a commandment. Ibn Ashur places it after the creation and prostration accounts already told in al-Baqara and al-A'raf. [5 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 20:117/q01/p2 The retelling warns Adam's descendants through their ancestor's lapse and directs the deficient toward repentance. — Tabari says Adam's descendants generally follow his path except those God protects. Biqa'i says the sura first summarizes then details Adam's disobedience to show God's forbearance, power over rejecters, the danger of forbidden acts and the need for prompt repentance. [2 notes: TAB-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]

## 20:117/q02 Who is the enemy pointed out to Adam?  [إِنَّ هَٰذَا عَدُوٌّۭ · referent]
- 20:117/q02/p1 It is Iblis or Satan, whom Adam had just seen refuse to bow. — Tabari and Nasafi cite his refusal to prostrate and disobedience; Bursevi says the demonstrative points to the figure whose act Adam witnessed. Jishumi says his deception and whispering threaten the couple; Tabataba'i places the identification immediately after the refusal. [8 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI; JISHUMI; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 20:117/q03 Why is Iblis Adam's enemy?  [عَدُوٌّۭ لَّكَ وَلِزَوْجِكَ · interpretation]
turns on: The sources offer envy, ignorance, opposed origins, refusal, and Satan's banishment as explanations; Tabataba'i rejects the motives he attributes to Razi.
- 20:117/q03/p1 He resented Adam's favor and honor and envied him and his wife. — Razi, Bursevi and Alusi attribute the hostility to envy. Ibn Ashur says Satan thought himself more deserving of honor and envied the couple's knowledge of names, which enables thought, guidance and instruction (2:31). Islahi connects jealous refusal with Satan's challenge that humans deserved neither superiority nor Paradise, his respite and warning of Hell (7:14-22). Bursevi draws the general lesson that an envier works to ruin another's circumstances. [7 notes: RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; TABATABAI(الرازي)-]
- 20:117/q03/p2 Iblis was an ignorant elder hostile to a young scholar, Adam. — Razi invokes Adam's knowledge of names (2:31) and Iblis's ignorant claim of superiority by ancestry. Bursevi adds a Persian couplet contrasting sight of Adam's body with sight of the divine light entrusted to him, and the maxim that one is an enemy of what one does not know; Alusi repeats the ignorant-versus-learned explanation. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); TABATABAI(الرازي)-]
- 20:117/q03/p3 Their opposing origins produced inherited hostility: Iblis from fire and Adam from earth and water. — Razi and Bursevi contrast fire with Adam's water and earth; Alusi says Adam was made from clay and Hawwa from Adam. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); TABATABAI(الرازي)-]
- 20:117/q03/p4 His expulsion from divine proximity and curse after humanity's precedence caused his hostility. — Tabataba'i appeals to Satan's own speech (15:39): honor and prostration concerned humankind as a species, whose precedence led to Satan's banishment and curse (17:62). He dismisses Razi's proposed motives as specious. [2 notes: TABATABAI; TABATABAI+]
- 20:117/q03/p5 His refusal to prostrate showed that he did not accept Adam. — Tabari cites the refusal and disobedience (2:34); Nasafi says he did not accept Adam. [2 notes: TAB-FULL; NASAFI-FULL]

## 20:117/q04 What does the demonstrative 'this' contribute?  [هَٰذَا · rhetoric]
- 20:117/q04/p1 Pointing to Satan conveys contempt and makes the witnessed figure vivid. — Bursevi says Adam had just witnessed Satan's deed. Ibn Ashur compares the contemptuous demonstrative with the pagans' dismissive reference to the man criticizing their gods (21:36), and contrasts this verse with al-A'raf 7:22, which names Satan directly. [3 notes: BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 20:117/q05 Who is Adam's 'zawj,' and what does that word denote?  [زَوْجِكَ · referent]
- 20:117/q05/p1 Adam's zawj is Eve; the term denotes either member of a pair accompanied by another of its kind. — Thalabi, Baghawi, Ibn Kathir and Bursevi name Eve or Hawwa. Bursevi defines zawj for either sex. Bint al-Shati notes the Quran's use of zawj for Adam's spouse in 2:35, 7:19 and 20:117. [6 notes: THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; BINTSHATI-IJAZ]

## 20:117/q06 Why is the preposition repeated in 'to you and to your wife'?  [لَّكَ وَلِزَوْجِكَ · grammar]
- 20:117/q06/p1 The repeated lam is required to coordinate with a genitive pronoun and can also emphasize that Eve is independently Satan's enemy. — Alusi attributes the grammatical requirement to most grammarians and says obligation can coexist with Ibn Malik's contextual emphasis. Some whom he cites stress that enmity toward Eve is intrinsic, not merely derived from enmity toward Adam. Burhan reports both a strengthening lam and attachment to an implied predicate specifying the enemy as yours. [4 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); BURHAN]

## 20:117/q07 Why call Eve 'your wife' rather than naming her?  [وَلِزَوْجِكَ · rhetoric]
- 20:117/q07/p1 The relational name strengthens Adam's aversion to Satan and the warning. — Alusi says calling her Adam's wife rather than Hawwa intensifies the warning. Ibn Ashur says this verse, unlike al-A'raf's general statement (7:22), identifies Adam as the original target and Eve as the extension through her relation to him. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 20:117/q08 What does the warning against Satan expelling the couple require, and why is expulsion assigned to him?  [فَلَا يُخْرِجَنَّكُمَا مِنَ ٱلْجَنَّةِ · interpretation]
- 20:117/q08/p1 Adam and Eve must not heed Satan or put themselves within his power; his temptation causes the act leading to expulsion, though God effects it. — Tabari and Ibn Atiyya say obedience to Satan brings disobedience and expulsion. Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan and Study Quran identify accepting his temptation, the latter naming the forbidden tree. Kashshaf, Baydawi, Nasafi, Ibn Kathir, Tabataba'i and Alusi explain the prohibition indirectly as preventing Satan from becoming the cause; Tabataba'i adds vigilance against his scheme. Razi, Bursevi and Abu Hayyan distinguish Satan as cause from God as actual expeller. Samin says the surface prohibition addresses Iblis but intends Adam and Eve; Biqa'i says that form spares her direct prohibition and intensifies warning; Ibn Ashur says it pointedly forbids Adam being swayed, not merely letting news of his deed reach God. Alusi compares 7:2. Yahya says their disobedience would lead God to expel them. [24 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN]

## 20:117/q09 How does the fa connect the warning to the enemy statement?  [فَلَا يُخْرِجَنَّكُمَا · grammar]
- 20:117/q09/p1 It makes the prohibition follow from Satan's enmity or from God's disclosure of that enmity. — Alusi gives both possible antecedents; Ibn Ashur says an enemy seeks his opponent's harm, hence the need to beware. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 20:117/q10 What hardship does 'you will suffer' mean?  [فَتَشْقَىٰٓ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether shaqa names all earthly distress or especially earning sustenance; Alusi favors the broader scope, while others cite labor and the following verses.
- 20:117/q10/p1 It is the broad toil and distress of earthly life after the Garden, including the effort needed for food, clothing, drink and shelter. — Ibn Abbas via Wahidi calls it worldly toil and distress; al-Hasan says all descendants labor and no one escapes toil. Qushayri says both spouses face hardship. Alusi explicitly prefers this breadth over restriction to earning, despite support in 20:118-119. Tabataba'i says the following verses explain the necessities. Biqa'i says it is worldly, not ultimate misery, since Adam later entered the Garden and the consequence after fa cannot fail. Raghib, cited by Bursevi, distinguishes worldly and afterlife hardship and personal, bodily and external forms; al-Qamus gives shaqa as hardship and difficulty with a long final vowel. Asad renders it becoming unhappy and points to his note on the Garden. [19 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس,الحسن); JISHUMI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(المفسرون); QUSHAYRI; QURTUBI-FULL(الحسن); RAZI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(الراغب,القاموس); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 20:117/q10/p2 It is specifically the labor of earning food or a livelihood by one's own hands and sweat. — Muqatil contrasts effortless eating in the Garden with work afterward. Yahya reports work by hand and sweat of brow; Tabari, Thalabi, Baghawi, Nasafi, Ibn Kathir and Study Quran speak of earning by labor. Wahidi transmits Zayd ibn Ali's gloss; al-Farra via Qurtubi says eating from one's own work. Suddi specifies plowing, planting, kneading and baking; Baghawi's version adds harvesting and grinding. Baydawi cites 20:118-119 for the male livelihood reading. Study Quran cites 2:36 and 7:24-25 for life on earth. [22 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(بعضهم); TAB-FULL; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(السدي,زيد بن علي); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(السدي); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(فرقة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(المفسرون); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قول أول); SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); STUDYQURAN; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 20:117/q10/p3 It is bodily hardship assigned to Adam specifically, distinct from the shared expulsion. — Qurtubi reports this view and says the following account of Garden life clarifies bodily hardship. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]
- 20:117/q10/p4 It can be called unhappiness or wretchedness, contrasted with happiness. — Asad gives the literal sense 'so that thou wilt become unhappy' and refers to his Garden note; Raghib, quoted by Bursevi, contrasts hardship with happiness across worldly and afterlife categories. [2 notes: ASAD-NOTES; BURSEVI(الراغب)]

## 20:117/q11 What reports illustrate Adam's labor after descent?  [فَتَشْقَىٰٓ · reports]
turns on: The accounts differ on the ox's color and Adam's blaming Eve, and one account uses grain and a loaf instead.
- 20:117/q11/p1 A red ox or bull was sent to Adam; he plowed with it and wiped sweat from his brow. — Sa'id ibn Jubayr is named by Thalabi, Wahidi, Jishumi, Baghawi, Tabrisi, Ibn al-Jawzi and Qurtubi; Tabari names Sa'id. Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, Razi and Nasafi give the account without naming him. Abu Hayyan's Ibn Jubayr version says Adam ate by his hand's labor. Details vary between being given the animal and descending with it. [13 notes: TAB-FULL(سعيد); THALABI(سعيد بن جبير); WAHIDI-BASIT(سعيد بن جبير); JISHUMI(سعيد بن جبير); BAGHAWI-FULL(سعيد بن جبير); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(سعيد بن جبير); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(سعيد بن جبير); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(سعيد بن جبير); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن جبير)]
- 20:117/q11/p2 Adam worked an ox, recognized the warned toil, and blamed Eve; the blame recurred among descendants. — Sufyan's transmission from Sa'id ibn Jubayr in Durr mentions Adam's recognition, his blame of Eve, and descendants repeating that blame when working oxen; no color is supplied. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(سعيد بن جبير)]
- 20:117/q11/p3 Adam worked a piebald ox, recognized the toil, and blamed Hawwa. — Alusi attributes this to Sa'id ibn Jubayr and names Abd ibn Humayd, Ibn Asakir and others as transmitters; he states no grading. The color is piebald rather than the red of other reports. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(سعيد بن جبير)]
- 20:117/q11/p4 The toil consisted of repeated agricultural and food preparation tasks. — Suddi in Wahidi lists plowing, planting, kneading and baking; Baghawi's Suddi report adds harvesting and grinding. Ibn al-Jawzi attributes a comparable list to the exegetes. [3 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(السدي); BAGHAWI-FULL(السدي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(المفسرون)]
- 20:117/q11/p5 Gabriel gave Adam Garden grain, and a rolling loaf taught him that worldly food would require toil. — Qurtubi reports that Adam planted, harvested, processed and baked the grain; he chased the loaf when it rolled away. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]

## 20:117/q12 Why is 'you will suffer' singular after the dual warning?  [فَلَا يُخْرِجَنَّكُمَا فَتَشْقَىٰٓ · rhetoric]
turns on: Explanations appeal to Adam's role in the household, his status as addressee, shared consequences, verse cadence, courtesy, or bodily toil; Tabataba'i rejects the provider and cadence proposals.
- 20:117/q12/p1 Adam bears or represents the household's labor as its head and provider, so his hardship includes his wife's. — Mawardi, Qushayri, Jishumi, Baghawi, Tabrisi, Ibn al-Jawzi's Mawardi report, Qurtubi, Biqa'i and others connect the form to the man's work or maintenance. Kashshaf, Razi and Baydawi say a household head's hardship and happiness encompass the family. Abu Hayyan says household hardship follows Adam's; Ibn Ashur adds concision and says one spouse's suffering affects the other. Thalabi and Baghawi stress that Adam's toil was greater. Bursevi says it principally fell on him; Alusi says he is primary in household affairs. Tabataba'i rejects the provider explanation because the following verses also address Adam singularly, which that explanation would require to be dual. [26 notes: THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(فرقة); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الماوردي); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قول أول); SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); IBNASHUR-FULL; BURHAN; ITQAN; TABATABAI(قيل)-]
- 20:117/q12/p2 Adam is the initial, principal or direct addressee; naming his consequence suffices within an Arabic singular-for-dual construction. — Tabari and al-Farra compare the singular sitter of the two recording angels in 50:17. Ibn Qutayba and Burhan compare Pharaoh's address to Moses and Aaron naming Moses alone. Ibn Atiyya, cited also in Burhan and Itqan, calls Adam the first addressee and main subject. Tabataba'i says the covenant and speech were directed to Adam and the account shifts to dual only where both must be included. Ibn Ata instead says Adam knew the special rank of direct address and Eve followed him. Itqan observes Arabic singular for dual. [14 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; TAB-FULL; SULAMI(ابن عطاء); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء); IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الفراء); QURTUBI-FULL; BURHAN(ابن عطية); ITQAN(ابن عطية); TABATABAI]
- 20:117/q12/p3 The singular preserves the surrounding verse endings and cadence. — Thalabi, Qushayri, al-Farra via Wahidi, Jishumi, Baghawi, Kashshaf, Tabrisi, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Samin, Bursevi, Alusi and Ibn Ashur give this explanation, often alongside household reasons. Tabataba'i dismisses it. [14 notes: THALABI; QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(قيل); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(قيل)-]
- 20:117/q12/p4 Both spouses share the cause and effects, so the singular includes Eve and can extend to descendants. — Mawardi says the cause applies equally to both; Qushayri says God knew both would suffer. Jishumi and Tabrisi give the shared cause. Qurtubi says the singular intends the wife too. Sufyan says Eve entered with Adam and compares singular addresses to the Prophet including others in 65:1 and 66:1, extending the meaning to descendants. Ibn Ashur says one spouse's suffering affects the other. [8 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; JISHUMI; TABRISI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL; DURR-FULL(سفيان بن عيينة); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 20:117/q12/p5 The woman is courteously left unnamed in the singular consequence. — Burhan and Itqan report polite omission, the latter citing a saying that good manners conceal women. [2 notes: BURHAN; ITQAN]
- 20:117/q12/p6 Only Adam is assigned bodily hardship, though both are expelled. — Qurtubi reports that the following Garden descriptions clarify this distinction. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]

## 20:117/q13 What is the grammatical mood and force of 'fa-tashqa'?  [فَتَشْقَىٰٓ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the verb is the syntactic consequence of the prohibition or a fresh indicative prediction; Samin and Alusi reject the latter force.
- 20:117/q13/p1 It is subjunctive after an implied an in response to the prohibition, expressing its consequence. — Samin explicitly supplies the implied an. Abu Hayyan and Alusi allow this analysis; Alusi favors consequence and rejects mere prediction. Biqa'i says the predicate after fa cannot fail, so ultimate misery cannot be intended. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 20:117/q13/p2 It is indicative in a new sentence: 'then you will suffer.' — Abu Hayyan allows a separate prediction. Samin reports the Shaykh's proposal but considers it remote or impossible because the point is more than prediction; Alusi likewise rejects the predictive force. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ)-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 20:117/q14 What does the warning imply about the consequence of disobedience?  [فَلَا يُخْرِجَنَّكُمَا فَتَشْقَىٰٓ · theology]
- 20:117/q14/p1 The prohibition is advisory; disobedience brings natural earthly toil, not an afterlife penalty for a legislated sin. — Tabataba'i says the warning preceded religious legislation and explains the result as labor for human needs. Biqa'i argues that the threatened misery cannot be ultimate misery since Adam later entered the Garden and the consequence after fa is certain. [2 notes: TABATABAI; BIQAI-FULL]
- 20:117/q14/p2 Even a warned believer may fail to recall the enemy and later regret a settled decree. — Qushayri says Adam's faith, warning and certainty were unsurpassed, yet he failed to recall the enemy during the whispering, then regretted and wept when hardship came. Bursevi says awareness of the kinds of misery makes one recoil, while caution cannot defeat destiny. [3 notes: QUSHAYRI; BIQAI-FULL]

## 20:117/q15 What duty toward a wife is drawn from Adam's singular toil?  [فَتَشْقَىٰٓ · law]
- 20:117/q15/p1 A husband owes his wife maintenance, covering food, drink, clothing and housing. — Qurtubi derives the duty from Adam bearing the toil and lists the four necessities. Tabrisi and Biqa'i connect Adam's work to support and care for his wife; Biqa'i adds that an upright husband guides her to good while a corrupt one leads her to harm. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; TABRISI; BIQAI-FULL]

## 20:117/q16 How do the next verses explain this threatened hardship?  [فَتَشْقَىٰٓ · links]
- 20:117/q16/p1 Garden freedom from hunger, nakedness, thirst and sun exposure displays the food, clothing, drink and shelter that expulsion would cost. — Razi identifies these four protections as the explanation of misery. Biqa'i names satiety, drink, clothing and shelter as supports of sufficiency and says their negated opposites make Adam recoil from the cause while learning destiny's force. Tabataba'i and Baydawi cite 20:118-119 as support for the needs and effort involved; Qurtubi names the four maintenance necessities. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI; BAYDAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 20:117/q16/p2 The worldly hardship here leads into the later mention of hardship for one who rejects guidance. — Raghib, cited by Bursevi, connects the worldly sense here with the afterlife hardship mentioned in 20:123. [1 notes: BURSEVI(الراغب)]

## 20:117/q17 What practical lesson do the sources draw from warning about an enemy?  [إِنَّ هَٰذَا عَدُوٌّۭ لَّكَ وَلِزَوْجِكَ · inward]
- 20:117/q17/p1 Listening to an enemy brings regret after it can no longer help. — Qushayri draws this lesson and cites Adam's later regret and weeping despite unsurpassed faith and certainty; he says Adam did not recall the divine identification when Satan whispered. Bursevi generalizes that envy turns a person into an enemy bent on ruining another. [4 notes: QUSHAYRI; BURSEVI]
- 20:117/q17/p2 Adam concealed his enemy's hostility and failed to ask God for protection, so he listened to the enemy's speech and oath. — Al-Wasiti is reported by Sulami; his explanation ties the lapse to reliance on what the enemy said and swore. [1 notes: SULAMI(الواسطي)]

## 20:117/q18 How is this account connected with the other Quranic accounts of Adam and Satan?  [* · links]
- 20:117/q18/p1 It retells the warning, temptation and fall already narrated elsewhere, adapting the details and style to this sura. — Kuran Yolu compares earlier accounts in Sad and al-A'raf, including the conditional Garden stay, enticement and expulsion, citing 2:30 and 7:11. Study Quran points to 7:21-22 for the warning and 2:35-37 and 7:19-23 for deception, fall and remorse. Ibn Ashur says al-Baqara and al-A'raf already told creation and prostration and notes this verse's demonstrative versus naming Satan in 7:22; he also notes the fuller identification of Adam and Eve as targets here. Islahi draws on 7:14-22 for Satan's challenge, respite and the warning. [7 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 20:117/q19 What does the adjacent mention of Iblis's refusal mean lexically?  [* · meaning]
- 20:117/q19/p1 Iblis may derive from bewildered despair or be foreign; iba' is an especially strong refusal. — Bursevi quotes al-Qamus for both etymologies of Iblis, and al-Mufradat for the distinction that every iba' is refusal but not every refusal is iba'. These glosses explain the preceding refusal that prompts this warning. [2 notes: BURSEVI(القاموس,المفردات)]
