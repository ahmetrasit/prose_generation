# 26:181: 10 questions

## 26:181/q01 What does the command to fulfill the measure require?  [أَوْفُوا۟ ٱلْكَيْلَ · interpretation]
- 26:181/q01/p1 Give the full amount due, without shortfall, when measuring goods. — Muqātil, Yaḥyā b. Sallām, al-Ṭabarī, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Jishumī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, al-Bursawī, and Ibn ʿĀshūr explain fulfillment as complete measure and protection of people's rights. Al-Jishumī says īfāʾ is delivery of the entire due. Al-Kashshāf distinguishes complete, deficient, and excessive measures; only the complete amount is required. Al-Ṭabrisī extends the no-shortfall rule to weight, length, and counting. Ibn Kathīr requires full measure to buyers and in one's own purchases: give as one takes. Al-Biqāʿī similarly says sellers should give customers what they expect for themselves. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī identifies kayl as determination of goods by volume. [18 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; THALABI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 26:181/q02 Who are the loss-makers, and what does the word mean?  [ٱلْمُخْسِرِينَ · meaning]
- 26:181/q02/p1 They cause others loss by reducing their measure, weight, or due rights. — Yaḥyā b. Sallām also transmits al-Suddī's explanation that both measure and weight must not be reduced. Al-Wāḥidī reports the commentators' identification of those who reduce measure or weight, and Abū ʿUbayd's equivalence of khasartu and akhsartu the scale as making it deficient; he compares the short-measurers of 83:3 and says yukhsirūn is linguistically permissible. Al-Jishumī and al-Ṭabrisī explain mukhsir as one who reduces another's capital, the reverse of increasing it or making a profit. Ibn al-Jawzī glosses akhsartu the measure as making it deficient; al-Bursawī equates khasartuhu and akhsartuhu as reducing someone's due. Al-Baghawī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, and al-Ālūsī emphasize deprivation of people's rights. Ibn ʿĀshūr derives the short-measurer from the sense of causing another loss. Muqātil, al-Ṭabarī, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī's Wajīz, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Biqāʿī likewise describe cheating or diminished dues. [22 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيد,المفسرون); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 26:181/q03 Must one give more than the full measure?  [أَوْفُوا۟ ٱلْكَيْلَ · law]
- 26:181/q03/p1 No; excess is commendable generosity, while only the full due is required. — Al-Kashshāf distinguishes complete, deficient, and excessive measurement and infers from the verse's silence about excess that it is good but not required. Al-Nasafī repeats that full measure is commanded and short measure forbidden, while additional measure is commendable. Abū Ḥayyān says withholding such generosity is not blameworthy. [3 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 26:181/q04 What force does 'do not be among the loss-makers' add?  [وَلَا تَكُونُوا۟ مِنَ ٱلْمُخْسِرِينَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the construction emphasizes disowning a settled practice or should simply be read without a limiting implication.
- 26:181/q04/p1 It strengthens the ban by disowning the practice or settled company of short-measurers. — Al-Ālūsī allows that the emphatic construction intensifies the prohibition and says it reinforces the preceding command. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the formulation more forcefully repudiates their practice, comparing the wording in Noah's story at 26:116. Al-Biqāʿī reads it as warning against becoming an established class of short-changers: a passing impulse may be resisted and erased, while habitual cheating impoverishes one's religion. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 26:181/q04/p2 The wording carries no restrictive implication; it reinforces the command without limiting the ban to habitual offenders. — Al-Ālūsī gives this as an alternative, comparing the prohibition of doubled usury at 3:130, from which no permission for lesser usury is inferred. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 26:181/q05 How does the measure here relate to the following qistās command?  [ٱلْكَيْلَ · links]
turns on: Whether qistās denotes a weighing scale or volume measurement; the notes also differ over its linguistic origin.
- 26:181/q05/p1 Measure concerns volume; the straight qistās is a scale for just weighing. — Muqātil calls qistās a Roman word for balance. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī distinguishes full volume measurement from just weighing on a straight scale, applying fairness in giving and receiving. Al-Ālūsī says the following order to weigh concerns goods measured by weight. [3 notes: MUQATIL; TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 26:181/q05/p2 Qistās itself is measurement by volume. — Al-Jishumī rejects an association with Greek because the Qur'an is Arabic; he allows that the word is Arabized or shared across languages. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 26:181/q06 What wrongdoing of Shuayb's people does this injunction address?  [* · interpretation]
- 26:181/q06/p1 They cheated in commercial measurement and deprived people of property and rights. — Muqātil adds that Shuayb forbids denying rights and spreading corruption through sins. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says cheating scales and diminishing property was their named offense beyond disbelief. Ibn ʿĀshūr says they also shirked, undervalued goods, and spread corruption after the general call to worship at 26:177; their merchants colluded, leaving buyers compelled to deal on those terms. Iṣlāḥī portrays Midian's skilled traders as drawn into cheating weights through indifference to God and the Hereafter and Satan's influence. The Study Quran calls their practices corrupt and predatory, with parallels at 7:85 and 11:84–86, and reports al-Ṭabarī and al-Thaʿlabī on the ban on cheating in buying and selling. [7 notes: MUQATIL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN(Ṭ, Th)]

## 26:181/q07 What travel advice is reported about communities that measure fairly or cheat?  [* · reports]
- 26:181/q07/p1 Do not hurry to leave a land of fair measures, but quickly leave one where people cheat. — Ibn Abī Ḥātim transmits this counsel from Saʿīd b. al-Musayyib. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(سعيد بن المسيب)]

## 26:181/q08 What caused Shuayb's people's destruction in the reported account?  [* · reports]
- 26:181/q08/p1 They were destroyed for shaving off even tiny amounts, taking with a heavy measure and giving with a light one. — Ibn Abī Ḥātim transmits this report from Khalaf b. Ḥawshab. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(خلف بن حوشب)]

## 26:181/q09 What harm or moral principle explains the ban on cheating?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes stress different, compatible grounds: spiritual damage, violation of property rights, or wider economic and social harm.
- 26:181/q09/p1 Greed and neglect of God lead to cheating, which damages one's religion. — Al-Biqāʿī identifies greed for money as the underlying problem and says shortchanging others impoverishes one's religion, though a passing impulse can be resisted. Iṣlāḥī says Midian's traders became open to Satan's distortion of trade when they grew indifferent to God and the Hereafter. [3 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 26:181/q09/p2 Short measurement oppresses buyers and amounts to illicit taking of their property. — Ibn ʿĀshūr names short measure and weight as oppression and illicit consumption, compounded by merchants' collusion that forced buyers into these dealings. Al-Qurṭubī requires just weighing and delivery of people's due. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 26:181/q09/p3 Fraud spreads among traders, widening exploitation and threatening society's economic balance. — Iṣlāḥī calls fraudulent weighing a contagious economic disease drawing traders and businesses into broader deception. He says God's ordering of the world by balance guides justice across society and the economy, whose balance can collapse. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 26:181/q10 How does this injunction fit the surrounding prophetic narratives?  [* · links]
- 26:181/q10/p1 Shuayb turns from the general call to worship to detailed rules for transactions. — Ibn ʿĀshūr links the turn to the earlier call at 26:177 and to his people's shirk, cheating, undervaluing of goods, and corruption. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 26:181/q10/p2 The messengers' shared message is piety, obedience, exclusive worship, and no demand for payment. — Al-Qurṭubī explains the repeated prophetic answer through these common demands. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]
- 26:181/q10/p3 Shuayb's warning against economic misconduct parallels Lot's warning against moral misconduct and ends with the familiar fate and repeated verses. — Iṣlāḥī identifies Shuayb's people here as Midian and places their persistence, destruction, and the closing repeated verses in the sūra's pattern of nations. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
