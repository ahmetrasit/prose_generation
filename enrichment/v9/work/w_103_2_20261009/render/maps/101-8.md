# 101:8: 15 questions

## 101:8/q01 What are the mawāzīn, and what is weighed?  [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether mawāzīn names an actual instrument, weighty deeds, or a figure for judgment, and whether deeds themselves can be weighed.
- 101:8/q01/p1 A real balance weighs deeds or their written records at the Resurrection. — Ibn Atiyya attributes a balance with a beam to most scholars, jurists, and hadith scholars, saying its familiar form makes divine judgment intelligible and trusted. Ibn Abbas in Razi describes a tongue and two pans: beautiful obedient deeds outweigh and lead to Paradise, whereas ugly unbelievers' deeds are light and lead to Fire; al-Hasan affirms two pans without further description. Qurtubi specifies written records of good and bad deeds on a pan and tongue and refers to earlier discussions. Study Quran distinguishes Qur'anic balance as revelation and just order (6:152, 7:85, 11:84–85, 42:17, 55:7–9, 57:25) from scales for deeds at Resurrection (21:47). Islahi calls it a just balance of deeds. [7 notes: IBNATIYYA(جمهور العلماء والفقهاء والمحدثين); RAZI(ابن عباس,الحسن); QURTUBI-FULL; STUDYQURAN; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; TABATABAI]
- 101:8/q01/p2 Mawāzīn are deeds that possess weight and worth before God. — Al-Farra in Razi treats mawāzīn as a plural of mawzūn and compares ordinary expressions about one thing being the measure or weight of another. Kashshaf gives this as an alternative to weighing scales. Tabatabai assigns weight and standing to faith and obedience, and none to disbelief and sins; Qutb speaks of values with no weight in God's assessment. [4 notes: RAZI(الفراء); KASHSHAF-FULL; TABATABAI; QUTB-ZILAL]
- 101:8/q01/p3 The scale is a metaphor for divine justice. — Mujahid in Ibn Atiyya denies a literal balance because the scale is the fairest familiar measure. The representative form of weighing conveys just judgment in Kuranyolu's account. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA(مجاهد); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 101:8/q01/p4 The balances are proofs and arguments. — Abd al-Aziz ibn Yahya in Qurtubi cites a poet speaking of a balance for every disputant. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(عبد العزيز بن يحيى)]
- 101:8/q01/p5 What is weighed may be records or visible representations of deeds, since vanished deeds cannot themselves be weighed. — The theologians reported by Razi object that deeds, especially after ceasing to exist, cannot be weighed. Their alternatives are weighing records, light and darkness denoting good and evil, or records embodied in beautiful and hideous forms, publicly disclosing each person's standing. [1 notes: RAZI(المتكلمون)]
- 101:8/q01/p6 The actual manner of weighing is beyond specification from revelation. — Kuranyolu says afterlife events are unseen and one cannot speculate beyond what revelation states about how deeds are weighed or measured. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 101:8/q02 Why is mawāzīn plural and assigned to a person?  [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the grammatical plural reflects multiple objects, multiple instruments, or individual experiences despite one instrument.
- 101:8/q02/p1 The plural covers the many different things belonging to a person that are weighed. — Ibn Atiyya explicitly explains the plural and the possessive in this way. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA]
- 101:8/q02/p2 There are multiple balances. — Qurtubi reports this interpretation and cites a poet's line that every event has its balance. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]
- 101:8/q02/p3 A single balance, held by Gabriel, is expressed in the plural. — Qurtubi reports the one-balance interpretation; Study Quran says most hold that one scale weighs all deeds despite the plural. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(Āl)]
- 101:8/q02/p4 The plural evokes each person's experience of weighing. — Study Quran reports this suggestion while noting the majority view of a single scale. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(Āl)]

## 101:8/q03 What makes a person's scales light?  [خَفَّتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether lightness describes the balance of bad against good, scarcity or loss of worthy good, following falsehood, or intrinsic lack of divine weight.
- 101:8/q03/p1 Bad deeds outweigh good deeds. — Wahidi, Baghawi, Razi, Ibn Kathir, Biqai, Bursevi, Alusi, Elmalili, Islahi, and Kuranyolu give the comparative account; Razi and Kuranyolu add that good deeds are few. Biqai calls the scales light or unsteady. Bursevi says both groups sin, but the blessed have fewer, outweighed sins; he cites the Prophet's instruction to Ali to follow a bad deed with a good one and God's forgiving name. Islahi says this outcome leads to Hell under God's just balance. [20 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 101:8/q03/p2 The person followed light falsehood instead of weighty truth. — Muqatil calls truth heavy and wholesome and falsehood light and vile; Abu Bakr in Wahidi, Kashshaf, and Razi relates their weight to following them in worldly life and says a scale holding only truth should be heavy and one holding falsehood light. Nasafi and Biqai attribute lightness to following falsehood; Biqai adds it felt light in worldly life. Islahi says only truth, meaning good deeds, has weight, and connects loss to denial of God's revelations (7:8–9). [14 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو بكر,مقاتل); KASHSHAF-FULL(أبو بكر); RAZI(أبو بكر,مقاتل); RAZI-FULL(أبو بكر); NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 101:8/q03/p3 There is no good deed of consequential weight. — Baydawi, Biqai, Bursevi, Alusi, and Elmalili give this as an alternative to bad deeds outweighing good; Biqai links the lack to following falsehood. Kuranyolu allows few or no good acts. Qutb says values without weight have no standing in God's judgment. [10 notes: BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; QUTB-ZILAL]
- 101:8/q03/p4 Good deeds and obedience are few or scarce. — Tabari says the good deeds weigh little; Tabrisi says few good deeds and little obedience. Ibn Atiyya says faith and deeds make a balance heavy and absence or scarcity makes it light. Razi and Kuranyolu combine few good deeds with wrongs outweighing them. [7 notes: TAB; IBNATIYYA; TABRISI; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 101:8/q03/p5 Shirk or corrupt belief nullifies the good deeds, leaving nothing weighty. — Muqatil says shirk's good deeds turn to wind-scattered ash; shirk is the foulest yet lightest thing in the balance. Islahi says any good deeds are voided by ill intent and heretical belief, while evil deeds carry no weight. [2 notes: MUQATIL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 101:8/q03/p6 Disbelief and sins have no weight before God, unlike faith and obedience. — Tabatabai says deeds are weighed by their standing with God; faith and obedience have weight and bring felicity, while disbelief and sins lack it and bring misery. Qutb describes the light scales as values without weight in divine assessment. [2 notes: TABATABAI; QUTB-ZILAL]

## 101:8/q04 Whose scales can be ruinously light?  [مَنْ خَفَّتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · referent]
turns on: Whether the verse identifies unbelief as the condition of ruin or states a general bad-over-good criterion.
- 101:8/q04/p1 The disbelievers, especially those whose shirk or denial voids their good deeds. — Qushayri identifies those light in obedience as disbelievers; Muqatil names shirk and its wind-scattered good deeds; Islahi links the loss to denying revelations and to ill intent and heretical belief. Ibn Atiyya says a believer's balance is never disastrously light, a point repeated in Ibn Atiyya Full. [6 notes: MUQATIL; QUSHAYRI; IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL(المؤمن); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 101:8/q04/p2 Anyone whose bad deeds exceed their good deeds is in the light-scale group. — The general descriptions by Wahidi, Baghawi, Razi, Ibn Kathir, Bursevi, Alusi, and others state the bad-over-good criterion without restricting the wording to a named people. Bursevi says both groups commit sins, though in the blessed group sins are fewer and outweighed. [10 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 101:8/q05 How does the singular man refer to those being weighed?  [مَنْ · grammar]
- 101:8/q05/p1 The singular relative expression denotes a plural group. — Islahi explicitly notes the singular man in the two scale descriptions has plural reference. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 101:8/q06 What outcome follows light scales?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the following image emphasizes the abiding destination or the manner of falling into it.
- 101:8/q06/p1 The person's abode or destination is Hawiya, the Fire or Hell. — Tabari says the person of scant good deeds has Hawiya as abode. Thalabi, Qushayri, Qatada in Tabari and Durr, and Kuranyolu identify the destination as Hell or Fire; Durr's versions call it abode, mother, destination, and master. Biqai describes a very low fiery abyss; Islahi says the next verse names it blazing fire and calls the punishment eternal. [11 notes: TAB(قتادة); THALABI; QUSHAYRI; BIQAI-FULL; DURR(قتادة); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 101:8/q06/p2 They plunge headfirst into Hell. — Tabari reports Abu Salih and Qatada saying they fall headfirst into Fire, and Tabari himself includes that descent; Thalabi attributes the head interpretation to Qatada and Abu Salih. Ikrima in Mawardi identifies the mother as the head and cites a poem addressed to Amr. [6 notes: TAB(أبو صالح,قتادة); THALABI(بعضهم); MAWARDI(عكرمة)]

## 101:8/q07 Why is the following Hawiya called the person's mother?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether mother names a sheltering destination, the head of the falling person, or an idiom of calamity.
- 101:8/q07/p1 Hell is the person's refuge and return, like a child's mother or one's home. — Ibn Zayd in Tabari and Mawardi says Hell is refuge and return, citing 3:151 and Umayya ibn Abi al-Salt's poem; Ibn Abbas says it is the only home and shelters the person as a woman shelters her child. Biqai likens the shelter to a mother or the earth to which one returns; Qatada in Durr calls it abode, mother, destination, and master. Tabari records a report from al-Ashath about believers' spirits saying the dead were taken to their mother, Hawiya. [9 notes: TAB(ابن زيد,ابن عباس,الأشعث بن عبد الله الأعمى); MAWARDI(ابن زيد); BIQAI-FULL; DURR(قتادة)]
- 101:8/q07/p2 Mother means the person's head, upon which the person falls. — Thalabi reports this interpretation from some, attributing its adoption to Qatada and Abu Salih. Ikrima in Mawardi describes the fall on the head and supports the image with a poem addressed to Amr. [3 notes: THALABI(بعضهم); MAWARDI(عكرمة)]
- 101:8/q07/p3 It is a familiar Arabic exclamation for someone in severe trouble. — Qatada in Tabari and Thalabi explains hāwat ummuh or hawat ummuh as idiomatic speech about a person in dire predicament; Tabari also reports the wording hāwat ummuh directly. [3 notes: TAB(قتادة); THALABI(قتادة)]

## 101:8/q08 Why is the Fire called Hawiya?  [* · meaning]
- 101:8/q08/p1 It is an abyss into which the person falls through deep reaches. — Mawardi derives the name from falling into it despite its deep abyss. Biqai depicts the sinner continually falling through its depths and describes it as very low and fiery. [4 notes: MAWARDI; BIQAI-FULL]

## 101:8/q09 What reports illustrate the weighing and its result?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports describe different illustrations or qualifications of the weighing, rather than competing versions of one event.
- 101:8/q09/p1 An angel weighs each person's deeds and announces lasting felicity for a heavy scale or lasting misery for a light one. — Thalabi transmits this from Anas ibn Malik, including the public proclamation. [1 notes: THALABI(أنس بن مالك)]
- 101:8/q09/p2 A margin of one good deed brings Paradise, and a margin of one bad deed brings Hell. — Bursevi transmits this statement from Ibn Masud. [1 notes: BURSEVI(ابن مسعود)]
- 101:8/q09/p3 A huge, tall, voracious man can weigh no more than a gnat's wing before God. — Abd al-Razzaq reports this saying of Ubayd ibn Umayr. [1 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(عبيد بن عمير)]
- 101:8/q09/p4 No thing put in the Balance weighs more than good character. — Study Quran reports this hadith. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]
- 101:8/q09/p5 Deeds are judged by intentions and each person receives what was intended. — Study Quran reports the Prophet's hadith. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(the Prophet)]
- 101:8/q09/p6 No one, even the Prophet, enters Paradise by deeds alone, but through God's mercy. — Study Quran quotes the Prophet's answer that only God's mercy would shelter him. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(the Prophet)]

## 101:8/q10 What principles govern the moral weight and recompense of deeds?  [* · theology]
- 101:8/q10/p1 Faithful obedience and good deeds have standing; disbelief and sins have none. — Tabatabai says faith and obedience bear weight before God and lead to felicity, while disbelief and sins lack weight and lead to misery. Islahi makes truth or good deeds alone weighty and attributes the light scale to denial of revelations (7:8–9). [2 notes: TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 101:8/q10/p2 Good deeds can erase evil and are rewarded tenfold, while evil is repaid only in kind. — Study Quran cites 41:34 and 11:114 for overcoming and erasing evil and 6:160 for the unequal recompense. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]
- 101:8/q10/p3 Only deeds help at judgment, and the just balance calls for responsible belief and conduct. — Islahi says God weighs deeds in a just balance, with eternal bliss or Hell according to which side prevails. Kuranyolu calls the scene illustrative imagery that teaches just judgment and responsible conduct and belief. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 101:8/q10/p4 A deed's intention affects its value. — Study Quran reports the Prophet's saying that deeds are judged by intentions; Islahi says ill intent and heretical belief nullify otherwise good deeds. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN(the Prophet); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 101:8/q10/p5 Divine mercy is needed for entry to Paradise even when deeds are weighed. — Study Quran reports the Prophet saying that no one enters through deeds alone, including himself; only God's mercy shelters him. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(the Prophet)]

## 101:8/q11 What inward meaning is given to light and heavy scales?  [خَفَّتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · inward]
turns on: The inward notes apply weight to spiritual ascent and lasting traits and lightness to veiling, appetite, and descent.
- 101:8/q11/p1 Spiritual ascent and enduring righteousness are heavy; descent into perishable sensual pleasures is light. — Qashani says this pattern rules out his earlier annihilation reading, for God's scale counts rising and ascending as weight and descent as lightness. Bursevi also says God's scale reverses ordinary scales: enduring good works rise, whereas passing sensual pleasures have no value. [2 notes: QASHANI; BURSEVI]
- 101:8/q11/p2 Light scales are psychic vices and evil deeds, ending in the hell of bodily nature and spiritual veiling. — Qashani makes bodily nature's abyss the owner's home. The Najmi interpretations cited by Bursevi describe a hot fire of ignorance, blindness, ego, passion, Satan, and the world. [2 notes: QASHANI; BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]
- 101:8/q11/p3 Godly attributes and character bring rest from the consequences of those qualities. — Bursevi reports this from the Najmi interpretations for the person whose weighed qualities are divine attributes and godly traits. [1 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]

## 101:8/q12 How does the verse fit the surrounding passage and sūra?  [* · links]
- 101:8/q12/p1 The Resurrection scene divides humanity into heavy and light scale groups with opposed outcomes. — Razi says God divides people after describing Resurrection; Alusi says the passage first describes what all share and then each group's state. Study Quran says the final six verses contrast good-deed-heavy and light scales. Tabatabai says the Meccan sura warns and promises about resurrection, with warning dominant. [4 notes: RAZI; ALUSI; STUDYQURAN; TABATABAI]
- 101:8/q12/p2 The ending with the wretched prepares the next sūra's warning about the cause of misery. — Biqai says al-Qaria first establishes the Hour and divides happy and wretched, ends with the wretched, and then the next sura opens with what caused that misery (101:1, 101:8–9; 102:1). [1 notes: BIQAI]
- 101:8/q12/p3 The passage uses a teasing question and delayed explanation to create rhetorical tension. — Corpus Coranicum compares the teaching questions of 101:1–11 with 90:12–16, 97:2–4, and 104:5–9, and with oath sequences such as Q 100. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 101:8/q12/p4 The opposed destinies are expressed with antithetical omitted clauses. — Biqai points to antithetical ellipsis in the clauses around the sinner's refuge. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 101:8/q13 What other Qur'anic passages are linked to this verse's weighing and outcome?  [* · links]
- 101:8/q13/p1 The light scales and abyss are paralleled in 7:9 and 23:103. — Study Quran names both parallels; Islahi cites 7:8–9 for the balance in which truth has weight and deniers lose. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 101:8/q13/p2 Earlier passages explain the weighing, and 69:21 describes the pleasing life of the other group. — Abu Hayyan points back to discussion at 7:8 and to Sura al-Haqqa's pleasing life at 69:21. Tabatabai likewise says the scale has been explained under earlier suras. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 101:8/q14 When was the sūra containing this verse revealed?  [* · sciences]
- 101:8/q14/p1 It is a Meccan sūra. — Tabatabai describes its Meccan setting and its dominant resurrection warning; Asad calls it early Meccan and tentatively places it after al-Tin (sura 95). [2 notes: TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES]
- 101:8/q14/p2 It was probably revealed after Sūrat al-Tīn. — Asad gives this tentative placement for the early Meccan sura, citing sura 95. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES]

## 101:8/q15 What does the omitted adjective imply about the scales' weight?  [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · rhetoric]
- 101:8/q15/p1 The implied weight is one of value; omitting an adjective magnifies the noun. — Burhan says omission of an adjective often magnifies an indefinite noun and that the implied weight here is one of value. [1 notes: BURHAN+]
