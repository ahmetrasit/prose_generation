# 103:2/q01 What is the grammatical answer to the oath? [إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَفِى خُسْرٍ · grammar]
- 103:2/q01/p1 The declaration that humanity is in loss answers the oath by time.
  reasons: Jishumi identifies the oath wāw and this clause as its answer; Ibn al-Jawzi reports al-Zajjaj; Study Quran takes verses 2–3 as the answer and compares 95:5–6; Corpus Coranicum describes an opening oath followed by rebuke. Al-Suyuti classifies al-ʿAṣr as a Qur'anic oath followed by a proposition about human action or condition: humanity is in loss. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the oath and emphatic particle strengthen the warning that loss envelops most people.

# 103:2/q02 Whom does al-insān designate? [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · referent]
turns on: Whether the definite singular is generic, as the following exception suggests, or points to a particular unbeliever or occasion.
- 103:2/q02/p1 Humanity as a whole, with believers subsequently excepted.
  reasons: Kaʿb includes Adam and his descendants; Abu Ubayda and al-Zajjaj call it a generic noun. The following exception proves collective scope for Jishumi, Razi, Samin, Elmalili and others. Bint al-Shati says limiting it to named Meccans would make that exception fail. Alusi says named losers are examples, not exclusive. Study Quran allows a historical emphasis on humanity's final phase; Elmalili especially includes the last era. Al-Zarkashi and al-Suyuti say the definite singular means every human; the following exception establishes its collective scope. Al-Zarkashi adds that replacing the generic article with 'all' preserves the sense. Ibn Ashur cites the explicit believer exception as a case of the Qur'an qualifying a broad proposition precisely. Ibn ʿĀshūr applies the comprehensive generic to people living when Islam appeared, especially those its call reached worldwide; the believer exception leaves the judgment true of others. Qurtubi calls the generic reading sound on account of the exception.

# 103:2/q03 How can singular al-insān be followed by an exception for many believers? [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · grammar]
- 103:2/q03/p1 A singular generic noun denotes the human collective.
  reasons: Abu Ubayda's Majaz compares 70:19–22, 69:47 and a line of al-Nabigha; Tabari reports the same construction elsewhere. Qurtubi compares generic al-nafs and its exceptions in a legal verse. Abu Hayyan and Samin infer generic scope from the exception. Al-Zarkashi and al-Suyuti explicitly infer collective reference from the believer exception; al-Zarkashi adds al-Hajjaj's sermon in which each hearer was addressed as 'O human,' and distinguishes a mentally conceived genus from its external instances (Ibn Babshadh).
