# 103:2/q10 How is the oath by time related to the human loss that follows? [إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَفِى خُسْرٍ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the oath evokes life's finite capital, the late hour and reckoning, or time's warning events.
- 103:2/q10/p1 Time is the valuable, vanishing opportunity in which human gain or loss is determined.
  reasons: Elmalili says time contains changes, wonders, blessing, harm and the chance to act; its passing consumes life unless used for faith and reform. He says the oath honors time rather than blaming it, and its senses of restraint and bestowal evoke both loss and gain. Kuranyolu similarly says God swears by precious time; Islahi relates it to responsibility for life's blessing. Baydawi says the oath indirectly denies that time causes loss; Alusi places responsibility on people amid time's blessings and opposites, while calling an identification of ʿasr with dahr unclear. Biqai associates ʿasr with time's essence and the extraction of essences.
  holders (14 notes): ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN; BAYDAWI; ALUSI; BIQAI
  notes: ELMALILI:103:1-3#2/r1 ELMALILI:103:1-3#2/r5 ELMALILI:103:1-3#2/r6 ELMALILI:103:1-3#2/r10 ELMALILI:103:1-3#2/r12 ELMALILI:103:1-3#2/r13 ELMALILI:103:1-3#3/r4 ELMALILI:103:1-3#4/r9 KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:103:1/r6 ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p433/r1 STUDYQURAN:103:intro/r3 BAYDAWI:103:1/r4 ALUSI:103:1/r19 BIQAI:103:2/r1
- 103:2/q10/p2 Late afternoon evokes the closing human era or the end of life and the reckoning of what was earned.
  reasons: Elmalili evokes creation late in the world's day, the era of Muhammad and the sorrow of returning without gain. Study Quran reads humanity in a final historical phase. Robinson relates the afternoon to merchants counting daily takings; Corpus Coranicum supports this through Q 104's contrast of wealth and damnation.
  holders (5 notes): ELMALILI; STUDYQURAN; CORPUSCORANICUM; CORPUSCORANICUM(Robinson)+
  notes: ELMALILI:103:1-3/r13 ELMALILI:103:1-3#4/r9 STUDYQURAN:103:2/r3 CORPUSCORANICUM:103:1-3:kommentar/r4 CORPUSCORANICUM:103:1-3:kommentar/r5
- 103:2/q10/p3 Time's calamities and transformations warn that human loss continues to its end.
  reasons: Elmalili explains Ali's interpretive dehr recitation as recurrent changes and calamities, while another view sees time's wonders as signs of the Creator's power. Tabari and Mawardi report Ali's reading of the oath as time's vicissitudes.
  holders (5 notes): ELMALILI; TAB(علي); MAWARDI(علي); ALUSI

- 103:2/q10/p4 An oath-to-assertion link should be tested rather than inferred from analogy alone.
  reasons: Bint al-Shati questions proposed links elsewhere: between the greatness of night and day and differing human striving, and between the falling star (53:1) and the Prophet's freedom from error. This note offers a methodological caution, not a specific alternative account of al-ʿAsr.

# 103:2/q11 What is the verse's relation to nearby sūras and other passages? [* · links]
turns on: Which nearby warning or parallel supplies the strongest context for the verse.
- 103:2/q11/p1 It follows al-Takāthur's warning that worldly rivalry wastes life and obscures future accountability.
  reasons: Abu Hayyan links 102:1,3 to distinction between believers and unbelievers. Ibn al-Zubayr, as quoted by Biqai, says accumulation narrows sight to the present; Biqai also connects human ignorance, ingratitude and imagined immortality across 100:6, 103:2 and 104:3. Elmalili, Islahi and Study Quran likewise link 102:1–2,8 with time's squandered blessing. Ibn al-Zubayr also connects the exception to those whose trade does not distract them in 24:37.

# 103:2/q12 What does the sūra as a whole teach through this declaration of loss and its exception? [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the focus is the sūra's comprehensive program or the function of its opening contrast.
- 103:2/q12/p1 It concisely teaches the path of individual and communal salvation.
  reasons: Shafiʿi is repeatedly reported to say reflection on this sūra would suffice; another report says it contains all Qur'anic sciences. Biqai maps its clauses onto knowledge of God and afterlife, worship, obstacles and patience, invoking Ghazali's six Qur'anic themes and a basmala contrast of transient creation and God's enduring mercy. Islahi calls it a condensed guide to personal and collective life; Kuranyolu and Qutb see the full duties and community identity in its brevity.
