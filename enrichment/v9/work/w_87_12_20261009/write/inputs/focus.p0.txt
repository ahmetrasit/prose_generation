# 87:12/q01 Who is the one destined for the Fire? [ٱلَّذِى · referent]
turns on: Whether the description is general, tied to a reported occasion, grounded in prior knowledge, or read as an inward spiritual condition.
- 87:12/q01/p1 The person who evades the reminder through contempt, obstinacy, or disbelief; the wording applies generally.
  reasons: Qatāda (TAB-FULL) contrasts God-fearing rememberers with one who disdains both the reminder and its people. Rāzī says the wording remains general even if an occasion involving named men is reported, and the sequence is rationally established. Qutb and Iṣlāḥī say the messenger reminds while people's reception determines their outcome; Iṣlāḥī says evaders take worldly life as all there is and do not fear the Hereafter, and their rejection reflects their own condition rather than any flaw in the message or preacher. Bursawī cites a sage's signs of wretchedness: gluttony, excessive sleep, persistent sin, hardness of heart, forgetting God, and facing the Almighty.
  holders (9 notes): TAB-FULL(قتادة); BURSEVI(بعض الحكماء); QUTB-ZILAL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNKATHIR; RAZI+

- 87:12/q01/p2 It was revealed concerning al-Walīd, ʿUtba, and Abī.
  reasons: Rāzī reports this attribution, while insisting that the wording has general application.
  holders (1 notes): RAZI
  notes: RAZI:87:12/r4
- 87:12/q01/p3 The person God already knew to be wretched.
  reasons: Muqātil grounds the fate in God's prior knowledge.
  holders (1 notes): MUQATIL

- 87:12/q01/p4 Inwardly, the most wretched is veiled from the Lord, unprepared, and spiritually distant.
  reasons: Qāshānī contrasts this person with one who has readiness but whose readiness has lapsed and who is veiled by the self's dark qualities.

# 87:12/q02 Does the most wretched person imply a less wretched group? [ٱلَّذِى · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the superlative entails a real comparison, or can function without a lesser counterpart.
- 87:12/q02/p1 Yes: the obstinate are most wretched, while the hesitant have some misery and the knowledgeable are happy.
  reasons: Rāzī raises the question posed by contrasting this person with those who remember and fear, then prefers a threefold distinction between knowers, the hesitant, and the obstinate who ignore the call (87:11).
  holders (2 notes): RAZI; RAZI+
  notes: RAZI:87:12/r5 RAZI:87:12/r9
- 87:12/q02/p2 No: the superlative can name the wretched without implying an actual lesser rival.
  reasons: Rāzī reports an explanation from 25:24, where Paradise's people are called better settled and rested without a required rival; another takes 'most wretched' simply as 'wretched,' as 'easier' means 'easy' in 30:27. An unnamed poet's 'more honored' and 'taller' likewise show superlatives without implied rivals.
  holders (3 notes): RAZI(شاعر غير مسمى)
  notes: RAZI:87:12/r6 RAZI:87:12/r7 RAZI:87:12/r8
sources: RAZI = Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, d. 606

# 87:12/q03 What does his burning in the Fire involve? [يَصْلَى ٱلنَّارَ · interpretation]
- 87:12/q03/p1 He enters and endures the Fire, suffering fully in both body and heart.
  reasons: Al-Ṭabarī calls it entry into Hell and emphasizes its heat and pain; al-Wāḥidī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, and Qutb describe the condemned one's entry or afterlife outcome. Al-Biqāʿī says the fire is endured directly and completely, by heart and body; his full account connects this to hard-hearted ignorance, blind imitation, or arrogant defiance.

# 87:12/q05 How is the lām in 'yasla' pronounced here? [يَصْلَى · readings]
- 87:12/q05/p1 It is pronounced emphatically here, while some readers soften lām at verse endings.
  reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī cites the preferred distinction: a preceding trigger calls for emphatic lām away from verse endings, while final lām is softened for euphony. He lists this verse's yasla al-nar among non-final examples and contrasts final examples in 75:31, 87:15, and 96:10; the cited set also includes 2:125, 17:18, 92:15, 84:12, 88:4, and 111:3.

  notes: NASHR:v2p112/r6 NASHR:v2p112/r7

# 87:12/q06 What is the greatest Fire, and what makes it greatest? [ٱلنَّارَ ٱلْكُبْرَىٰ · referent]
turns on: Whether 'greatest' compares the Hereafter with worldly fire, selects Hell's lowest layer or graded torments within Hell, or denotes spiritual estrangement.
- 87:12/q06/p1 It is Hell or the Hereafter's Fire, greater than the fire of this world.
  reasons: Yaḥyā ibn Sallām and al-Ḥasan explicitly contrast Hell or the afterlife fire with worldly fire; al-Zamakhsharī likewise calls worldly fire the lesser fire. ʿAṭāʾ calls it immense and terrifying; Muqātil, al-Baghawī, and al-Ṭabarī emphasize greater heat, severity, and pain. Bayḍāwī, Bursawī, and Ālūsī cite the report making earthly fire one seventieth of Hellfire, while Ālūsī also cites an Aḥmad version of one hundredth and suggests that seventy may express abundance. Al-Biqāʿī says the afterlife fire is seventy degrees greater than the intermediate realm's and worldly fire; Bursawī prefers the greater punishment of the Hereafter over worldly and barzakh punishments. Qutb adds intensity, duration, vastness, and a long stay. Ibn ʿĀshūr says 'greatest' magnifies terror and threat; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī prefers this apparent meaning. Al-Nasafī identifies Hell explicitly. Full and abbreviated sources repeat these grounds.

- 87:12/q06/p2 It is Hell's lowest and most severe level, assigned to unbelievers.
  reasons: Al-Farrāʾ identifies the lowest layer; al-Māwardī's two versions contrast its disbelievers' fire with the upper layer for sinful believers.
