# 96:3/q01 Why is the command to read repeated? [ٱقْرَأْ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the repetition is emphasis, distinguishes audiences or settings, or directly answers the Prophet's inability to read.
- 96:3/q01/p1 The second command reinforces the first, stresses reading, and prepares the declaration of divine generosity.
  reasons: Al-Wāḥidī says repetition establishes certainty; al-Baghawī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ālūsī, Ibn ʿĀshūr, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ describe emphasis. Bursevī and al-Ālūsī stress its obligation; Elmalılı says practice develops recitation and also permits dictation or recitation for others. Kur'an Yolu links the repetition to the importance of reading and knowledge, by which people are distinguished, citing 2:31. Al-Biqāʿī adds reassurance, calming fear and the shift from gradual creation to origination without causes. Ibn ʿĀshūr says Gabriel repeated God's wording to accustom Muhammad to reading. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ prefers this immediate context; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says public recitation is not apparent from the wording.

- 96:3/q01/p2 The first command is for personal or unrestricted recitation, and the second for proclaiming to others.

Rāzī and al-Ālūsī also transmit an unrestricted first reading; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects the private/public distinction as unsupported by the wording. Al-Wāḥidī's and Elmalılı's accounts of reciting for others overlap this purpose.

# 96:3/q02 What is the Prophet commanded to read, and how does the command relate to God's name? [ٱقْرَأْ · interpretation]
turns on: The omitted object of 'read' and which verb receives the name phrase; adjacency is al-Ālūsī's stated syntactic test.
- 96:3/q02/p1 He is to read the revelation or Qur'an given to him, beginning in his Lord's name.
  reasons: Al-Naḥḥās supplies the omitted object 'what was revealed to you' and says God will not withhold its reward. Al-Jishumī names the Qur'an. Al-Ālūsī and Qatādah say to begin with God's name; Qatādah says omitting the basmala or using an idol's name would fail the command. Al-Ālūsī prefers attaching 'in your Lord's name' to the adjacent first verb: placing the verb first foregrounds recitation in the opening sūra, while fronting the name would presuppose reading. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says revelation then specifies what is recited.
  holders (10 notes): NAHHAS; JISHUMI; ALUSI(قتادة); ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+; ALUSI+; BINTSHATI+
  notes: NAHHAS:v5p261/r7 JISHUMI:v10p7477/r11 ALUSI:96:3/r4 ALUSI:96:5/r13 ALUSI:96:5/r14 ALUSI:96:5/r15 ALUSI-FULL:v30p180#2/r1 ALUSI-FULL:v30p180#4/r5 ALUSI-FULL:v30p180#4/r6 BINTSHATI:v2p20/r4
- 96:3/q02/p2 The first command concerns God's name and the second the Qur'an.
  reasons: Al-Jishumī reports this alternative, distinguishing the content of the two commands.
  holders (1 notes): JISHUMI
  notes: JISHUMI:v10p7477/r11
- 96:3/q02/p3 The name phrase attaches to the second command, while the first establishes recitation itself.
  reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports an analysis that gives 'in God's name' its customary attachment, makes the first recite introduce reading, and connects 'in your Lord's name' with the second command. Its proponents answer that the first command already orders recitation.
  holders (2 notes): ALUSI(بعضهم، من علق الجار بالثاني)
  notes: ALUSI:96:5/r12 ALUSI:96:5/r16
- 96:3/q02/p4 The basmala's 'in the name' attaches to the first command and 'in your Lord's name' to the second, specifying the opening name.
  reasons: Al-Ālūsī calls this remote, since the name phrase normally attaches to the adjacent verb.
  holders (0 notes): ALUSI(بعضهم)-; ALUSI-FULL-

# 96:3/q03 How is 'your Lord is the Most Generous' constructed? [وَرَبُّكَ ٱلْأَكْرَمُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the Lord clause stands independently or circumstantially, and whether al-akram or the following relative clause is its predicate.
- 96:3/q03/p1 It is an independent new sentence, with 'your Lord' as subject and 'the Most Generous' as predicate.
  reasons: Al-Baghawī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī, Bursevī, and al-Ālūsī place a sentence break after the command. Bursevī permits a pause after khalaq because Muhammad said he could not read. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it an explanatory answer to that concern. Corpus Coranicum notes parallels at 89:15, 82:6, and 55:27, and says Paret's examples at 6:133 and 18:58 can support a definite elative predicate.
  holders (11 notes): BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL(الجبائي); IBNASHUR; CORPUSCORANICUM
  notes: BAGHAWI:96:3/r1 BAGHAWI-FULL:v8p479/r3 IBNJAWZI-ZAD:v9p176/r1 QURTUBI:96:3/r1 QURTUBI-FULL:96:3/r1 BURSEVI:v10p363#3/r3 ALUSI:96:3/r2 ALUSI:96:5/r9 ALUSI-FULL:v30p180#4/r3 IBNASHUR:96:3/r2 CORPUSCORANICUM:96:3:anmerkung/r1
- 96:3/q03/p2 The Lord clause is circumstantial to the implied subject of 'read': read while your Lord is most generous.
  reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says this highlights the Prophet's benefactor; al-Ālūsī reports it as an alternative that can remove the literacy objection.
  holders (4 notes): BIQAI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL(الجبائي)
  notes: BIQAI:96:3/r2 ALUSI:96:4/r2 ALUSI:96:5/r9 ALUSI-FULL:v30p180#4/r3
- 96:3/q03/p3 Al-Akram is an adjective of 'your Lord'; the following relative clause supplies the predicate.
  reasons: Ibn al-Shaykh explicitly parses rabbuka as subject, al-akram as adjective and the relative clause as predicate. Corpus Coranicum reports Paret's preference for this reading. Elmalılı says this better fits the context and puts prophetic honor above the pen's honor; either definite-predicate order suggests exclusivity.
  holders (4 notes): BURSEVI(ابن الشيخ); ELMALILI; CORPUSCORANICUM; CORPUSCORANICUM(Paret)+
