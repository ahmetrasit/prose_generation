# 96:3/q07 Why is teaching and knowledge linked to God's generosity here? [وَرَبُّكَ ٱلْأَكْرَمُ · links]
- 96:3/q07/p1 The move from a clot to knowledge shows God's greatest gift and humanity's highest created rank.
  reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī calls teaching by the pen the highest generosity; al-Nasafī likewise ranks imparted knowledge highly. Rāzī says the rise from lowly origin to knowledge of realities proves a wise directing Lord, and knowledge is humanity's noblest trait. Sayyid Quṭb says only divine power and generosity can raise a clot to a learner. Ibn Kathīr calls the opening God's first mercy and blessing, joining creation from a clot with teaching. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī and Elmalılı speak of movement from the lowest state to the highest, rational knowledge followed by conveyed knowledge.

# 96:3/q08 What do the opening verses establish about God and human obligation? [* · theology]
turns on: The notes move from proof by creation and teaching to claims about divine action; al-Qāshānī supplies an inward reading.
- 96:3/q08/p1 Creation and teaching prove a knowing, wise, powerful, merciful Lord, whom people should know and worship.
  reasons: Rāzī says creation from a clot rationally proves power, wisdom, knowledge and mercy; life's change into knowledge discloses direction and determination. Al-Bayḍāwī calls knowing God the first obligation and says the opening first points to His existence, power and wisdom. Al-Jishumī says the creator and master benefits others, hence deserves worship. Ibn ʿĀshūr says lordship entails existence and oneness, creation and teaching indicate acts, and al-Akram conveys perfection and freedom from deficiency.

- 96:3/q08/p2 Divine generosity cannot aim at a gain or require compensation; God does not impose beyond capacity or without reward.
  reasons: Rāzī defines generosity as fitting benefit without recompense, counting praise, reward and escaping blame as returns. His school argues that an end gained through an act would make God dependent and previously deficient. Abū ʿAlī says generosity bars an unrewarded demand or one beyond capacity. Al-Bayḍāwī contrasts God's uncompensated giving and fearless forbearance with created generosity.

# 96:3/q09 May people be called 'the Most Generous'? [ٱلْأَكْرَمُ · theology]
- 96:3/q09/p1 Some object to applying the divine epithet to people and urge reverence toward God.
  reasons: Abū Ḥayyān condemns Christian use of epithets such as al-Akram and al-Rashīd as personal honorifics. Al-Ālūsī records objections even though correspondents use the title for rulers and others, including non-Muslims; he urges gratitude and honoring the Lord.

# 96:3/q10 What happened when the command to read first came? [ٱقْرَأْ · reports]
turns on: The transmitted accounts differ in their additional details: a written cloth or the washing and weighing episode.
- 96:3/q10/p1 At Hira, after true dreams and retreat, Gabriel repeatedly commanded Muhammad to read, pressed him three times, and recited the opening passage.
  reasons: ʿĀʾishah's versions name true dreams, seclusion and Khadījah's provisions; Muhammad answered that he could not read, and returned trembling. Ibn Hishām's ʿUbayd report adds annual Ramadan retreat, feeding the poor, a return circumambulation, a silk cloth, and the words fixed in his heart. Al-Baghawī gives no grading; al-Wāḥidī says Bukhārī and Muslim transmit the account, and Ibn Kathīr cites both Ṣaḥīḥs through al-Zuhrī. Ibn ʿĀshūr says ʿĀʾishah narrates directly from the Prophet and the retained passage could be recited at once; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ notes no grading.
