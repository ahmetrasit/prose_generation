# 96:3: 18 questions

## 96:3/q01 Why is the command to read repeated?  [ٱقْرَأْ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the repetition is emphasis, distinguishes audiences or settings, or directly answers the Prophet's inability to read.
- 96:3/q01/p1 The second command reinforces the first, stresses reading, and prepares the declaration of divine generosity. — Al-Wāḥidī says repetition establishes certainty; al-Baghawī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ālūsī, Ibn ʿĀshūr, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ describe emphasis. Bursevī and al-Ālūsī stress its obligation; Elmalılı says practice develops recitation and also permits dictation or recitation for others. Kur'an Yolu links the repetition to the importance of reading and knowledge, by which people are distinguished, citing 2:31. Al-Biqāʿī adds reassurance, calming fear and the shift from gradual creation to origination without causes. Ibn ʿĀshūr says Gabriel repeated God's wording to accustom Muhammad to reading. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ prefers this immediate context; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says public recitation is not apparent from the wording. [21 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI(أبو مسلم); BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ALUSI-FULL+; BINTSHATI+]
- 96:3/q01/p2 The first command is for personal or unrestricted recitation, and the second for proclaiming to others. — Abū ʿAlī and al-Jubbāʾī distinguish private reading from conveying the message. Rāzī and al-Ālūsī also transmit an unrestricted first reading; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects the private/public distinction as unsupported by the wording. Al-Wāḥidī's and Elmalılı's accounts of reciting for others overlap this purpose. [12 notes: JISHUMI(أبو علي); TABRISI(الجبائي); RAZI(بعضهم); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI(الجبائي,قيل); ALUSI-FULL(الجبائي); TABATABAI(قيل); BINTSHATI(بعض المفسرين); TABATABAI-]
- 96:3/q01/p3 The first command is to learn from Gabriel and the second to teach. — Rāzī gives learning then teaching as one distinction; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ records it from earlier exegetes. [3 notes: RAZI(بعضهم); RAZI-FULL; BINTSHATI(بعض المفسرين)]
- 96:3/q01/p4 The two commands distinguish recitation in prayer from recitation outside prayer. — Rāzī and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ give prayer first and outside prayer second. Al-Ālūsī also reports relating the second to prayer mentioned at 96:10; the directional variants differ. [7 notes: RAZI(بعضهم); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(الجبائي); BINTSHATI(بعض المفسرين)]
- 96:3/q01/p5 Both commands order public proclamation of the Qur'an. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports this view and rejects it because the wording does not disclose public recitation. [1 notes: TABATABAI(قيل); TABATABAI-]
- 96:3/q01/p6 The second command answers the Prophet's claim that he cannot read, assuring him that God will enable him. — Al-Bayḍāwī allows a response to 'I cannot read'; al-Qurṭubī and Kur'an Yolu say the unlettered Prophet can be taught; Ibn ʿĀshūr reads the explanatory sentence with ʿĀʾishah's report. Al-Biqāʿī says the Lord who trained him can make him read. Al-Ālūsī also records a circumstantial clause removing the excuse. [9 notes: QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI; ALUSI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; IBNASHUR]

## 96:3/q02 What is the Prophet commanded to read, and how does the command relate to God's name?  [ٱقْرَأْ · interpretation]
turns on: The omitted object of 'read' and which verb receives the name phrase; adjacency is al-Ālūsī's stated syntactic test.
- 96:3/q02/p1 He is to read the revelation or Qur'an given to him, beginning in his Lord's name. — Al-Naḥḥās supplies the omitted object 'what was revealed to you' and says God will not withhold its reward. Al-Jishumī names the Qur'an. Al-Ālūsī and Qatādah say to begin with God's name; Qatādah says omitting the basmala or using an idol's name would fail the command. Al-Ālūsī prefers attaching 'in your Lord's name' to the adjacent first verb: placing the verb first foregrounds recitation in the opening sūra, while fronting the name would presuppose reading. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says revelation then specifies what is recited. [10 notes: NAHHAS; JISHUMI; ALUSI(قتادة); ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+; ALUSI+; BINTSHATI+]
- 96:3/q02/p2 The first command concerns God's name and the second the Qur'an. — Al-Jishumī reports this alternative, distinguishing the content of the two commands. [1 notes: JISHUMI]
- 96:3/q02/p3 The name phrase attaches to the second command, while the first establishes recitation itself. — Al-Ālūsī reports an analysis that gives 'in God's name' its customary attachment, makes the first recite introduce reading, and connects 'in your Lord's name' with the second command. Its proponents answer that the first command already orders recitation. [2 notes: ALUSI(بعضهم,من علق الجار بالثاني)]
- 96:3/q02/p4 The basmala's 'in the name' attaches to the first command and 'in your Lord's name' to the second, specifying the opening name. — Al-Ālūsī calls this remote, since the name phrase normally attaches to the adjacent verb. [0 notes: ALUSI(بعضهم)-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 96:3/q03 How is 'your Lord is the Most Generous' constructed?  [وَرَبُّكَ ٱلْأَكْرَمُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the Lord clause stands independently or circumstantially, and whether al-akram or the following relative clause is its predicate.
- 96:3/q03/p1 It is an independent new sentence, with 'your Lord' as subject and 'the Most Generous' as predicate. — Al-Baghawī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī, Bursevī, and al-Ālūsī place a sentence break after the command. Bursevī permits a pause after khalaq because Muhammad said he could not read. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it an explanatory answer to that concern. Corpus Coranicum notes parallels at 89:15, 82:6, and 55:27, and says Paret's examples at 6:133 and 18:58 can support a definite elative predicate. [11 notes: BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL(الجبائي); IBNASHUR; CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 96:3/q03/p2 The Lord clause is circumstantial to the implied subject of 'read': read while your Lord is most generous. — Al-Biqāʿī says this highlights the Prophet's benefactor; al-Ālūsī reports it as an alternative that can remove the literacy objection. [4 notes: BIQAI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL(الجبائي)]
- 96:3/q03/p3 Al-Akram is an adjective of 'your Lord'; the following relative clause supplies the predicate. — Ibn al-Shaykh explicitly parses rabbuka as subject, al-akram as adjective and the relative clause as predicate. Corpus Coranicum reports Paret's preference for this reading. Elmalılı says this better fits the context and puts prophetic honor above the pen's honor; either definite-predicate order suggests exclusivity. [4 notes: BURSEVI(ابن الشيخ); ELMALILI; CORPUSCORANICUM; CORPUSCORANICUM(Paret)+]
- 96:3/q03/p4 Al-Akram is the predicate, and the following relative clause is an adjective. — Elmalılı gives this alternate parsing; he says its definite predicate also conveys exclusivity. [1 notes: ELMALILI]

## 96:3/q04 Why say 'your Lord' here?  [رَبُّكَ · rhetoric]
- 96:3/q04/p1 The title stresses God's care for Muhammad and recalls the command to read in His name. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says rabbuka replaces the expected pronoun to emphasize divine care and to connect the Lord-title with the earlier command. Al-Biqāʿī says the Lord nurtured and trained Muhammad; Ibn ʿAṭiyyah and Abū Ḥayyān contrast Him with alleged pagan lords, assuring the Prophet of support. [7 notes: IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI; IBNASHUR]

## 96:3/q05 What does 'al-Akram' mean here?  [ٱلْأَكْرَمُ · meaning]
turns on: The semantic scope of generosity and whether the definite elative compares God with givers or denotes unrestricted supremacy.
- 96:3/q05/p1 God is generous beyond every giver, the source of gifts that no other can originate. — Al-Māwardī and al-Qushayrī give the ordinary sense 'generous'; al-Qurṭubī prefers it because preceding favors prove generosity. Al-Khaṭṭābī allows an elative equal to the positive. Al-Jishumī and al-Ṭabrisī say God initiates every blessing, directly or by its means. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Bayḍāwī and others cite countless gifts, forbearance, repentance and forgiveness. Ibrāhīm al-Yashkurī says God feeds a servant who worships another. Rāzī adduces a poem on increased kindness after offense and distinguishes divine gifts from human gifts sought for praise, reward or protection. Ibn ʿĀshūr defines karam as giving beneficial gifts, including existence and sustenance. Study Quran says Qur'anic application of karīm to God introduced a new divine attribute to pagan Arabs. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ objects to restricting the definite unqualified form to finite comparison. [39 notes: TAB; MAWARDI(إبراهيم بن عيسى اليشكري); MAWARDI-FULL(إبراهيم بن عيسى اليشكري); QUSHAYRI; JISHUMI; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الخطابي); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI(الزمخشري,الفخر الرازي); STUDYQURAN; QURTUBI+; BINTSHATI-; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(مفسرون)-]
- 96:3/q05/p2 Al-Akram expresses God's absolute, unbounded generosity and perfection, without specifying another giver for comparison. — Ibn ʿĀshūr treats the form as intensity rather than comparison; al-Biqāʿī includes perfection of essence, attributes and acts without defect. Elmalılı calls gifts limitless and gratuitous. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ argues that definite, unqualified afʿal and fuʿlā express an unrestricted maximum, citing 87:1, 92:20 and images of the greatest sign, blow, calamity, fire and model; explicit min or a named object limits a true comparison, as in 2:191. She contrasts 49:13's human scope and the genuinely comparative 'best of plotters' in 27:50 and 3:54; she rejects an argument that 'the Highest' implies a lesser lord and rejects explaining the form as rhyme. Her Quranic survey uses karīm for God, throne, messenger, angel, scripture and rewards, and notes ironic 'mighty, noble' at 44:47. [13 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; BINTSHATI; BINTSHATI-IJAZ]
- 96:3/q05/p3 Al-Akram means the Forbearing, who overlooks ignorance and does not hasten punishment. — Al-Kalbī's gloss is relayed by al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī; al-Wāḥidī adds wisdom. Al-Jishumī reports the same sense. Al-Qurṭubī also reports 'overlooks servants' ignorance' without explicit mention of delayed punishment. [11 notes: THALABI(الكلبي); WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI(الكلبي); BAGHAWI-FULL(الكلبي); QURTUBI(الكلبي,قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(الكلبي)]
- 96:3/q05/p4 The root carries nobility and honor, opposed to baseness or humiliation. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ distinguishes karam from baseness and ikrām from humiliation, citing God's honoring or humiliating people at 22:18 and 49:13. Study Quran says pre-Islamic karāmah included lineage, character and magnanimity. [3 notes: BINTSHATI; STUDYQURAN]

## 96:3/q06 How does the generous Lord encourage the Prophet to read and proclaim?  [ٱقْرَأْ وَرَبُّكَ ٱلْأَكْرَمُ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether generosity assures the ability to recite, reward for recitation, support for proclamation, or a sincere response.
- 96:3/q06/p1 God can teach the unlettered Prophet to recite and retain revelation. — Al-Qurṭubī says the Lord will help and teach one who cannot read. Ibn ʿĀshūr uses the Prophet's 'I cannot read' in ʿĀʾishah's report: knowledge arrives by dictation, instruction or inspiration, as Adam learned names without literacy. He says the relative clause is placed between subject and predicate to answer the writing objection. Kur'an Yolu allows direct teaching without an ordinary teacher or instrument; al-Ṭabrisī says God helps retain the Qur'an. [9 notes: QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); TABRISI(قيل); ALUSI; IBNASHUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 96:3/q06/p2 God will reward recitation, including tenfold reward for each letter. — Al-Naḥḥās says the Lord will not withhold the reward for reading. Rāzī and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ transmit the ten-rewards-per-letter explanation; al-Ṭabrisī says the Prophet will be rewarded. [5 notes: NAHHAS; TABRISI(قيل); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BINTSHATI(الفخر الرازي)]
- 96:3/q06/p3 God will support, strengthen and make the Prophet prevail in the difficult mission. — Al-Qāḍī lists strengthening, aid, victory and steadfastness. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah says the Lord, unlike idols, will support and elevate him. Rāzī says God would not impose the hard task of preaching and withhold aid; al-Ālūsī adds strength to memorize and deliver the Qur'an. [11 notes: JISHUMI(أبو علي والقاضي); IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(قيل); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI(الفخر الرازي)]
- 96:3/q06/p4 Read sincerely for God, trusting the generous Lord to give beyond expectation. — Rāzī offers this exhortation instead of reading from personal appetite; his full treatment combines it with the other encouragements. [2 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL]

## 96:3/q07 Why is teaching and knowledge linked to God's generosity here?  [وَرَبُّكَ ٱلْأَكْرَمُ · links]
- 96:3/q07/p1 The move from a clot to knowledge shows God's greatest gift and humanity's highest created rank. — Al-Zamakhsharī calls teaching by the pen the highest generosity; al-Nasafī likewise ranks imparted knowledge highly. Rāzī says the rise from lowly origin to knowledge of realities proves a wise directing Lord, and knowledge is humanity's noblest trait. Sayyid Quṭb says only divine power and generosity can raise a clot to a learner. Ibn Kathīr calls the opening God's first mercy and blessing, joining creation from a clot with teaching. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī and Elmalılı speak of movement from the lowest state to the highest, rational knowledge followed by conveyed knowledge. [14 notes: KASHSHAF; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ELMALILI(Beyzâvî); QUTB-ZILAL]

## 96:3/q08 What do the opening verses establish about God and human obligation?  [* · theology]
turns on: The notes move from proof by creation and teaching to claims about divine action; al-Qāshānī supplies an inward reading.
- 96:3/q08/p1 Creation and teaching prove a knowing, wise, powerful, merciful Lord, whom people should know and worship. — Rāzī says creation from a clot rationally proves power, wisdom, knowledge and mercy; life's change into knowledge discloses direction and determination. Al-Bayḍāwī calls knowing God the first obligation and says the opening first points to His existence, power and wisdom. Al-Jishumī says the creator and master benefits others, hence deserves worship. Ibn ʿĀshūr says lordship entails existence and oneness, creation and teaching indicate acts, and al-Akram conveys perfection and freedom from deficiency. [7 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:3/q08/p2 Divine generosity cannot aim at a gain or require compensation; God does not impose beyond capacity or without reward. — Rāzī defines generosity as fitting benefit without recompense, counting praise, reward and escaping blame as returns. His school argues that an end gained through an act would make God dependent and previously deficient. Abū ʿAlī says generosity bars an unrewarded demand or one beyond capacity. Al-Bayḍāwī contrasts God's uncompensated giving and fearless forbearance with created generosity. [9 notes: JISHUMI(أبو علي والقاضي); RAZI(أصحابنا); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 96:3/q08/p3 In an inward reading, God restores the Prophet after annihilation so His generosity and attributes appear. — Al-Qāshānī says God granted the Prophet His essence and attributes; leaving him annihilated would prevent generosity and any attribute from being manifest. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 96:3/q09 May people be called 'the Most Generous'?  [ٱلْأَكْرَمُ · theology]
- 96:3/q09/p1 Some object to applying the divine epithet to people and urge reverence toward God. — Abū Ḥayyān condemns Christian use of epithets such as al-Akram and al-Rashīd as personal honorifics. Al-Ālūsī records objections even though correspondents use the title for rulers and others, including non-Muslims; he urges gratitude and honoring the Lord. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 96:3/q10 What happened when the command to read first came?  [ٱقْرَأْ · reports]
turns on: The transmitted accounts differ in their additional details: a written cloth or the washing and weighing episode.
- 96:3/q10/p1 At Hira, after true dreams and retreat, Gabriel repeatedly commanded Muhammad to read, pressed him three times, and recited the opening passage. — ʿĀʾishah's versions name true dreams, seclusion and Khadījah's provisions; Muhammad answered that he could not read, and returned trembling. Ibn Hishām's ʿUbayd report adds annual Ramadan retreat, feeding the poor, a return circumambulation, a silk cloth, and the words fixed in his heart. Al-Baghawī gives no grading; al-Wāḥidī says Bukhārī and Muslim transmit the account, and Ibn Kathīr cites both Ṣaḥīḥs through al-Zuhrī. Ibn ʿĀshūr says ʿĀʾishah narrates directly from the Prophet and the retained passage could be recited at once; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ notes no grading. [27 notes: IBNHISHAM(عبيد); TAB(عائشة); WAHIDI-ASBAB(عائشة رضي الله عنها); BAGHAWI(عائشة); BAGHAWI-FULL(عائشة أم المؤمنين); IBNKATHIR(عائشة); DURR(عائشة,عبد الله بن شداد); DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ITQAN(عبيد بن عمير); ALUSI; IBNASHUR-FULL(عائشة); BINTSHATI(عائشة أم المؤمنين)]
- 96:3/q10/p2 At Hira, an angel brought written opening verses on silk or brocade. — Ibn Hishām's ʿUbayd account uses a written silk cloth; al-Zuhrī and ʿAmr b. Dīnār and ʿAmr b. Jābir describe brocade inscribed through verse five; al-Itqān reports silk in ʿUbayd's and al-Zuhrī's versions. These accounts vary in material and named transmitter. [5 notes: IBNHISHAM(عبيد); DURR(الزهري وعمرو بن دينار,عمرو بن جابر); ITQAN(الزهري,عبيد بن عمير)]
- 96:3/q10/p3 Gabriel appeared vast, opened and washed Muhammad's chest, taught five verses, and weighed him against men. — A second ʿĀʾishah account in al-Durr adds Michael's prediction that Muhammad's community would follow. [1 notes: DURR(عائشة)]

## 96:3/q11 What happened after the first encounter at Hira?  [* · reports]
turns on: These reports supply different episodes and sequence details after the first encounter.
- 96:3/q11/p1 Khadijah reassured the frightened Prophet by his virtues, and Waraqa identified Gabriel as Moses' angel and foresaw opposition. — Reports list kinship, truthfulness, bearing burdens, feeding or helping the needy, hospitality and aid in hardship, with small variations. Khadījah took Muhammad to Waraqa; Ibn Kathīr says he was a Christian relative versed in Arabic Gospel writing. Waraqa predicted expulsion, promised help if alive, then died. Ibn ʿĀshūr takes Khadījah's request that Waraqa hear Muhammad as evidence he could already recite the passage. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ's version adds a later sight of Gabriel in the sky; no grading is given. [19 notes: TAB(عائشة,عبد الله بن شداد); WAHIDI-ASBAB(عائشة رضي الله عنها); BAGHAWI(عائشة); BAGHAWI-FULL(عائشة أم المؤمنين); IBNKATHIR(خديجة,ورقة بن نوفل); DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI(عائشة أم المؤمنين)]
- 96:3/q11/p2 Revelation paused; in grief Muhammad approached peaks, and Gabriel reassured him of his mission. — Al-Baghawī and Ibn Kathīr identify the peak episodes as al-Zuhrī's 'what reached us' addition, not a directly quoted part of ʿĀʾishah's account. Ibn Shaddād connects the pause with al-Ḍuḥā's reassurance at 93:1–3. [8 notes: TAB(عبد الله بن شداد); BAGHAWI(الزهري); BAGHAWI-FULL(عائشة أم المؤمنين); IBNKATHIR]
- 96:3/q11/p3 Later revelations included Nūn and the Pen, al-Muddaththir, and al-Ḍuḥā. — ʿĀʾishah's sequence in al-Ṭabarī cites 68:1–4, 74:1–2 and 93:1–2. Mujāhid's transmission specifically says Nūn and the Pen followed Iqraʾ; Ibn Shaddād links al-Ḍuḥā to a delay. [6 notes: TAB(عائشة,عبد الله بن شداد,مجاهد)]

## 96:3/q12 Which part of this sura was revealed first?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether 'first sura' fixes the entire sura's timing or the five-verse opening; several reports explicitly stop at verse five.
- 96:3/q12/p1 The opening five verses, through 'what he did not know,' came first at Hira; the rest came later. — ʿĀʾishah's first encounter and Ibn ʿAbbās's account end at verse five. Al-Wāḥidī and al-Durr relay scholars heard by Muḥammad b. ʿAbbād who distinguish the later remainder. Al-Baghawī ascribes the five-verse view to most exegetes. Al-Burhān says al-Ḥākim authenticated ʿĀʾishah's Bukhārī report and Muslim gives it through 96:5. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites al-Rāzī's account that later opposition preceded the rest, and says al-Ṭabarī's reports and the biography stop at five. Ibn ʿĀshūr marks 96:6–10 as a new subject. [31 notes: TAB(عائشة); WAHIDI-ASBAB(بعض علمائهم,عائشة رضي الله عنها); JISHUMI(جماعة من المفسرين); BAGHAWI(أكثر المفسرين,عائشة); BAGHAWI-FULL(أكثر المفسرين,عائشة أم المؤمنين); IBNKATHIR(عائشة); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURHAN(عائشة); DURR(ابن عباس,الزهري وعمرو بن دينار,بعض علمائهم,عائشة,عبد الله بن شداد,عمرو بن جابر); DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ITQAN(الزهري); IBNASHUR-FULL(عائشة); BINTSHATI(أبو حيان,الفخر الرازي,عائشة أم المؤمنين); BINTSHATI+]
- 96:3/q12/p2 Iqraʾ or this sura was the first Qur'anic revelation, stated without fixing its initial extent. — Al-Ṭabarī transmits this from ʿUbayd, Abū Rajāʾ through Abū Mūsā, ʿAṭāʾ, Mujāhid and ʿĀʾishah; al-Jishumī cites ʿĀʾishah, Mujāhid and ʿAṭāʾ. Ibn Hishām, al-Itqān and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ also identify Iqraʾ as first. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says Ibn Isḥāq and al-Ṭabarī report no disagreement and quotes Abū Ḥayyān rejecting rival first-revelation claims for al-Muddaththir or al-Fātiḥa. Kur'an Yolu also calls it first. [21 notes: IBNHISHAM(عبيد); TAB(أبو رجاء العطاردي,عائشة,عبيد بن عمير,عطاء بن يسار,مجاهد); JISHUMI(عائشة ومجاهد وعطاء بن يسار); BAGHAWI(أكثر المفسرين); BAGHAWI-FULL(أكثر المفسرين); ITQAN(عبيد بن عمير); ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI(أبو حيان); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 96:3/q13 Where was the sura revealed and how many verses does it contain?  [* · sciences]
turns on: The two cited counts are seventeen and nineteen; both identify the sura as Meccan.
- 96:3/q13/p1 It is Meccan and has seventeen verses. — Al-Jishumī states both the classification and the count. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 96:3/q14 What reward is reported for reciting this sura?  [* · reports]
- 96:3/q14/p1 A reciter receives reward as though reciting the entire set of shorter suras. — Al-Bayḍāwī's full text transmits this from the Prophet; the note gives no grading. [1 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 96:3/q15 What does the mention of teaching by the pen imply about writing?  [* · links]
- 96:3/q15/p1 God taught people writing; reports and poetry trace its presence among Arabs. — Ibn ʿĀshūr supplies the two omitted objects of 'taught by the pen' as writers or people and the act of writing. He reports a chain from Murāmir ibn Murrah through Aslam ibn Sidrah to Ḥarb ibn Umayyah and from al-Anbār to the Ḥijāz, while Ḥimyar had an earlier Musnad script. Abū Ḥayyah al-Numayrī's verse about a Jew's writing hand associates writing with the People of the Book. [3 notes: IBNASHUR(أبو حية النميري)]

## 96:3/q16 How does this opening relate to the Qur'an as a whole?  [* · links]
- 96:3/q16/p1 It previews the Qur'an's themes and can be called its title. — Al-Suyūṭī's al-Itqān says the opening commands reading in God's name, signals legal knowledge and religious principles, and tells of God teaching humanity what it did not know. [1 notes: ITQAN]

## 96:3/q17 How is the verse read inwardly?  [* · inward]
- 96:3/q17/p1 God's gift of essence and attributes assures the Prophet's restoration after annihilation. — Al-Qāshānī says continued annihilation would leave no generosity or divine attribute to appear. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 96:3/q18 What do analogies elsewhere in the Qur'an show about literal number and comparative forms?  [* · rhetoric]
- 96:3/q18/p1 A stated comparator makes an elative comparative, while unqualified forms denote an unrestricted maximum; the dual of two gardens is literal. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites 'best of plotters' in 27:50 and 3:54 as a true response to human plots. She rejects al-Farrāʾ's claim that the two gardens of 55:46 are one chosen for cadence, since 55:48–49 continue the dual address to humans and jinn. This supports her refusal to explain al-Akram as finite comparison or rhyme. [3 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الفراء); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الملاحدة)-]
