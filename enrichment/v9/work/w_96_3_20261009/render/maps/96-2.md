# 96:2: 20 questions

## 96:2/q01 What does the creation verb convey here?  [خَلَقَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the noteworthy feature is the perfect's aspect or the shortened verbal form.
- 96:2/q01/p1 Its perfect form states a repeatedly occurring, generic act of divine creation. — Asad reads the past tense as continual repetition. Corpus Coranicum calls the perfect aspectual rather than simply past, comparing divine-action perfects in 87:4-5, 53:43-44 and 85:13. [3 notes: ASAD-NOTES; CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 96:2/q01/p2 The verb's shortened form signals the small, humble beginning from which creation grows. — Al-Burhan relates the omitted final nun to the despised drop and to development that God alone fully encompasses. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 96:2/q02 How does this clause relate grammatically to the preceding 'who created'?  [خَلَقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · grammar]
turns on: How the first verb's unexpressed object is construed, and whether repetition supplies specification, explanation, or emphasis.
- 96:2/q02/p1 The first 'created' has no expressed object and asserts God's creating as such. — Razi allows an absolute or intransitive first verb, emphasizing that God alone originates. Ibn Ashur also allows omission for generality, with the object anticipated by this clause. [3 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; IBNASHUR]
- 96:2/q02/p2 The first verb refers to all creation, and this clause specifies human creation within it. — Razi treats the unrestricted object as everything; Ibn Ashur calls the second clause a detailed or partitive apposition, first establishing dependence on God and then reinforcing proof with detail. Al-Burhan, Bint al-Shati and Islahi also describe general creation followed by specification. [8 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BURHAN; BINTSHATI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 96:2/q02/p3 The second clause explains the first verb's initially unspecified object, drawing attention to human creation. — Razi, Zamakhshari as reported by Abu Hayyan, Baydawi, Nasafi, Bursevi, Alusi and Ibn Ashur use clarification after initial vagueness to magnify the marvelous human constitution; Baydawi allows an independent new statement or completion of the relative description. Ibn Ashur says the object can be understood retroactively. [16 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN(الزمخشري); BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:2/q02/p4 The second 'created' repeats the relative clause for verbal emphasis. — Al-Samin compares an Arabic construction meaning 'the one who came, came Zayd.' [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 96:2/q03 Who is 'the human' in this verse?  [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · referent]
turns on: Whether al-alaq here is the blood stage of Adam's descendants or hand-clinging clay associated with Adam.
- 96:2/q03/p1 Humanity generically, especially Adam's descendants born through gestation. — Mawardi, Wahidi, Baghawi, Qurtubi and others say all people or Adam's children. Ibn Atiyya excludes Adam himself, who was made from clay, and says the mind extends the generic reference to animals. Biqai notes familiarity with one's own species. Corpus Coranicum relates early Meccan creation language to embryos, citing 95:4, 90:4, 70:19 and 55:14. Itqan contrasts the first 'human' as species with later referents. [40 notes: TAB; NAHHAS; THALABI; MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI; KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI; ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM; TABRISI+]
- 96:2/q03/p2 Adam, created from clay that sticks to the hand. — Tabrisi transmits this competing account but prefers humanity's origin in blood. Alusi calls the Adam reading unacceptable. Biqai observes that al-alaq can also denote sticky clay and reports that Arabs acknowledged blood and clay as human origins. [3 notes: TABRISI(قيل); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; TABRISI-; ALUSI(بعض الناس)-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 96:2/q04 What does 'ʿalaq' mean here?  [عَلَقٍ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the expression names congealed blood, attachment broadly, a fertilized ovum, or sticky earth.
- 96:2/q04/p1 A moist, clinging piece of congealed blood or an embryonic clot. — Tabari calls it blood. Mawardi, Jishumi and Qurtubi derive the name from sticking while wet and say dryness ends that designation; Qushayri distinguishes congealed from flowing blood. Mawardi and Qurtubi cite poetry about vein or aorta blood. Tabarsi and Ibn Ashur also note a leech sense; Ibn Ashur says the small dark-red clot resembles the aquatic leech. Qutb places the fixed blood point at the womb. Islahi compares 22:5, 23:14, 32, 75 and 76; Study Quran calls it gestation's third stage in 22:5, 23:14, 40:67 and 75:38. [36 notes: TAB; THALABI; MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL(الشاعر); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ELMALILI(Müfessirîn); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 96:2/q04/p2 A thing that clings or attaches, including material attachment and figurative bonds. — Elmalili lists clot, attached embryo, leech, pulley, rope, well-used path and spiritual love. Bint al-Shati notes attachment in love or hatred and the familiar expression for pregnancy. Corpus Coranicum compares embryonic sequence in 75:37-39 and 23:12-14. Elmalili prefers a singular 'attachment' broad enough for spiritual relation and all creative stages. [5 notes: ELMALILI; BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM; ELMALILI+]
- 96:2/q04/p3 The fertilized ovum attached to the uterine wall. — Kuranyolu lists attachment, affection, clotted blood and blood-sucking worm as dictionary senses, then takes the referent as the attached fertilized ovum; it cites 22:5 and 23:14. Asad contrasts this biologically simple origin with intellectual and spiritual potential. [2 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ASAD-NOTES]
- 96:2/q04/p4 Sticky clay or earth associated with human creation. — Tabrisi reports Adam made from hand-sticking clay but prefers the blood-origin account. Biqai says al-alaq can name mud that sticks to a hand and that Arabs recognized both blood and sticky earth as origins. [3 notes: TABRISI(قيل); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; TABRISI-; ALUSI(بعض الناس)-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 96:2/q05 Why is 'ʿalaq' plural or collective?  [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ عَلَقٍ · grammar]
- 96:2/q05/p1 The generic 'human' has a collective sense, so plural 'ʿalaq' matches all humans although each begins from one ʿalaqa. — Farra, Tabari and others compare singular/plural pairs such as shajara/shajar and qasaba/qasab. Kashshaf and Razi cite 103:2. Samīn says each person comes from a clot. Ibn Ashur calls alaq a collective noun. Farra, Wahidi, Ibn Jawzi, Bursevi and Alusi add that it fits the verse endings. Elmalili reports Baydawi's grammatical account. ## 96:2/q06 What does 'min' mark?  [مِنْ · grammar]
- 96:2/q06/p1 It marks the starting material or point of origin. — Ibn Ashur explicitly describes min as denoting inception or starting point. [2 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 96:2/q07 Why is the human singled out after creation in general?  [خَلَقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether specification chiefly marks human honor, a persuasive proof, explanatory magnification, or an inward account of prophethood.
- 96:2/q07/p1 Humans are noble or honored, and revelation addresses them. — Kashshaf, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Samin and Bursevi cite the addressee of revelation and rank among earthly creatures; Abu Hayyan notes Zamakhshari's suggestion concerning angels. Qurtubi says explicit naming honors humanity. Bint al-Shati adds reading, learning and responsibility; Baydawi relates it to worship. [15 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI; RAZI; RAZI-FULL]
- 96:2/q07/p2 Human formation is an especially wondrous and accessible proof of the Creator. — Ibn Atiyya points to intellect, perception, ligaments and bones. Tabrisi calls the move from all creatures to humanity specification after generality, comparing general unseen belief and the afterlife and Labid's line on blame and slowness. Biqai calls humanity the highest animal, a summary of the cosmos; Baydawi, Alusi and Ibn Ashur say its constitution establishes worship or divine oneness. Ibn Ashur cites 51:21 because each can reflect on oneself. Bursevi calls creation the earliest blessing and evidence of existence, knowledge and wisdom. [17 notes: IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(لبيد); BAYDAWI; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:2/q07/p3 The wording first leaves creation unspecified, then names humanity to magnify its marvelous making. — Kashshaf invokes 55:1-3. Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi and Alusi describe initial vagueness followed by explanation. [13 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN(الزمخشري); BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI(الزمخشري)]
- 96:2/q07/p4 It concerns the Prophet's return to embodied human form needed for revelation and prophethood. — Qashani's inward reading treats composite form and veiling as conditions of descent and prophetic reception. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 96:2/q08 Why mention the clot stage of human origin?  [مِنْ عَلَقٍ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the stage is cited for its lowliness, audience familiarity, place in formation, life-bearing quality, or a prophetic allusion.
- 96:2/q08/p1 The low beginning and mature, rational or knowledgeable human reveal God's favor, power and wise direction. — Mawardi contrasts a lowly clot with upright reason and discernment; Jishumi urges gratitude and worship. Qurtubi, Baydawi, Razi, Bursevi, Alusi and Tabatabai stress the ascent to human perfection, knowledge and writing; Razi cites 96:4-5 and calls knowledge humanity's noblest trait. Asad reads simplicity against spiritual and intellectual potential. Ibn Ashur speaks of hidden capacities and earthly sovereignty. Ibn Kathir repeatedly says the passage calls attention to humanity's beginning in a clinging clot. [24 notes: MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI; TABRISI; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; ELMALILI(Beyzâvî,Müfessirîn); BINTSHATI(الزمخشري); IBNKATHIR]
- 96:2/q08/p2 This familiar stage makes the argument accessible to an audience that did not accept Adam's creation from clay. — Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan say the original creation was not established for unbelievers, whereas descendants' gestation was familiar. Alusi reports al-Bahr's explanation but says it leaves the choice of this stage insufficiently explained. [7 notes: IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI(البحر); ALUSI-FULL(البحر)]
- 96:2/q08/p3 The clot is closer to the developed human than semen or soil. — Alusi reports this explanation but observes that semen or soil may seem even clearer signs of divine power. [2 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 96:2/q08/p4 The clot is chosen as the start of embryonic formation after male and female fluids combine. — Ibn Ashur says the male fluid alone cannot produce an embryo and that mixed fluids become a clot after time. He calls the leech-like microscopic fertilized ovum a scientific miracle, compares 22:5 and his tenth introduction. Study Quran cites the subsequent flesh stage in 22:5 and 23:14. Corpus Coranicum compares 75:37-39 and 23:12-14. Bint al-Shati disputes embryology as the intended lesson because the first audience could not have understood such science. [6 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN; CORPUSCORANICUM; BINTSHATI-]
- 96:2/q08/p5 The clinging substance is singled out because it carries life. — Biqai calls blood the source and carrier of life, even saying the soul is named for this life-bearing material; God may use causal processes despite power to create directly. [5 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL]
- 96:2/q08/p6 It recalls clinging blood removed from the Prophet's chest in preparation for revelation. — Alusi reports an interpretation linking the pre-prophetic chest opening to al-Sharh 94:1, especially if its opening means literal chest-splitting. [2 notes: ALUSI(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]

## 96:2/q09 What divine claim does human creation establish?  [خَلَقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ مِنْ عَلَقٍ · theology]
turns on: Which inference the notes draw from creation: exclusive lordship, a knowing cause, divine attributes, the ability to teach, or resurrection.
- 96:2/q09/p1 God alone creates and so alone merits divinity and worship. — Razi's theologians say creation distinguishes God's essence and capacity to originate defines divinity, using Pharaoh and Moses in 26:23-26; idols are carved by humans. Razi invokes 43:87 and 16:17: acknowledged creator and noncreator cannot be equal. Abu Hayyan notes Arabs called idols lords, so the Creator attribute excludes them. Ibn Ashur sees a reproach to polytheists for missing proof within themselves. [12 notes: RAZI(الأصحاب); RAZI-FULL(أصحابنا); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI(آخرون من السلف,أبو حيان); TABATABAI]
- 96:2/q09/p2 Ordered development establishes a knowing, choosing Lord rather than self-sufficient natural causation. — Razi says a created cause needs a cause, while an eternal cause either necessitates its effects or acts by choice; ordered beneficial changes imply knowledge and choice, with 16:17. He recounts Abu Hanifa advising Zufar to present opponents' arguments before his own, as an example of gradual persuasion. Biqai says God employs causes in the world yet could create directly. [5 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL]
- 96:2/q09/p3 The created human supplies rational proof of divine power, wisdom, knowledge and mercy. — Razi links the clot to divine attributes and the pen to transmitted rulings, so knowing lordship grounds prophethood. Baydawi calls knowing God the first obligation; Baydawi and Ibn Ashur see the opening as proofs of existence, oneness, acts and perfection. Kuranyolu says understanding Creator and creation supports human development. [7 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 96:2/q09/p4 The Creator who formed a person from a clot can enable the unreading Prophet to recite. — Elmalili applies both material-clot and broad-attachment readings to the reading command. [1 notes: ELMALILI]
- 96:2/q09/p5 Human origins show that God can resurrect people and will hold them accountable. — Biqai argues resurrection from dust is easier to envisage than first creation from clinging blood and urges action. Islahi infers purposeful creation, judgment and humility rather than pride. Bint al-Shati compares later origin-and-return passages, 80:17, 86:5, 36:77, 18:37, 22:5, 76:2 and 92:13, emphasizing judgment rather than embryology. [9 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BINTSHATI]

## 96:2/q10 What first duty toward knowing God is drawn from the opening?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether the obligation is the knowledge itself, reflection, or the intention to reflect.
- 96:2/q10/p1 The first duty is to know God. — Razi reports this as one theological formulation. Baydawi says the opening therefore begins with evidence of God's existence, power and wisdom. [3 notes: RAZI(المتكلمون); RAZI-FULL(المتكلمون); BAYDAWI-FULL]
- 96:2/q10/p2 The first duty is to consider how to know God. — Razi lists it among the theologians' disputed formulations. [2 notes: RAZI(المتكلمون); RAZI-FULL(المتكلمون)]
- 96:2/q10/p3 The first duty is to resolve to undertake that inquiry. — Razi lists it as the third formulation and says theologians agree on the general concern while disputing its wording. [2 notes: RAZI(المتكلمون); RAZI-FULL(المتكلمون)]

## 96:2/q11 What legal implication is taken from the verse's blood and earth imagery?  [عَلَقٍ · law]
turns on: The notes make different legal uses of the shared term and of material transformation.
- 96:2/q11/p1 A word shared by blood and sticky earth can carry both senses at once. — Biqai cites al-Shafi'i's use of this verse as evidence for applying a homonym to both meanings simultaneously. [2 notes: BIQAI(الشافعي); BIQAI-FULL]
- 96:2/q11/p2 Blood and earth in their original states are inedible, but transformation can make their products lawful. — Biqai says blood transformed into flesh and earth mixed with water transformed into dates or grain become lawful; reddish semen has not thereby transformed. He compares changes of legal status in marriage and other matters and says eating blood conveys its qualities. [6 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL]

## 96:2/q12 How does human development mirror the Prophet's development?  [خَلَقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ مِنْ عَلَقٍ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the link is an analogy of development or direct reassurance about providential care.
- 96:2/q12/p1 The transition from clot to formed human parallels the Prophet's move from ignorance to completed prophethood. — Mawardi offers this as a second possible purpose; Tabrisi adds the development into balanced humanity and the Prophet's preparation. [3 notes: MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI]
- 96:2/q12/p2 The mention reassures Muhammad that his Lord had cared for him since his earliest stage. — Bint al-Shati reports this from some early authorities. [1 notes: BINTSHATI(بعض السلف)]

## 96:2/q13 How does the verse connect reading and human creation?  [خَلَقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · links]
turns on: Whether creation establishes the purpose of reading, a progression to knowledge, or the Prophet's newly enabled recitation.
- 96:2/q13/p1 The human was created for Quranic reading and understanding. — Alusi reports Zamakhshari's reading of the creation clause after the reading command, compared with al-Rahman 55:1-3. [2 notes: ALUSI(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري)]
- 96:2/q13/p2 Creation, reading and teaching form a movement from human origins to knowledge and revealed guidance. — Razi connects the lowly clot with knowledge through 96:4-5 and the rational proof of lordship with transmitted revelation. Baydawi similarly moves from rational to transmitted knowledge. Ibn Kathir says writing preserves knowledge, distinguishes mental, spoken and written knowledge, cites aphorisms on writing and acting on knowledge, and says Adam's teaching distinguished him from angels. Bint al-Shati reads creation, pen and teaching together. [11 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR; ELMALILI(Beyzâvî); BINTSHATI-IJAZ; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 96:2/q13/p3 The first recitation itself granted the unreading Prophet ability and fixed the revealed text. — Elmalili relates the third command to 29:48 and 42:52. Ibn Ashur infers immediate recitation from Muhammad returning with the passage and Khadija's request to Waraqa; Gabriel's repeated command accustomed him to reading. [3 notes: ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 96:2/q14 What was first revealed, and how much of this sura came first?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether 'first sura' means the opening five verses or the complete chapter, and the competing priority claims.

- 96:2/q14/p2 Al-ʿAlaq is described as the first sura revealed, without necessarily fixing the extent of the first installment. — Tabari transmits Aisha, Abu Raja, Ata, Mujahid and Ubayd's formulations of 'first sura'; Jishumi relays Aisha, Mujahid and Ata. Itqan relays Ubayd's first-sura claim. [12 notes: TAB(أبو رجاء العطاردي,عائشة,عبيد بن عمير,عطاء بن يسار,مجاهد); JISHUMI(عائشة ومجاهد وعطاء بن يسار); ITQAN(عبيد بن عمير)]
- 96:2/q14/p3 Al-Muddaththir or al-Fatiha came first. — Bint al-Shati reports Abu Hayyan's judgment that these alternative priority claims are unsound. [0 notes: BINTSHATI(أبو حيان)-]

## 96:2/q15 What happened when the opening verses were delivered at Hira?  [* · reports]

- 96:2/q15/p1 After true dreams and seclusion, Gabriel repeatedly commanded reading, pressed Muhammad three times, and delivered the opening verses. — Aisha is reported by Tabari, Wahidi, Baghawi, Ibn Kathir, Durr, Ibn Ashur and Bint al-Shati. - 96:2/q15/p2 Gabriel brought a written silk or brocade sheet bearing the opening passage. — Ibn Hisham's Ubayd report adds annual Ramadan retreat, feeding the poor, Kaaba circumambulation, three embraces, and waking with the text fixed in Muhammad's heart. - 96:2/q15/p3 Gabriel appeared vast, opened and washed Muhammad's chest, taught five verses, and weighed him against men. — Durr transmits this second account from Aisha; Michael then says Muhammad's community will follow. [1 notes: DURR(عائشة)]

## 96:2/q16 What happened after Muhammad returned from the first revelation?  [* · reports]
turns on: Whether the note concerns immediate reassurance and Waraqa or the subsequent pause and revelations.
- 96:2/q16/p1 Khadija reassured him through his virtues, and Waraqa identified Gabriel as Moses' angel and predicted opposition. — Tabari, Baghawi and Ibn Kathir list kinship, truthful speech, support for the needy, hospitality and help in hardship; Waraqa promised support if still alive. Ibn Shaddad adds the later pause and al-Duha reassurance. Bint al-Shati's Aisha account says Muhammad saw Gabriel across the sky. Baghawi notes no grading for its report. [12 notes: TAB(عائشة,عبد الله بن شداد); WAHIDI-ASBAB(عائشة رضي الله عنها); BAGHAWI(عائشة); BAGHAWI-FULL(عائشة أم المؤمنين); IBNKATHIR(خديجة,ورقة بن نوفل); BINTSHATI(عائشة أم المؤمنين)]
- 96:2/q16/p2 Revelation paused, and later passages including Nun, al-Muddaththir and al-Duha followed. — Tabari's Aisha report lists Nun and the Pen, al-Muddaththir and al-Duha. Ibn Shaddad links the pause to 93:1-3. Baghawi preserves al-Zuhri's Bukhari addition, introduced as 'what reached us,' about grief and repeated approaches to mountain peaks until Gabriel reassured him. [6 notes: TAB(عائشة,عبد الله بن شداد); BAGHAWI(الزهري); BAGHAWI-FULL(عائشة أم المؤمنين)]

## 96:2/q18 How does this verse connect with neighboring suras and the rest of al-ʿAlaq?  [* · links]
turns on: The sources link the verse to earlier suras through human origin and destiny, prophetic rank, or the reported chest opening.
- 96:2/q18/p1 Al-Tin's best human form is followed by al-ʿAlaq's account of human material origin and later destiny. — Alusi links 95:4 to the material origin and afterlife. Ibn al-Zubayr, reported by Biqai, connects creation in the best form and decline in 95:4-5 with divergent destinies under prior will, citing 32:13; God's wise judgment in 95:7-8 concerns the community despite address to the Prophet, as 39:65 does. The Qur'an's guidance explains beginnings and ends, and sūra order is based on arrangement, not revelation chronology. [7 notes: ALUSI-FULL; BIQAI(أبو جعفر ابن الزبير)]
- 96:2/q18/p2 The preceding suras show the Prophet's highest rank, while this sura also portrays a person who forbids prayer. — Ibn al-Zubayr compares promised satisfaction and expanded breast, 93:5 and 94:1, with Moses' prayer at 20:25; he contrasts the blocker of prayer in 96:9 with the command to disobey him in 96:19. [2 notes: BIQAI(أبو جعفر ابن الزبير)]
- 96:2/q18/p3 The clot's creation and the return to God frame the sura's story of accountability. — Bint al-Shati links human origin to 92:13 and says the first revelation sketches the human story later expanded in passages on resurrection and judgment. Ibn Ashur marks verse 5 as the end of the first passage and verses 6-10 as a new subject. [5 notes: BINTSHATI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:2/q18/p4 The clot can recall the Prophet's chest opening, connecting al-ʿAlaq to al-Sharh. — Alusi reports this reading of 94:1, especially if its wording refers to literal chest-splitting. [2 notes: ALUSI(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]

## 96:2/q19 What wider Quranic themes are anticipated by the opening passage?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the opening is related to wider creation and accountability passages or to letters, writing and the Quranic challenge.
- 96:2/q19/p1 Its reading command and creation, pen and teaching themes preview the Quran's guidance and human accountability. — Itqan calls the opening a title of the Qur'an because it names reading in God's name, legal and religious knowledge, and divine teaching. Bint al-Shati connects creation to later origin, death and resurrection passages; Corpus Coranicum contrasts early Meccan womb-development texts (86:5-7, 80:18-19, 75:38, 77:20, 70:39) with later Adam-earth accounts (55:14, 18:37, 22:5, 23:12-14, 32:7-9, 40:67). [5 notes: ITQAN; BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 96:2/q19/p2 Reading and the pen connect to the disjointed nun and the Quran's later challenge to opponents. — Bint al-Shati says the isolated nun in 68:1 is a letter whose sound gains significance in words; the Prophet had not read earlier scripture (29:48), yet opponents resisted a human messenger. She says nine early Meccan suras opened with letters amid accusations of folklore, poetry, soothsaying and magic; the Quran moved from eloquence to explicit challenge and recalled punished peoples. [4 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ]
