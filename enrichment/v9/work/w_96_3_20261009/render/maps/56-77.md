# 56:77: 15 questions

## 56:77/q01 What does the pronoun in 'indeed it' refer to?  [إِنَّهُۥ · referent]
turns on: Whether the unexpressed antecedent is the familiar revelation as a whole or the preceding discourse.
- 56:77/q01/p1 The Qur'an as a whole, already known to the hearers. — Mujahid calls it the Qur'an from God's Book; Razi says the revelation sent to Muhammad was already known despite charges of poetry and magic; Ibn Ashur says mental presence supplies the unexpressed antecedent; Alusi says the referent is the Qur'an itself, not merely its quality as a recitation; Study Quran allows the whole Quran. [6 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس وغيره); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN(R)]
- 56:77/q01/p2 The preceding discourse of this sura, including its proofs and oath. — Razi specifies monotheism, resurrection, their proofs and the oath, which opponents called Muhammad's invention; Study Quran allows what has been discussed in the sura. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(R)]

## 56:77/q02 What does the name Qur'an mean and how is it derived?  [لَقُرْءَانٌۭ · meaning]
turns on: Whether it is a proper name or derived, and, if derived, which root and morphological account explain its sense and hamza.
- 56:77/q02/p1 A recitation or recited speech, derived from reading; a verbal noun can name what is read. — Razi compares verbal noun used for object in 13:31 and 31:11; al-Lihyani calls it a verbal noun like ghufran; Ibn Ashur stresses repeated reading and contemplation and cites 10:61; Study Quran gives the linguistic sense 'noble recitation'; Burhan also describes sequential recitation. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL; BURHAN; ITQAN(اللحياني); ALUSI-FULL(اللحياني); IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 56:77/q02/p2 A proper, non-derived name for God's particular revelation, pronounced without hamza. — Isma'il ibn Qustantine argues that derivation from reading would make every recited text a Qur'an; al-Shafi'i reportedly hamzated qara'tu but not Quran and compared its proper-name status to Torah and Gospel; al-Wahidi transmits his view; al-Suyuti chooses it, while Alusi criticizes that as unargued deference and prefers al-Zajjaj. [7 notes: BURHAN(إسماعيل بن قسطنطين,الشافعى,بعضهم); ITQAN(الشافعي,جماعة); ALUSI-FULL(الشافعي); ALUSI-FULL(السيوطي)+; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 56:77/q02/p3 Derived from qar', gathering or collecting. — Al-Jawhari gives the root; Abu Ubayd says it gathers suras; al-Raghib says it gathers fruits of earlier scriptures but cautions that not every collection is called Quran, perhaps a point about usage; al-Harawi says anything gathered has been read; al-Zajjaj compares collecting water in a basin and takes the hamza as original, with its omission due to lightening; Alusi accepts this analysis on the common hamzated form. [10 notes: BURHAN(أبو عبيد,الجوهرى,الراغب,الهروى); ITQAN(الزجاج); ALUSI-FULL(أبو عبيدة,الراغب,الزجاج); ALUSI-FULL+; BURHAN(بعض المتأخرين)-]
- 56:77/q02/p4 Derived from joining together its suras, verses and letters. — Al-Ash'ari compares qiran joining pilgrimage rites; Alusi says the proposed n is original and there is no hamza. [3 notes: BURHAN(الأشعرى); ITQAN(الأشعري); ALUSI-FULL(الأشعري); ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج)-]
- 56:77/q02/p5 Derived from qara'in, because its verses confirm and resemble one another. — Attributed to al-Qurtubi and al-Farra; this treats n as radical and the form as unhamzated. Al-Zajjaj calls the derivation mistaken, explaining missing hamza as lightening and vowel transfer; al-Farisi notes that analysis. [3 notes: BURHAN(القرطبى); ITQAN(الفراء); ALUSI-FULL(الفراء); BURHAN(الزجاج)-; ITQAN(الزجاج)-; ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج)-]
- 56:77/q02/p6 A noun for what is recited, rather than a verbal noun transferred to its object. — Razi compares qurban for what is offered and hulwan for what is given or tasted; he says this validates jurists' al-jubran for what effects compensation. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 56:77/q02/p7 Derived from bringing forth or making something manifest and articulate. — A later scholar cites 75:17 to distinguish gathering from Quran; Qutrub compares the reciter bringing it out of his mouth to an expression about a she-camel bringing forth a fetal membrane. [2 notes: BURHAN(بعض المتأخرين); ALUSI-FULL(قطرب)]

## 56:77/q03 How is the hamza of Qur'an read and explained?  [لَقُرْءَانٌۭ · readings]
turns on: Whether the pronounced or lightened hamza reflects the word's derivation or a regional reading practice.
- 56:77/q03/p1 The hamza is original and pronounced; omission is phonetic lightening with its vowel moved to the preceding consonant. — Al-Zajjaj rejects an unhamzated derivation; al-Farisi records the vowel-transfer explanation. Alusi favors it because six of seven readers pronounce hamza and the hamzated form is common. [4 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج); ALUSI-FULL+; BURHAN(الزجاج)+; ITQAN(الزجاج)+]
- 56:77/q03/p2 The hamza is omitted or softened in the reading qurān. — Al-Bayhaqi reports al-Shafi'i's unhamzated Quran despite his hamzated qara'tu; al-Wahidi and al-Suyuti report Ibn Kathir's reading; Qiraat-ER calls dropping or softening it standard Hijazi practice. [3 notes: BURHAN(البيهقى); ITQAN(الشافعي); QIRAAT-ER(Ibn Kathīr and others)]

## 56:77/q04 Why call the revelation a Qur'an here?  [لَقُرْءَانٌۭ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the disputed assertion is its reception as a lasting recitation or the entire noble description.
- 56:77/q04/p1 Calling it Qur'an establishes received, enduring recitation and rebuts the claim that Muhammad invented it. — Razi prefers this: Muhammad received and recited it, and it will be recited perpetually in this life and the next; he separately says the designation counters self-composition. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; RAZI-FULL+]
- 56:77/q04/p2 The deniers rejected the full characterization 'noble Qur'an,' not the bare fact that it could be recited. — Razi reports this as an alternative answer to why the descriptive name is asserted. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 56:77/q05 What makes the Qur'an karīm, or noble?  [كَرِيمٌۭ · interpretation]
turns on: Which aspect of nobility the adjective foregrounds: intrinsic excellence, benefit, divine honor, moral guidance, transmission, protection, or theological status.
- 56:77/q05/p1 It possesses excellent, praiseworthy qualities and is free of baseness or defect. — Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan take the adjective as praise excluding defects; Ibn al-Jawzi and Study Quran call it comprehensive of praised qualities; Qushayri excludes baseness; Razi defines true nobility by pure origin and manifest excellence, with eloquent wording and sound meaning, and rejects reducing it to giving; Ibn Ashur says its comprehensive guidance, proof, refutation, clarity, concision, eloquence and sound surpass earlier books, citing 27:29. Others call it excellent, beautiful, precious, or pleasing among books. [22 notes: QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الطيبي); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(R, Sh); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 56:77/q05/p2 It gives abundant benefit, guidance, knowledge, reward, and good for this life and the next. — Mawardi adds benefit to memorizer and reciter; Tabarsi cites proofs leading to truth and reward for reciting and acting; Razi says jurists, sages and writers each find what they need and its exact words are easy to memorize; Baydawi, al-Tibi, Bursevi and Elmalili point to foundational knowledge for both lives. Alusi reports a dispute whether generosity for benefit is metaphorical (al-Tibi) or literal under a broad sense of praiseworthy qualities. Alusi also reports an honor-in-God's-sight sense but calls its implied preposition unnecessary. Qurtubi describes healing for believers' hearts. [19 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الطيبي); ELMALILI; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; RAZI-FULL-]
- 56:77/q05/p3 God has honored and exalted it as His speech and revelation. — Maqatil explicitly attributes its exaltation to God; believers and heaven's people honor their Lord's speech and revelation (Qurtubi); Bag hawi and Tabarsi cite its being God's speech; al-Kashshaf and others give 'honored by God.' Alusi says this reading returns to the preceding excellence sense and needs an unnecessary implied preposition. [11 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(مقاتل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI]
- 56:77/q05/p4 It directs people to noble character, lofty aims and virtuous deeds. — Sulami and Bursevi state this directly; Mawardi includes noble character and lofty matters; Qushayri says it guides noble character; Qurtubi speaks of noble character and weighty meanings; Kuranyolu includes morals among its riches. [6 notes: SULAMI; MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BURSEVI(بعضهم); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 56:77/q05/p5 It came from the Noble Lord through noble messengers to the noblest human recipient. — Sulami describes a noble intermediary and noblest creature; Qushayri specifies generous Lord, noble Messenger and angel; Biqai specifies highest King, Holy Spirit, best of creation, eloquent Arabic and inimitability; Bursevi repeats the transmission chain. [4 notes: SULAMI(قيل); QUSHAYRI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 56:77/q05/p6 It confers honor on its memorizer and reciter. — Mawardi says both are honored; Qurtubi says it honors the memorizer and magnifies the reciter. [2 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]
- 56:77/q05/p7 It is noble through protection, inimitability, and sublime contents. — Tabarsi adds immunity from alteration, inimitability, rulings and admonitions; Kuranyolu says the final perfected book is protected from falsehood and full of proof, knowledge and wisdom; Biqai calls its Arabic form inimitable; Kashshaf notes the protected book's inaccessibility to all but purified angels if it means the Preserved Tablet. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; BIQAI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 56:77/q05/p8 It remains fresh through every repetition. — Razi contrasts ordinary speech, which grows stale, and says even angels who knew it before Muhammad hear it with a new listener's delight. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 56:77/q05/p9 Its ease overcomes its difficulty. — Thalabi reports this explanation of generosity. [1 notes: THALABI]
- 56:77/q05/p10 It is uncreated. — Abd al-Aziz ibn Yahya al-Kinani states this; Qushayri draws it from nobility's exclusion of baseness; Qurtubi reports it as a gloss of karim. [3 notes: THALABI(عبد العزيز بن يحيى الكناني); QUSHAYRI; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]

## 56:77/q06 Is the Qur'an here originated or uncreated?  [لَقُرْءَانٌۭ كَرِيمٌۭ · theology]
turns on: Whether the Qur'an's descriptors and the nature of speech imply temporal origination or eternal speech.
- 56:77/q06/p1 It is uncreated. — Al-Kinani states this; Qushayri reads it from karim and the exclusion of baseness; Qurtubi reports it as one gloss. Tabarsi distinguishes the eternal attribute of God as noble giver from the Qur'an's plentiful benefits; Burhan says God's speech was made heard and understood by Gabriel without specifying modality. Qashani calls the Qur'an ancient. [4 notes: THALABI(عبد العزيز بن يحيى الكناني); QUSHAYRI; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); QASHANI]
- 56:77/q06/p2 It is originated rather than eternal. — Jishumi says speech is an accident that does not persist and descends from its locus or its sign's locus; its descriptions as discourse, revelation, guarded book and untouchable fit neither literal nor figurative eternity. [2 notes: JISHUMI]

## 56:77/q07 What is the object and force of the preceding oath?  [إِنَّهُۥ لَقُرْءَانٌۭ كَرِيمٌۭ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the oath directly authenticates the Qur'an or principally asserts the earlier proofs of monotheism and resurrection.
- 56:77/q07/p1 The oath affirms that the revelation to Muhammad is a noble Qur'an. — Ibn Atiyya identifies the Qur'an as the object; Qurtubi calls this the oath's answer against sorcery, soothsaying and fabrication; Ibn Kathir says the tremendous oath magnifies the great book. Sa'id ibn Jubayr takes la as extra; other exegetes take it as a meaningful response to denial, citing Aisha's oath usage; Arabists quoted by al-Tabari read 'it is not as you claim' before a fresh oath. Alusi says the description confirms and glorifies the preceding oath. [10 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن جرير,سعيد بن جبير,مفسرون آخرون); ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 56:77/q07/p2 The oath's more apparent object is divine oneness and resurrection. — Razi prefers these themes, which the preceding discourse has argued for. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL+]

## 56:77/q08 How does the intervening conditional affect the oath and this description?  [لَقُرْءَانٌۭ كَرِيمٌۭ · grammar]
- 56:77/q08/p1 It is a parenthetical interruption between connected elements, magnifying the oath and the Qur'an. — Nasafi and Alusi place 'if you only knew' between noun and adjective; Alusi says its unexpressed answer denies knowledge or implies reverence and action. Suyuti places the interruption between oath and description and says it signals grandeur beyond their knowledge; Qudama defines i'tirad as syntactically ungoverned insertion for a purpose beyond clearing ambiguity, and al-Tibi says unexpected useful meaning makes it effective. Zarkashi rejects a restriction on parentheticals: they may occur with or without waw, as in this passage and 34:31. [6 notes: NASAFI-FULL; BURHAN; ITQAN(الطيبي,قدامة); ALUSI-FULL]

## 56:77/q09 What does the affirmation teach about the Qur'an's origin and authority?  [* · interpretation]
- 56:77/q09/p1 It is God's exalted revelation to Muhammad, immune to charges of magic, soothsaying or human invention. — Qurtubi calls it praiseworthy revelation and a miracle; Razi frames the denial as poetry or magic and the rebuttal as received recitation; Alusi stresses revealed rather than composed. Islahi says the oath testifies to divine origin, the protected book and access only by pure near angels, never jinn or devils; the section warns Quraysh against ignoring the message. Tabatabai cites other verses about a guarded tablet, exalted wise source and protected book, with enduring unchanging realities beneath the meanings. [10 notes: RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 56:77/q10 How does this affirmation connect with the sura's argument?  [* · links]
- 56:77/q10/p1 After proofs of oneness and resurrection, the oath establishes the divine Qur'an that teaches those truths. — Tabatabai says deniers reject the truths by denying the Qur'an, so the proofs come first and the revelation's source is affirmed afterward; Razi describes the preceding discussion as monotheism, resurrection and their proofs; Islahi treats verses 75–96 as declaring the Qur'an's exalted and protected status, with a warning to Quraysh. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 56:77/q11 What inward reading is given to the noble Qur'an and its hidden book?  [لَقُرْءَانٌۭ كَرِيمٌۭ · inward]
- 56:77/q11/p1 It is exalted, ancient, comprehensive knowledge deposited as Qur'anic intellect in the Prophet's heart. — Qashani makes the hidden book the Prophet's heart, beyond the senses except to purified angels. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 56:77/q12 How many and which broad names does the Qur'an bear?  [لَقُرْءَانٌۭ · sciences]
turns on: Whether the names are counted individually or reduced to two fundamental names.
- 56:77/q12/p1 It bears many names and descriptions, with catalogues ranging beyond fifty. — Zarkashi reports al-Harali's more than ninety and Qadi Abu al-Ma'ali's fifty-five, including Book, Qur'an, Speech, Light, Guidance, Mercy, Criterion and Healing (44:2, 9:6, 4:174, 31:3, 10:58, 25:1, 17:82); his further catalogue includes admonition, reminder, noble, exalted, wisdom, wise, guardian, blessed and God's rope (10:57, 21:50, 56:77, 43:4, 54:5, 10:1, 5:48, 38:29, 3:103). Suyuti lists firmest handhold, truth, justice, command, herald, glad tidings, glorious, Psalms, bearer of good news and warning, mighty, proclamation and stories, citing 2:256, 31:22, 39:33, 6:115, 65:5, 3:193, 2:97, 27:2, 85:21, 21:105, 41:3–4, 41:41, 14:52, 12:3 and 80:13–14; Alusi reports Shidla's fifty-five and Suyuti's discussion. [4 notes: BURHAN; ITQAN; ALUSI-FULL(شيدلة)]
- 56:77/q12/p2 The many names ultimately reduce to Qur'an and Furqan. — Alusi compares this reduction to divine names returning to attributes of beauty and majesty. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 56:77/q13 What do the Qur'an's other titles say about it?  [لَقُرْءَانٌۭ · meaning]
turns on: The titles point to different functions and qualities rather than a single disputed etymology.
- 56:77/q13/p1 It clarifies, guides and distinguishes truth from falsehood. — Zarkashi explains guidance, clarification, manifest and Furqan; Furqan also distinguishes believers from unbelievers and hypocrites and explains Umar's title al-Faruq. Suyuti explains al-mubin similarly; 'guardian' testifies that earlier scriptures came from God and 'confirming' affirms prophets or scriptures before alteration. [7 notes: BURHAN; ITQAN]
- 56:77/q13/p2 It conveys remembrance, warning, glad tidings and the prophetic message. — Zarkashi says remembrance or dhikr recounts past nations and admonishes; dhikra recalls God's oneness on which believers were created; stories recount earlier peoples; bearer of glad tidings and warner promises Paradise and warns of Fire; message and balagh express the Prophet's delivery and informing people. Wahy is swift, secret communication by speech to prophets or angels, inspiration to bees, or ants' signs. [7 notes: BURHAN]
- 56:77/q13/p3 Its titles point to divine benefit, healing, mercy, light and wise rule. — Zarkashi says kalam impresses a new benefit on the listener's mind; light clarifies subtle lawful and unlawful matters; healing cures unbelief through faith and ignorance through learning and practice; understanding brings mercy; wise verses establish lawful and unlawful rulings, defy imitation and restrain indecency through practice. [5 notes: BURHAN]
- 56:77/q13/p4 Its titles mark its preservation, inimitability and descent from God. — Zarkashi says aziz means imitators cannot match its reports and recitation in striking composition (17:88), while another account says a reciter who practices it is not humiliated; Razi says those who turn from it retain nothing, unlike forgotten books. Majid means noble and free of addition, loss and alteration; the Qur'an is miraculous; tanzil means God made Gabriel hear and understand His speech as He willed, without modality, and Gabriel conveyed it. As hakim it also defies imitation. [7 notes: RAZI-FULL; BURHAN]
- 56:77/q13/p5 As hakim it makes a devoted reader independent of other sciences. — Razi states this distinct effect of engaging with the Qur'an wholeheartedly. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 56:77/q14 What is said about the Qur'an's opening letters?  [لَقُرْءَانٌۭ · rhetoric]
- 56:77/q14/p1 Each sura's opening letters suit the frequency of letters in that sura's vocabulary. — Zarkashi says that compared with similarly sized suras, words made from a sura's opening letters recur more often, making another opening unsuitable. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 56:77/q15 What examples are given of hidden insight drawn from Qur'anic verses?  [* · inward]
- 56:77/q15/p1 Ibn Barjan's reading of 30:2–4 was used to predict Jerusalem's conquest after Aleppo. — Ibn Khallikan reports that after Saladin took Aleppo in Safar, judge Muhyi al-Din recited a poem predicting Jerusalem's conquest in Rajab; the judge cited Ibn Barjan's tafsir, which the historian later found along with its calculation. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(إبن خلكان,القاضي محيي الدين)]
- 56:77/q15/p2 Ibn al-Kamal inferred Sultan Selim's conquest of Egypt from 21:105. — Alusi gives this as a well-known example. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(إبن الكمال)]
- 56:77/q15/p3 Sufi masters may possess valid insights beyond a reader's present understanding. — Alusi urges fairness toward masters of the Muhammadan spiritual circle, suspicion of one's own limitations, and compares trusting their perception to trusting those who sighted the crescent. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
