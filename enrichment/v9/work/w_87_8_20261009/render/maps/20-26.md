# 20:26: 22 questions

## 20:26/q01 What does 'make easy' mean?  [وَيَسِّرْ · meaning]
- 20:26/q01/p1 Make easy rather than difficult. — Al-Jishumī explicitly defines taysīr by its opposite, making difficult. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 20:26/q02 How is the request to make the affair easy constructed?  [وَيَسِّرْ لِىٓ أَمْرِى · grammar]
- 20:26/q02/p1 The verse uses 'make the affair easy for me,' a construction distinct from 'make me easy for something.' — Al-Samīn contrasts yassartuhu li-kadhā, illustrated by 92:7, with yassartu lahu kadhā here; the note's verse reference is garbled as '9, 2, :, 7.' [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 20:26/q03 What does 'for me' add to the request?  [لِىٓ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether lī primarily intensifies the expression, connects the benefit to Moses, or specifies how ease affects him.
- 20:26/q03/p1 It leaves the object initially implicit and then specifies 'my affair,' emphasizing the plea. — Al-Zamakhsharī says the general request followed by particulars is stronger than a direct request; al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī say the initial lī delays specification, and al-Ālūsī notes the clause would be complete without it. Al-Nasafī treats the repetition of general and particular as reinforcement; al-Suyūṭī explains that clarification after suspense strengthens understanding and pleasure. [5 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ITQAN]
- 20:26/q03/p2 It marks a stronger personal connection between Moses and the request. — Al-Ālūsī reports this view and compares 21:1; he says it is compatible with the emphasis through delayed specification. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 20:26/q03/p3 It shows that the benefit of the eased task returns to Moses. — Al-Intiṣāf says God is unaffected by the opening of Moses's chest; al-Ālūsī applies the same reasoning to easing his affair. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الانتصاف)]
- 20:26/q03/p4 It specifies ease for Moses personally while the mission retains its demands. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says lī specifies the task entrusted to Moses; Moses seeks personal ease, not a reduced or compromised mission. [2 notes: TABATABAI; TABATABAI+]

## 20:26/q04 How is the first-person yāʾ in 'for me' read?  [لِىٓ · readings]
turns on: The transmitted treatment of the first-person yāʾ by each reader.
- 20:26/q04/p1 It is opened, yielding liya before amrī. — Ibn Mujāhid assigns opening here to Nāfiʿ and Abū ʿAmr while noting their exceptions elsewhere; Ibn al-Jazarī adds Abū Jaʿfar and calls the group the two Medinese readers and Abū ʿAmr. Qiraat-ER states that the open vowel before amrī is a canonical variant. The lists also name other first-person yāʾs and their differing treatment. [5 notes: IBNMUJAHID(أبو عمرو,نافع); NASHR(المدنيان وأبو عمرو,نافع وأبو عمرو وأبو جعفر); QIRAAT-ER(Y-iḍ)]
- 20:26/q04/p2 It is kept still, yielding lī before amrī. — Ibn Mujāhid assigns sukūn here to Ibn Kathīr, and to Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī, Ibn ʿĀmir, and ʿĀṣim through Abū Bakr; their other listed first-person yāʾs vary or are all still respectively. Qiraat-ER says this too is canonical. [3 notes: IBNMUJAHID(ابن كثير,حمزة، الكسائي، ابن عامر، عاصم عن أبي بكر); QIRAAT-ER(Y-iḍ)]

## 20:26/q05 How should a recited yāʾ be articulated?  [لِىٓ · readings]
- 20:26/q05/p1 Give each yāʾ its proper light articulation and length, learned through oral practice. — Ibn al-Jazarī says a moving yāʾ should be gentle without becoming hamza, a madd yāʾ should receive its due length especially before another moving yāʾ, and a doubled yāʾ should remain one clear articulation without exaggerated stretching or constriction. Repetition with a teacher fixes the sound. [3 notes: NASHR]

## 20:26/q06 What is Moses asking God to ease in 'my affair'?  [أَمْرِى · interpretation]
turns on: The scope of 'my affair' and whether ease names the mission's means, its outcome, all prophetic duties, or Moses's own capacity.
- 20:26/q06/p1 His commissioned mission of conveying God's message to Pharaoh and calling him to truth. — Muqātil contrasts ease with difficulty; Ibn ʿAbbās calls it the task for which Moses was sent. Al-Ṭabrisī mentions entering a tyrant's presence; al-Bayḍāwī, al-Bursevī, and al-Ālūsī mention creating means and removing obstacles, and al-Ālūsī adds patience under trials and dependence on God. Ibn ʿĀshūr names steadiness, courage, eloquence, and persuasive proof; al-Iṣlāḥī asks for a conducive route; Abū Ḥayyān notes Pharaoh's tyranny and the hardships of divine vicegerency. Al-Suyūṭī says the mission's coming hardship makes the plea apt. Al-Qurṭubī links heart, task, and tongue as aids in conveying. Asad states the request generally. [20 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 20:26/q06/p2 The difficult mission remains intact, but God makes it manageable for Moses himself. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī stresses that Moses did not seek a smaller mission or relaxation of its demands; lī marks personal facilitation. [2 notes: TABATABAI; TABATABAI+]
- 20:26/q06/p3 All prophetic duties toward God and people, including revelation, worship, and preaching. — Al-Ālūsī reports a link to the whole divine address from 'I am your Lord': subtle knowledge, revelation, full worship, and preaching's hardships. Another account adds duties in the upper and lower worlds, whose demands can impede each other. Al-Ālūsī himself says the Pharaoh command implies all duties and the petition seeks help to fulfill them perfectly, without restricting the prayer to the earlier address. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); ALUSI-FULL+]
- 20:26/q06/p4 Securing acceptance of God's religion among the mission's audience and support against opponents. — Al-Qāshānī interprets ease through the intended audience's receptivity and aid against resistance. [1 notes: QASHANI]
- 20:26/q06/p5 Actual execution and attainment of the mission's goal, beyond inward readiness. — Al-Rāzī reports this reading as distinguishing the request for an open breast from the achieved result of the mission. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 20:26/q06/p6 The ability to bear hearing other people's speech after hearing God's speech. — Al-Qushayrī places the eased affair alongside expansion of the breast and the burden of returning from divine speech to human speech. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]

## 20:26/q07 How does God bring about the requested ease?  [وَيَسِّرْ أَمْرِى · theology]
turns on: Whether facilitating action is described as God's creation of ease and will, as grace activating motives, or as grace made fitting by the request.
- 20:26/q07/p1 God creates ease and the servant's effective will. — Al-Rāzī reports the Sunnī explanation. He argues no act happens without the servant's will; if the servant created that will, another will would be needed without end, so God creates it. He also reads breast expansion as recipient readiness and eased affair as effective agency, both granted by God's decree, wisdom, and power. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL(أهل السنة)]
- 20:26/q07/p2 God supplies motives and incentives through facilitative acts of grace. — Al-Rāzī reports this as the Muʿtazilite account. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(المعتزلة)]
- 20:26/q07/p3 The request can make further divine graces fitting at that time. — Al-Rāzī says God has already given every grace that could properly have been given, while some graces become fitting only after Moses asks. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 20:26/q08 How does the eased affair fit with the other requests?  [وَيَسِّرْ لِىٓ أَمْرِى · links]
- 20:26/q08/p1 It follows inner expansion and precedes effective speech in a sequence from readiness to conveying the mission. — Al-Rāzī says breast expansion gives spiritual light, ease helps acquire it, and the loosened tongue conveys it; the soul receives unseen forms through thought and teaches through speech. Al-Nasafī and al-Biqāʿī say the eased affair clarifies or reinforces the general request for an expanded breast. Al-Qurṭubī groups heart, task, and tongue as aid to delivery. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 20:26/q08/p2 The prayer reflects the particular demand of being sent to Pharaoh in this sūra. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī contrasts Ṭā Hā's atāhā, where Moses is sent only to Pharaoh, with the broader mission in al-Naml; Moses then asks for breast expansion and ease. He cites 20:11, 20:24, and 27:12. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT]

## 20:26/q09 What inward meanings are given to Moses's requests?  [وَيَسِّرْ لِىٓ أَمْرِى · inward]
turns on: Whether the requests are read as self-effacement in divine knowledge, ascent to encounter, or acquisition of good character.
- 20:26/q09/p1 They seek self-effacement, knowledge of God, and speech confined to what comes from Him. — Ibn ʿAṭāʾ asks to see none but God, act and speak by knowledge of Him, lose self-regard, speak of God's favor and nearness, open the breast to hear Him, ease the affair by abiding with Him, and loosen the ego's knot from the tongue. The reports vary in expression while sharing this inward course. [3 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء)]
- 20:26/q09/p2 They seek a progression into direct encounter with God. — Al-Ḥusayn describes capacity for confrontation, an eased course to God by God, and a loosened tongue; once granted, Moses sees only God's part in himself and receives all he asked. He refers to 20:36. [1 notes: SULAMI(الحسين)]
- 20:26/q09/p3 They ask only for good character traits. — Al-Sulamī reports this concise reading from al-Junayd. [1 notes: SULAMI(الجنيد)]

## 20:26/q10 Why does Moses make these requests?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes give practical preparation, prolonged divine conversation, and nearness through prayer as motives.
- 20:26/q10/p1 The mission's burden makes him seek the means and strength needed to fulfill it. — Al-Qushayrī says obligation presupposes ability and Moses asks for the necessary preparation. Al-Rāzī notes his weakness, limited means, and powerful opponent, treating prophethood as a fifth gift with mission as its corresponding service. Al-Iṣlāḥī contrasts Moses's felt weight of leadership with worldly leaders' hunger for status. Kuranyolu says Moses fears failure and seeks grace; God accepts his requests for heart, speech, ease, and Aaron's help. [4 notes: QUSHAYRI; RAZI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 20:26/q10/p2 He continues asking because he cannot readily leave conversation with God. — Al-Qushayrī reports that Moses prolongs his prayers through the remaining requests because he can hardly stop speaking with God. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]
- 20:26/q10/p3 Supplication itself brings nearness to God. — Al-Rāzī says Moses prays here seeking nearness and promises to establish that prayer brings it. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 20:26/q11 What does the prayer teach later readers?  [* · interpretation]
- 20:26/q11/p1 Callers to divine unity should rely on God under every condition. — Kuranyolu applies episodes in Moses's life especially to the Prophet and to all callers to divine unity. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 20:26/q11/p2 It is a prayer Muslims recite in difficulty and anxiety. — The Study Quran reports this common devotional use. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 20:26/q12 What caused Moses's speech impediment, and how was he spared?  [* · reports]
- 20:26/q12/p1 As a child he put a coal in his mouth during a test after pulling Pharaoh's beard. — Yaḥyā ibn Sallām says God later granted Moses's requests. Pharaoh intended to kill the child, but his wife said he lacked understanding and offered a date and a coal; Moses chose the coal. Mujāhid says Pharaoh called him an enemy and his wife devised the test to divert punishment. The accounts vary in emphasis, not in the test's outcome. [3 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(مجاهد)]

## 20:26/q13 What report shows the Prophet reciting this prayer?  [* · reports]
- 20:26/q13/p1 Asmāʾ bint ʿUmays saw him pray with Moses's requests at Mount Thabīr. — The reported prayer asks for an expanded breast, easy task, clear speech, and Aaron's support so the two can worship and remember God abundantly. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(أسماء بنت عميس)]

## 20:26/q14 How had God prepared Moses's mission before this prayer?  [* · links]
- 20:26/q14/p1 God gave him access to Pharaoh's household and saved him after the killing of a man. — Kuranyolu says Moses's upbringing in Pharaoh's house prepared access to Pharaoh, and divine favor saved him from the killing's consequences so he could free Israel; it cites 2:49, 2:59, and 28:3. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 20:26/q15 What relation between divine perfection and creating others does al-Rāzī discuss here?  [* · theology]
- 20:26/q15/p1 God is eternally perfect in Himself and perfects others in time without any eternal defect. — Al-Rāzī resumes his discussion of supplication under 2:286. He calls perfection in oneself and perfecting others the highest degree; eternally perfecting what is already complete is impossible, so its absence implies no deficiency. Other impossibilities, such as making a self or counting endless motions, likewise do not imply incapacity. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 20:26/q16 Why does God bring some possibilities into existence and not all?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether selective creation implies arbitrary withholding when human standards are applied to God's acts.
- 20:26/q16/p1 Creating all possibilities is impossible and would remove the distinction between capable agency and natural necessity. — Al-Rāzī says possible nonexistents are infinite, so creating all would put an infinity into existence; if all were created, no later creation could occur, seemingly turning capacity into incapacity. Necessarily producing all things would resemble natural necessity, which he calls deficient, rather than free capacity. He says divine mercy's general hospitality extends existence, invoking 7:156. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 20:26/q16/p2 Selective creation seems arbitrary or ungenerous toward the infinitely many left nonexistent. — Al-Rāzī reports the banquet objection: the few existent recipients against infinitely many excluded ones imply either unexplained entitlement or purposeless selection. Another objection says giving and withholding appear neither generous nor stingy, seemingly unworthy of the Most Generous. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; RAZI-FULL-]
- 20:26/q16/p3 Human categories of choosing and generosity do not govern God's action. — Al-Rāzī rejects the objections as projections of human choice and cites 21:23: God is not questioned about His acts, whereas people are. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 20:26/q17 How does al-Rāzī describe mercy's successive gifts?  [* · theology]
- 20:26/q17/p1 Mercy grants existence, then life, then power, then reason and guidance to selected recipients. — Al-Rāzī says animals exceed inanimates through awareness and tool use; life to inanimacy is like light to darkness and existence to nonexistence. Living beings perceive pleasure, pain, good, and evil, so power lets them seek benefit and escape harm. God grants power to some living beings as life to some existents and existence to some nonexistents. Reason completes four gifts, granting insight and guidance; he calls the last gift best by analogy with the final Prophet and humans among bodily creatures. Innate truths guide like stars; reason infers a maker from created knowledge and gifts. He cites a saying to the intellect about humiliation, reward, and punishment without grading it. [9 notes: RAZI-FULL(الله تعالى)]

## 20:26/q18 How are divine gifts matched with worship and mission in al-Rāzī's discussion?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the parallel stops at four gifts and acts of prayer or adds prophethood and its mission.
- 20:26/q18/p1 Four gifts correspond to four services of prayer. — Al-Rāzī reports an interpretation pairing existence, life, power, and reason with standing, recitation, bowing, and prostration, citing 20:14. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 20:26/q18/p2 Prophethood is a fifth gift, matched by service in the prophetic mission. — Al-Rāzī reports this further reading; Moses asks for help because his means are limited, his opponent powerful, and he is weak before the task. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 20:26/q20 What do the attached notes say about strange desert perceptions and unseen beings?  [* · theology]
turns on: How to weigh perceptual distortion against scriptural and transmitted testimony about unseen beings.
- 20:26/q20/p1 Some apparent forms and sounds may be distortions of ordinary perceptions. — Ibn Qutayba cites unattributed verses about a night echo and indistinct humanlike speech; al-Akhṭal's fox seeming an armored horse; al-Nābigha's elevated herder seeming a bird; and Ibn Aḥmar's multiplied shapes and pecking chameleon. He allows unusual desert events but says they do not disprove what people really hear and see. [6 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL(ابن أحمر,الأخطل,النابغة,شاعر مجهول)]
- 20:26/q20/p2 Jinn, Satanic contact, and unseen realities must be accepted with the scriptural and reported evidence. — Ibn Qutayba says scripture treats jinn as accountable people, addresses them, calls them men, and mentions human relations (72:6; 55:74); a group heard the Qur'an and warned its people (46:29), while 2:275 describes Satanic touch. He cites sound reports on unseen attendants and argues accepting jinn and Satan entails possible possession, sounds, and forms. He compares evasive belief to someone claiming only a morning drink while secretly gulping it. Belief in resurrection, prophetic report, and divine questioning also entails accepting barzakh torment and angels' grave questioning. He rejects calling Arabs universally ignorant or dishonest because scripture, prophets, earlier revelations, and other peoples corroborate them. [7 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL]

## 20:26/q21 How do the attached notes explain the Qur'an's seven letters and their tribal language?  [* · readings]
turns on: Which dialects supply the seven expressions, especially whether distinctively Yemeni forms are included.
- 20:26/q21/p1 The seven letters are forms drawn from seven Arabian tribal dialects, chiefly the relatively unaltered central dialects. — Al-Qāḍī permits transmitted differing forms. Abū ʿUbayd and others explain the letters as seven tribal languages, each contributing part. Ibn ʿAṭiyya begins with Quraysh and Banū Saʿd, then nearby Meccan tribes; his fuller list includes Kināna, Hudhayl, Thaqīf, Khuzāʿa, Asad, Ḍabba and associates, Tamīm, Qays, and related groups. Thābit ibn Qāsim gives a seven-group list: Quraysh, Kināna, Asad, Hudhayl, Tamīm, Ḍabba and associates, Qays. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says their eloquence and freedom from foreign influence made inimitability evident; central Ḥijāz, Najd, and Tihāma had less foreign contact. He says Iraq's neighboring languages, northern Syria, and later Mecca, Medina, and Ṭāʾif were affected by outsiders; later Basran and Kufan philologists preferred intermediate tribes, though the cities were purer in the Prophet's time. Seven tribal expressions may render one meaning with different eloquence and concision; Ibn ʿAbbās learned the meaning of faṭara from a Bedouin's claim to originate a well. [11 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبو عبيد وغيره,القاضي,ثابت بن قاسم)]
- 20:26/q21/p2 Distinctively Yemeni language is excluded, except words adopted by Ḥijāz Arabs. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya disputes the broader Yemeni inclusion attributed to Abū ʿUbayd and al-Mubarrad; he says distinctive expressions such as al-zakhīkh and al-qalūbī do not occur in the Qur'an. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL+]

## 20:26/q22 How does al-Ālūsī explain the neighboring request for an opened chest?  [* · interpretation]
- 20:26/q22/p1 The chest means the heart, the faculty or means of perception. — Al-Ālūsī reports the formal scholars' alternatives: the heart itself perceives, or it is the means by which perception occurs. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(علماء الرسوم)]
