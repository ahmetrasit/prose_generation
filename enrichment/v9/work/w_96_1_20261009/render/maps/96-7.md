# 96:7: 20 questions

## 96:7/q01 What does an do in this clause?  [أَن · grammar]
- 96:7/q01/p1 It introduces the cause of transgression: because he sees himself self-sufficient. — Al-Akhfash, as reported by Wahidi and Razi, supplies an omitted causal lam; Qurtubi and Ibn Ashur likewise attach the clause to yatgha. Corpus Coranicum calls it explanatory and causal and compares 43:5. Biqai says the emphatic formulation meets people's denial of their own tyranny. [13 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(الأخفش); RAZI(الأخفش); RAZI-FULL(الأخفش); BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 96:7/q02 Who does the pronoun in ra'ahu denote, and what kind of seeing permits this construction?  [رَّءَاهُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether ra'a is restricted here to mental judgment or may also be visual perception; the latter changes istaghna from second object to circumstantial clause.
- 96:7/q02/p1 The human sees or judges himself inwardly; cognitive ra'a takes the coreferential pronoun and istaghna as its second object. — Farra distinguishes two-complement cognition verbs from ordinary one-object verbs, for which Arabic uses nafs, citing forms such as azunnuni and ra'aytuni; he calls the self word omitted. Tabari, Nahhas, Farisi, Zamakhshari, Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Alusi and Ibn Ashur give the subject-and-predicate or two-object analysis. Kashshaf and Nasafi exclude physical sight on their grammatical account. Ibn Atiyya calls it perception close to knowledge. Ibn Ashur cites 17:62; Corpus Coranicum notes Tabari's parallel. Farra notes that forms like adimtuni are not standard, while Alusi and Ibn Ashur mention faqada and adima as exceptional parallels. [42 notes: FARRA; TAB; TAB-FULL; NAHHAS; FARISI-HUJJA; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء); KASHSHAF; IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI(الفراء); RAZI-FULL(الفراء); QURTUBI(الفراء); QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR(الفراء); TABATABAI; CORPUSCORANICUM; WAHIDI-QT; BAGHAWI]
- 96:7/q02/p2 Visual seeing can also have the shared subject and object; istaghna then describes the person's apparent state. — Samin and Alusi report grammarians who extend the mental construction to sight, citing Aisha's account of having only the two black foods and poetry about seeing oneself a target for spears. Ibn Ashur names Qatari ibn al-Fuja'a for the spear line and al-Nimr ibn Tawlab for a rare parallel. Alusi parses istaghna as a circumstantial clause: outward appearance alone can provoke arrogance; he cites 42:27. [10 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI(جماعة,عائشة,قائل); ALUSI-FULL(جماعة); IBNASHUR]

## 96:7/q03 How is ra'ahu read?  [رَّءَاهُ · readings]
turns on: The transmitted consonantal length and the scope of imala; the short form's status turns on its transmission and whether rare Arabic deletion justifies it.
- 96:7/q03/p1 The full form retains the alif, with open vowels rather than imala. — Ibn Mujahid assigns open vowels to Nafi and Hafs; Farisi says neither inclines the ra. Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and Ibn Ashur say most readers retain the alif; Qurtubi prefers lengthening. Nashr says al-Zaynabi alone transmitted Qunbul's full form, while Ibn Mujahid may have refused it as an error according to the report there. [11 notes: IBNMUJAHID(ابن عامر وعاصم في رواية أبي بكر وحمزة والكسائي); FARISI-HUJJA(نافع وحفص عن عاصم); IBNATIYYA(الجمهور); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الجمهور); ABUHAYYAN(الجمهور); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; NASHR(ابن مجاهد); IBNASHUR(ابن مجاهد عن قنبل); QURTUBI-FULL(مجاهد وحميد وقنبل عن ابن كثير)+; QURTUBI(مجاهد)+]
- 96:7/q03/p2 The full form has imala on the ra, with the alif retained. — Ibn Mujahid and Farisi attribute this to Ibn Amir, Abu Bakr from Asim, Hamza and Kisa'i. Farisi explains the reported kasra of ra as imala carried back from hamza and notes that some reliable readers incline ra, hamza and alif together. [6 notes: IBNMUJAHID(ابن عامر وعاصم في رواية أبي بكر وحمزة والكسائي); FARISI-HUJJA(ابن عامر وعاصم في رواية أبي بكر وحمزة والكسائي); IBNATIYYA(الجمهور); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الجمهور)]
- 96:7/q03/p3 Abu Amr opens the ra and inclines or kasra-marks the hamza. — Ibn Mujahid, Farisi and Jishumi report the vowel combination; Farisi distinguishes the hamza's imala from the open ra. [4 notes: IBNMUJAHID(أبو عمرو); FARISI-HUJJA(أبو عمرو); JISHUMI(أبو عمرو)]
- 96:7/q03/p4 A transmitted short form omits the alif after hamza. — Ibn Mujahid calls Qunbul's form erroneous; Farisi argues rare deletion in an irregular imperfect, Ru'bah's wasani, and metathesized poetic man ra do not license it, citing 12:31 and a jussive poetic form. Ibn Atiyya calls it lightening or deletion of the weak final radical. Qurtubi also names Mujahid and Humayd. Abu Hayyan and Samin defend a transmitted form with Arabic parallels, including al-Ajjaj's wasani and law tara ahl Makka, even if non-analogical. Nashr says most Qunbul transmitters, including Ibn Mujahid and Ibn Shanabudh, gave the short form, while critics disputed Ibn Mujahid's report and cited al-Khuza'i; Nashr answers that suspected mistakes need not be passed on. Ibn Ashur rejects dismissing a form explicitly ascribed to Qunbul but says no one else reports it. Alusi relays al-Bahr's defense. Qiraat-ER says it changes prosody but not root meaning. [20 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(رؤبة); IBNATIYYA(ابن كثير من طريق قنبل); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن كثير من طريق قنبل); ABUHAYYAN(الجمهور); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(العجاج); NASHR(الناس); ALUSI-FULL(قنبل); IBNASHUR(ابن مجاهد عن قنبل); QIRAAT-ER; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; ABUHAYYAN+; ALUSI(البحر)+; IBNASHUR+; SAMIN-DURR(قنبل)+; IBNMUJAHID(ابن كثير عن قنبل)-; FARISI-HUJJA(ابن كثير فيما قرأت على قنبل)-; QURTUBI(مجاهد)-; QURTUBI-FULL(مجاهد وحميد وقنبل عن ابن كثير)-; ALUSI(قنبل)-; NASHR(ابن مجاهد)-]

## 96:7/q04 What self-sufficiency does istaghna describe?  [ٱسْتَغْنَىٰٓ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the perceived support is money, one's own effort, or supposed independence from the Lord; some notes distinguish the felt state from its arrogant consequence.
- 96:7/q04/p1 He feels wealthy or financially secure. — Mawardi offers wealth as one alternative; Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Alusi and Ibn Ashur describe riches. Ibn Ashur says the sin and ta intensify the sense, comparing istajaba and istaqarra; Corpus Coranicum prefers the state of considering oneself sufficient over translating the word as acting arrogantly. Alusi calls money the apparent, familiar opposite of poverty. [13 notes: MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL(الكلبي); WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI; IBNASHUR; ALUSI-FULL+; CORPUSCORANICUM+; RAZI-; RAZI-FULL-; BINTSHATI-]
- 96:7/q04/p2 He imagines himself independent of God, whether through wealth, clan, or strength. — Ibn Abbas as reported by Mawardi says independent of his Lord. Tabrisi lists kin, wealth and force but insists the belief, not actual possessions, produces tyranny. Qurtubi and Alusi report clan, helpers and supporters; Alusi finds this less apparent than financial wealth. Bint al-Shati associates this claim with acquired knowledge, not money or status. Study Quran calls claimed independence from God the root of rebellion. [14 notes: MAWARDI(ابن عباس); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس); TABRISI; QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; ALUSI(قيل); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; BINTSHATI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 96:7/q04/p3 He supposes his wealth comes from his own striving and power. — Razi prefers this account and reads the sin as seeking. He argues effort can end in starvation and the rich will learn the gift was not their own power; Solomon and Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf were rich yet humble, while greater property gives a rational person more reasons to depend on God. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; RAZI+]
- 96:7/q04/p4 He sees himself as needing no one or as already fulfilled. — Ibn Qutayba, as reported by Ibn Jawzi, says he sees himself as needing nobody. Elmalili includes the sense of being already fulfilled; Biqai describes becoming blind to a need after feeling independent of its object. [4 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة); BIQAI; ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 96:7/q04/p5 The word denotes intensified sufficiency, opposite to need, rather than arrogance itself. — Ibn Ashur derives intensity from the added sin and ta. Bint al-Shati contrasts sufficiency with need and cites 93:8; Corpus Coranicum recommends translating the perception of sufficiency, since arrogant behavior follows from it. [3 notes: IBNASHUR; BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM+]

## 96:7/q05 Who is the human described here?  [* · referent]
turns on: Whether the general noun denotes humanity as a recurring type or points first to the reported Meccan antagonist.
- 96:7/q05/p1 Human beings generally are vulnerable to this pattern. — Tabari speaks of a person given creation, knowledge and favors; Qushayri, Tabrisi, Ibn Kathir, Biqai, Qutb and Kuranyolu extend it to people generally. Bint al-Shati reports Razi's generic reading. Elmalili says Abu Jahl exemplifies a general warning. Faith or God's protection can restrain it, according to Qutb and Ibn Atiyya. [19 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB; TAB-FULL; QUSHAYRI; IBNATIYYA; TABRISI; IBNKATHIR; BIQAI; ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; BINTSHATI(الفخر الرازي); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 96:7/q05/p2 Abu Jahl is the immediate example or occasion. — Zajjaj, Kalbi, Muqatil, Damghani, Ibn Jawzi and Kuranyolu identify him. Wahidi and Baghawi say he upgraded clothing, mount, food and drink when rich; Ibn Ashur confines his direct story to 96:9 onward and sees 96:6-8 preparing for it. Qurtubi also reports an Abu Jahl occasion. [15 notes: ZAJJAJ; MAWARDI(الكلبي); MAWARDI-FULL(الكلبي); WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); DAMGHANI; BAGHAWI(مقاتل); BAGHAWI-FULL(مقاتل); IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(بعض المفسرين); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Müfessirlerin çoğunluğu)]
- 96:7/q05/p3 The warning can cover Abu Jahl and his companions, or Pharaoh's divine claim. — Bursevi mentions these as possible concrete descriptions. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 96:7/q06 Why does perceived sufficiency lead to transgression?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the mechanism is felt financial or social security, claiming credit for one's wealth, or imagined independence through acquired knowledge.
- 96:7/q06/p1 Feeling rich or independent breeds arrogance, disbelief and exceeding one's bounds. — Tabari contrasts this with God's gifts of sound creation, knowledge and favors; Ibn Kathir adds joy and boasting. Qushayri says witnessing one's actual need prevents it. Tabrisi says belief rather than actual wealth is decisive; Alusi's visual parse makes even the appearance of wealth enough and cites 42:27. Biqai says people change conduct toward what they think they no longer need. Ibn Ashur traces contempt and abuse to the belief that others need the rich man and that weapons, servants and allies protect him; religious or rational restraint can check this. Qutb stresses failure to thank the giver. Corpus Coranicum distinguishes perceived sufficiency from arrogance itself. [44 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB; TAB-FULL; SULAMI(ابن عطاء); QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA; TABRISI; QURTUBI; BAYDAWI-FULL; QASHANI; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 96:7/q06/p2 Transgression follows a false claim that wealth came from one's own effort rather than divine gift. — Razi prefers this over treating wealth as inevitably corrupting. Solomon and Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf had wealth without arrogance; people who work hard may starve, and possessing riches increases the need to preserve oneself, property and dependents. [6 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; RAZI+]
- 96:7/q06/p3 Acquired knowledge can make a person imagine independence from the Creator. — Bint al-Shati rejects explaining the passage by absorption in money or status and says knowledge tempts the human to exceed his creaturely place; she notes the shift from knowledge and status to the warning. [2 notes: BINTSHATI; BINTSHATI+]
- 96:7/q06/p4 Wealth and knowledge both can breed tyranny; ascetic discipline protects against desire. — Yahya ibn Muadh says hunger helps asceticism, which guards worship; Ibn Ata says wealth injures one whose rightful condition is poverty and prompts insolence toward the Enricher. [2 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء,يحيى بن معاذ)]

## 96:7/q07 How does the transgression appear in conduct?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes apply the general transgression either to outward luxury, unbelief and abuse, Quraysh's rejection, or the later act of forbidding prayer.
- 96:7/q07/p1 It appears as arrogance, disbelief, ingratitude and overstepping toward God and people. — Tabari says gifts are met with disbelief; Ibn Kathir calls it exultation and boasting. Ibn Ashur traces contempt and abuse to feeling protected by servants, weapons and allies. Qutb lists rebellion and lack of gratitude; Kuranyolu adds boasting, disobedience and a claim to control one's fate. [13 notes: TAB; TAB-FULL; SULAMI(ابن عطاء); IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 96:7/q07/p2 A newly wealthy man, identified by Muqatil as Abu Jahl, upgrades dress, mount, food and drink as he rises in status. — Kalbi and Qatada describe changing clothing, food and other comforts; Muqatil specifies Abu Jahl and his mount. The versions vary in mentioning drink and whether the subject is generic or named. [6 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); BAGHAWI(الكلبي,مقاتل); BAGHAWI-FULL(الكلبي,مقاتل); TABRISI(قتادة)]
- 96:7/q07/p3 Among the Quraysh it appears as rejecting guidance while disguising love of the world behind objections. — Islahi says wealth makes them indifferent to God and that the stated objections conceal this motive. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 96:7/q07/p4 The transgressor can forbid worship instead of following guidance and enjoining piety. — Bint al-Shati reports Razi's generic interpretation linking the transgression to the following depiction of a man who bars worship. [1 notes: BINTSHATI(الفخر الرازي)]

## 96:7/q08 What happened when Abu Jahl threatened the Prophet at prayer?  [* · reports]
- 96:7/q08/p1 He threatened to trample the praying Prophet, retreated before a fiery barrier and angels, and was spared their seizure. — Abu Hurayra reports a trench of fire, terror and wings; the Prophet said angels would have seized or torn him apart. Ibn Abi Hatim and Ibn Kathir note uncertainty whether the verse's revelation was named within Abu Hurayra's report. Ibn Kathir names Ahmad, Muslim, Nasa'i and Ibn Abi Hatim as transmitters through Mu'tamir. Kuranyolu treats the event as occasioning 96:6-19; Zajjaj and Elmalili also connect Abu Jahl's threat to this passage, with Elmalili naming Bukhari and Tirmidhi for Ibn Abbas's version. [5 notes: ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(أبو هريرة); IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو هريرة); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Müfessirlerin çoğunluğu); ELMALILI(İbn Abbas)]

## 96:7/q09 What does the report about Mecca's mountains say?  [* · reports]
- 96:7/q09/p1 Abu Jahl asked for mountains of gold, or gold and silver, to make his people rich; Gabriel offered it with a warning, and the Prophet declined. — Qurtubi's Ibn Abbas via Abu Salih version says mountains of gold, polytheists gaining wealth, transgressing and abandoning their religion; Alusi's wording adds silver and Quraysh. The threatened punishment is likened to the people of the Table if they disbelieved afterward. Both say the Prophet refused to spare them, knowing they would not accept in Qurtubi's account. No grading is supplied by Alusi. [4 notes: QURTUBI(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس); ALUSI(أبو جهل); ALUSI-FULL]

## 96:7/q10 How do these verses relate to the first revelation and Abu Jahl's later opposition?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether the Abu Jahl incident dates the whole sura or only later material, and where its direct application begins.
- 96:7/q10/p1 The first five verses came earlier; later verses about Abu Jahl were joined to them. — Qurtubi compares the commanded placement with 2:281 appended to earlier al-Baqara verses. Elmalili reports later revelation on Abu Jahl's threat. Ibn Ashur reports the objection that obligatory prayer began only at the Night Journey, motivating an interval and later attachment. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; ELMALILI(İbn Abbas); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:7/q10/p2 The whole sura may have been revealed when Abu Jahl forbade prayer. — Qurtubi records this alternative, which would put the whole sura after the first revelations. Ibn Ashur reports that some consequently deny this sura was first revelation. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:7/q10/p3 Verses 96:6-8 introduce the general vice, while 96:9 onward directly addresses Abu Jahl. — Ibn Ashur reconciles the accounts by treating human transgression as preparation for the concrete opponent. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 96:7/q11 Who is addressed by the rebuke kalla, and how does it connect to this verse?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether kalla points forward to a rebuked human opponent or continues the address to the Prophet; its value as rebuke or affirmation also matters.
- 96:7/q11/p1 Kalla rebukes human ingratitude and the later opponent who bars prayer. — Tabari says it rebukes receiving God's gifts with disbelief. Baydawi connects it to rejection of favor; Kuranyolu calls it a negative restraint against arrogance and turning from truth. Ibn Ashur says an initial rebuke can point forward to the man forbidding prayer, comparing an initial particle in Imru al-Qays. [5 notes: TAB; BAYDAWI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:7/q11/p2 The Prophet remains the addressee: he is warned to continue the command to read. — Elmalili prefers this because it preserves continuity with the opening; the warning about humanity and Abu Jahl then follows. He reports an alternative taking the warning as first addressed to Abu Jahl but says it disconnects the opening. [3 notes: ELMALILI; ELMALILI+]
- 96:7/q11/p3 Abu Jahl is first addressed by the warning. — Elmalili reports an interpretation attaching the warning to the relative phrase and objects that it disconnects the beginning of the sura. [1 notes: ELMALILI; ELMALILI-]
- 96:7/q11/p4 Kalla can be affirmative, meaning indeed, while the address stays with the Prophet. — Elmalili reports this reading as another way to maintain the Prophet's address. [1 notes: ELMALILI]

## 96:7/q12 What contrast does Ibn Masud draw when citing these verses?  [* · reports]
- 96:7/q12/p1 The scholar and worldly seeker are both insatiable, but knowledge brings divine approval while worldly pursuit deepens transgression. — Ibn Abi Hatim, Ibn Kathir and Durr transmit the comparison and connect it with 35:28 on learned fear of God. Ibn Kathir also gives a version raised to the Prophet that states the two are insatiable; the repeated Durr entries occur under several verses. [11 notes: IBNABIHATIM(ابن مسعود,عبد الله); IBNKATHIR(عبد الله); IBNKATHIR-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم,عبد الله بن مسعود); DURR(ابن مسعود)]

## 96:7/q13 What warnings about future prosperity are associated with this verse?  [* · reports]
turns on: One set warns about a community's future wealth; another illustrates the recurring human appetite behind that warning.
- 96:7/q13/p1 The Prophet warned that abundance and competition for worldly goods could destroy his community as they destroyed earlier peoples. — Burhan reports fear of worldly adornment and cites 6:32. Biqai transmits forecasts of conquest, regular rich meals and worldly competition; some listeners judged former days better for worship, but the Prophet corrected them that their present poverty was better. Biqai also applies it to the community receiving earth's treasures. [5 notes: BURHAN(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); BIQAI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); BIQAI-FULL]
- 96:7/q13/p2 Reports about an unfilled appetite illustrate how satisfaction invites further desire. — Biqai cites the saying that only dust fills a human belly and the last person to leave Hell, who repeatedly asks for more after promising not to; the versions vary in emphasis on the final abode and successive gifts. [4 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL]

## 96:7/q14 What response to the warning should a person cultivate?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The sources emphasize either grateful awareness of need or active restraint of the conduct that perceived power produces.
- 96:7/q14/p1 Recognize dependence on God, thank Him for gifts, and restrain pride and desire. — Qushayri says seeing one's need instead of admiring oneself prevents transgression; Yahya ibn Muadh recommends asceticism and hunger to cut desire. Qutb says wealth should bring recognition and gratitude. Kuranyolu stresses that God owns and may remove every resource and that humans also need society. Ibn Kathir says God will question how wealth was acquired and spent; Elmalili points to inevitable return to the Lord. [11 notes: QUSHAYRI; SULAMI(ابن عطاء,يحيى بن معاذ); IBNKATHIR-FULL; ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 96:7/q14/p2 Religious or rational restraint must check the powerful person's contempt and abuse. — Ibn Ashur explains how perceived protection through servants, arms and allies leads to mistreatment and calls the verse a psychological warning. [2 notes: IBNASHUR]

## 96:7/q15 How does this warning relate to knowledge at the sura's beginning?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether knowledge in the opening functions chiefly as the praised alternative to riches or as another gift that can tempt self-importance.
- 96:7/q15/p1 The opening praises knowledge, while the ending condemns wealth and urges learning and religion over worldly riches. — Razi names 96:4-5 and 96:6 as the contrast. Tabari and Qutb also recall that the transgressor received knowledge and other favors from God, making disbelief ingratitude. [5 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; TAB; QUTB-ZILAL]
- 96:7/q15/p2 Acquired knowledge can itself become a ground for arrogant self-sufficiency. — Bint al-Shati reads the movement from bestowed knowledge to the human's pride in knowledge and status; her warning invokes one's place as a creature. Qashani also warns against claiming independent perfection after receiving knowledge. [3 notes: BINTSHATI; QASHANI]

## 96:7/q16 What inward lesson is drawn from perceived self-sufficiency?  [* · inward]
turns on: Whether the inner danger is the veil of riches and desire or the self's claim to perfection through knowledge.
- 96:7/q16/p1 Seeing oneself as rich veils one's need and breeds tyranny; remembering poverty before God counters it. — Ibn Ata says wealth harms one whose proper state is poverty and makes him insolent toward the Enricher; Yahya ibn Muadh recommends asceticism and hunger against sinful desire. Qushayri says the person who witnesses need instead of admiring self does not transgress. [4 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء,يحيى بن معاذ); QUSHAYRI]
- 96:7/q16/p2 God's gift of knowledge must not be taken as independent spiritual perfection. — Qashani applies the passage to the Prophet before firm establishment in abiding, warning against selfhood and the claim of independent perfection. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 96:7/q17 How is istaghna linked with piety elsewhere in the Qur'an?  [* · links]
- 96:7/q17/p1 Self-sufficiency contrasts with taqwa and fear of God. — Corpus Coranicum compares 92:5 and 92:8-9, 80:5 and 80:9, plus 64:6 and 96:12, and notes an implicit contrast resembling a theme in late antique Christian monastic piety. Bint al-Shati cites 93:8 for need and sufficiency. [3 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM; BINTSHATI]

## 96:7/q18 What is said of the sura's place and length?  [* · sciences]
- 96:7/q18/p1 The sura is Meccan and has seventeen verses. — Jishumi supplies both classifications. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 96:7/q19 What virtue is reported for reciting this sura?  [* · sciences]
- 96:7/q19/p1 A reciter receives a reward as though reciting all the shorter suras. — Baydawi Full reports this promise from the Prophet without a grading in the note. [1 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 96:7/q20 Why is wealth set before children in a cited comparison?  [* · links]
- 96:7/q20/p1 Wealth is the more persistent temptation and can prompt transgression. — Ibn al-Hajib, reported in Itqan, says spouses precede children because spouses are more often enemies, and wealth precedes children because its temptation is more constant. [1 notes: ITQAN(ابن الحاجب)]
