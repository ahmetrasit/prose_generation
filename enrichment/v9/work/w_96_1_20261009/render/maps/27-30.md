# 27:30: 30 questions

## 27:30/q01 What do the two pronouns in 'it is' refer to?  [إِنَّهُۥ وَإِنَّهُۥ · referent]
- 27:30/q01/p1 The first refers to the letter; the second to its writing or contents. — Ibn al-Jawzi and al-Alusi distinguish the letter from its opening or unmentioned contents. Al-Baydawi allows the first to mean the letter or its heading, and the second its text or content. Al-Mawardi, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, al-Tabrisi and others explain that the queen identifies its sender and then its opening. [9 notes: MUQATIL; IBNABIHATIM(السدي); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 27:30/q02 Why are the two clauses introduced with emphatic inna?  [إِنَّهُۥ وَإِنَّهُۥ · rhetoric]
- 27:30/q02/p1 They answer implied questions about who sent the noble letter and what it contains. — Al-Kashshaf calls the second clause a resumption explaining the contents; al-Razi, al-Nasafi, Bursevi, al-Samin and al-Alusi identify the implied questions. Al-Alusi says the inferred questions warrant emphatic inna; al-Qurtubi allows an implied 'the speech' or a fresh sentence with the usual kasra. [9 notes: NAHHAS; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(العامة); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 27:30/q03 How are the two inna clauses read and grammatically construed?  [إِنَّهُۥ وَإِنَّهُۥ · readings]
turns on: The transmitted kasra, fatha, conjunction and Ubayy variants require different links to the preceding 'letter' and different treatments of an; al-Samin weighs the grammatical analyses.
- 27:30/q03/p1 Both have kasra, making independent explanatory statements. — Al-Farra says both instances are read with kasra. Al-Qurtubi supplies an implicit 'the speech' or treats the second as a new sentence. Al-Samin says the common reading answers implied questions; Abu Hayyan says most readers use kasra. [4 notes: FARRA; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(العامة)]
- 27:30/q03/p2 Both hamzas have fatha, with the clauses appositional to 'letter.' — Ibn Abi Abla and Ikrima transmit the reading. Al-Farra and al-Nahhas permit apposition; al-Razi and al-Baydawi explain substitution for kitab. Al-Samin calls substitution, by inclusion or full substitution, the strongest analysis; Makki reports al-Farra allowed the form in speech, and al-Samin notes it is also transmitted as a reading. [13 notes: FARRA; NAHHAS(الفراء); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن أبي عبلة); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(عكرمة، وابن أبي عبلة); SAMIN-DURR(عكرمة,مكي); ALUSI-FULL(عكرمة وابن أبي عبلة); SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء)+]
- 27:30/q03/p3 Both hamzas have fatha because an omitted preposition or causal lam explains the letter's nobility. — Al-Farra and al-Nahhas describe accusative after an omitted preposition; al-Kashshaf, al-Razi, al-Baydawi, Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi connect the clauses to the reason the letter is noble. Al-Samin attributes the omitted causal lam to al-Zamakhshari. [9 notes: FARRA; NAHHAS(الفراء); KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(عكرمة، وابن أبي عبلة); SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL(عكرمة وابن أبي عبلة)]
- 27:30/q03/p4 The open clauses are governed as a predicate of karim. — Abu al-Baqa proposes this, but al-Samin finds it weak. [0 notes: SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء)-]
- 27:30/q03/p5 A reading adds a coordinating waw before the clause identifying Solomon. — Attributed to Abd Allah. Al-Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, al-Razi and al-Samin link it to the queen's previous statement that she received a letter. Al-Alusi records Abu Hayyan's coordination with 27:29 and another circumstantial parsing. Al-Qurtubi mentions the variant; Abu Hayyan describes a conjunction before the second clause. [7 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(عبد الله); IBNATIYYA-FULL(عبد الله); RAZI-FULL(عبدالله); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(عبد الله); ALUSI-FULL(عبد الله)]
- 27:30/q03/p6 Ubayy reads an with open hamza and quiescent nun before Solomon and the basmala, primarily as an explanatory particle. — Al-Farra cites wa-an before the basmala and says the opened particle joined to a clause can be lightened; he connects it with the following an la ta'lu and compares repeated an with 23:35. Ibn Atiyya specifies omission of ha. Al-Kashshaf, al-Razi, Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi mention explanatory an; al-Samin prefers this because a speech-like expression precedes it. [10 notes: FARRA(أبي); KASHSHAF-FULL(أبي); IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبي); RAZI-FULL(أبي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبي); SAMIN-DURR(أبي); ALUSI-FULL(أبي); SAMIN-DURR+]
- 27:30/q03/p7 Ubayy's an is a lightened emphatic particle with an omitted subject. — Al-Farra and Abu Hayyan mention lightening; al-Alusi also allows it. Al-Samin rejects the omitted-subject version under Basran rules: the subject must be the pronoun of circumstance, explained by a complete clause. [3 notes: FARRA(أبي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبي); ALUSI-FULL(أبي); SAMIN-DURR-]

## 27:30/q04 What does 'from Solomon' express?  [مِن سُلَيْمَٰنَ · grammar]
- 27:30/q04/p1 Min marks the letter's origin, with a person standing for its place of origin. — Al-Burhan says min normally marks the beginning of an extent when paired with ila, literally or in sense; here Solomon stands for the source place. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 27:30/q05 What inward meaning is given to Solomon's name?  [سُلَيْمَٰنَ · inward]
- 27:30/q05/p1 It points to the Essence granting preparedness and the faculties that actualize intellect and moral perfection. — Al-Qashani associates the name with potential made actual and fitting perfection in qualities and attributes. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 27:30/q06 Did Solomon put his name before God's name in the actual letter?  [مِن سُلَيْمَٰنَ بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the queen reproduced the physical order, summarized it, or distinguished a heading from its body; Abu Hayyan proposes protection of God's name, which al-Alusi disputes.
- 27:30/q06/p1 The letter itself began by naming Solomon and then gave the basmala. — Ibn Atiyya allows an eloquent sequence of address, divine name and demand. Abu Hayyan favors the apparent order and suggests protecting God's name from a disrespectful response by the unbelieving queen. Al-Biqai says this order does not detract from praise and can favor acceptance because people recognize truth through people. Al-Alusi reports a heading with Solomon's name. Al-Alusi rejects Abu Hayyan's protective explanation. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان)+; RAZI-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 27:30/q06/p2 The basmala came first; the queen named the sender before quoting or summarizing the letter. — Al-Razi says Solomon opened with the basmala, while the order belongs to her report. Abu Hayyan transmits this alternative. Yazid ibn Ruman's transmitted letter begins with the basmala, then names Solomon and addresses Bilqis daughter of Dhi Sharh and her people. Al-Alusi says a signature after the basmala could have supplied his identity. [6 notes: IBNABIHATIM(يزيد بن رومان); RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; DURR-FULL(يزيد بن رومان); ALUSI-FULL(يزيد بن رومان)]
- 27:30/q06/p3 Solomon's name was an outer heading; the basmala began the written body. — Abu Hayyan gives this alternative, and al-Alusi reports a view treating the first clause as the heading. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
- 27:30/q06/p4 The queen summarized the sender and opening, so her order does not settle the original order. — Ibn Atiyya allows summary instead of exact sequence; Islahi says she did not read the whole letter aloud and notes ancient royal letters named sender and recipient. Tabatabai treats the message as meaning rather than verbatim wording. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; TABATABAI]

## 27:30/q07 In letters, should the sender or recipient be named first?  [مِن سُلَيْمَٰنَ · law]
turns on: Prophetic and companion precedent for naming the sender first versus later communal custom allowing or favoring the recipient first.
- 27:30/q07/p1 Name the sender first. — Ibn Sirin reports the Prophet contrasting Persian practice of beginning with leaders and instructing a man to begin with himself. Anas says the Companions began letters to the Prophet with their own names despite his greater honor. Ibn al-Arabi says this was earlier messengers' custom. Al-Alusi notes that early letters named sender and recipient without a following prayer. [6 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الربيع عن أنس,محمد بن سيرين); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو بكر بن العربي,أنس); ALUSI-FULL(أنس)]
- 27:30/q07/p2 Naming the recipient first is also permissible, and may be preferable under prevailing custom. — Abu al-Layth appeals to communal consensus and practice; al-Qurtubi reports that in his time this was preferable except when addressing a servant or child. Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi transmit the permissive view. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو الليث); ALUSI-FULL(أبو الليث); QURTUBI-FULL(أبو الليث)+]

## 27:30/q08 What is implicit in 'in the name of God'?  [بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ · grammar]
- 27:30/q08/p1 An unstated clause supplies the action, such as 'this is in God's name' or 'in God's name at the start of everything.' — Abu Ubayda in Majaz gives both examples of the understood wording. [1 notes: MAJAZ]

## 27:30/q09 Why is the alif of ism omitted in writing bism?  [بِسْمِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Symbolic meaning of the spelling versus conventional abbreviation, with al-Kisai and the majority differing on the conditions for omission.
- 27:30/q09/p1 Its omission signifies the name's unique exalted rank, its relation to God, and manifestation with hiddenness. — Al-Burhan associates the written omission with the divine name gathering all names. [1 notes: BURHAN]
- 27:30/q09/p2 It is an abbreviation caused by frequent usage. — Al-Burhan reports outward scholars. The majority drop alif only when ism is joined to Allah and preceded by ba. Al-Kisai does not require that restriction and permits examples such as bism al-malik. [2 notes: BURHAN(الكسائي,علماء الظاهر)]

## 27:30/q10 What does the basmala say about God and His mercy?  [بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · theology]
turns on: The notes draw different theological implications from God's name and the two mercy attributes.
- 27:30/q10/p1 It names the worship-worthy God and His encompassing majesty and generosity, with special mercy to whom He wills. — Al-Biqai says the divine name establishes Solomon's prophethood and describes general majesty and particular gifts. Al-Razi says the formula gives the knowledge needed before action and establishes God as existent, knowing, powerful, living, willing, wise and merciful. [3 notes: BIQAI-FULL; RAZI-FULL]
- 27:30/q10/p2 Al-Rahman denotes mercy to all in this world and the next, and al-Rahim special mercy in the next. — Bursevi gives this distinction in his esoteric exposition. [1 notes: BURSEVI]
- 27:30/q10/p3 Even idolaters acknowledge God's supreme lordship, so the divine name and attributes ennoble the letter. — Tabatabai says Sabian sun worshippers honor God as Lord of lords and understand His attributes as absence of ignorance, incapacity, death and cruelty. [1 notes: TABATABAI]

## 27:30/q11 What inward meanings are read in the letters of the basmala?  [بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · inward]
turns on: These are distinct letter-symbol readings reported by Bursevi.
- 27:30/q11/p1 Its letters signify divine permanence, splendor, sovereignty, oneness, beauty, majesty and identity. — Bursevi assigns ba to permanence, sin to splendor, mim to sovereignty, alif to oneness, the two lams to beauty and majesty, and ha to divine identity. [1 notes: BURSEVI]
- 27:30/q11/p2 The opening ba suffices because all existence is covered by God's general or particular mercy. — Bursevi transmits this from some great Sufis. [1 notes: BURSEVI(بعض الكبار)]
- 27:30/q11/p3 Its nineteen letters send grace over seventeen thousand worlds. — Bursevi quotes Jami's Persian couplet with those numbers. [1 notes: BURSEVI(المولى الجامي)]

## 27:30/q12 What makes this letter 'noble'?  [مِن سُلَيْمَٰنَ بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · interpretation]
turns on: The stated grounds are God's name, Solomon's authority together with it, God's precedence in the formula, or the seal.
- 27:30/q12/p1 Its divine opening gives it nobility. — Al-Qushayri reports this and applies it to prayer: prayer is noble with the naming formula and deficient without it. Ibn Atiyya cites a Prophetic report that speech without the basmala is deficient. Tabatabai says even idolaters acknowledge God's lordship and the letter's association with God raises its standing. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan call the basmala a noble, eloquent and meaningful opening across languages and religious traditions. [6 notes: QUSHAYRI(قيل); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABATABAI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 27:30/q12/p2 Both Solomon's authority and the divine opening explain its nobility. — Al-Kashshaf's and al-Razi's causal analyses tie the opened clauses to karim. Tabatabai explicitly invokes Bilqis's knowledge of Solomon's authority and the opening, citing 27:42. [3 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 27:30/q12/p3 Its nobility comes from naming God first instead of starting with the sender-and-recipient formula. — Al-Qushayri reports this separate explanation. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI(قيل)]
- 27:30/q12/p4 A letter's seal completes and ennobles it. — Al-Qurtubi cites a hadith calling the seal a letter's nobility; Ibn al-Muqaffa says an unsealed letter disrespects a brother, and Umar calls one unfinished or exposed. Solomon's letter was reportedly sealed with his signet. [4 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(ابن المقفع,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,عمر بن الخطاب); THALABI]

## 27:30/q13 Was Solomon the first to write the full basmala?  [بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · reports]
turns on: Whether the reported first use concerns the formula itself or only the Arabic wording and order of a translated original.
- 27:30/q13/p1 No one before Solomon wrote the full formula; it was unfamiliar to the queen and her people. — Qatada says earlier writing used 'In Your name, O Allah.' Al-Mawardi and al-Tabrisi say Solomon first began with the full formula; al-Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan and Ibn Kathir transmit the claim. A report from Caesar says he had not seen a basmala-marked letter like the Prophet's since Solomon's. [8 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(العلماء); DURR-FULL(سعيد بن المسيب,قتادة)]
- 27:30/q13/p2 The wording does not establish uniqueness to Solomon, though its specific Arabic expression or sequence might have been unique. — Al-Alusi says the wording suggests prior use and considers a unique Arabic form possible because Solomon's original was translated. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 27:30/q14 How did the Prophet's written invocation develop?  [بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · reports]
turns on: The chronology of the basmala's revelation relative to this verse and whether intermediate written forms are counted.
- 27:30/q14/p1 It progressed in stages from 'In Your name, O God' through forms associated with 11:41 and 17:110 to the full basmala at this verse. — Al-Sha'bi supplies this sequence in Abd al-Razzaq, al-Mawardi, al-Durr and al-Alusi; the report in Ibn Abi Hatim says Arabs questioned al-Rahman and 17:110 permitted either divine name. Al-Mawardi calls the full formula the third written form. Islahi says the queen's emphasis illuminates this history. [9 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(الشعبي); IBNABIHATIM(الشعبي,ميمون بن مهران); MAWARDI-FULL(الشعبي); DURR-FULL(الشعبي); ALUSI-FULL(الشعبي); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 27:30/q14/p2 He continued writing 'In Your name, O God' until this verse, then used the full basmala. — Maymun ibn Mihran, Abu Malik and Qatada transmit this shorter chronology. Al-Alusi notes Abu Dawud's mursal report from Abu Malik and parallel accounts. Ibn Kathir relays Maymun; al-Alusi says subsequent use of the full basmala became established. [7 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(ميمون بن مهران); DURR-FULL(أبو مالك,قتادة,ميمون بن مهران); ALUSI-FULL(أبو مالك,ميمون بن مهران وقتادة); ALUSI-FULL-]
- 27:30/q14/p3 Opening letters with the basmala fits its earlier revelation as the opening of God's Book, so a late adoption chronology is implausible. — Al-Alusi invokes its prior revelation and 1:1, says the reported late chronology is nearly impossible, and affirms that its earlier revelation should stand. He also says opening with the full formula after this verse became the Prophet's settled practice. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+]

## 27:30/q15 Was the basmala itself the first revelation?  [بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · sciences]
turns on: Whether the basmala accompanying the first sura is counted as a separate first-revelation candidate.
- 27:30/q15/p1 It was first in an absolute sense because every sura's revelation includes its basmala. — Al-Suyuti argues it need not be a separate first-revelation opinion; al-Alusi accepts this conclusion, citing 1:1. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الجلال السيوطي)+]
- 27:30/q15/p2 The soundest named first-revelation view is 96:1, with alternatives naming 74:1, al-Fatiha or the basmala. — Al-Alusi reports al-Suyuti's list; the distinction of basmala as a separate view is qualified by al-Suyuti's argument. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الجلال السيوطي)]

## 27:30/q16 How should the basmala be laid out and followed in written correspondence?  [بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ · law]
turns on: The noted difference is early epistolary practice versus later scribal recommendations.
- 27:30/q16/p1 Put it on its own line and align the following prayer carefully. — Al-Alusi reports some eminent scholars' recommendation and al-Suli's manual: start at the page margin, align the prayer, avoid a short spill beyond the basmala, and do not center it. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الصولي,بعض الأجلة)]
- 27:30/q16/p2 Early letters did not add a prayer after the basmala; they simply named the sender and recipient. — Al-Alusi distinguishes early practice from the later layout with a following prayer. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 27:30/q17 Is this basmala a complete verse, and how does it relate to other sura basmalas?  [بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · sciences]
turns on: The notes address different aspects of the formula's placement and verse status.
- 27:30/q17/p1 Here it is not a complete verse as the basmala in Hud is; the basmalas opening suras are separate verses revealed 114 times. — Bursevi compares 11:41 and counts one opening basmala for each sura. [1 notes: BURSEVI]
- 27:30/q17/p2 This is the Qur'an's sole repetition of the full basmala inside a sura. — The Study Quran states the uniqueness of its placement. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 27:30/q18 What special virtue is reported for this verse's opening formula?  [بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · reports]
- 27:30/q18/p1 A prophetic report says this verse was not given to another prophet before Solomon and Muhammad. — Burayda's report appears in Ibn Abi Hatim, al-Mawardi and Ibn Kathir; Ibn Kathir calls its chain weak and the report strange. [3 notes: IBNABIHATIM(بريدة); MAWARDI-FULL(رسول الله ﷺ); IBNKATHIR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNKATHIR-FULL-]

## 27:30/q19 How does this basmala relate inwardly to Bara'a?  [بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · inward]
- 27:30/q19/p1 It is, in truth, the basmala of Bara'a, bestowed on animals believing in Solomon because Bara'a's people were disavowed. — Bursevi transmits this from some great Sufis. [1 notes: BURSEVI(بعض الكبار)]

## 27:30/q20 How much of Solomon's letter did the Qur'anic account preserve?  [* · reports]
turns on: Reports differ on whether the Qur'anic wording exhausts the original letter or omits addresses and greetings.
- 27:30/q20/p1 The letter contained only the wording given in the Qur'anic account. — Ibn Jurayj and Mujahid explicitly say no more was written; Muqatil includes the identification, basmala, prohibition of arrogance and demand for submission. Al-Tabari, al-Tha'labi and al-Durr transmit the concise-content claim; the wording of the notes varies between this verse alone and the whole quoted demand. [6 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(ابن جريج); IBNABIHATIM(مجاهد); THALABI(ابن جريج); DURR-FULL(ابن جريج,مجاهد)]
- 27:30/q20/p2 The letter also gave sender and recipient names or genealogy, a greeting, and the call to submit. — Wahb says it named sender and recipient; Yazid ibn Ruman calls Solomon son of David and Bilqis daughter of Dhi Sharh and her people. The fuller text attributed to Mujahid says 'God's servant Solomon' to the queen of Sheba, with peace to followers of guidance; al-Kashshaf transmits a similar greeting. The reported fuller versions vary in naming and order. [6 notes: TAB-FULL(وهب بن منبه); IBNABIHATIM(يزيد بن رومان); WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); KASHSHAF-FULL; DURR-FULL(مجاهد,يزيد بن رومان)]

## 27:30/q21 Did the queen quote the letter exactly or summarize it?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The explanatory particle introducing the demand, and whether her report preserves wording and order or conveys meaning.
- 27:30/q21/p1 She gave a concise, eloquent reproduction of the letter's sequence and wording. — Ibn Atiyya allows address followed by God's name and the command as the actual wording. Some exegetes in Tabatabai take the first clause as a fresh answer and the next as a quotation; Tabatabai rejects this because it makes the explanatory particle in the following command redundant, and calls the attempted verbal-noun repair weak. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABATABAI(جمع من المفسرين); TABATABAI-]
- 27:30/q21/p2 She summarized the letter's meaning, naming its sender and opening without necessarily quoting every word or preserving its order. — Ibn Atiyya presents this alternative. Tabatabai prefers it because the explanatory particle introduces the message's meaning without redundancy: God's name opens the letter, then it prohibits arrogance and commands submission. Islahi says she did not read all of it aloud; it could still have been very short, as Muhammad's letters were. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; TABATABAI+]

## 27:30/q22 In what language was Solomon's original letter written?  [* · reports]
turns on: Whether the Arabic Qur'anic wording reproduces the letter's original language or renders its meaning.
- 27:30/q22/p1 The wording suggests Arabic because Sheba's Himyarites were Arabs. — Elmalili gives this inference from the recipients' identity. [1 notes: ELMALILI]
- 27:30/q22/p2 It could have been Hebrew, with the Qur'anic Arabic translating or summarizing it. — Elmalili expressly allows this alternative; al-Alusi likewise says Solomon's original could have been translated, affecting claims about the formula's unique Arabic wording. [3 notes: ELMALILI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 27:30/q23 How was the letter delivered and found?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports differ on the exact place and manner of the hoopoe's delivery.
- 27:30/q23/p1 The hoopoe dropped it through the queen's sun or palace window despite locked doors. — Wahb says it carried the letter in its leg and dropped it through the sun window. Qatada describes the queen's lineage, appearance, large council and palace location, then a locked-window entry. Al-Kalbi says it crossed seven rooms, put the sealed letter by the sleeping queen, pecked her awake and returned to the window; guards saw no entrant. Al-Kalbi and al-Suddi say she initially thought it came from heaven or God, until she read Solomon's name. [6 notes: TAB-FULL(قتادة,وهب بن منبه); WAHIDI-BASIT(السدي,الكلبي)]
- 27:30/q23/p2 The hoopoe dropped the letter into the middle of her throne. — Ibn Abbas says she then read Solomon's message. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 27:30/q23/p3 Solomon sent the letter with the hoopoe to verify its report and told it to return with the queen's response. — Kuranyolu relates the dispatch to the preceding account. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 27:30/q24 What did the letter demand of the queen and her people?  [* · interpretation]
- 27:30/q24/p1 They should not oppose, defy, refuse or disobey Solomon, and should come to him submitting. — Yahya, Qatada, al-Suddi, Sufyan and Zuhayr give the near-synonymous glosses; Abd al-Rahman ibn Zayd says refusal would bring war and also accepts the literal arrogance sense. Ibn Abbas calls 'Muslims' monotheists; Zuhayr says sincerely devoted, Sufyan obedient. Kuranyolu says the demand thus combines political and religious submission. Ibn Kathir says the recipients recognized the letter as coming from God's prophet and knew they could not oppose him. [12 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس,السدي,زهير بن محمد,سفيان,عبد الرحمن بن زيد بن اسلم,قتادة); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL]

## 27:30/q25 How is the letter's prohibition of arrogance alternatively read?  [* · readings]
- 27:30/q25/p1 Al-Ashhab al-Uqayli and Muhammad ibn al-Sumayqa read 'do not exceed' with ghayn; it conveys the standard reading's sense of arrogance. — Al-Qurtubi derives the variant from a verb meaning to exceed and act arrogantly. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الأشهب العقيلي ومحمد بن السميقع)]

## 27:30/q26 Why is Solomon's letter so brief, and what does its brevity show?  [* · rhetoric]
- 27:30/q26/p1 It exemplifies prophetic and especially Solomonic eloquence: a few clear words give the necessary knowledge and command. — Yahya, Qatada and al-Mawardi describe prophetic letters as concise. Mansur and Sufyan ibn Mansur call Solomon the most eloquent and briefest writer; Ibn Kathir calls it exceptionally clear. Al-Razi says prophets address only what is needed, with knowledge before action and the basmala establishing divine attributes. Al-Tibi calls the heading, content and request concision by reduction; al-Itqan also notes a notion of concision as equality of wording and meaning. Islahi compares Muhammad's concise letters. [13 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL(قتادة); IBNABIHATIM(قتادة,منصور); THALABI(قتادة,منصور); MAWARDI-FULL; RAZI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; DURR-FULL(سفيان بن منصور); ITQAN(الطيبي); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 27:30/q27 What practices for opening and sealing letters are drawn from this account?  [* · law]
- 27:30/q27/p1 Begin letters with the basmala and seal them. — Al-Qurtubi says writers agree on the basmala and sealing to avoid suspicion, with textual precedent. Al-Tha'labi and al-Kashshaf report Solomon scented the letter with musk and sealed it. Anas says the Prophet made a ring because foreign recipients accepted only sealed letters; Umar says an unsealed letter is unfinished or exposed, and Ibn al-Muqaffa calls it disrespectful. Al-Alusi calls the Prophet's later use of the basmala established practice. [8 notes: THALABI; KASHSHAF-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(أنس,ابن المقفع,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,عمر بن الخطاب); ALUSI-FULL]

## 27:30/q28 Must a recipient answer a letter containing a greeting?  [* · law]
- 27:30/q28/p1 Yes; an absent sender's written greeting is like a present person's greeting, so answering is required. — Al-Qurtubi draws the analogy and reports Ibn Abbas treating the reply as obligatory. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس)]

## 27:30/q29 May 'noble' be used as an epithet for a letter?  [* · law]
- 27:30/q29/p1 Use 'lofty' for correspondence; reserve 'noble' as an epithet for the Qur'an. — Ibn al-Arabi cites 41:41-42 to preserve reverence for the Qur'an while honoring authority in correspondence. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(القاضي أبو بكر بن العربي)]

## 27:30/q30 What does this letter contribute to the sura's account of Solomon and Sheba?  [* · links]
- 27:30/q30/p1 Its basmala and summons make the invitation both religious and political, leading into the queen's embrace of faith in the one God. — Kuranyolu links the letter to Solomon testing the hoopoe's news and asking for the queen's reply; Study Quran describes the encounter's endpoint as her faith. [3 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
