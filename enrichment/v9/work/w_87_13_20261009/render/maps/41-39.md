# 41:39: 27 questions

## 41:39/q01 What do His signs disclose?  [ءَايَٰتِهِۦٓ · interpretation]
- 41:39/q01/p1 His created works are proofs of God’s existence, oneness, exclusive creative power, and ability to act, even though He is unseen. — Muqātil says created works make the unseen God’s oneness known. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the signs proofs attributed to God because they show existence and unity; night and day are beyond any other maker (2:164), and the sun and moon’s continuing motion, rather than their creation alone, is the pertinent sign (41:12). Bursevī calls the revived earth a proof of power. [6 notes: MUQATIL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BURSEVI]

## 41:39/q02 Who is addressed by “you see,” and what can be seen?  [أَنَّكَ تَرَى · referent]
- 41:39/q02/p1 The address may be to Muhammad or any observer; every rational person can apprehend the sign. — Bursevī names Muhammad and any observer. Al-Qurṭubī extends it to every rational person. Biqāʿī says one sees part of the earth directly and infers the rest from it because all is subject to the same divine power. [3 notes: BURSEVI; QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]

## 41:39/q03 How is “that you see the earth humbled” parsed?  [أَنَّكَ تَرَى ٱلْأَرْضَ خَٰشِعَةًۭ · grammar]
- 41:39/q03/p1 The an-clause is a nominative subject, with khāshiʿa a circumstantial accusative; an implicit governing verb is supplied where a clause cannot begin independently. — Al-Naḥḥās reports Sībawayh’s nominative subject analysis despite his usual bar on beginning with an, gives his own ḥāl analysis for khāshiʿa, and reports al-Māzinī’s estimated kayfa istaqarra Zayd as an example of supplying a governing verb. ʿAbd al-Qāhir says coordinated clauses forming a condition must be parsed as a grammatical whole. [4 notes: NAHHAS(المازني,سيبويه); JURJANI-DALAIL]

## 41:39/q04 What is the earth’s state when it is خَٰشِعَةًۭ?  [خَٰشِعَةًۭ · meaning]
- 41:39/q04/p1 It is dry, dusty, worn, broken, and bare of vegetation, appearing lowered or subdued. — Muqātil gives dry, dusty, broken and plantless; Qatāda gray, dusty and worn; al-Suddī dead, dry and crumbling; al-Zajjāj compares hāmida. Al-Māwardī says drought humbles an abandoned land, whereas inhabited flourishing land is esteemed. Ibn ʿAṭiyya adds roughness and scorching wind; al-Qurṭubī contrasts a gray khāshiʿa land with a dusty uninhabited place. Al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, Biqāʿī, Bursevī and al-Ālūsī add lowness, drooping or submission. [22 notes: MUQATIL; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB-FULL(السدي,قتادة); ZAJJAJ; MAWARDI-FULL(السدي,قتادة); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(قتادة والسدي,قيل); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 41:39/q05 Why portray barren earth as humbled?  [خَٰشِعَةًۭ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the wording is a borrowed quality, an implied comparison of the entire transformation, or an ethical image.
- 41:39/q05/p1 Humility is borrowed metaphorically for the land’s low, bare, diminished appearance. — Al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī and al-Ālūsī identify the borrowing from submission. Ibn ʿAṭiyya likens drought-struck earth to a downcast person almost weeping; Biqāʿī likens it to a powerless flattened person; Bursevī to an overlooked lowly person. Ibn ʿĀshūr treats humbled and shook as mapped parts of one personifying metaphor. [8 notes: RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 41:39/q05/p2 The whole scene is a representative comparison: a shabby, neglected person becomes lively and proud after receiving adornment. — Al-Ālūsī prefers the implied whole-scene comparison, its humbling and swaying standing in for the omitted comparison. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī compares a poor person transformed by wealth into a splendid figure. Ibn ʿĀshūr maps the earth’s initial abasement and later shoulder-shaking to a person. [3 notes: TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 41:39/q05/p3 The humble image warns the arrogant and anticipates the humble person’s rise. — Elmalılı describes earth as a bowed-neck person and explicitly draws both lessons. [1 notes: ELMALILI]

## 41:39/q06 Why does this verse say “humbled” here and stop before describing sprouting?  [خَٰشِعَةًۭ ٱهْتَزَّتْ وَرَبَتْ · rhetoric]
- 41:39/q06/p1 The wording fits the surrounding scene of worship; explicit sprouting and emergence would not fit it. — Biqāʿī says this depiction resembles a worshipper more closely than al-Ḥajj 22:5. Al-Sāmarrāʾī contrasts the worship setting at 41:37 with 22:5 and explains the stopping point. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; SAMARRAI-ASRAR]

## 41:39/q07 What is the water sent down onto the earth?  [ٱلْمَآءَ · referent]
- 41:39/q07/p1 It is rain. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Nasafī and al-Ālūsī identify it as rain. Al-Ṭabarī specifies rain from the sky. Biqāʿī adds that rain may come from clouds or be driven from high places and carry growth-suitable soil even to saline land such as Egypt. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TAB-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]

## 41:39/q08 Why does the wording switch to “We sent down”?  [أَنزَلْنَا · rhetoric]
- 41:39/q08/p1 The majestic plural marks power and greatness while stressing that sending rain belongs to God alone. — Biqāʿī treats the rainfall as one of the strongest signs of the One. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 41:39/q09 How does the earth “shake” after rain?  [ٱهْتَزَّتْ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the observed movement belongs primarily to plants, to the loosened soil, or to a personified expression of joy.
- 41:39/q09/p1 It stirs as vegetation emerges or moves across it. — Muqātil, al-Ṭabarī, Mujāhid, al-Kalbī and others explain it by sprouting or moving growth; al-Qurṭubī adds that ihtazz denotes movement and cites a poet’s quivering sword-blade image. Biqāʿī intensifies the motion as great, abundant and quick, as if self-initiated; Ibn ʿĀshūr and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī locate the movement in the plants. [20 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(مجاهد); MAWARDI-FULL(الكلبي,مجاهد); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; TABRISI(الكلبي); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الشاعر,مجاهد); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 41:39/q09/p2 Water loosens the soil and makes it crack open for plants. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya explains the movement as the earth’s parts loosening and splitting. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 41:39/q09/p3 The shaking figuratively expresses the earth’s joy at rain. — Al-Qurṭubī reports the comparison with the earth’s splitting for plants being called laughter. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]

## 41:39/q10 What does “it swelled” describe?  [وَرَبَتْ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the increase describes the soil’s physical elevation or the amount and growth of its vegetation.
- 41:39/q10/p1 The wetted soil expands, rises and often splits before plants emerge. — Al-Ṭabarī says swelled and reports al-Suddī’s expanded; Qatāda observes rain in the surface swelling; Mujāhid says it rises before shoots. Al-Zajjāj distinguishes unhamzated rabat as growing or swelling; Ibn ʿAṭiyya describes swelling with water and rising surface. Al-Qurṭubī cites rabwa and rābiya for raised ground; al-Rāzī, Bursevī, al-Ālūsī and Ibn ʿĀshūr add lifting and cracking. Bursevī cites al-Rāghib’s nurturing-to-grow gloss and the swelling of a horse after exertion or fear. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī gives growth, flourishing and rising. [22 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(السدي,قتادة,مجاهد); ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(مجاهد); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(الراغب); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 41:39/q10/p2 It increases its vegetation or abundant crop yield. — Muqātil speaks of increased plants. Al-Kalbī links rising to abundant yield; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī describes the plants growing upward. Al-Zajjāj gives growth as the unhamzated sense. [5 notes: MUQATIL; MAWARDI-FULL(الكلبي); TABRISI(الكلبي); TABATABAI; ZAJJAJ]

## 41:39/q11 How are the shaking, swelling and emergence ordered?  [ٱهْتَزَّتْ وَرَبَتْ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the two verbs follow actual time, reverse a swelling-before-sprouting sequence, or describe one state.
- 41:39/q11/p1 The soil rises first, then vegetation emerges and moves, so the wording may state the events out of temporal order. — Mujāhid places rising before sprouts, and al-Māwardī explicitly reverses the wording to “rose, then moved.” Al-Qurṭubī reports this sequence as rabat then ihtazzat. [5 notes: TAB-FULL(مجاهد); MAWARDI-FULL(مجاهد); QURTUBI-FULL(مجاهد); TABRISI]
- 41:39/q11/p2 The shaking and swelling may occur before or after shoots; vegetation first moves outward and then expands in several directions. — Al-Qurṭubī reports either timing, with growth raising soil and later growth increasing height, length and width. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]
- 41:39/q11/p3 Shaking and swelling name a single state of vegetation emerging. — Al-Qurṭubī reports this additional view. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]
- 41:39/q11/p4 After rain the earth moves, swells, then splits to bring out plants. — Al-Wāḥidī gives this explicit sequence; Biqāʿī describes cracking, surface-covering growth, roots branching and stems thickening until passage becomes hard. [2 notes: WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BIQAI-FULL]

## 41:39/q12 How is وَرَبَتْ read?  [وَرَبَتْ · readings]
turns on: Presence of a hamza after bāʾ and the associated nuance of elevated ground versus swelling or increase.
- 41:39/q12/p1 The majority read rabat without hamza. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya attributes the reading to the majority; Ibn al-Jazarī says other readers omit Abū Jaʿfar’s added hamza. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الجمهور); NASHR(أبو جعفر)]
- 41:39/q12/p2 A variant reads rabaʾat with a hamza after bāʾ, meaning rose or increased. — Al-Zajjāj gives the variant and contrasts its rising sense with rabat’s swelling. Al-Naḥḥās derives it from a lookout rising to a high place. Ibn ʿAṭiyya names Abū Jaʿfar ibn al-Qaʿqāʿ and al-Ruʾāsī from Abū ʿAmr; al-Qurṭubī names Abū Jaʿfar and Khālid. Ibn al-Jazarī names Abū Jaʿfar in this verse and al-Ḥajj; Ibn ʿĀshūr and the qirāʾāt note likewise name him. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī gloss the variant as rose or increased, with al-Ālūsī transmitting al-Zajjāj’s distinction. [11 notes: ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS(أبو جعفر); IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبو جعفر بن القعقاع والرؤاسي عن أبي عمرو); QURTUBI-FULL(أبو جعفر وخالد); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASHR(أبو جعفر); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو جعفر); QIRAAT-ER(H)]

## 41:39/q13 What is the derivation and dual of ربت?  [وَرَبَتْ · grammar]
turns on: The root and orthography implied by the Qurʾanic form and whether the dual follows the Basran or Kufan analysis.
- 41:39/q13/p1 It derives from rabā yarbū and its dual is rabawān. — Al-Naḥḥās reports Sībawayh’s derivation and says Abū Jaʿfar prefers this Basran analysis because the Qurʾanic form supports it; he cites 30:39. Abū Isḥāq rebukes the Kufans for extending an orthographic mistake to the dual. [2 notes: NAHHAS(سيبويه); NAHHAS+]
- 41:39/q13/p2 Its dual is rabiyān and rabā is written with yāʾ. — Al-Naḥḥās reports the Kufan account; Abū Jaʿfar and Abū Isḥāq reject its spelling basis. [1 notes: NAHHAS(الكوفيون); NAHHAS-]

## 41:39/q14 In what sense is the earth revived?  [أَحْيَاهَا · rhetoric]
- 41:39/q14/p1 Its life is a figure for greenness, renewed freshness, flowering and seeds sprouting; God is literally the one who causes this revival. — ʿAbd al-Qāhir distinguishes figurative earthly life from the literal attribution of revival to God. Bursevī calls it the stirring of growth powers and the renewal of freshness by varied plants. Ibn ʿĀshūr says rain makes buried seeds sprout and the relative subject explains why God is called the reviver. [3 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 41:39/q15 What does reviving the earth establish about the dead?  [إِنَّ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَحْيَاهَا لَمُحْىِ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰٓ · theology]
turns on: Whether the contextual force of the analogy is bodily resurrection or the possible awakening of closed hearts.
- 41:39/q15/p1 God can and will raise dead human bodies after death, as rain revives barren ground. — Muqātil addresses doubters; al-Ṭabarī says even decayed bodies can be restored; al-Māwardī calls observable earth evidence for deniers, moving from visible to unseen. Al-Rāzī says scattered parts can be reassembled and life, intellect and ability restored because all possibles are within power. Biqāʿī adds that plant remains decay into soil just as bodies do. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the likeness persuasive rather than demonstrative, fitting deniers’ appeal to customary improbability; the closing power claim strengthens it. Al-Qurṭubī links 22:5 and Study Quran lists 7:57, 16:65, 22:5, 25:48–89, 29:63, 30:19, 30:24, 30:50, 35:9, 36:33, 43:11, 45:5 and 50:9–11. [31 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 41:39/q15/p2 Here the earth’s revival especially illustrates God’s power to give spiritual life to closed hearts. — Asad acknowledges the common bodily-resurrection symbolism but reads this context of 41:33–39 as a promise of spiritual awakening; it encourages hope that deniers may understand the Qurʾan. Kur’an Yolu says the absolute life-giving power can also encompass enlightenment and mental clarity. [3 notes: ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 41:39/q16 How will God revive the dead at resurrection?  [لَمُحْىِ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰٓ · reports]
- 41:39/q16/p1 Water will revive them between the two trumpet blasts, just as rain revives earth. — Al-Ṭabarī transmits this specific account from al-Suddī. [1 notes: TAB-FULL(السدي)]

## 41:39/q17 Why is resurrection stated as an emphatic new sentence?  [إِنَّ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَحْيَاهَا لَمُحْىِ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰٓ · rhetoric]
- 41:39/q17/p1 The new, emphasized sentence draws the analogous conclusion and answers denial; inna binds it to the preceding sign. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the analogy motivates the new sentence and its emphasis addresses denial. ʿAbd al-Qāhir reports Abū al-ʿAbbās’s answer to al-Kindī that assertion, response to a question and response to denial require distinct forms; he says inna coheres with what precedes in a way omission or fāʾ does not, citing Bashshār’s line about starting early and an Arab’s camel-song line. He also explains that adding a complement shapes the second verb’s asserted meaning, as in in atāka Zayd atāka li-ḥāja and a proverb about speech and courage. [7 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; JURJANI-DALAIL(ابن الأنباري,بشار,بعض العرب)]

## 41:39/q18 How is the form لَمُحْىِ explained?  [لَمُحْىِ · grammar]
- 41:39/q18/p1 The yāʾ first loses its heavy ḍamma and then a yāʾ drops because two vowelless letters meet. — Al-Naḥḥās gives the two phonological steps. [1 notes: NAHHAS]

## 41:39/q19 What is included in God’s power over every thing?  [إِنَّهُۥ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍۢ قَدِيرٌ · theology]
- 41:39/q19/p1 His power covers all things He wills, including life, death and resurrection; no intended act escapes Him. — Muqātil names resurrection and all else; al-Ṭabarī says no intention defeats or eludes Him. Ibn ʿAṭiyya reads “thing” as an existent in ordinary language. Al-Rāzī and Biqāʿī reason that all possible acts fall under complete power. Al-Bayḍāwī names life and death. Bursevī says qadīr intensifies power and God will fulfill His promise; al-Ālūsī says “every thing” explicitly includes resurrection. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the conclusion makes the analogy forceful because the creator alone can do either act. [17 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 41:39/q20 What wisdom lies behind reviving the dead?  [لَمُحْىِ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰٓ · theology]
- 41:39/q20/p1 Resurrection fulfills God’s promise and makes recompense possible. — Bursevī states that God will fulfill His promise and gives recompense as the wisdom of raising the dead. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 41:39/q21 How can the earthly revival be read as a change in hearts?  [ٱلْأَرْضَ خَٰشِعَةًۭ أَحْيَاهَا · inward]
turns on: Whether the inward “rain” is mercy and knowledge yielding repentance or trial that activates latent sin.
- 41:39/q21/p1 Awareness of sin humbles hearts, and divine mercy makes them flourish in regret, repentance and forgiveness. — Al-Qushayrī compares parched winter earth with hearts made humble by sin-awareness, then turned to by God. Study Quran transmits his reading as drawing near to God with regret and repentance. Biqāʿī says knowledge waters soul-earth, bringing remorse, pardon, obedience, nearness and purifying manifestations. [3 notes: QUSHAYRI; BIQAI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(al-Qushayrī)]
- 41:39/q21/p2 Divine care restores slack or veiled seekers to obedience, intimacy and spiritual service. — ʿAmr ibn ʿUthmān compares the heart to earth: mercy, God’s gaze and beneficence awaken hidden deposits into humility, awe, compassion, counsel and enduring fear of sin. Al-Qushayrī says reminders renew a servant’s station and fidelity after lapses; for gnostics, caring regard makes gardens of intimacy green and removes impediments after bad etiquette. His conclusion includes inattentive hearts and those dead to service. [4 notes: SULAMI(عمرو بن عثمان المكي); QUSHAYRI]
- 41:39/q21/p3 Remembrance, knowledge and divine love revive a soul dried by heedlessness. — Study Quran reports this from Aj, with water of life or wine of Divine Love. Bursevī names faith’s light, sincere seeking, longing, grace and mercy as what revives hearts. Asad applies the image to hearts closed to God’s existence and power and holds out hope that deniers may understand; Kur’an Yolu includes vitality, health, productivity, mental clarity and enlightened hearts within God’s life-giving. [5 notes: STUDYQURAN(Aj); BURSEVI; ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 41:39/q21/p4 Worldly trial can stir the dormant ego into sins rather than true spiritual life. — Bursevī says the earth of human nature dries without motives, but trial and istidrāj water it into forbidden acts. A Mathnawī dragon image shows deprivation only appears to subdue the ego; self-delight distracts from the goal, leaving no hope of success. [3 notes: BURSEVI]

## 41:39/q22 How does the verse relate to the surrounding discourse and other revival passages?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes give both an immediate contextual sequence and intertextual parallels, which are complementary.
- 41:39/q22/p1 It moves from heavenly signs to an earthly sign within an argument for exclusive divinity that also makes room for resurrection. — Biqāʿī contrasts Yāsīn 36:33, where resurrection and earth’s proof are more prominent. Ibn ʿĀshūr traces the passage from created worlds to their states (41:9, 41:12), from celestial to earthly conditions, and a return from third-person narration to direct address (41:9, 41:13, 41:34); resurrection is embedded in the exclusive-creation argument. He says the sun and moon’s unspecified motions leave all their conditions as evidence, while their creation recalls the heavens. Islahi sees proofs of oneness and Hereafter followed by censure of opposition. Study Quran situates 33–39 as respectful conduct with disbelievers between 29–32 and 40–46. [8 notes: BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 41:39/q22/p2 The earth-to-resurrection argument recurs elsewhere in the Qurʾan. — Al-Ṭabarī notes the same wording in 22:5. Al-Qurṭubī points back to al-Ḥajj; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says earlier sūras treated the proof. Study Quran lists 7:57, 16:65, 22:5, 25:48–89, 29:63, 30:19, 30:24, 30:50, 35:9, 36:33, 43:11, 45:5 and 50:9–11. [6 notes: TAB-FULL(قتادة); QURTUBI-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]

## 41:39/q23 What response should believers draw when others deny resurrection or truth?  [* · interpretation]
- 41:39/q23/p1 Continue inviting others to God and hope that deniers may come to understand. — Elmalılı joins the revival argument to perseverance in calling to God. Asad says believers should not abandon hope for those who deny truth. Kur’an Yolu identifies Arab polytheists’ denial of afterlife, accountability and resurrection as the occasion answered by the sign; Islahi says resistance to clear signs is condemned. [4 notes: ELMALILI; ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 41:39/q24 What encounter involving Samnūn is reported?  [* · reports]
- 41:39/q24/p1 He swayed during circumambulation, fainted when asked about standing before God, and recited poetry on waking. — Bursevī transmits the account from a righteous observer. [1 notes: BURSEVI(بعض الصالحين)]

## 41:39/q25 How do distant comparisons and novel figurative predications create rhetorical force?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the comparison merely recalls a familiar object or establishes an unfamiliar image with its own novel properties.
- 41:39/q25/p1 A likeness between distant kinds is more surprising and pleasing than a familiar likeness. — ʿAbd al-Qāhir contrasts eye and narcissus with the more particular Pleiades as grape cluster, silver bridle or necklace; a cited poem’s blue violet resembles early flames on sulfur tips among red gems, stronger than narcissus as jeweled bowls. The unfamiliar setting gives delight that a same-kind flower or ordinary colored object would lack. [5 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR]
- 41:39/q25/p2 A novel metaphor can posit a new kind of subject, whose unusual property cannot be conveyed by ordinary simile markers. — ʿAbd al-Qāhir says al-Buḥturī makes a praised man an established, new moon-like being that leaves one spot dark. Kāf, kaʾanna, taḥsab and takhāl instead suggest uncertainty about an understood predicate or familiar metaphor; here the phenomenon itself is new. [3 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR]
- 41:39/q25/p3 A plain comparison to the familiar moon would make that moon illuminate the earth but leave the speaker’s saddle dark. — ʿAbd al-Qāhir rejects this reading of al-Buḥturī’s poem as absurd. [0 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR-]

## 41:39/q26 How do composition and neighboring meanings affect rhetorical excellence?  [* · rhetoric]
- 41:39/q26/p1 Force and beauty arise from words arranged to express a meaning; grammatical relations, placement and context determine their effect. — ʿAbd al-Qāhir says merit lies in the expressed form, not sounds alone; an image of ravines flowing toward a person loses sweetness if its adverbials move. He distinguishes wording and composition and reads “the head blazed with gray” (19:4) through predication to what is related to the true agent, with shayban clarifying it. He says adding an object changes a transitive verb’s asserted meaning, illustrating with a proverb and in atāka Zayd atāka li-ḥāja. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports his view that grammar belongs in the discourse’s order and that a word’s excellence depends on fit with neighbors, as one word can please in one place and feel heavy in another. [9 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(عبد القاهر)]

## 41:39/q27 When does a figurative description state a real truth?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether imagined possession counts as the beneficial use that gives the figurative wealth claim its truth.
- 41:39/q27/p1 A figure can express a substantive truth when its predicate captures the relevant function or condition. — ʿAbd al-Qāhir distinguishes the earth’s figurative life from God’s literal reviving act. In his wealth example, wealth matters for beneficial use; an unused hoard leaves a miser poor in the relevant sense.
