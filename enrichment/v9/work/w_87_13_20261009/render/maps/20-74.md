# 20:74: 16 questions

## 20:74/q01 Who speaks the warning beginning here?  [إِنَّهُۥ · referent]
turns on: Whether the quotation of the magicians continues; Ibn Ashur and Islahi appeal to parallel tellings and the changed register.
- 20:74/q01/p1 It is God's own statement, interrupting the magicians' speech. — Wahidi ends the quotation before this verse; Ibn Atiyya says God addresses Muhammad to display Pharaoh's evil and the magicians' virtue; Tabrisi places the end of their account here; Ibn Ashur calls it an admonitory insertion before the exodus and finds magicians' authorship unlikely because parallel versions do not recount such speech; Islahi cites its majestic afterlife register, difference from surrounding speech, and the insertion at 20:53–59. Jishumi and Baghawi also report this view. [8 notes: JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); IBNATIYYA-FULL(فرقة); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); IBNASHUR-FULL+; ISLAHI-TADABBUR+; TABRISI+; WAHIDI-BASIT+]
- 20:74/q01/p2 It continues the believing magicians' address to Pharaoh. — Ibn Atiyya says they admonish Pharaoh and explain their choice; Ibn Kathir says they warn of enduring punishment and encourage eternal reward. Wahidi, Jishumi, Baghawi and Qurtubi report the attribution; Baghawi adds the criminal is one who dies in polytheism. Kashshaf reports a related dispute over whether the three preceding verses quote the magicians or are God's account. [7 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(آخرون); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(فرقة); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); IBNKATHIR-FULL+; IBNASHUR-FULL-]

## 20:74/q02 What is the pronoun in innahu, and how does its clause work?  [إِنَّهُۥ مَن يَأْتِ رَبَّهُۥ مُجْرِمًۭا فَإِنَّ لَهُۥ جَهَنَّمَ · grammar]
- 20:74/q02/p1 The opening hāʾ is the pronoun of the matter, explained by the following conditional sentence. — Sibawayh says the explicit pronoun allows a conditional after inna. Nahhas and Samin call the conditional sentence its predicate. Wahidi compares 12:90 and also describes the pronoun as awaiting explanation. Qurtubi says Arabic could omit innahu and cites a poet's church line for the implied affair; Burhan explains its masculine form despite feminine Jahannam by the sense that a guilty arrival is punished with Hell. Razi, Baydawi and Nasafi identify the pronoun likewise. [10 notes: SIBAWAYH; NAHHAS; WAHIDI-BASIT; QURTUBI-FULL(شاعر); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURHAN]

## 20:74/q03 What force does the opening innahu add?  [إِنَّهُۥ · rhetoric]
- 20:74/q03/p1 It introduces and reinforces the serious statement that follows. — Bursevi glosses the opening as 'it is the case' and connects it with God's superiority to Pharaoh; Alusi says the pronoun of affair alerts the listener to the gravity of the statement and strengthens it. [2 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 20:74/q04 What is meant by coming to one's Lord?  [يَأْتِ رَبَّهُۥ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether 'coming to the Lord' describes bodily movement to God's location or arrival at the promised meeting.
- 20:74/q04/p1 It means reaching God's appointed meeting or promised place in the afterlife, without implying a divine location. — Abu Ali specifies the promised place, the afterlife; Qurtubi calls it the appointed meeting. Razi treats the coming as metaphorical and compares Abraham's 'I am going to my Lord' at 37:99. Alusi reports the answer as arrival at the place of God's promise or similar. [4 notes: JISHUMI(أبو علي); QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(المجسمة); RAZI-FULL+]
- 20:74/q04/p2 Bodily coming to the Lord implies that God occupies a place. — Razi attributes this inference to corporealists; Alusi attributes it to anthropomorphists. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL(المجسمة); ALUSI-FULL(المجسمة); RAZI-FULL-]

## 20:74/q05 Who is the criminal meant here?  [مُجْرِمًۭا · referent]
turns on: Whether mujrim is used in its broad sense of sin or its Quranic contextual sense of persistent unbelief; the reported gloss through Ata singles out Pharaoh-like wrongdoing.
- 20:74/q05/p1 An unbeliever or polytheist, especially one who dies persisting in that state. — Muqatil addresses Pharaoh as a polytheist; Ibn Abbas via al-Dahhak says unbeliever. Tha'labi, Wahidi Wajiz, Baghawi, Baydawi, Bursevi and Alusi specify persistence until death, sometimes with sins or disobedience. Qurtubi prefers this over 'sinner': Hell's description accords with 4:56, and the next verse contrasts a believer. Ibn Ashur cites Quranic usage at 83:29; Alusi reports Sunnis citing 74:41, 74:46 and 83:29. Asad reports Baydawi's description of conscious, persistent denial in contrast to the next verse. [18 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); TABRISI(ابن عباس عن الضحاك); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES(Baydawi); ALUSI-FULL(أهل السنة)+; IBNASHUR-FULL+; QURTUBI-FULL+]
- 20:74/q05/p2 A sinner or wrongdoer, understood from committing crimes, sins, or disobedience. — Ibn Atiyya says one who acquired sins and offenses; Biqai says one who breaches God's commands; Ibn Ashur defines crime as sin and a vile deed. Razi excludes a merely minor sinner because mujrim is a term of blame. Alusi reports the Mu'tazilite root explanation: originally cutting fruit, then doing something reprehensible. Islahi specifies one who has not repented; Baydawi, Bursevi and Alusi include disbelief and disobedience together. Qurtubi reports the sinner gloss but prefers unbeliever. [12 notes: JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(المعتزلة); IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ASAD-NOTES]
- 20:74/q05/p3 Someone who committed wrongdoing like Pharaoh's. — This is Ibn Abbas's gloss in the report through Ata, cited by Wahidi and Tabrisi. [2 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); TABRISI(ابن عباس عن عطاء)]
- 20:74/q05/p4 In an inward reading, one burdened with bodily dispositions that incline toward natural bodies. — Qashani locates this at the minor resurrection and says such dispositions carry the criminal there. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 20:74/q06 Why is the arriving person called a criminal for earlier conduct?  [يَأْتِ رَبَّهُۥ مُجْرِمًۭا · rhetoric]
- 20:74/q06/p1 The person is named by the criminal state or acts he had in worldly life. — Burhan states that the name carries over from the worldly state. Itqan compares adults still called orphans and former spouses called husbands. [2 notes: BURHAN; ITQAN]

## 20:74/q07 How is mujriman construed grammatically?  [مُجْرِمًۭا · grammar]
- 20:74/q07/p1 It is a circumstantial accusative describing the subject of 'comes.' — Samin al-Durr identifies it as the hal of the subject of ya'ti. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 20:74/q08 Does this threat establish permanent Hell for a believer guilty of a major sin?  [مَن يَأْتِ رَبَّهُۥ مُجْرِمًۭا فَإِنَّ لَهُۥ جَهَنَّمَ لَا يَمُوتُ فِيهَا وَلَا يَحْيَىٰ · theology]
turns on: The scope of mujrim and conditional man; whether faith and deeds retain reward after a major sin; the force of intercession reports, forgiveness, and the promise at 20:75.
- 20:74/q08/p1 Yes: every major sinner is a criminal and the conditional threat assigns such a person Hell. — Razi reports the Mu'tazila arguing that conditional man ranges over individuals, each exception still leaving a general threat. They say punishment cancels prior obedience's reward; otherwise the sinner should not be cursed or legally penalized. They cite 5:38 to argue deterrent penalty excludes praise and reward. Alusi reports their root-based account of mujrim as a reprehensible actor. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL(المعتزلة); ALUSI-FULL(المعتزلة); RAZI-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL(أهل السنة)-; IBNASHUR-FULL-]
- 20:74/q08/p2 No definite permanent threat follows for sinful believers; they can leave Hell or receive pardon. — Ibn Atiyya distinguishes unbelievers, whose skins and punishment renew, from sinful believers released by intercession. Razi reports a sound tradition that anyone with a particle of faith leaves the Fire, while noting an objection that a solitary report cannot displace mass-transmitted Quran; a counterargument allows specification in law but says probabilistic evidence cannot settle belief. Razi says faith's reward may repel sin's punishment, cursing a major sinner is impermissible, and a legal penalty can be trial or deterrence; he rejects privileging 5:38 over reward texts since believers include thieves and thieves include believers. He says conditional man is only probabilistically general and refers to al-Mahsul. Alusi reports Sunni arguments from the term's Quranic use for unbelievers, possible forgiveness, uncertain conditional universality, and rejection of an intermediate state between belief and unbelief. Ibn Ashur says other evidence proves the unbeliever's eternity but not a sinful believer's, against Mu'tazilites and Kharijites. [12 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL(الخصم); ALUSI-FULL(أهل السنة); IBNASHUR-FULL+; RAZI-FULL+]
- 20:74/q08/p3 The contrast with believers and the unending punishment show that a major sinner is not the criminal described here. — Some of Razi's fellow theologians cite 20:75 and 83:29 and say a major sinner does not remain in the state of neither dying nor living. Razi objects that their believer contrast presupposes a status the Mu'tazila deny; severe Hell punishment can fit a major sinner, as 3:192 indicates. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL(بعض المتكلمين من أصحابنا); RAZI-FULL-]
- 20:74/q08/p4 The warning is conditioned on absence of repentance, cancelling reward, or divine forgiveness. — An objector reported by Razi says the threat is already agreed to depend on no repentance and no reward cancelling punishment. Alusi reports Sunnis allowing forgiveness. Razi says an added repentance condition is unnecessary: a repentant person does not arrive as a criminal. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL(معترض آخر); ALUSI-FULL(أهل السنة); RAZI-FULL-]
- 20:74/q08/p5 The following promise for a believer with righteous deeds creates a countervailing general text for one who later commits a major sin. — Razi identifies this as the sound objection to the universal threat, citing 20:75; his subsequent discussion weighs whether faith's reward or sin's punishment prevails. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL+]

## 20:74/q09 What does 'for him is Hell' indicate?  [فَإِنَّ لَهُۥ جَهَنَّمَ · interpretation]
- 20:74/q09/p1 Hell is the criminal's due destination or dwelling. — Jishumi calls it his dwelling; Biqai calls it an abode of humiliation. Ibn Ashur says the lam marks deservedness and an inevitable end in Hell. Ibn Kathir places arrival before God on Judgment Day before entry into Hell; Asad likewise describes the sinner appearing before God and facing Hell. Nasafi identifies the pronoun in lahu with the criminal. [6 notes: JISHUMI; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES]

## 20:74/q10 How is the clause 'he neither dies nor lives' grammatically attached?  [لَا يَمُوتُ فِيهَا وَلَا يَحْيَىٰ · grammar]
- 20:74/q10/p1 It can be a circumstantial clause attached to the pronoun in lahu or to Jahannam. — Samin al-Durr says each possible antecedent has a pronoun represented within the clause. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 20:74/q11 What does it mean that Hell's inhabitant neither dies nor lives?  [لَا يَمُوتُ فِيهَا وَلَا يَحْيَىٰ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether denial of death and life describes ongoing painful existence, a soul suspended at the throat, spiritual deprivation, or an inward condition.
- 20:74/q11/p1 There is no relieving death and no pleasant or beneficial life; torment continues while its victim remains alive enough to feel it. — Muqatil cites 87:1; Wahidi reports Ibn Abbas, Kalbi and Mubarrad, who says Arabic uses 'neither living nor dead' for useless life. Mawardi and Ibn al-Anbari cite poems about misery without death or tasted life; Wahidi records a similar line by Ubayd Allah and Ibn Mirdas's line about results falling short of their purposes. Razi notes a living body must be alive or dead, so life is denied by quality; Ibn Ashur says the negated life is one free of pain and compares the nonliteral double denial with 2:68 and 24:35, adducing Abbas ibn Mirdas. Ibn Kathir cites 35:36, 87:11–13 and 43:77: death does not end torment, punishment is not eased, and pleas to die fail. Ibn Atiyya describes renewed skins and punishment; Biqai contrasts Pharaoh's punishment, which would end in death. Tabatabai contrasts worldly punishment and cites 20:71. Study Quran cites 14:16–17 and 87:13. The remaining commentators similarly stress no respite, escape, comfort or benefit. [34 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس,ابن مرداس,الكلبي,المبرد,عبيد الله بن عبد الله بن عتبة بن مسعود); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن الأنباري); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL(عباس بن مرداس); TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 20:74/q11/p2 The unbeliever's soul stays in the throat, neither departing in death nor settling into life. — Mawardi gives this as an alternative and Qurtubi reports it as another explanation. [2 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]
- 20:74/q11/p3 The condemned undergo neither spiritual rebirth nor peaceful extinction. — Asad attributes this explanation to Baghawi and Baydawi. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES(Baghawi, Baydawi)]
- 20:74/q11/p4 In the minor resurrection, bodily disposition leaves the soul unable to escape pain through natural death or escape sin's effects through true life. — Qashani gives this inward reading. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 20:74/q12 What do the prophetic reports say happens to people brought out of Hell?  [لَا يَمُوتُ فِيهَا وَلَا يَحْيَىٰ · reports]
turns on: The transmitted descriptions differ in the named river and the comparison for renewed growth.
- 20:74/q12/p1 Permanent inhabitants neither die nor live; others are burned to charcoal, then revived by Paradise's river water like seeds in flood silt. — Wahidi attributes the report through Abu Nadr from Abu Sa'id to the Prophet without grading. Ibn Kathir cites Abu Sa'id's report, transmitted by Ahmad and included by Muslim, and says intercessors bring the groups to the rivers. Razi cites a sound report that anyone with a particle of faith eventually leaves the Fire. [3 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(النبي); IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو سعيد الخدري); RAZI-FULL]
- 20:74/q12/p2 Permanent inhabitants never die; others touched or killed by fire are brought by intercessors to the river of Life and regrow like floodborne gourds or cucumbers. — Ibn Kathir's second report says the Prophet recited this verse in a sermon and compares the growth to cucumbers; Durr also says cucumbers. Alusi says gourds and cites Muslim, Ahmad, Ibn Abi Hatim and Ibn Mardawayh without assigning a grade. [3 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو سعيد الخدري); DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ALUSI-FULL(أبو سعيد الخدري؛ رسول الله)]

## 20:74/q13 How should the report that fire 'kills' sinful believers be understood?  [لَا يَمُوتُ فِيهَا · theology]
turns on: Whether the emphatic wording of the prophetic report requires actual departure of the soul; Alusi says death has only one kind, though its causes vary.
- 20:74/q13/p1 They literally die in Hell, unlike its permanent unbelieving inhabitants, and remain there until God wills their release. — Some cited by Alusi take the report's emphatic verbal noun to rule out metaphor; Alusi compares emphatic wording in 4:164. On this view they suffer the fire and, after death, deprivation of Paradise. Ibn Atiyya also reports an authentic hadith saying they are made to die a death, though he explains it differently. [4 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم,ذلك القائل); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحديث الصحيح); ALUSI-FULL-]
- 20:74/q13/p2 Their 'death' is figurative: the fire makes them like the dead, without full awareness of torment. — Others cited by Alusi say an emphatic verbal noun may accompany figurative usage, as when someone is said to have been 'killed' with a beating. Ibn Atiyya explains the hadith against the premise that anyone actually dies in the afterlife; he distinguishes these believers from unbelievers whose punishment renews. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحديث الصحيح)+]

## 20:74/q14 How does this warning relate to the following promise to believers?  [مُجْرِمًۭا لَا يَمُوتُ فِيهَا وَلَا يَحْيَىٰ · links]
- 20:74/q14/p1 The believer's arrival, righteous deeds and high ranks form the contrasting outcome, helping identify the criminal as an unbeliever. — Qurtubi says the believer dies affirming God, performs commanded acts while avoiding prohibitions, and receives exalted ranks beyond description; he uses the contrast to identify the criminal as a polytheist. Bursevi likewise contrasts persistent unbelief with the believer. Razi cites 20:75 both as the contrast invoked by his fellow theologians and as a general promise that may apply to a believer who later sins. Asad reports Baydawi's contrast with mu'min in the next verse. [8 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI; RAZI-FULL(بعض المتكلمين من أصحابنا); ASAD-NOTES(Baydawi)]

## 20:74/q15 Why are the two conditional outcomes placed here in the story?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the immediate explanatory emphasis is God's enduring superiority to Pharaoh or the believers' pursuit of forgiveness and the story's admonition.
- 20:74/q15/p1 They explain and confirm that God is better and more enduring than Pharaoh's threatened punishment. — Bursevi explicitly connects the opening to the preceding declaration. Alusi says both conditional statements confirm it and refute Pharaoh's boast. Ibn Kathir reads the magicians as warning Pharaoh of God's eternal punishment while encouraging eternal reward. [3 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 20:74/q15/p2 The warning explains the magicians' pursuit of forgiveness and serves as admonition within the narrative. — Tabatabai says a person whose sins are not forgiven remains a criminal, explaining forgiveness as faith's goal. Ibn Ashur says the divine interruption confirms the believers' words before the exodus; Ibn Atiyya says it displays Pharaoh's evil and the sorcerers' virtue through the story. [3 notes: TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(فرقة)]

## 20:74/q16 How does the verse about Fire reaching hearts illuminate this punishment?  [لَا يَمُوتُ فِيهَا وَلَا يَحْيَىٰ · links]
- 20:74/q16/p1 Pain reaches the heart, where it would normally kill, yet Hell's inmate endures a deathlike state without dying. — Ibn Qutayba explains 104:6–7 as Fire rising over or overlooking hearts, comparing one who climbs a mountain and looks down; hearts are singled out because pain reaching them kills, while this verse shows that the damned do not actually die. [2 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL]
