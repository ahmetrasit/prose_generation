# 87:13/q01 What does “then” add to the threat? [ثُمَّ · rhetoric]
- 87:13/q01/p1 It raises the threat to a worse degree: enduring between death and life is more terrible than entering and burning in the Fire.
  reasons: Al-Kashshāf, al-Rāzī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Bursawī, al-Ālūsī, and Ibn ʿĀshūr call this a progression in severity. Al-Samīn says it is rank rather than ordinary time. Al-Biqāʿī stresses the great interval or fluctuating degrees of torment, whose full severity God alone knows; al-Ālūsī says permanence there is worse than entering it. Ibn ʿĀshūr explains that burning remains fully felt because death gives no relief.

# 87:13/q03 What condition do the denials of death and life describe? [لَا يَمُوتُ فِيهَا وَلَا يَحْيَىٰ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the denials describe continuing sensation without benefit, an Arabic distress idiom, a soul's physical state, figurative lack of escape, or inward spiritual states; Ibn ʿĀshūr disputes an inferred intermediate state and al-Ālūsī challenges the throat and deliverance readings.
- 87:13/q03/p1 The condemned remain alive and conscious of torment, unable to die for relief or to enjoy a beneficial, pleasant life.
  reasons: Muqātil adds repeated burning and restoration, fire for clothing, flesh for food, and no mercy, satiety, or relief (69:36). Al-Māwardī and al-Qurṭubī cite a poem of a soul unable to end torment or savor life. Al-Rāzī cites 35:36 and Arab usage for severe misery. Ibn Kathīr says continued consciousness allows pain, citing 43:77 and 35:36. Al-Biqāʿī relates lack of useful life to failure to purify oneself, believe, and pray. Al-Itqān says a thing may be denied when its defining benefit is absent. Al-Ālūsī stresses enduring hardship. Elmalılı calls it unsurpassable misery; Quṭb says they wish for death; Ibn ʿĀshūr says sensation never ceases. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says neither death nor blessed life ends punishment; Kur'an Yolu says persistence without positive life conveys eternal horror.
