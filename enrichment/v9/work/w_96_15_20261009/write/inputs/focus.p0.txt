# 96:15/q01 What does kallā say here? [كَلَّا · interpretation]
turns on: The notes differ over whether kallā directly rebukes conduct, declares ignorance of divine sight, or reverses Abū Jahl's boast.
- 96:15/q01/p1 It rebukes and restrains the man who forbids worship.
  reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says it denies Abū Jahl's boast that he can trample Muḥammad's neck; al-Kashshāf, al-Nasafī and al-Rāzī connect it to forbidding God's worship and commanding al-Lāt or idols. Abū Ḥayyān extends the rebuke to similar preventers; al-Biqāʿī calls the offender's blindness chosen, and Quṭb calls this the passage's decisive threat against tyranny.

# 96:15/q02 Why is kallā placed here? [كَلَّا · rhetoric]
- 96:15/q02/p1 This later kallā reinforces an earlier rebuke; its placement resolves suspense created by the earlier one.
  reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the closing position is the natural place for this counterpart; Corpus Coranicum observes that kallā also opens the rebuke in 96:6–8 and the final call in 96:19.

# 96:15/q03 What must the offender stop? [لَّمْ يَنتَهِ · interpretation]
- 96:15/q03/p1 He must stop forbidding prayer, denying, insulting, opposing or harming Muḥammad.
  reasons: Muqātil names denial and turning away; al-Wāḥidī adds insult and harm; al-Biqāʿī says the conditional leaves room for stopping and repentance while preserving responsibility. Bursevī specifies repentance and submission before death. Al-Ṭabarī links the repeated 'Have you seen?' clauses in 96:9,11,13 to the same forbider who denies and turns away.

# 96:15/q04 How does the oath and condition work? [لَئِن لَّمْ يَنتَهِ لَنَسْفَعًۢا · grammar]
turns on: The disagreement concerns the function of the lām before in.
- 96:15/q04/p1 The lām in la-in signals an implied oath; la-nasfaʿan answers it, supplying the omitted answer to the condition.
  reasons: Al-Zarkashī calls the lām preparatory to the oath's answer and compares 59:12 and 3:12; al-Wāḥidī calls it an oath lām and notes that an initial lām attaches only to a noun or noun-like form. Ibn ʿĀshūr states that the condition's own apodosis is omitted; the light emphatic nūn commonly follows affirmative oaths.
  holders (10 notes): BURHAN(الواحدي); BURSEVI; ALUSI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BURHAN+; IBNASHUR+; BURHAN(أثيرالدين)-

# 96:15/q06 What does nasfaʿ mean? [لَنَسْفَعًۢا · meaning]
turns on: Arabic usage cited for seizure and pulling, burning and darkening, striking, branding, or humiliation; some commentators combine these effects.
- 96:15/q06/p1 To seize, grasp and pull forcefully.
  reasons: Abū ʿUbayda and Ibn Qutayba compare taking someone by the hand; Muʾarrij calls it Qurashī usage. Al-Mubarrad stresses a painful tug, and al-Farrāʾ includes crushing or bending the forelock. Al-Azharī, ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib and other cited poets describe grabbing a horse or man's forelock. Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿIkrima and al-Ḥasan gloss it as taking hold; 55:41 supplies a Qurʾānic parallel. Al-Biqāʿī gathers dragging, pushing and striking in the violent act; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ notes the verb occurs only here in the Qurʾān.
