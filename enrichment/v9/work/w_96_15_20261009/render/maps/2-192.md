# 2:192: 14 questions

## 2:192/q01 What does intahaw mean in Arabic?  [ٱنتَهَوْا۟ · meaning]
- 2:192/q01/p1 It means to desist or abstain, originally by responding to a prohibition and then more broadly by leaving an act or intention. — Al-Ṭabrisī contrasts nahy, which forbids by a negative command and disapproval, with amr, which urges by a positive command and the commander's intent; he adduces nahy as a pond holding water back, nuhya for intellect, tanhiya for a rainwater hollow, and inhāʾ for bringing something to a limit. Ibn ʿĀshūr explains the broadened sense because prohibition asks one to leave an act whether already begun or not; he cites al-Nābigha's line forbidding Banū Dhibyān certain acts. Abū Ḥayyān glosses intahā as desisting. [5 notes: TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL(النابغة)]

## 2:192/q02 How is intahaw formed?  [ٱنتَهَوْا۟ · grammar]
- 2:192/q02/p1 It is an iftaʿala form from nahā, describing a subject's action upon himself. — Abū Ḥayyān compares iḍṭaraba. Al-Samīn derives intahaw from intahiyū: either the yāʾ is deleted after a heavy ḍamma, or it becomes alif after moving and the alif is deleted when two sukūns meet. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]

## 2:192/q03 Which opponents are addressed by the condition?  [ٱنتَهَوْا۟ · referent]
- 2:192/q03/p1 Arab polytheists are addressed. — Elmalılı points to 2:191's reference to expelling Muslims from where they had been expelled; People of the Book could submit by paying the poll tax, whereas Arab polytheists had to accept Islam or face the sword. [2 notes: ELMALILI]

## 2:192/q04 What must the opponents desist from?  [ٱنتَهَوْا۟ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the condition follows the immediate command to fight, the promise of divine forgiveness, or both fighting and shirk in the preceding context; 2:193 and 9:6 are also invoked.
- 2:192/q04/p1 They must cease fighting and disbelief or shirk and enter Islam. — Muqātil adds affirmation of God's oneness; al-Ṭabarī adds repentance and return from past sins; Ibn Kathīr specifies fighting in the sanctuary and says even killing Muslims there can be forgiven. Abū Ḥayyān calls this reading good because both fighting and shirk precede the clause. Al-Ālūsī likewise cites both in the context. Bursevī says combat alone cannot entitle them to forgiveness, much less mercy. Al-Qurṭubī cites 8:38. [12 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(مقاتل); THALABI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; STUDYQURAN-]
- 2:192/q04/p2 They must leave disbelief through repentance and enter Islam. — Mujāhid glosses desistance as repentance; al-Ṭabrisī and al-Ālūsī specify repentance from unbelief. Al-Ḥasan and Abū Ḥayyān reason that forgiveness and mercy do not apply to one remaining an unbeliever; Abū Ḥayyān prefers this on the context and 8:38. Al-Rāzī distinguishes public profession, which protects life, from genuine faith, which earns reward and forgiveness. Study Quran reports this as an interpretation of idolaters' conversion. [11 notes: TAB-FULL(مجاهد); IBNABIHATIM(مجاهد); TABRISI(مجاهد); RAZI-FULL(الحسن); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; DURR-FULL(مجاهد); ALUSI-FULL(مجاهد); STUDYQURAN(some commentators); ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; STUDYQURAN-]
- 2:192/q04/p3 They need only stop fighting and hostility here. — Ibn ʿAbbās says permission to fight aims to stop the enemy's fighting; al-Samīn supplies 'from fighting' as the omitted complement. Al-Biqāʿī includes fighting's preliminaries. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ limits this clause to combat at the Sacred Mosque and assigns cessation through Islam to 2:193. Study Quran objects to making conversion the end of hostilities: fighting and expulsion have political causes, and 9:6 grants safe conduct to an idolater seeking asylum. [9 notes: FARRA; RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(others); RAZI-FULL(الحسن)-; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; BURSEVI-]

## 2:192/q05 What is understood but unstated in the conditional sentence?  [فَإِنِ ٱنتَهَوْا۟ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌۭ رَّحِيمٌۭ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the omission concerns what the opponents desist from or the ruling that follows their desistance.
- 2:192/q05/p1 The complement of 'desist' is omitted and supplied by the preceding context. — Al-Ṭabrisī says the preceding context makes the abbreviated clause clear; al-Samīn explicitly supplies 'from fighting.' [2 notes: TABRISI; SAMIN-DURR]
- 2:192/q05/p2 An effect or consequent ruling is implied, with God's forgiveness and mercy given as its reason. — Abū Ḥayyān begins to report an omitted apodosis but the excerpt stops before its wording. Ibn ʿĀshūr supplies 'then forgive them,' since God's forgiveness does not logically follow from enemy cessation, and says the wording urges believers' forgiveness. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ describes the cause put in place of the effect to explain the ruling. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 2:192/q06 What should believers do when the opponents stop fighting?  [فَإِنِ ٱنتَهَوْا۟ · law]
- 2:192/q06/p1 They must cease fighting or killing those opponents, and forgive them. — Al-Farrāʾ says there is no retaliation against those who desist, while wrongdoing remains with those who initiated and persisted. Al-Rāzī says the fighting obligation ends on desistance though some legal penalties may remain, citing 8:38. Ibn ʿĀshūr says believers should make forgiveness their practice; Kur'an Yolu forbids violence against those who have stopped. Abū Ḥayyān reports relief of Muslims' duty to fight as the consequence under a combat-only reading. [6 notes: FARRA; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 2:192/q07 When does the verse remain operative or become abrogated?  [فَإِنِ ٱنتَهَوْا۟ · sciences]
- 2:192/q07/p1 On a combat-only reading it is abrogated; on the two readings involving abandonment of disbelief it remains operative. — Abū Ḥayyān makes the verse's status depend on the sense given to desistance; he refers to the two preceding interpretations as operative. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 2:192/q08 How does this desistance clause relate to the one in 2:193?  [فَإِنِ ٱنتَهَوْا۟ · links]
- 2:192/q08/p1 Each clause limits its nearest preceding statement: this one concerns fighting at the Sacred Mosque, while 2:193 concerns ending all fighting by accepting Islam. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ appeals to the apparent unity and immediate context of the passage to distinguish the two clauses rather than treat them as repetition. [2 notes: TABATABAI]

## 2:192/q09 How is desistance read inwardly?  [ٱنتَهَوْا۟ · inward]
- 2:192/q09/p1 Cease the self's and intrusive thoughts' rivalry with God, surrender to His decree, and follow the ruling of the moment without personal preference. — Al-Qushayrī identifies the self as one's fiercest enemy, calls for its discipline until human traces and self-directed planning disappear, and tells the guardian of secrets to quiet inner disputes. Once the self surrenders, only the negligent remain liable and the obedient are released from the command's burden. Where two claims both belong to God, choosing for even slight personal interest veils the heart. [4 notes: QUSHAYRI]

## 2:192/q10 What does maghfira mean?  [غَفُورٌۭ · meaning]
- 2:192/q10/p1 It is covering a sin so that legal judgment treats it as though it never happened. — Al-Ṭabrisī gives this sense of maghfira. [1 notes: TABRISI]

## 2:192/q11 Whom does God's forgiveness and mercy benefit here, and how?  [فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌۭ رَّحِيمٌۭ · referent]
turns on: Whether the opponents' cessation includes repentance and faith, permitting forgiveness for them, or only ends combat and thus relieves Muslims of fighting.
- 2:192/q11/p1 They benefit the repentant former opponents: God forgives earlier sins and grants them mercy. — Al-Akhfash supplies the understood object 'toward them.' Muqātil and Ibn Abī Ḥātim name former shirk; al-Ṭabarī names past sins and the hereafter's reward; al-Wāḥidī names unbelief and fighting; Saʿīd ibn Jubayr places mercy after repentance. Al-Nasafī says God mercifully accepts repentance and faith; al-Biqāʿī says God has always been Forgiving and Merciful toward anyone who repents; Ibn Kathīr includes even killings in the sanctuary; Abū Ḥayyān says Islam erases what came before. [16 notes: MUQATIL; AKHFASH; TAB-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(سعيد بن جبير,مقاتل); THALABI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+]
- 2:192/q11/p2 On a combat-only reading, mercy is directed to Muslims by lifting their duty to fight. — Abū Ḥayyān reports this further view: cessation concerns fighting alone, and forgiveness does not mean forgiveness for still unbelieving opponents. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 2:192/q12 Does the verse support acceptance of repentance for deliberate murder?  [فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌۭ رَّحِيمٌۭ · law]
- 2:192/q12/p1 Yes; if repentance from the graver sin of shirk or disbelief is accepted, repentance from intentional murder is accepted too. — Al-Ṭabrisī argues from the acceptance of a polytheist's repentance; al-Rāzī applies this to every sin and rejects the claim that repentance for intentional murder is never accepted, observing that a repentant unbeliever may also have killed. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī's report from al-Baḥr repeat the comparison with disbelief. Ibn Kathīr says even killing Muslims in the sanctuary is forgivable upon repentance. [5 notes: TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(البحر); IBNKATHIR-FULL]

## 2:192/q13 What setting or transmission is reported for this passage?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports give different sequences around Hudaybiya; one note supplies a transmission chain summary without its interpretation.
- 2:192/q13/p1 After the Prophet was blocked at Hudaybiya, an attempted polytheist attack led to permission to fight. — Kur'an Yolu reports that after the Hijra the Prophet set out with many Muslims to visit Mecca, Quraysh blocked them at Hudaybiya, and he sought a peaceful settlement before a polytheist unit attempted an attack; it points to 2:194. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 2:192/q13/p2 The passage set fighting guidelines as Muslims approached the next year's pilgrimage after the Hudaybiya treaty. — Study Quran reports that Muslims had turned back from ʿumrah in 628 under treaty terms and feared an attack and fighting in the sacred precinct during sacred months. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(some commentators)]
- 2:192/q13/p3 Al-Durr al-Manthūr transmits a preceding interpretation through several routes from Ibn ʿAbbās and others. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ notes the multiple transmission routes without restating that interpretation in this excerpt. [1 notes: TABATABAI]

## 2:192/q14 Were the verses in this passage revealed together?  [* · sciences]
- 2:192/q14/p1 Their apparent unity indicates that they were revealed together. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ argues from the passage's apparent unity. [1 notes: TABATABAI]
