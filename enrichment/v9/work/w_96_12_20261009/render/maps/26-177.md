# 26:177: 10 questions

## 26:177/q01 Who were the people Shuʿayb addressed in relation to Midian?  [لهم · referent]
turns on: Whether the different names designate two nations or one community; the notes appeal to Shuʿayb's kinship, the differing punishment reports, and the shared warning about measures and weights.
- 26:177/q01/p1 They were a people distinct from Midian, and Shuʿayb was sent to both. — Abū Zayd as reported by al-Thaʿlabī distinguishes Shuʿayb's own Midian from the nomadic Thicket people. Al-Wāḥidī's exegetes, al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Ālūsī, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, and al-Sāmarrāʾī distinguish the groups; al-Wāḥidī cites 7:85 and 11:84, and al-Sāmarrāʾī adds 23:36. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah reports seven Thicket towns separate from Shuʿayb's Midian. Ibn Abī Ḥātim transmits al-Rabīʿ's account of a further mission to the Thicket herders. Ibn al-Jawzī reports Muqātil's sequence, Midian followed by the Thicket. Abū Ḥayyān reports a rural versus town distinction. Ibn Kathīr relays ʿIkrimah's two-mission story but calls it weak; he also relays a report attributed to the Prophet via ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAmr calling them two nations, but calls it strange and doubts its prophetic attribution. Ibn al-Jawzī reports Muḥammad b. Kaʿb's canopy punishment for Midian, which would be a shared fate if the peoples differed. Al-Ālūsī calls Ibn ʿAbbās's equation of the two an unusual report. [20 notes: IBNABIHATIM(الربيع); THALABI(أبو زيد); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس,المفسرون); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(محمد بن كعب القرظي,مقاتل بن سليمان); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(عكرمة); BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; SAMARRAI-ASRAR(الحديث,الكشاف); IBNKATHIR-FULL(عبد الله بن عمرو)-]
- 26:177/q01/p2 The Thicket people and Midian were the same community described by different names. — Ibn Jarīr, reported by Ibn al-Jawzī, identifies them as one people. Abū Ḥayyān reports this from Ibn ʿAbbās; al-Ālūsī calls that report unusual. Ibn Kathīr argues that Shuʿayb was their kinsman and that the shared command to give full measure and weight confirms a single community; he rejects the two-nation explanation, questions the prophetic attribution of the contrary report, and calls ʿIkrimah's two-mission report weak. Qatādah, as reported by Ibn Kathīr, calls Shuʿayb's people both the People of the Well and the People of the Thicket, citing 50:12 and 50:14. Iṣlāḥī calls the section an account of Shuʿayb and Midian. [7 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(محمد بن كعب القرظي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNKATHIR-FULL(عبد الله بن عمرو)+]

## 26:177/q02 What particular identity or setting is reported for the people addressed?  [لهم · referent]
turns on: The notes give different reports of tribal identity, number of settlements, pastoral setting, and another name; they do not argue directly among these details.
- 26:177/q02/p1 They were pastoral or rural Thicket people, distinct from Midian's town dwellers. — Al-Rabīʿ, transmitted by Ibn Abī Ḥātim, says herders moved to a spring-fed grove when the lowlands dried, and Shuʿayb warned them before they denied him. Abū Ḥayyān reports the rural versus town distinction; al-Biqāʿī contrasts pastoral people with Shuʿayb the city dweller. Abū Zayd, reported by al-Thaʿlabī, calls them nomads. [4 notes: IBNABIHATIM(الربيع); THALABI(أبو زيد); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 26:177/q02/p2 They were people of Juthām. — Ibn ʿAbbās, as reported by al-Wāḥidī, says Shuʿayb was sent to his own people and another people, the Thicket people of Juthām; al-Wāḥidī mentions 7:85 and 11:84. [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس)]
- 26:177/q02/p3 They inhabited seven towns. — Ibn ʿAṭiyyah reports seven towns and says Shuʿayb, being from Midian, was not one of their inhabitants. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 26:177/q02/p4 Shuʿayb's people were also called the People of the Well. — Qatādah, as reported by Ibn Kathīr, identifies them with both the People of the Well and the Thicket people, mentioning 50:12 and 50:14. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(قتادة)]

## 26:177/q03 What does the name of the people addressed, al-Aykah or Laykah, denote?  [لهم · meaning]
turns on: Whether the designation is a common word for trees, one tree, a region, or an independent proper name.
- 26:177/q03/p1 It denotes a dense grove or thicket of intertwined trees. — Al-Ṭabarī calls aykah the singular of ayk and says Arabs use it for densely interwoven trees, citing al-Nābighah's dove-in-a-grove line. Al-Bayḍāwī describes soft trees near Midian. Al-Ālūsī reports the interwoven-tree sense, and cites al-Bukhārī's gloss of both al-Aykah and Laykah as the thicket. Al-Thaʿlabī treats al-Ayka and al-Layka as dialectal forms. [5 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(البخاري)]
- 26:177/q03/p2 It is a particular intertwined dawmah tree. — Al-Bayḍāwī offers this as another view of the grove; al-Ālūsī includes the dawmah tree among the reported interwoven trees. [2 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 26:177/q03/p3 Al-Aykah names the whole country. — Al-Ālūsī reports this alternative, comparing its name to Mecca and Bakka; he says the Thicket people remain distinct from Midian on either the grove or country interpretation. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 26:177/q03/p4 Laykah is the proper name of a settlement, possibly a foreign name. — Abū ʿUbaydah, as reported by al-Ālūsī, cites an exegesis book naming Laykah as a settlement. Al-Bayḍāwī notes the place-name analysis. Al-Ālūsī says absence of an attested Arabic l-y-k root does not exclude a foreign name, and explains its diptote form by proper-name status, foreign origin, and femininity. Al-Zamakhsharī disputes the place-name interpretation, saying Laykah is otherwise unknown. [3 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL(ابن كثير ونافع وابن عامر); ALUSI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري)-]

## 26:177/q04 How is the people's name read and analyzed?  [لهم · readings]
turns on: Whether the no-hamza form can be a genuine proper name and diptote reading or is merely phonetic spelling mistakenly reanalyzed as a place name.
- 26:177/q04/p1 Laykah is read without hamza, with the shifted vowel or a fatḥa, and may be treated as a proper name. — Al-Bayḍāwī names Ibn Kathīr, Nāfiʿ, and Ibn ʿĀmir and explains the vowel shift and spelling without alif. Al-Ālūsī names the two Haramain readers and Ibn ʿĀmir for fatḥa on lām and diptote treatment here and in Ṣād; he also reports the hamza-deletion spelling. Abū ʿUbaydah cites an exegesis book calling Laykah a settlement. Al-Ālūsī defends the reading as one of the seven and mutawātir, argues a foreign proper name can be diptote despite the unattested Arabic root, and says denial of the mutawātir reading approaches apostasy; he names al-Mubarrad, Ibn Qutaybah, al-Zajjāj, al-Fārisī, and al-Naḥḥās as predecessors of al-Zamakhsharī in rejecting it. [8 notes: THALABI; BAYDAWI-FULL(ابن كثير ونافع وابن عامر); ALUSI-FULL(أبو عبيدة,الحرميان); ALUSI-FULL+; ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري)-]
- 26:177/q04/p2 Al-Aykah is also read with hamza, as the word for the thicket. — Al-Thaʿlabī says al-Ayka and al-Layka are dialectal forms both read in the Qurʾān; al-Ālūsī reports that al-Bukhārī gives both and glosses them as the thicket. Al-Ālūsī contrasts the spelling of the hamza form with Laykah's pronunciation-based spelling. [3 notes: THALABI; ALUSI-FULL(البخاري)]
- 26:177/q04/p3 Laykah is only a pronunciation-based spelling of al-Aykah; treating it as an independent accusative place name is a mistake. — Al-Zamakhsharī, as reported by al-Ālūsī, says the codex's lack of alif misled readers into treating Laykah as a place name; these two spellings may simply follow pronunciation, the story is one, and Laykah is unknown. Al-Ālūsī rejects this as unsupported because the sevenfold reading is mutawātir, also citing a report from an exegesis book; he adds the possibility of a foreign proper name. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL-]

## 26:177/q05 How is Shuʿayb introduced in this address?  [شعيب · rhetoric]
- 26:177/q05/p1 He is named as the speaker without the title 'their brother'. — Al-Ālūsī expressly notes the opening's wording: Shuʿayb spoke to them, with no fraternal title. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 26:177/q06 Why is Shuʿayb not called 'their brother' here?  [شعيب · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the omission marks lack of kinship with a separate people, association with tree worship despite kinship, or simple brevity.
- 26:177/q06/p1 He belonged to Midian, not to the Thicket people, so he was not their kinsman. — Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī's exegetes, al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī reporting Muqātil, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, and al-Sāmarrāʾī contrast this verse with the 'brother' wording for Midian at 7:85 or 11:84. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah reports that Shuʿayb came from Midian outside their seven towns. Al-Bayḍāwī calls him an outsider to the grove's people. Al-Biqāʿī explains the distinction as a city dweller addressing pastoral people and says the sending of prophets from towns honors settled communities for their shared wisdom and blessing. Al-Sāmarrāʾī contrasts the other Shuʿarāʾ prophets addressing their own peoples, cites al-Kashshāf's same explanation and a hadith that Midian's brother was sent to both peoples, and says contextual differences guide choice of expression. [15 notes: THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(مقاتل بن سليمان); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI; SAMARRAI-ASRAR(الحديث,الكشاف); IBNKATHIR-FULL-]
- 26:177/q06/p2 Although he was their kinsman, the fraternal title is omitted because they were associated with worship of the Thicket tree. — Ibn Kathīr prefers identifying these people with Midian and says their tree worship explains the omission even though Shuʿayb was related to them. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL+]
- 26:177/q06/p3 The fraternal title is omitted for brevity, though the peoples are the same. — Ibn Jarīr's explanation is reported by Ibn al-Jawzī alongside his identification of the Thicket people with Midian. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(محمد بن كعب القرظي)]

## 26:177/q07 What does Shuʿayb ask with 'ألا تتقون'?  [ألا تتقون · interpretation]
- 26:177/q07/p1 He calls them to fear God and guard against disobedience and its punishment, including shirk. — Yaḥyā b. Sallām glosses the question as being wary of God. Al-Biqāʿī glosses taqwā as fear of God. Al-Ṭabarī specifies fear of punishment for disobeying their Lord; al-Bursawī specifies punishment for associating partners with Him. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the summons forbids shirk because the people were polytheists, citing Hūd 11:84. Muqātil and al-Qurṭubī place this call with Shuʿayb's claim to be a trustworthy messenger and command to obey; Muqātil also notes he sought no payment and entrusted his reward to the Lord of the worlds, while al-Ṭabarī says obedience leads to right guidance. [7 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 26:177/q08 What tone does 'ألا تتقون' give Shuʿayb's appeal?  [ألا تتقون · rhetoric]
- 26:177/q08/p1 It is a gentle and tactful appeal. — Ibn ʿAṭiyyah describes the question in these terms and mentions 79:18. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL]

## 26:177/q09 Why does Shuʿayb's call repeat the formula used by earlier prophets?  [* · links]
- 26:177/q09/p1 The recurring call expresses the prophets' single faith and shared mission. — Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, Ibn ʿAṭiyyah, al-Ṭabrisī, and al-Biqāʿī say the repeated words show shared piety, obedience, sincere or exclusive worship, and taking no fee for preaching. Al-Wāḥidī cites 26:106-109 and includes delivering the message; al-Ṭabrisī adds avoiding sins and says no fee removes a deterrent to acceptance; al-Biqāʿī adds counsel, integrity, and honesty. Yaḥyā b. Sallām calls it a repeated exhortation. Ibn ʿĀshūr notes that Shuʿayb opens like earlier messengers for a reason explained previously. Iṣlāḥī says the opening repeats material of the preceding prophetic account and has already been explained. [10 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 26:177/q10 How does this episode connect with the surrounding and parallel accounts?  [* · links]
- 26:177/q10/p1 It continues the pattern of rejected prophetic warnings and points readers to parallel accounts of Midian. — Al-Thaʿlabī says the preceding destruction of Lot's people is a sign: most did not believe, while God is mighty and merciful. Iṣlāḥī compares Lot's warning about moral misconduct with Shuʿayb's warning about economic misconduct; the people persisted, shared the other nations' fate, and their account closes with repeated verses. Asad directs readers to his note on 7:85 and the fuller Shuʿayb and Midian narrative at 11:84-95. Ibn ʿĀshūr invokes 11:84 for the people's polytheism. [4 notes: THALABI; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
