# 96:7/q04 What self-sufficiency does istaghna describe? [ٱسْتَغْنَىٰٓ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the perceived support is money, one's own effort, or supposed independence from the Lord; some notes distinguish the felt state from its arrogant consequence.
- 96:7/q04/p1 He feels wealthy or financially secure.
  reasons: Mawardi offers wealth as one alternative; Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Alusi and Ibn Ashur describe riches. Ibn Ashur says the sin and ta intensify the sense, comparing istajaba and istaqarra; Corpus Coranicum prefers the state of considering oneself sufficient over translating the word as acting arrogantly. Alusi calls money the apparent, familiar opposite of poverty.

- 96:7/q04/p2 He imagines himself independent of God, whether through wealth, clan, or strength.
  reasons: Ibn Abbas as reported by Mawardi says independent of his Lord. Tabrisi lists kin, wealth and force but insists the belief, not actual possessions, produces tyranny. Qurtubi and Alusi report clan, helpers and supporters; Alusi finds this less apparent than financial wealth. Bint al-Shati associates this claim with acquired knowledge, not money or status. Study Quran calls claimed independence from God the root of rebellion.

# 96:7/q05 Who is the human described here? [* · referent]
turns on: Whether the general noun denotes humanity as a recurring type or points first to the reported Meccan antagonist.
- 96:7/q05/p1 Human beings generally are vulnerable to this pattern.
  reasons: Tabari speaks of a person given creation, knowledge and favors; Qushayri, Tabrisi, Ibn Kathir, Biqai, Qutb and Kuranyolu extend it to people generally. Bint al-Shati reports Razi's generic reading. Elmalili says Abu Jahl exemplifies a general warning. Faith or God's protection can restrain it, according to Qutb and Ibn Atiyya.

# 96:7/q06 Why does perceived sufficiency lead to transgression? [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the mechanism is felt financial or social security, claiming credit for one's wealth, or imagined independence through acquired knowledge.
- 96:7/q06/p1 Feeling rich or independent breeds arrogance, disbelief and exceeding one's bounds.
  reasons: Tabari contrasts this with God's gifts of sound creation, knowledge and favors; Ibn Kathir adds joy and boasting. Qushayri says witnessing one's actual need prevents it. Tabrisi says belief rather than actual wealth is decisive; Alusi's visual parse makes even the appearance of wealth enough and cites 42:27. Biqai says people change conduct toward what they think they no longer need. Ibn Ashur traces contempt and abuse to the belief that others need the rich man and that weapons, servants and allies protect him; religious or rational restraint can check this. Qutb stresses failure to thank the giver. Corpus Coranicum distinguishes perceived sufficiency from arrogance itself.
