# 18:35: 19 questions

## 18:35/q01 What happened when the man entered his garden?  [وَدَخَلَ جَنَّتَهُۥ · reports]
- 18:35/q01/p1 He led his believing companion through the garden to show off its beauty and wealth. — Al-Wāḥidī reports the commentators and Ibn al-Jawzī say he took his Muslim brother by the hand; al-Kashshāf names the owner Qaṭrus and says he showed both gardens and boasted of wealth; al-Baghawī specifies fruit; al-Bayḍāwī says they toured together. Al-Qurṭubī reports this as one account. Bursawī says Qaṭrus showed one garden to his brother; Iṣlāḥī stresses that the entrance accompanied proud speech. [9 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 18:35/q02 Why is his garden singular although he has two?  [جَنَّتَهُۥ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the singular marks sequential physical entry, a connected pair, or all the owner's worldly possessions; the Hudhalī grammatical analogy and the entry narrative are disputed.
- 18:35/q02/p1 He entered one of the two gardens at a time. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān say simultaneous entry into both is impossible; Ibn ʿĀshūr says he entered one first. Al-Nasafī allows one of the pair, while al-Bayḍāwī, Bursawī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī give successive entry as one explanation. Abū Ḥayyān calls this the meaning of the report and objects to al-Zamakhsharī's collective reading. [9 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(بعضهم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL+]
- 18:35/q02/p2 The two adjoining gardens count as one orchard or a collective garden. — Yaḥyā and al-Suddī describe one orchard divided by a river; al-Nasafī mentions a shared wall despite the river. Al-Bayḍāwī and Bursawī cite adjacency; al-Farrāʾ cites this verse's singular for treating the pair as one; al-Biqāʿī calls the singular generic. Abū al-Baqāʾ takes one for a pair and cites a Hudhalī verse, but al-Samīn says its pronouns agree with a broken plural; al-Ālūsī calls al-Suddī's account unpersuasive. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī permits a generic singular. [10 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; NASAFI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURHAN(الفراء); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(الشيخ); SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء,الزمخشري); TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL(السدي)-]
- 18:35/q02/p3 His sole garden is his whole worldly estate, with no share in the promised Paradise. — Al-Zamakhsharī says neither individual orchard is specifically designated; al-Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān report the same contrast with Paradise. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Biqāʿī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī include it; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls it subtle. Al-Samīn replies to the Sheikh's objection that al-Zamakhsharī need not mean simultaneous entry. Al-Ālūsī prefers this collective sense because context has the companion entering with him; Abū Ḥayyān rejects it in favor of entry into one garden. [10 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري); TABATABAI(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL+; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]

## 18:35/q03 To whom do the pronouns in the passage refer?  [وَدَخَلَ جَنَّتَهُۥ وَهُوَ لِّنَفْسِهِۦ · referent]
- 18:35/q03/p1 The four pronouns refer to the garden owner. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī states this explicitly while explaining the singular garden as generic, successive entry, or the worldly pair together. [1 notes: TABATABAI]

## 18:35/q04 How does the clause 'while wronging himself' work grammatically?  [وَهُوَ ظَالِمٌۭ لِّنَفْسِهِۦ · grammar]
- 18:35/q04/p1 It is a circumstantial clause of the subject of 'entered,' with 'to himself' governed by 'wronging.' — Al-Samīn says the lām is additional because ẓālim is a derived governing form. Al-Ālūsī also identifies the phrase as circumstantial and connects it to the danger of disbelief and lost blessings. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]

## 18:35/q05 In what way was he wronging himself?  [وَهُوَ ظَالِمٌۭ لِّنَفْسِهِۦ · interpretation]
turns on: The notes variously name disbelief and resurrection denial, ingratitude for God's gift, and pride or reliance on wealth; several combine them.
- 18:35/q05/p1 He disbelieved in God, associated partners with Him, or denied resurrection. — Ibn ʿAbbās says he left faith for disbelief; Qatāda and Ibn Zayd add corrupt beliefs and doubt about rising. Al-Zajjāj says disbelief consigns a person to lasting Fire, the gravest self-wrong. Other sources add disobedience, rebellion, vanity, weak certainty and reliance on visible causes; Ibn ʿĀshūr specifies shirk and denial of resurrection. Al-Jishumī explains the related word radd as returning oneself to unbelief. [21 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس,الزجاج); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(قتادة وابن زيد); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]
- 18:35/q05/p2 He was ungrateful for God's favor and used blessings for boasting instead of gratitude. — Al-Zamakhsharī says admiration and boasting exposed him to divine anger; al-Rāzī says the clause interrupts the story to show misuse of blessings for ingratitude and denial of resurrection. Qatāda in al-Durr calls it ingratitude for his Lord's favor. Al-Ālūsī says he misplaced gratitude and humility; the Study Quran cites 3:117 and 10:44 for self-wronging through sin and disbelief. [6 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; DURR-FULL(قتادة رضي الله عنه); ALUSI-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 18:35/q05/p3 He was conceited, proud of his wealth, and relied on it rather than on his Lord. — Al-Bayḍāwī combines conceit with disbelief; al-Biqāʿī says wealth displaced reliance on God. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī adds pride toward his companion, self-admiration and latent shirk through forgetting God; Iṣlāḥī describes proud speech, and the Study Quran says he refused to acknowledge God as provider. Al-Nīsābūrī gives an inward reading of wealth and status as blameworthy inclinations. [8 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN; ALUSI-FULL(النيسابوري)]

## 18:35/q06 Why is his speech introduced after the account of his entry?  [وَدَخَلَ جَنَّتَهُۥ وَهُوَ ظَالِمٌۭ لِّنَفْسِهِۦ قَالَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the later companion's speech is another circumstantial clause or a new explanatory sentence.
- 18:35/q06/p1 The speech explains the self-wrong and answers the implied question of what he said. — Al-Ālūsī says the speech answers a question prompted by the unjust entry; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says its clause is separated for an implied question. Al-Rāzī calls the self-wronging description an interruption that exposes ingratitude and resurrection denial. Al-Samīn prefers a new explanatory clause for the later companion's speech over treating it as circumstantial. [4 notes: ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; RAZI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 18:35/q06/p2 The later 'his companion said' may be circumstantial. — Al-Samīn records this possibility for qāla lahu ṣāḥibuhu, but prefers a new explanatory clause. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 18:35/q07 What force does 'I do not think' have here?  [مَآ أَظُنُّ · meaning]
turns on: Whether ẓann retains a sense of supposition or conveys practical certainty.
- 18:35/q07/p1 It expresses supposition or lack of certainty. — Muqātil glosses aẓunnu as 'suppose'; Yaḥyā ibn Sallām explains it as 'I am not certain.' [2 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM]
- 18:35/q07/p2 Ẓann can convey knowledge or near certainty; he could hardly conceive the garden's end. — Bursawī says ẓann is often borrowed for knowledge or near certainty. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the negative form portrays permanence as so secure that destruction does not enter his mind. [2 notes: BURSEVI; TABATABAI]

## 18:35/q08 How do grammarians analyze the construction after 'I think'?  [مَآ أَظُنُّ · grammar]
turns on: Whether an indefinite licenses the separation pronoun; negation changes the indefinite's force.
- 18:35/q08/p1 An indefinite cannot be followed by huwa as a separation pronoun in this construction. — Sībawayh gives the Basran objection: an indefinite cannot take the relevant adjective or apposition. He cites lā yastaqīm aẓunnu rajulan khayran minka, while negation allows an indefinite with the force of aḥad. Yūnus reports Abū ʿAmr judged the usage ungrammatical and calls Ibn Marwān's accusative reading of 15:71 an error; al-Khalīl regards huwa as a genuine pronoun like abūhu, not an empty particle like mā, even between definites. [4 notes: SIBAWAYH(الخليل,يونس)]
- 18:35/q08/p2 Huwa can be a separation pronoun after an indefinite by analogy with definites. — Sībawayh reports this as the Medinan grammarians' analysis; the Basrans, Abū ʿAmr, and al-Khalīl object on syntactic grounds. [1 notes: SIBAWAYH(أهل المدينة); SIBAWAYH(الخليل,يونس)-]

## 18:35/q09 What does 'perish' mean and how is its verb formed?  [تَبِيدَ · meaning]
- 18:35/q09/p1 Bāda yabīdu means perish, disappear, or be destroyed; its verbal nouns include bayd, biyūd, and baydūda. — Muqātil glosses tabīda as perish. Al-Jishumī gives bayd and also the sense 'except'; Abū Ḥayyān gives baydūda and cites poetry where bāda means a household perished. Al-Samīn gives biyūdan and baydūdatan, analyzing the latter as fayʿalūla after deletion of one yāʾ, like kaynūna. Bursawī says disappear; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says destruction and extinction; Ibn ʿĀshūr says perish. Sībawayh pairs bādat tabīdu biyūdan with ghābat al-shams ghuyūban and strong-root julūs and nufūr. [9 notes: MUQATIL; JISHUMI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; SIBAWAYH]

## 18:35/q10 What wider morphology illustrates the verbal noun of tabīda?  [تَبِيدَ · grammar]
- 18:35/q10/p1 Medially weak verbs have several verbal-noun and participle patterns, including the verse's bādat tabīdu biyūdan. — Sībawayh compares bādat with ghābat and regular julūs/nufūr. He surveys bayʿ/kayl with bāʾiʿ/kāʾil, ziyāra/ʿiyāda/ḥiyāka avoiding faʿūl, khawf/khāʾif and transitive analogies, nayl/dhām/ʿāb or ʿayb, sūʾ/qūt and ʿiyāfa, suʾūr/ghuʾūr, qiyām/ṣiyām/iyāb, other yāʾ-root alternatives, dawām/zawāl/rawāḥ, and lāʿ versus lāʾiʿ. Al-Akhṭal's line attests suʾūr; al-ʿAjjāj's line attests its hollow-verb form. Sībawayh compares strong-root forms throughout and notes both wāw and yāʾ roots can avoid faʿūl. [13 notes: SIBAWAYH(الأخطل,العجاج)]

## 18:35/q11 What does 'this' designate in his claim?  [هَٰذِهِۦٓ · referent]
turns on: Whether the demonstrative reaches beyond the visible orchard to the world's created order.
- 18:35/q11/p1 It designates the garden he entered, or its fruit. — Abū Ḥayyān explicitly identifies the demonstrative with the garden entered. Al-Ṭabrisī allows the garden and its fruit; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī also says the destruction spoken of is the garden. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says this narrow reference would express shallow arrogance rather than a theory about cosmic order. [4 notes: TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; TABATABAI; IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 18:35/q11/p2 It designates the whole world or cosmos. — Al-Ṭabrisī gives this as an alternative; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says it would be doubt about the cosmos's created order. Al-Jishumī reports that the man may be denying that the world ends. Al-Wāḥidī and al-Zajjāj take him to deny the world's passing. Abū Ḥayyān calls a cosmic reference remote, and al-Ālūsī rejects cosmic readings as strained. [6 notes: TABRISI(قيل); IBNATIYYA-FULL; JISHUMI; WAHIDI-BASIT; ZAJJAJ; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 18:35/q12 How long did he think the garden would last?  [أَبَدًۭا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether abad is literal eternity, the man's lifetime or another very long span, and whether the claim concerns the orchard or the cosmos.
- 18:35/q12/p1 He imagined the garden would never perish at all. — Muqātil reports the plain claim; al-Baghawī connects it with denial of the Hour. Al-Wāḥidī says the thriving garden led him to deny God's power to end this world. Al-Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī and al-Suddī report the same boast; Ibn Kathīr cites flourishing crops and streams, weak certainty and attachment to this life. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls it a settled assumption of permanence; Iṣlāḥī calls the garden lush and the belief arrogant. [10 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; DURR-FULL(السدي رضي الله عنه); IBNKATHIR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; TABATABAI; YAHYA-SALLAM(الحسن)-; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 18:35/q12/p2 He expected it to survive for his own lifetime, with 'forever' meaning that long span. — Al-Ḥasan denies that he meant literal eternity and compares 104:3, wealth's supposed power to immortalize. Al-Jishumī reports this as one reading. Abū Ḥayyān says he keeps tending the garden with wealth and servants; Bursawī connects it with long hope and heedlessness. Al-Ālūsī prefers this over literal eternity and cosmic readings; Ibn ʿĀshūr says abad means a very long duration, encouraged by strong trees, abundant water and shade. [6 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(الحسن); JISHUMI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 18:35/q12/p3 He supposed the world's states never end, or that an eternal world continually replaces its produce. — Abū Ḥayyān reports these possibilities, saying the first contradicts sense and calling cosmic reference remote. Al-Jishumī reports the everlasting-world alternative. Al-Ālūsī rejects such readings as strained. [2 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI(قيل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 18:35/q13 What bred his confidence that the garden would endure, and what does it illustrate?  [مَآ أَظُنُّ أَن تَبِيدَ هَٰذِهِۦٓ أَبَدًۭا · interpretation]
- 18:35/q13/p1 Abundance, long hopes and heedlessness made him trust a fragile worldly blessing. — Al-Wāḥidī points to the thriving orchard; al-Bayḍāwī and al-Nasafī cite prolonged hope and delusion, and al-Nasafī says many wealthy Muslims implicitly assume the same. Al-Biqāʿī adds greed; Ibn Kathīr names flourishing crops and streams, weak certainty and attachment. Ibn ʿĀshūr points to strong trees, water and shade. The Study Quran connects excess hope with mortality and cites 28:88, 55:26 and 104:3; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says attachment to apparent permanence produces complacency or despair. [10 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN; TABATABAI; BURSEVI]

## 18:35/q14 How does the garden owner's claim connect with the rest of the parable and other verses?  [* · links]
turns on: The sources draw narrative, moral and inward connections at different levels of the parable.
- 18:35/q14/p1 His confidence in worldly success leads to resurrection doubt and expectation of greater favor in the hereafter. — Al-Qurṭubī says he takes present favors as proof of divine honor and a better return; al-Suddī reports his boast about an even better garden if raised. Iṣlāḥī says the parable exposes opponents who infer from worldly success that their beliefs are true and the afterlife is imaginary, or that they will fare better there than believers. Al-Jishumī links the inherited-wealth brothers, one charitable and one mocking, with 37:50. Al-Nīsābūrī's inward account calls expected better garden a delusion about God. [5 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; DURR-FULL(السدي رضي الله عنه); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; JISHUMI; ALUSI-FULL(النيسابوري)]
- 18:35/q14/p2 The arrogant owner contrasts with Dhū al-Qarnayn's gratitude and trust in God's promise. — Iṣlāḥī contrasts the owner's supposedly indestructible lush garden with Dhū al-Qarnayn crediting his achievement to God and remembering God's certain promise. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 18:35/q14/p3 An inward reading makes the gardens desire and world, their fruit cravings, and the eventual regret a wasted life. — Al-Nīsābūrī also reads boasting as attachment to wealth and status, better return as delusion about God, and turning hands over as remorse for unused good potential. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(النيسابوري)]

## 18:35/q15 How is the following verse's return pronoun read?  [* · readings]
turns on: Which codex reading is followed and whether the nearest referent is both gardens.
- 18:35/q15/p1 The dual minhumā refers to both gardens. — Al-Qurṭubī attributes the dual to Meccan, Medinan and Syrian codices and prefers it because both gardens are the nearest referent. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL+]
- 18:35/q15/p2 The singular minhā is also transmitted. — Al-Qurṭubī attributes it to Basran and Kufan codices but favors the dual. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]

## 18:35/q16 What broader examples show that figurative wording can redirect judgment?  [* · rhetoric]
- 18:35/q16/p1 Poetic word choice can invert honor and blame in a hearer's judgment. — Al-Jurjānī says poetry can elevate the low, lower the honored, and turn suspicion into apparent proof. His examples include unjust judgment of wisdom, an orator's silencing, Ibn Sukra's poetry as fire and subtle spells, al-Ḥuṭayʾa making Banū Anf al-Nāqa's insult into pride, Saʿd's name becoming an ominous insult, and a pun disparaging Kathīr despite al-Ṣāḥib's praise. He concludes one name can build or destroy reputations. Ibn Qutayba's separate poetry examples take izār as the woman or chastity and ʿAdī ibn Zayd's ṣulb as lineage, origin or family support. [10 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL(عدي بن زيد)]

## 18:35/q17 What broader rhetorical method and account of Quranic arrangement accompany these notes?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: These are separate methodological claims in the peripheral notes, not rival readings of the verse.
- 18:35/q17/p1 Begin with widely intelligible examples before treating subtle metaphor, simile and representation. — Al-Jurjānī says shared, obvious examples establish his principles persuasively before strange poetry; he then asks whether simile and representation are synonymous or one is narrower. [2 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR]
- 18:35/q17/p2 Quranic arrangement and inimitability lie in syntactic relationships among words. — Al-Jurjānī says words gain place and mutual dependence through syntactic meanings and rules; an alternative account of inimitability would need another miracle or ṣarfa, which denies intrinsic inimitability. [2 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL]

## 18:35/q18 What wider points about speech and its evidence accompany these notes?  [قَالَ · theology]
turns on: The peripheral debate concerns how divine speech relates to knowledge, expression and audible calling, and whether poetic evidence is admissible.
- 18:35/q18/p1 The tongue interprets the heart, likened to a king commanding troops. — Al-Ālūsī transmits this as a report attributed to Abū Hurayra. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أبو هريرة)]
- 18:35/q18/p2 A Christian poet's line can be cited; its absence from old collections does not disprove it. — Al-Ālūsī rejects objections based on the poet's hostility and Ibn al-Khashshāb's inability to find the line; he says the wording accords with the beloved's speech and even with what opponents of inner speech implicitly concede. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 18:35/q18/p3 The divine-speech dispute is subtle and consequential; an added eternal attribute of volition or demand lacks warrant. — Al-Ālūsī describes controversy, hardship and imprisonment and says his view distills predecessors. Ghānī Zāda distinguishes eternal divine knowledge from inner speech that brings meanings into created verbal expression; al-Ālūsī rejects the proposed eternal attribute because Quran, Sunna, Companions, Successors and reason do not establish it as speech. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(غنى زاده)]
- 18:35/q18/p4 Moses actually heard God's speech as letters and sound. — Al-Ālūsī attributes this to al-Māturīdī, al-Ashʿarī and other investigators and cites 19:52, 26:10, 28:30, 20:12 and 27:8. Arabic use of 'call' and hadith also support audible speech in his view. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 18:35/q19 What disciplines and evidence are said to guide interpretation?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether inspired insight counts as a necessary discipline or as a different, unprovable kind of interpretation; the personal-opinion report's soundness is disputed.
- 18:35/q19/p1 Exegesis draws on rhetoric, hadith, legal theory, theology and readings, with morphology, derivation and jurisprudence also proposed. — Al-Ālūsī assigns rhetoric expressive effects, hadith occasions and abrogation, legal theory scope and commands, theology doctrine and readings possible meanings. Al-Suyūṭī adds morphology, derivation and jurisprudence; al-Ālūsī says the first two follow from the listed disciplines. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(السيوطي)]
- 18:35/q19/p2 Divinely bestowed insight is a required interpretive discipline. — Al-Suyūṭī cites a report that acting on known knowledge brings unknown knowledge; al-Ālūsī objects that inspired insight reveals hidden secrets rather than basic Quranic meanings and often cannot be articulated or proved. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(السيوطي); ALUSI-FULL-]
- 18:35/q19/p3 Inspired insight concerns hidden interpretation rather than basic exegesis. — Al-Ālūsī says its recipients often cannot articulate or demonstrate it, though practice may bring it. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 18:35/q19/p4 A reported prohibition says interpretation by personal opinion errs even if correct. — Al-Ālūsī attributes the report to Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī and al-Nasāʾī and notes that its soundness is disputed. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أبو داود والترمذي والنسائي)]
