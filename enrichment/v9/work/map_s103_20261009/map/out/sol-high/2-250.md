# 2:250: 29 questions

## 2:250/q01 What does barazū mean at the encounter?  [بَرَزُوا۟ · meaning]
- 2:250/q01/p1 They emerged from cover onto open, level ground, visible to the opposing army. — Al-Tabari relates tabarraza, going out to relieve oneself, to open ground and taghawwata to low ground. Al-Razi and al-Samin explain that mubāraza names exposed face-to-face combat; al-Biqa'i stresses leaving concealment. Al-Jishumi and al-Tabrisi gloss the root as appearing; al-Tabataba'i applies it to emerging for battle. [12 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 2:250/q02 Who came out to face Jalut?  [بَرَزُوا۟ لِجَالُوتَ وَجُنُودِهِۦ · referent]
- 2:250/q02/p1 Talut and the believers with him faced Jalut and his forces. — Al-Baghawi calls Jalut's forces polytheists; al-Biqa'i and Ibn Kathir stress the believers' small, weak force against a numerous, strong foe. Al-Bursevi says the frightened became brave before the collective prayer. Muqatil calls them the companions of the chamber. [11 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 2:250/q03 How does the lām in li-Jālūt work?  [لِجَالُوتَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the prepositional phrase depends on the stated verb or an omitted circumstantial expression.
- 2:250/q03/p1 It attaches directly to barazū, expressing their advance toward Jalut. — Al-Samin reports this as one possible analysis. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]
- 2:250/q03/p2 It belongs to an implied circumstantial phrase: they advanced intending to meet Jalut. — Al-Samin reports an omitted sense of intended encounter. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 2:250/q04 Who was Jalut?  [جَالُوتَ · referent]
turns on: The sources identify his people differently: Amalekites, Canaanites, or Philistines.
- 2:250/q04/p1 He was the king and commander of the Amalekites. — Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi give this identification. Al-Kashshaf traces him to ʿAmliq son of ʿAd and says his helmet weighed three hundred ratls; al-Qurtubi reports a one-mile shadow and a claimed Berber descent from him. Ibn Atiyya calls Jālūt a non-Arabic name adapted to Arabic. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 2:250/q04/p2 He was a Canaanite king in Syria during the Israelites' time. — Al-Biqa'i gives this identification. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 2:250/q04/p3 He commanded the powerful Philistines and was exceptionally large and strong. — Kur'an Yolu gives this description as the source of Israelite fear and demoralization. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 2:250/q05 How large was Jalut's army?  [جُنُودِهِۦ · reports]
turns on: Two transmitted counts give different numbers of troops.
- 2:250/q05/p1 It numbered three hundred thousand horsemen. — Ibn Atiyya cites a report; al-Qurtubi reports the same number and contrasts ʿIkrima's smaller figure. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 2:250/q05/p2 It numbered ninety thousand. — Al-Qurtubi transmits this figure from ʿIkrima in contrast to the three hundred thousand report. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(عكرمة)]

## 2:250/q06 What is conveyed by calling God rabbanā?  [رَبَّنَآ · rhetoric]
- 2:250/q06/p1 It acknowledges their servitude and invokes God's nurturing lordship to reform and support them. — Al-Samin and al-Bursevi draw on the sense of rabb; al-Alusi emphasizes God's nurturing lordship. [3 notes: SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:250/q07 What does 'pour patience upon us' convey?  [أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًۭا · meaning]
- 2:250/q07/p1 It asks God to grant patience in a full, abundant outpouring, as though emptying a vessel over them. — Ibn Qutayba, al-Zajjaj, al-Wahidi, al-Jishumi, al-Tabrisi and al-Razi explain the vessel image and the root's sense of emptiness; al-Jishumi cites 28:10. Muqatil compares pouring molten metal at 18:96. Al-Razi says the image implies all contents poured and made to remain. Al-Samin says ʿalā makes patience encompass them; al-Alusi adds cooling after withheld water and the indefinite noun magnifies patience. Ibn Ashur cites Abu Kabir al-Hudhali's poetry; al-Tabataba'i says divine imparting fits each person's capacity. Al-Damghani illustrates the sense of pouring out with both this prayer for patience and the molten metal poured over the wall in 18:96. [26 notes: MUQATIL; MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; ZAJJAJ; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; NASAFI-FULL; DAMGHANI]

## 2:250/q08 What patience did the fighters seek?  [صَبْرًۭا · interpretation]
- 2:250/q08/p1 They sought self-restraint and endurance in battle, especially restraint from fleeing. — Al-Jishumi defines sabr as restraining the self from what attracts it and says the fighters sought endurance in jihad. Al-Tabrisi says restraint from flight is rewarded. Al-Bursevi names battle's hardships; al-Biqa'i wants patience sufficient for effective blows. Ibn Abi Hatim transmits ʿAli b. al-Husayn's broader report: the patient enter Paradise before reckoning for enduring obedience and avoiding sin. [8 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(علي بن الحسين)]

## 2:250/q09 What does making their feet firm mean?  [وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether firm feet chiefly pictures inner resilience or also refers to the ground underfoot.
- 2:250/q09/p1 It means steadfast hearts, courage and resolve so they hold their ground and do not flee. — Ibn Abi Hatim reports Ibn Ishaq's gloss. Al-Jishumi derives thabāt from remaining in place and refers to God's firm word supporting truth. Ibn Atiyya says firm feet evoke stability; Ibn Ashur and al-Tabataba'i explain the metaphor through slipping in fear versus planted feet. Al-Biqa'i adds forceful blows, and al-Bursevi and al-Alusi reject limiting it to physical immobility. Al-Damghani says feet stand for the believers' hearts and takes the request as asking God to make those hearts patient in battle. Ibn al-Jawzi's Nuzha reports that firm feet mean firm hearts, because the heart makes the feet stand firm. [22 notes: MUQATIL; IBNABIHATIM(محمد بن إسحاق); THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; DAMGHANI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA]
- 2:250/q09/p2 It includes secure physical footing on slippery battlefield ground. — Abu Hayyan explicitly includes firm footing on slippery terrain among the requests at face-to-face battle. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 2:250/q10 By what means might God steady them?  [وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا · interpretation]
- 2:250/q10/p1 God may strengthen the believers through courage and by weakening, frightening, dividing, or disabling their enemy. — Al-Wahidi allows strong hearts, enemy discord, fear and similar means. Al-Kashshaf names courage and terror; al-Nasafi names the two sides' hearts. Al-Qadi adds calamity, disease, a leader's death and guiding some enemies to see their error, causing disagreement. [6 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; RAZI-FULL(القاضي)]

## 2:250/q11 What aid do they ask for in 'help us'?  [وَٱنصُرْنَا · meaning]
- 2:250/q11/p1 They ask God for assistance and success against the unbelieving enemy, ending in its defeat and rout. — Al-Jishumi defines nasr as assistance and describes success in combat. Ibn Abi Hatim transmits Ibn Ishaq's gloss; al-Tabrisi describes aid against an enemy. Al-Alusi specifies the enemy's defeat and rout, and Ibn Atiyya traces the root of defeat from parts folding into one another to an army's retreat. Ibn al-Jawzi's Nuzha reports from the exegetes that naṣr can mean victory over an enemy and cites 3:147 as an example. [13 notes: IBNABIHATIM(محمد بن إسحاق); THALABI; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أهل التفسير)]

## 2:250/q12 How can divine victory be given?  [وَٱنصُرْنَا · interpretation]
turns on: The notes give distinct ways divine aid can operate, one through battlefield conditions and another through angels.
- 2:250/q12/p1 God can strengthen believers and undermine the enemy through fear, weakness, apparent numbers, or division. — Al-Jishumi specifies stronger believing hearts, weaker unbelieving hearts, terror and discord. Al-Tabrisi adds exposing weakness and imagined numbers, calling victory a divine لطف while distinguishing it from other لطف. [2 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI]
- 2:250/q12/p2 God can support believers through angels. — Al-Jishumi gives Badr as the parallel and cites 3:123. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 2:250/q13 Who are 'the unbelieving people'?  [ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلْكَٰفِرِينَ · referent]
- 2:250/q13/p1 They are Jalut's people and troops. — Al-Jishumi identifies the group directly; the battle context supplies the same referent in al-Baghawi and al-Alusi. [4 notes: JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:250/q14 Why call the enemy 'the unbelieving people'?  [ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلْكَٰفِرِينَ · rhetoric]
- 2:250/q14/p1 It presents the battle as for God's cause and gives the reason the believers seek victory over them. — Al-Qushayri says they sought victory against God's enemies rather than revenge for personal loss; acting for and by God brought victory. Al-Biqa'i contrasts God's neglected rights with personal advantage and notes the enemy's power. Al-Alusi says the noun phrase instead of a pronoun states why victory is deserved. [3 notes: QUSHAYRI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:250/q15 Why are patience, firm feet, and victory requested in this order?  [أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًۭا وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا وَٱنصُرْنَا · rhetoric]
- 2:250/q15/p1 Patience is the foundation, steadfast resistance follows, and victory is the final aim. — Al-Baydawi says victory usually follows firm footing. Al-Bursevi says firmness arises from patience. Al-Alusi adds that patience alone may lack resistance and courage without victory is fruitless; his reported alternative notes that wāw rather than fāʾ lets the mind infer the order, citing al-Sakkākī. Al-Razi relates the three requests to courage, enabling equipment and circumstances, and strength to overcome. [5 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); RAZI-FULL]

## 2:250/q16 Does the prayer make God the creator of patience and steadfastness?  [أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًۭا وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا · theology]
turns on: Whether the requests name God as creator of the inner dispositions themselves or as creator of external causes that foster them.
- 2:250/q16/p1 Yes: it directly asks God for human resolve and the resulting steadfastness. — Al-Razi reports the Ashʿarite argument and adopts the apparent attribution: patience is resolve and firmness is stillness; departure from that apparent sense needs evidence. He argues that even God-given causes either change motives or do not, and either way the Muʿtazilite account cannot escape divine determination. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL(الأصحاب); RAZI-FULL+]
- 2:250/q16/p2 The prayer asks God to create the external causes of patience and firmness. — Al-Qadi lists enemy fear, division, calamities, disease, a leader's death and some enemies' recognition of error as causes that embolden believers. Al-Razi says this figurative reading abandons the verse's apparent attribution without evidence; his motives argument is directed against it. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL(القاضي); RAZI-FULL-]

## 2:250/q17 What circumstances prompted the fighters' prayer?  [وَلَمَّا بَرَزُوا۟ لِجَالُوتَ وَجُنُودِهِۦ قَالُوا۟ · interpretation]
- 2:250/q17/p1 The smaller force faced a frightening, better equipped army and turned together to God for strength. — Ibn Abi Hatim transmits al-Rabiʿ on Jalut's numbers. Al-Razi and al-Qurtubi say the disparity prompted prayer, comparing the followers' prayer at 3:146–147. Al-Bursevi says the brave strengthened fearful hearts first; al-Alusi says the whole army prayed humbly, disavowing its own power. Kur'an Yolu describes Israelite fear, encouragement from a courageous faithful few, material and spiritual prerequisites, and the prayer itself as an exhortation to resist. [10 notes: IBNABIHATIM(الربيع); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; BIQAI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL]

## 2:250/q18 What practice does this prayer teach believers?  [* · interpretation]
- 2:250/q18/p1 Believers should ask God for perseverance and aid, relying on Him in all affairs. — Al-Jishumi says seeking God's help and the ability to persevere is praiseworthy and that recounting earlier believers teaches later believers to rely on God. Al-Razi says the Prophet regularly prayed for help in battle, including at Badr for God's promise and for protection from enemy harm; al-Qurtubi recounts his battle prayer and urgent Badr supplication until his cloak fell. [5 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]

## 2:250/q19 How does this prayer connect with the surrounding account and other verses?  [* · links]
- 2:250/q19/p1 It echoes earlier believers' battle prayer and anticipates God's help to the steadfast. — Al-Razi and al-Qurtubi cite 3:146–147. Ibn al-Jawzi reports al-Hasan's gloss of 'by God's permission' as God's help and says God's being with the steadfast means victory and assistance; al-Jishumi compares angelic help at Badr in 3:123. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الحسن); JISHUMI]

## 2:250/q20 What response to the prayer is understood before the next verse's victory?  [* · rhetoric]
- 2:250/q20/p1 An unstated divine answer granted patience and victory, after which they routed the enemy by God's leave and David killed Jalut. — Al-Thaʿlabi supplies the implied response linking the supplication to the following narrative. [1 notes: THALABI]

## 2:250/q21 Why is the alif omitted in the written form of 'barazū' here?  [بَرَزُوا۟ · rhetoric]
- 2:250/q21/p1 Its omission portrays the encounter as spiritual and elevated, while the spelling at David's physical killing of Jalut retains alif. — Al-Burhan compares 2:251 and adduces parallel spellings: spiritual versus tangible gates in 39:73, 38:50 and 15:44; locusts and frogs at 7:133; varied amthāl in 43:17, 52:24, 17:48 and 25:9; trumpet blast and earth's crushing at 69:13 and 89:21; 'my book' and 'my account' at 69:19–20, and 69:25,28. For subḥān at 17:93 he prefers omission at a disputed place because the Prophet's elevated presence is not distracted by worldly movement. [8 notes: BURHAN]

## 2:250/q22 How was David identified and prepared as Jalut's challenger?  [* · reports]
turns on: The accounts differ in David's place among Jesse's sons and in the signs, volunteer episode, and arms that led to his duel.
- 2:250/q22/p1 Samuel received signs identifying Jesse's youngest shepherd son, David, and David went to fight with trust in God. — Al-Thaʿlabi says Jalut challenged Talut, Samuel prayed, a horn of oil and iron furnace marked the warrior, twelve tall sons failed, and God chose by hearts rather than bodies. Jesse initially hid slight David among sheep; Samuel saw his mercy carrying sheep across floodwater and anointed him as oil overflowed. David cited rescues from beasts, then declined Talut's horse, armor and weapons as useless without God. Ibn Abi Hatim's al-Suddi adds his sling feats, submission of beasts and mountains, and armor fitting him. [8 notes: THALABI; IBNABIHATIM(السدى)]
- 2:250/q22/p2 David was Jesse's seventh and youngest son in Talut's army; revelation told Samuel he would kill Jalut. — Al-Kashshaf calls David a shepherd and says three stones later called to him. Its seventh-son count differs from al-Thaʿlabi's twelve older sons. [1 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL]
- 2:250/q22/p3 David came from his flock and volunteered despite his brothers' scorn, after which Talut armed him. — Al-Tabari transmits Ibn Jurayj, who places this after the river crossing and Talut's offer of half his kingdom. [1 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن جريج)]

## 2:250/q23 What do the accounts say of the stones and David's defeat of Jalut?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports differ in whose stones they were, what they did, and whether the blow or a later beheading killed Jalut.
- 2:250/q23/p1 Three speaking stones associated with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob joined, and their shot pierced Jalut's head and routed his army. — Al-Tabari's narrative names the patriarchs; his report from Ibn Jurayj through Mujahid says the stones recalled earlier victories, promised help, foretold Jalut's death and joined. Details of the stones' speech vary within this account. [2 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن جريج عن مجاهد)]
- 2:250/q23/p2 Three stones associated with Aaron, Moses, and one God reserved for David joined and killed Jalut. — Al-Thaʿlabi says the stones entered David's bag and spoke; David invoked the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, the fused stone flew on a God-driven wind through a helmet of three hundred iron pieces and the brain, killing thirty men behind Jalut. Jalut had mocked the sling and threatened David. David dragged his body to Talut. [4 notes: THALABI]
- 2:250/q23/p3 David used a sling and stones, hit Jalut between the eyes, routed the army and then beheaded him. — Ibn Abi Hatim transmits Wahb's account: David refused Talut's armor and fought after a wager over daughter and wealth. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(وهب بن منبه)]
- 2:250/q23/p4 The young shepherd David killed Jalut with a sling stone; other accounts say three stones called to him or merged. — The Study Quran points to the familiar account in 1 Samuel 17; al-Kashshaf mentions three calling stones, and al-Suddi's report in Ibn Abi Hatim has merging stones along with earlier signs of David. [3 notes: STUDYQURAN; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(السدى)]

## 2:250/q24 What reward was promised for defeating Jalut?  [* · reports]
turns on: Different speakers make offers in the reports, and Talut's promised share is described differently.
- 2:250/q24/p1 Talut promised his daughter and half his kingdom to the victor. — Al-Thaʿlabi reports this before David's duel and again when David volunteered; Ibn Jurayj in al-Tabari reports half the kingdom after the river crossing, without naming the daughter in that report. [3 notes: THALABI; TAB-FULL(ابن جريج)]
- 2:250/q24/p2 The wager promised Talut's daughter and rule over his wealth. — Ibn Abi Hatim transmits Wahb's version of the duel and its reward. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(وهب بن منبه)]
- 2:250/q24/p3 Jalut offered his own kingdom if Talut's champion killed him. — Al-Thaʿlabi reports this as Jalut's challenge to Talut; it is a separate offer from Talut's reward. [1 notes: THALABI]

## 2:250/q25 What followed the army's victory and David's marriage?  [* · reports]
- 2:250/q25/p1 The army returned safe and wealthy; David became acclaimed and married Talut's daughter. — Al-Thaʿlabi reports public praise, then Talut's demand for two hundred enemy foreskins as a dowry, which David brought strung together; Talut gave him his daughter and royal signet. Al-Kashshaf also says Talut married his daughter to David after Jalut's death. [3 notes: THALABI; KASHSHAF-FULL]

## 2:250/q26 How did Talut treat David after David became popular?  [* · reports]
- 2:250/q26/p1 Talut grew jealous, tried to kill David, pursued him, and David repeatedly escaped while sparing Talut. — Al-Thaʿlabi says Talut's daughter warned David; a wineskin in his bed took the blow. Talut then sealed his doors. God blinded guards on two visits: David left arrows once, then took a water jug and cup, beard hairs and garment piece. Spies pursued him; a spider web concealed his cave, after which he worshiped in mountains. Al-Kashshaf also reports envy and an attempt on David's life, followed by repentance. [8 notes: THALABI; KASHSHAF-FULL]

## 2:250/q27 What do reports say of Talut's repentance and death?  [* · reports]
- 2:250/q27/p1 After persecuting scholars, Talut sought repentance and died fighting after his ten sons died before him. — Al-Thaʿlabi relates killings of critical scholars and worshipers, the sparing of a woman versed in God's greatest name, graves that rebuked Talut, and the executioner's rooster parable. The saved woman prayed at Samuel's grave; he rose and prescribed renouncing the throne and death in God's cause for Talut and his sons. Talut wept, asked his sons to ransom him from the Fire, and they all died in battle. Al-Kashshaf briefly reports repentance. [15 notes: THALABI; KASHSHAF-FULL]

## 2:250/q28 What happened to David's rule after Talut died?  [* · reports]
- 2:250/q28/p1 David became Israel's king and inherited the earlier royal authority. — Al-Thaʿlabi says David beheaded the man who reported killing Talut, received Talut's treasuries and was made king by Israel; he says Israel united under no other one king and supplies David's genealogy through Jacob, Isaac and Abraham. Talut ruled forty years; al-Dahhak and al-Kalbi give David sixty-nine years after Jalut. The Study Quran transmits al-Tabari that David's wisdom meant Samuel's prophethood and sovereignty meant Saul's kingship, and points to 38:17–29 for David's later gifts. [6 notes: THALABI; STUDYQURAN(Ṭ)]

## 2:250/q29 What inward response does the commentator attach to these spiritually realized people?  [* · inward]
- 2:250/q29/p1 The spiritually realized are hidden and nameless, united in outward law and inward truth; the commentator prays to join them. — Al-Bursevi cites preceding Persian verses for their hiddenness and unity, then asks God for peace, mercy and inclusion among them. [2 notes: BURSEVI]
