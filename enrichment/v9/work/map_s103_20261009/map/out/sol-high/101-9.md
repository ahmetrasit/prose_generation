# 101:9: 14 questions

## 101:9/q01 What does ‘his mother is Hawiya’ mean?  [فَأُمُّهُۥ هَاوِيَةٌۭ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether umm denotes an abode by maternal analogy, the top of the head, the actual grieving mother in an idiom, or the sinner's origin; the following feminine pronoun and Arabic idiom/poetry are used to decide.
- 101:9/q01/p1 His final abode and only refuge is Hell, called his mother as a child returns to and shelters with its mother. — Muqatil calls it his origin and abode: neither earth bears him nor sky shades him; he compares umm al-qura (6:92). Al-Farra, Ibn Qutayba, Ibn Zayd, Ibn Abbas, al-Akhfash, Abu Ishaq and others explain a mother's shelter, nurture, feeding, dwelling or place of return. Ibn Qutayba compares figurative father language for God's mercy and Jesus' water and bread as parents, rejects a biological reading, and cites the Arab name mother for earth as origin, return and sustenance; Umayya's poem calls earth mother and refuge. Ibn Atiyya cites Utba's battle saying about earth as mother. Tabari cites 3:151 for the Fire as abode. Al-Akhfash cites a poem about a fallen mother; al-Nahhas also cites mother of the Qur'an and a poem for origin/core. Ibn Kathir reports the two principal interpretations without choosing. Alusi and Bursevi add that the analogy can taunt and that Fire encloses the sinner like a womb; Kuranyolu depicts Hell opening its arms. Asad says umm is what embraces or enfolds, Study Quran derives it from amma, directing one's course, and Elmalili calls the refuge metaphor painfully ironic. Biqai notes earth and dwelling are called mother because they embrace and shelter. Qutb says partial obscurity heightens the later disclosure. Al-Tabatabai calls it a rational metaphor. Al-Nasafi and al-Wahidi stress a mother's place of rest. The Fire is the only remaining shelter, not a literal parent. Damghani explicitly lists destination among five senses of umm, alongside origin, biological mother, wet nurse and the Prophet's wives. Itqan classifies Hell's motherhood as figurative, modeled on a human mother's literal sheltering of her child, and compares fire's figurative 'calling'. [83 notes: MUQATIL; FARRA; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL(أمية بن أبي الصلت); TAB(ابن زيد,ابن عباس); TAB-FULL(ابن زيد,ابن عباس); NAHHAS(الأخفش,قيل); MAWARDI(ابن زيد); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن زيد); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,الفراء,عطاء والكلبي وأكثر المفسرين); WAHIDI-QT; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL(قيل); IBNATIYYA(عتبة بن أبي سفيان,كثير من المفسرين); IBNATIYYA-FULL(كثير من المفسرين); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI(أمية بن أبي الصلت,ابن زيد,الأخفش,قتادة); QURTUBI-FULL(أمية بن أبي الصلت,ابن زيد,الأخفش,قتادة); BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR(ابن جرير,ابن زيد,قتادة); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن جرير,ابن زيد); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; DURR(ابن زيد,قتادة); DURR-FULL(ابن زيد,قتادة); BURSEVI; ALUSI(ابن زيد وغيره); ALUSI-FULL(ابن زيد وغيره); ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES(Asad); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN; DAMGHANI; DAMGHANI+; ELMALILI+; ITQAN+]
- 101:9/q01/p2 ‘His mother’ is his head or crown: he is cast headfirst into Hell. — Ibn Khalid/Abu Khalid al-Walibi glosses umm as the crown or top of the head. Ikrima, Abu Salih, Qatada, Ibn Abbas, al-Kalbi and others describe the headfirst plunge, sometimes to the bottom of Hell. Al-Mawardi cites a poem addressed to Amr; al-Nahhas reports the related fallen-mother poetic line. Ibn Kathir allows head or brain; Bursevi specifies umm al-ra's as brain or its thin covering. Ibn Ashur supplies the ellipsis umm ra'sihi and treats hāwiya as fallen, likening a fall on the crown to death. Elmalili gives either brain's root as Hawiya or a headfirst fall. Al-Qummi calls Hawiya the head itself. Al-Tabatabai objects that this leaves the next verse's feminine pronoun without an apparent antecedent. [33 notes: IBNABIHATIM(ابن خالد الوالبي,عكرمة); MAWARDI(عكرمة); MAWARDI-FULL(عكرمة); BAGHAWI(قتادة وأبو صالح); BAGHAWI-FULL(قتادة وأبو صالح); KASHSHAF(قتادة); KASHSHAF-FULL(قتادة); IBNATIYYA(أبو صالح وغيره); IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبو صالح وغيره); TABRISI(أبو صالح); RAZI(الأخفش والكلبي وقتادة); RAZI-FULL(الأخفش، الكلبي، قتادة); QURTUBI(عكرمة); QURTUBI-FULL(عكرمة); ABUHAYYAN(قتادة وأبو صالح وغيره); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قتادة وأبو صالح); IBNKATHIR(ابن عباس، وعكرمة، وأبو صالح، وقتادة); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس وعكرمة وأبو صالح وقتادة); DURR(أبو خالد الوالبي,أبو صالح,عكرمة); DURR-FULL(أبو خالد الوالبي,أبو صالح,عكرمة); BURSEVI(قتادة); ALUSI(قتادة وأبو صالح وعكرمة والكلبي); ELMALILI(Katâde, Ebû Sâlih, İkrime ve Kelbî); IBNASHUR; TABATABAI(القمي,قيل); TABATABAI-]
- 101:9/q01/p3 The phrase invokes the Arabic idiom ‘his mother fell’: he is ruined, with his mother imagined collapsing in grief at his death. — Qatada and Ibn Abbas cite hawat/hawiyat ummuhu for grave calamity; al-Zamakhshari, al-Razi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, Alusi and others explain the imagined bereavement. The idiom can be a curse, an omen, wonder or even praise of brave dead warriors: Ka'b ibn Sa'd al-Ghanawi and the Hamasa poet supply examples, as do an unnamed poet and a line about morning and night. Ibn Ashur compares thakalathu ummuhu, explains the ellipsis and detached min in Ka'b's elegy, and sees reuse of a fixed expression for an analogous fate; he also illustrates how Arabs depict fortune through a mother's joy with a Bedouin's remark about Abraham (4:125). A report about la umma laka is linked to this verse. Al-Kashf and al-Tibi praise this reading for its relation to the earlier satisfied life (101:7); Elmalili notes its subtle force but favors the refuge reading. Corpus Coranicum reports Fischer's preference and lexical evidence, but objects that the following teaching question normally points to the named Hawiya; Fischer's proposed later addition of verses 10–11 is called unsupported. It allows an echo of the idiom alongside a real abyss. [44 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB(قتادة); TAB-FULL(قتادة); BAGHAWI(قتادة); BAGHAWI-FULL(قتادة); KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA(آخرون); IBNATIYYA-FULL(آخرون,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); TABRISI(أبو صالح,قتادة); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI(كعب بن سعد الغنوي); ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; DURR(ابن عباس,قتادة); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,قتادة); BURSEVI(الكشاف); ALUSI(الكشف,قتادة,كعب بن سعد الغنوي); ELMALILI(Katâde); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; CORPUSCORANICUM(Fischer); CORPUSCORANICUM(Fischer)+; ELMALILI(Sâhib-Keşşâf ve Râgıb)+]
- 101:9/q01/p4 ‘Mother’ marks the Fire as the sinner's origin or native element, because he was created from or dominated by a fiery nature. — Biqai says the image suggests creation from Hell and a satanic, fiery nature. Bursevi says the fire-created unbeliever returns to his origin and calls this explanation apparent; he also lists refuge, taunt and womb-like enclosure. [2 notes: BIQAI; BURSEVI+]

## 101:9/q02 How is ‘fa-ummuhu’ read?  [فَأُمُّهُۥ · readings]
turns on: Attested vocalization and dialect/grammatical conditions; the directional and emended readings lack attestation.
- 101:9/q02/p1 The majority read ummuhu with a damma on the hamza. — Abu Hayyan explicitly contrasts the majority's damma with Talha's kasra; the full recension repeats the contrast. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN(الجمهور وطلحة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور)]
- 101:9/q02/p2 Talha reads fa-immuhu with kasra on the hamza and a doubled, damma-marked mim. — Ibn Atiyya records the vowels and doubling. Ibn Khalawayh transmits Ibn Durayd's claim that the kasra is a dialect form. Grammarians restrict such kasra to a preceding kasra or ya; al-Samin refers to the fuller discussion at 4:11. [8 notes: IBNATIYYA(طلحة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(طلحة); ABUHAYYAN(ابن خالويه وابن دريد,الجمهور وطلحة,النحويون); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR(النحويون,طلحة)]
- 101:9/q02/p3 Muqatil's directional construal would require fa-ummuhu with fatha, but no such reading is reported. — Al-Wahidi reports Muqatil as saying people head toward Hawiya, then rejects this as an established interpretation because the necessary fatha is unattested. [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); WAHIDI-BASIT-]
- 101:9/q02/p4 Bellamy conjectures the consonants fa-ummatun hawiya, ‘a steep downward path.’ — Corpus Coranicum says no such variant is attested and judges the proposed emendation an easier secondary reading. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Bellamy)]

## 101:9/q03 What is Hawiya, and what explains its name?  [هَاوِيَةٌۭ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the term denotes a general deep chasm/Hell, a particular lowest gate, or has a Hebrew derivation; lexicography, falling imagery and audience familiarity are cited.
- 101:9/q03/p1 Hawiya is Hell or a deadly deep abyss, named from falling into its depths. — Al-Layth and lexicographers give both Hell and a bottomless pit; the Kamus derives it from falling. Mawardi, Qurtubi, Jishumi and Tabatabai connect the name with the inhabitants' plunge, Jishumi saying from top to bottom and citing related hawā usage for movement, straying and destruction (28:57, 6:71, 53:53). Baghawi describes a chasm whose bottom cannot be reached. Qurtubi and Ibn Ashur describe mahwa/mahwi as low spaces between mountains, where falls kill; Qurtubi cites a verse. Kashshaf, Bursevi and Alusi cite a report of a seventy-year fall; the fuller Kashshaf ascribes it to the Prophet. Corpus Coranicum says independent dictionary equations with mahwa/mahwat support the topographical meaning, answers Sells's objection about early attestations with lexicography and rhyme, and compares Revelation 9:1,11; it regards Hell as proper name as possibly secondary. The next verse identifies it as a blazing fire. [48 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB(قتادة); TAB-FULL(قتادة); MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL(ابن زيد); QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الليث); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); TABRISI; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI(Kāmûs,Âlûsî); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES(Asad); CORPUSCORANICUM(Sells); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; CORPUSCORANICUM+]
- 101:9/q03/p2 Hawiya specifically names Hell's lowest gate. — Qurtubi reports the identification; Kashifi, as quoted by Bursevi, calls it the lowest of all Hell's gates. Alusi and Elmalili also report that some restrict the name to the lowest gate. [5 notes: QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI(الكاشفي); ALUSI; ELMALILI(Âlûsî)]
- 101:9/q03/p3 Hawiya comes from Hebrew howa, ‘destruction.’ — Torrey proposes the Hebrew derivation. Corpus Coranicum doubts that early Meccan listeners knew the rare Hebrew word, found only twice in the Hebrew Bible. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Torrey)]

## 101:9/q04 What does the following ‘what is it?’ refer to, and how does this verse prepare its answer?  [هَاوِيَةٌۭ · links]
turns on: Whether the feminine pronoun has the named Hawiya as its concrete antecedent or must recover calamity, Fire, or the preceding sentence from an idiomatic/head reading.
- 101:9/q04/p1 The pronoun refers to Hawiya; its initially obscure threat is explained as blazing Fire in verses 10–11. — Baydawi, Alusi and Elmalili point the pronoun directly to Hawiya. Alusi says delaying the explanation magnifies strangeness and terror; Qutb calls the image elegant and partly obscure. Biqai says the indefinite wording magnifies the place and denies rest or stable ground. Corpus Coranicum says the delayed clear threat closes the central eschatological sequence and compares teaching questions about obscure places at 83:7–8 and 83:18–19; it rejects Fischer's later-addition theory and Paret's nonspecific antecedent. Al-Tabatabai uses the clear antecedent to object to the head reading. Razi says the Event (101:1–3) is perceptible but the Abyss is not, accounting for different introductory questions. [13 notes: RAZI; BAYDAWI; BIQAI; ALUSI; QUTB-ZILAL; TABATABAI; CORPUSCORANICUM(Fischer); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; CORPUSCORANICUM+; ELMALILI+; CORPUSCORANICUM(Paret)-]
- 101:9/q04/p2 On the idiom reading, ‘it’ refers to the implied calamity; on the head reading, it evokes the understood Fire. — Kashshaf allows the calamity implied by the first interpretation or Hawiya. Alusi distinguishes the calamity in the imprecation reading, Hawiya in his reading, and the understood Fire in the head reading. Paret proposes reference to the whole preceding assertion, but Corpus Coranicum objects that the teaching-question pronoun usually has a concrete feminine referent. [3 notes: KASHSHAF; ALUSI; CORPUSCORANICUM(Paret)]

## 101:9/q05 What is the final ha in the following question?  [هَاوِيَةٌۭ · readings]
- 101:9/q05/p1 The final ha is a pausal letter, omitted by some in connected recitation and retained by others in agreement with the written codex. — Kashshaf gives the alternatives while discussing the following question's pronoun. [1 notes: KASHSHAF]

## 101:9/q06 What does the verse imply about the light-scale person's fate and deeds?  [* · interpretation]
- 101:9/q06/p1 The person with light scales has Hell as the destination, because good deeds are too few or outweighed by evil. — Tabari says good deeds weigh little and the person falls into Hell; Qushayri identifies the disbelievers whose obedience is light. Biqai says evil prevails or good is absent because they followed falsehood. Islahi says any apparent good is nullified by ill intent and heretical belief, leaving evil without weight. Kuranyolu explains few or no good deeds versus many bad deeds, the just judgment and need for responsible belief/conduct, while warning that revelation does not specify how the unseen weighing physically works. Bursevi says both groups sin, but one has fewer sins outweighed by good; the Prophet's advice to Ali to follow a bad deed with a good one illustrates divine forgiveness. He contrasts God's scale, where enduring good rises and weighs heavily while transient sensual pleasures fall and weigh lightly. Study Quran connects 7:9 and 23:103 and compares other verses about self-wronging and Hell as refuge (3:117, 7:177, 9:70, 10:44, 16:33, 16:118, 3:162, 3:197, 9:73, 13:18). [16 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB; TAB-FULL; QUSHAYRI; BIQAI-FULL; DURR(قتادة); BURSEVI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]

## 101:9/q07 What inward reality does the abyss represent?  [هَاوِيَةٌۭ · inward]
turns on: The inward accounts specify different contents of the light-scale person's abyss: corporeal nature or veiling and ignorance.
- 101:9/q07/p1 The abyss is the hell of bodily or corporeal nature for a person ruled by evil deeds and psychic vices. — Qashani places the light-scale person's home in bodily nature's hell. Bursevi says the truly weighed things are hidden primordial capacities and intellectual receptivities in right- and left-hand pans. [2 notes: QASHANI; BURSEVI]
- 101:9/q07/p2 The abyss is veiling and a hot fire of ignorance, blindness, ego, passion, Satan and the world. — Bursevi reports the Ta'wilat Najmiyya's inward reading as the origin of those with light scales. [1 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]

## 101:9/q08 What do reports about souls say about being taken to Hawiya?  [فَأُمُّهُۥ هَاوِيَةٌۭ · reports]
- 101:9/q08/p1 Believing souls welcome a new believer and ask about others; a person who died earlier but has not joined them was taken to mother Hawiya. — Al-Hasan transmits a Prophetic version calling Hawiya a wretched mother and nurse; al-Ash'ath transmits the exchange. Durr gives several Prophetic versions, including asking about living people, allowing the newcomer rest from worldly distress, and welcoming with merciful souls like news-bearers; Ibn Kathir notes Ibn Mardawayh's longer marfu version from Anas. Abu Ayyub's version includes inspecting deeds, rejoicing at good and praying over bad; Ibn al-Mubarak says Salam al-Tawil raised it from Thawr to the Prophet. Sa'id ibn Jubayr says dead relatives rejoice or grieve at news and ask whether a deceased has arrived. Al-Tabatabai notes parallel reports from Anas, al-Hasan and al-Ash'ath. [15 notes: MUJAHID(الحسن); ABDURRAZZAQ(أشعث بن عبد الله الأعمى); TAB(الأشعث بن عبد الله الأعمى); TAB-FULL(الأشعث بن عبد الله الأعمى); IBNKATHIR-FULL(الأشعث بن عبد الله الأعمى); DURR(أبو أيوب الأنصاري,الأشعث بن عبد الله الأعمى,رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم,سعيد بن جبير); DURR-FULL(الأشعث بن عبد الله الأعمى,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); TABATABAI(أبو أيوب الأنصاري,أنس بن مالك والحسن والأشعث بن عبد الله الأعمى)]

## 101:9/q09 What happens in Wahb's village story associated with Hawiya?  [هَاوِيَةٌۭ · reports]
- 101:9/q09/p1 A punished village's souls are buried in Hawiya, identified as Sijjin, a vast fiery mass. — Wahb says Jesus questions a village where people, jinn, animals and birds died; their sins were serving false powers and loving worldly life. One respondent says the others are muzzled with fiery bridles, while he was caught in their punishment despite differing from them and fears his own fate. Jesus tells his companions that barley bread, plain water and sleeping on dung among dogs are better if accompanied by well-being in both worlds. [4 notes: DURR(عيسى عليه السلام,وهب بن منبه)]

## 101:9/q10 Why use this word and this compact wording?  [فَأُمُّهُۥ هَاوِيَةٌۭ · rhetoric]
turns on: The notes offer rhyme and antithetical ellipsis as distinct explanations of the wording.
- 101:9/q10/p1 The rare word Hawiya helps preserve the sura's rhyme. — Itqan says the Qur'an often chooses rarer synonyms, listing dayza, al-Hutamah, Saqar, Laza and Hawiya, in part for each sura's rhyme. Corpus Coranicum also invokes rhyme against doubts about the topographical sense. [2 notes: ITQAN; CORPUSCORANICUM(Sells)]
- 101:9/q10/p2 The compressed clause uses ellipsis and a contrasting pattern with the earlier life of contentment. — Biqai says a hostile life is omitted in the second clause and a mother in the first (101:7), producing antithetical ellipsis. Al-Kashf favors the idiom's emphatic contrast with the pleasurable life, while al-Tibi calls it clearest; Alusi notes room for discussion. [3 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI(الكشف)]
- 101:9/q10/p3 The mother image is a metaphor: Hell becomes the sinner's refuge and return as a mother shelters her child. — Itqan treats this as a metaphor with a literal human mother and a figurative mother in Hell, comparing fire's figurative 'calling'. [1 notes: ITQAN]

## 101:9/q11 How does this ending connect to the sura and its neighbors?  [* · links]
- 101:9/q11/p1 The sura moves from the Hour to two opposed outcomes and ends with the wretched; the next sura opens with the cause of their misery. — Biqai relates 101:1, 101:8–9 to the opening of sura 102. Alusi says the passage first describes what all share, then each group's particular state. Study Quran says the final six verses contrast heavy and light scales. Tabatabai describes the Meccan sura as a resurrection warning with glad tidings, dominated by warning. Corpus Coranicum says the Hell revelation closes its central eschatological sequence and compares riddling tension in Q 100, 90:12–16, 97:2–4 and 104:5–9. [6 notes: BIQAI; ALUSI; TABATABAI; CORPUSCORANICUM; STUDYQURAN]

## 101:9/q12 When was the sura containing this verse revealed?  [* · sciences]
- 101:9/q12/p1 It is an early Meccan sura, probably after al-Tin. — Asad's introduction places it after sura 95; Tabatabai also calls it Meccan. [2 notes: ASAD-NOTES; TABATABAI]

## 101:9/q13 How do the condemned fall into Hell?  [فَأُمُّهُۥ هَاوِيَةٌۭ · interpretation]
- 101:9/q13/p1 They fall into the Fire on their heads. — Abu Salih and Qatada explicitly describe the headfirst fall in Tabari's two recensions. [4 notes: TAB(أبو صالح,قتادة); TAB-FULL(أبو صالح,قتادة)]

## 101:9/q14 How is the preceding contented life described?  [* · links]
- 101:9/q14/p1 The life is called content because its possessor is content with it, or because it causes contentment. — Ibn Ashur calls radiya a transferred description of life, furnishing the preceding contrast to the miserable fate of this verse. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
