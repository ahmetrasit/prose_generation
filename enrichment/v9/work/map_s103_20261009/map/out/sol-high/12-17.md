# 12:17: 25 questions

## 12:17/q01 What happened when the brothers brought this story to Jacob?  [قَالُوا۟ يَٰٓأَبَانَآ · reports]
turns on: The reports give different amounts of dialogue and different accounts of Jacob's physical reaction.
- 12:17/q01/p1 They returned at night, feigning grief, and told Jacob they had raced, left Joseph with their things, and lost him to a wolf. — Muqatil places their return at the evening prayer and calls the outing hunting; Ibn Kathir says they had already put Joseph in the well. Qurtubi says darkness made their excuse easier and cites a saying about favors at night and excuses for daytime wrongs. Bursevi says Jacob asked after them, their sheep, and Joseph, and they said the matter was worse. [5 notes: MUQATIL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI; BIQAI-FULL]
- 12:17/q01/p2 Jacob asked what had happened and where Joseph was, heard the wolf story, cried out, and asked for Joseph's shirt. — Thalabi's al-Suddi says Jacob first feared harm to the brothers or sheep; Study Quran's Bg says they told him the sheep were safe before his question. Qurtubi gives the dialogue and his request for the shirt. [3 notes: THALABI(السدي); QURTUBI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(Bg)]
- 12:17/q01/p3 Jacob fainted at the news and did not revive until dawn. — Qurtubi's al-Suddi and Ibn Hibban report Judah found no sign of life, lamented losing brother and father, and Reuben supported Jacob on waking; Wahb reports Judah checking breath and pulse and rebuking the brothers. Study Quran's Q adds that they poured water on Jacob. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(السدي,وهب); STUDYQURAN(Q)]

## 12:17/q02 What did the brothers mean by نَسْتَبِقُ?  [نَسْتَبِقُ · interpretation]
turns on: The text names no equipment or venue; the attributed shooting reading, reports, word form, and practical purpose supply the proposed details.
- 12:17/q02/p1 They raced one another on foot to see who was fastest. — Al-Suddi, Muqatil, al-Jubba'i and others are cited; Wahidi and Razi answer the objection that adult running is childish by its use in war and protecting flocks, as do Qushayri and Alusi. Tabatabai locates it in open country; Study Quran's R says wolves had taken sheep. Islahi cites the race to the door at 12:25. [27 notes: TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL(الزجاج); WAHIDI-BASIT(السدي؛ مقاتل); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الزجاج); TABRISI(الجبائي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(السدي); RAZI-FULL(السدي ومقاتل); QURTUBI-FULL(أبو نصر القشيري,السدي); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(السدي); TABATABAI(الزمخشري); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN(R)]
- 12:17/q02/p2 They competed in archery or shooting. — Ibn Qutayba notes sabq can mean a prize; Mawradi derives the form from sabāq. Thalabi, Wahidi, Tabarsi, Qurtubi, and Alusi cite a reading ascribed to ʿAbd Allah, with forms recorded as nantaḍil, nantaṣil, or nintadil, meaning shooting. Wahidi and Razi cite the Prophetic prize rule and comparison of arrow distance. Alusi favors archery on the reading's evidence; Qurtubi reports Azhari's distinction between arrow contests and horse races. [22 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الزجاج); TABRISI(الجبائي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL(أبو نصر القشيري,الزجاج); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; TABATABAI(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج)+; WAHIDI-BASIT(أكثر المفسرين؛ الزجاج؛ ابن قتيبة)+]
- 12:17/q02/p3 They went hunting or competed to catch game. — Muqatil calls the outing hunting; Mawradi offers catching game as one possible contest, as do Ibn Jawzi, Abu Hayyan, and Alusi. [5 notes: MUQATIL; MAWARDI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(مقاتل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 12:17/q02/p4 They competed at practical work, including watering, herding, or gathering wood. — Mawradi proposes their work; Abu Hayyan and Alusi mention divided farm or flock tasks. [3 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 12:17/q02/p5 They could have raced on horseback. — Abu Nasr al-Qushayri lists horseback competition alongside archery and running. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(أبو نصر القشيري)]
- 12:17/q02/p6 The form means striving for precedence in a mutual race; iftiʿāl and tafāʿul can overlap. — Kashshaf, Baydawi, Nasafi, Samin, Alusi, and Zamakhshari as quoted by Tabatabai compare the derived forms; Raghib defines sabq as preceding in movement. Tabatabai says each agent's self-directed effort entails comparison, citing good deeds and the door race against al-Manar's proposed distinction. Jishumi calls istibaq racing to get ahead. [12 notes: JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI(الراغب,الزمخشري); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 12:17/q02/p7 Istibāq specifically means trying to get ahead, in distinction from reciprocal racing. — Al-Manar applies the distinction here and contrasts racing to good deeds and to the door; Tabatabai rejects it because those examples also involve striving to get ahead. [1 notes: TABATABAI(صاحب المنار); TABATABAI-]

## 12:17/q03 What does the verse imply about the legitimacy and purpose of racing?  [نَسْتَبِقُ · law]
- 12:17/q03/p1 Racing and archery are legitimate practices, especially as training for war or defense. — Jishumi reports permissibility of foot racing and wrestling while noting debate over payment and binding contracts. Tabarsi says archery is permitted by consensus and horse and camel races by his school. Ibn al-ʿArabi cites the Prophet's foot and horse races and his two races with Aisha; Qurtubi cites Salama ibn al-Akwaʿ's race reported by Muslim, the Prophet's horse races in Malik from al-Hafya and Thaniyyat al-Wadaʿ, and travelers' archery in Muslim. Bukhari's al-ʿAdba report adds the Prophet's lesson that God lowers worldly things that rise. [8 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن العربي); STUDYQURAN(R)]

## 12:17/q04 Which contests may carry a prize or wager?  [نَسْتَبِقُ · law]
turns on: The disagreement concerns the scope of the prize hadith, military benefit, and whether a paid contest becomes gambling.
- 12:17/q04/p1 Prizes are confined to archery, camel racing, and horse racing. — Qurtubi cites consensus, Abu Hurayra's hadith in al-Nasa'i, and reliance by Hijazi and Iraqi jurists; Shafiʿi calls wagering elsewhere gambling. Wahidi and Razi cite the same threefold Prophetic rule. The purported addition 'or wing' was, Qurtubi says, fabricated by Abu al-Bakhtari for al-Rashid and scholars rejected his reports. [5 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أكثر المفسرين؛ الزجاج؛ ابن قتيبة); RAZI-FULL(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL]
- 12:17/q04/p2 Only horses and archery may carry prizes, with horse racing preferable. — Malik grounds the restriction in military usefulness; Qurtubi observes that the hadith's wording also includes camels. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(مالك)]
- 12:17/q04/p3 Only horse racing may carry a wager. — Qurtubi attributes this to jurists who treat horse racing as the customary Arab race. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]
- 12:17/q04/p4 Any contest may carry a wager. — Qurtubi attributes this to ʿAtaʾ but argues it would permit gambling, which is agreed to be forbidden. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]
- 12:17/q04/p5 Foot racing and wrestling with prizes are forbidden. — Tabarsi attributes this to his school and al-Shafiʿi; Jishumi also notes disagreement over compensation. [2 notes: TABRISI; JISHUMI]
- 12:17/q04/p6 Foot racing and wrestling with prizes are allowed. — Tabarsi attributes permission to Abu Hanifa and some colleagues of his own school; Jishumi records the related dispute over compensation. [2 notes: TABRISI; JISHUMI]

## 12:17/q05 What stake arrangements are permitted in a race?  [نَسْتَبِقُ · law]
turns on: Whether the third entrant has a real chance to win and thereby prevents a two-person stake from being gambling.
- 12:17/q05/p1 A voluntary known prize from a donor or a one-sided stake is allowed. — Qurtubi says neither arrangement is disputed; the winner may take the one-sided stake and the loser cannot reclaim it. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]
- 12:17/q05/p2 Two-sided stakes require a genuine third contestant who could win without risking money. — Qurtubi describes how either racer or the third entrant receives both stakes if victorious; Abu Ali ibn Khayran says the entrant's ability must be uncertain, whence muhallil. A Prophetic report and Saʿid ibn al-Musayyib support the qualifying horse, as do Shafiʿi and most scholars. Malik first dispensed with it but later required it, a view Qurtubi calls better. Without one, Qurtubi reports consensus that the wager is forbidden gambling. [5 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(أبو علي بن خيران); QURTUBI-FULL(مالك)+]
- 12:17/q05/p3 A qualifying third entrant is unnecessary for two-sided stakes. — Qurtubi reports Malik's earlier view; Malik later changed it and Qurtubi favors his later requirement. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(مالك); QURTUBI-FULL-]

## 12:17/q06 What makes a race or archery contest properly specified?  [نَسْتَبِقُ · law]
- 12:17/q06/p1 The course, competitors, and victory criterion must be specified appropriately. — Qurtubi derives from Malik's report a known distance, comparable horses, and separate distances for trained and untrained horses; he reserves the military Sunnah for horses prepared against enemies, not civil conflict. He also requires a known finish for camels, and a target, round count, and hit criterion for archery. He reports Umar's preference that the owner ride, while Qurtubi requires an adult rider; Shafiʿi and Muhammad ibn al-Hasan measure a lead by muzzle or hindquarters, also for archers. In a report of the Prophet racing Abu Bakr and Umar, Abu Bakr's second place means his horse's head reached the Prophet's horse's rear. [5 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الشافعي,عمر بن الخطاب)]

## 12:17/q07 What is the brothers' مَتَٰعِنَا?  [مَتَٰعِنَا · referent]
- 12:17/q07/p1 Their clothes and other belongings or baggage. — Ibn Abbas says clothes and belongings; Baghawi, Ibn Jawzi, Abu Hayyan, Qurtubi, and Study Quran's Bg emphasize garments. Abu Hayyan says they took off their clothes while competing and sees this as evidence Joseph was young rather than adolescent. Bursevi includes provisions and notes matāʿ means food containers at 12:65. [17 notes: MUQATIL; IBNABIHATIM(السدي); THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(Bg)]

## 12:17/q08 Why did they say they left Joseph by the belongings?  [وَتَرَكْنَا يُوسُفَ عِندَ مَتَٰعِنَا · interpretation]
turns on: The narrative leaves their stated reason for leaving him implicit; sources infer it from his placement among their things.
- 12:17/q08/p1 They present Joseph as guarding the baggage. — Muqatil and Tabarsi make him the watcher; Qurtubi also says he guarded clothes and other belongings. Mawradi says this may have been their reason for leaving him. [4 notes: MUQATIL; MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 12:17/q08/p2 They present the place as safe, showing care for Joseph and denying neglect. — Mawradi proposes concern for him. Alusi says clothes and provisions kept him within sight, briefly alone, unlikely to face danger; he also quotes Shaykh al-Islam on the safe setting. [3 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(شيخ الإسلام)]

## 12:17/q09 What did they claim the wolf had eaten?  [فَأَكَلَهُ ٱلذِّئْبُ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the pronoun and plain wording refer to Joseph or allow a displaced reference to the baggage.
- 12:17/q09/p1 They claimed the wolf killed Joseph and ate him, or some of him. — Razi favors this reading; Alusi calls it the plain sense. Ibn Ashur says 'ate him' can designate a whole person while meaning some of him, citing 5:3 and a lion example; he takes the singular wolf collectively as a pack leaving no trace, which explains the absence of burial. Bursevi says their sequence depicts an immediate attack after leaving him. [14 notes: MUQATIL; IBNABIHATIM(السدي); THALABI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(علي بن الحسين عليه السلام); ALUSI-FULL+; RAZI-FULL+]
- 12:17/q09/p2 They may have meant that the wolf attacked the belongings rather than Joseph. — Razi records the proposal that the brothers exposed the baggage to a wolf but prefers the first interpretation; Alusi rejects the proposal as departing from the plain wording. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 12:17/q10 Why did the brothers choose a wolf for their story?  [فَأَكَلَهُ ٱلذِّئْبُ · rhetoric]
- 12:17/q10/p1 They reused Jacob's earlier stated fear of a wolf, though the excuse was weak and exposed their deception. — Mawradi, Qurtubi, and Islahi say they borrowed his own warning; Ibn Kathir calls the admission of likely disbelief calculated tact, since the coincidence sounds unlikely. Islahi calls it unintelligent; Biqai cites their suspicious behavior and telltale signs, despite no prior proven lie. [5 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 12:17/q11 How is ٱلذِّئْبُ read?  [ٱلذِّئْبُ · readings]
turns on: Whether to realize or soften the hamza, including the analogy Kisai raises with the fish wording.
- 12:17/q11/p1 The hamza is articulated: al-dhiʾbu. — Ibn Jinni reports Hamza ibn Habib's reading against Kisai's; Tabarsi attributes articulation to readers other than his listed softeners. Qiraat ER calls it the standard connected reading; in pause the final consonant becomes silent. Kisai asks why Hamza hamzates wolf but not the fish at 37:142; the excerpt gives no reply. Ibn Jinni notes that early reading arguments began with analogy of forms or roots and later added grammar and citations. [6 notes: IBNJINNI-MUHTASAB(الكسائي,حمزة بن حبيب); TABRISI; QIRAAT-ER]
- 12:17/q11/p2 The hamza is softened or dropped, yielding al-dhību. — Ibn Jinni reports Kisai's unhamzated form; Tabarsi lists Kisai, Khalaf, Abu Jafar, Warsh, al-Asha, and al-Yazidi in all three occurrences. Qiraat ER describes compensatory lengthening. Tabarsi also gives plurals adhuʾub, dhawāʾib, and dhuʾbān and the wind verb tadhāʾabat. [4 notes: IBNJINNI-MUHTASAB(الكسائي); TABRISI; QIRAAT-ER]

## 12:17/q12 What does مُؤْمِنٍۢ لَّنَا mean?  [مُؤْمِنٍۢ لَّنَا · meaning]
- 12:17/q12/p1 Someone who believes, credits, or accepts their account. — Ibn Qutayba derives iman from affirmation and cites ordinary speech, 40:12, and prayer called iman at 2:143; Bursevi distinguishes verbal declaration of a speaker's truth from inward acceptance and defines truth against falsehood. Tabatabai notes the verb takes li or bi, citing 29:26; Ibn Ashur cites 29:26 and 10:83 for li before a person. Wahidi points to discussion at 2:3. Tabari's al-Suddi glosses it this way, while Tabari rejects reading the brothers as confessing lies or accusing Jacob of disbelief in known truth. [21 notes: MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; TAB-FULL(السدي); ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(السدي,محمد بن إسحاق); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(عامة المفسرين؛ أصحاب المعاني); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; BURSEVI]

## 12:17/q13 What other senses and theological uses of īmān and al-Muʾmin are brought to bear?  [مُؤْمِنٍۢ · theology]
turns on: Whether the divine name is explained through affirmation or the granting of safety.
- 12:17/q13/p1 A human believer affirms; God is al-Muʾmin by confirming His promise or accepting a servant's faith. — Both Ibn Qutayba works give this distinction. Gharib further warns that the divine attribute cannot be conjugated at will but must follow a transmitted form from Prophet or imams. [3 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL]
- 12:17/q13/p2 The divine name al-Muʾmin may derive from granting security: none is safe unless God makes them safe. — Ibn Qutayba offers this alternative etymology alongside confirmation. [1 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB]

## 12:17/q14 Why say مُؤْمِنٍۢ rather than مُصَدِّق?  [مُؤْمِنٍۢ · rhetoric]
- 12:17/q14/p1 Muʾmin combines believing with giving security or reassurance, outweighing muṣaddiq's verbal echo of ṣādiqīn. — Burhan says the added security sense better suits their request. Itqan says meaning takes priority over sound embellishment and the verse keeps muʾmin despite the available rhyme. [3 notes: BURHAN; ITQAN]

## 12:17/q15 Why did they expect Jacob not to believe them?  [وَمَآ أَنتَ بِمُؤْمِنٍۢ لَّنَا · interpretation]
turns on: The sources point to prior distrust, absence or contradiction of evidence, and the rhetorical use of an anticipated refusal; these reasons can coexist.
- 12:17/q15/p1 His love for Joseph and prior suspicion of the brothers would make this particular account unbelievable to him. — Zajjaj says a prophet cannot reject known truth. Tabari says Jacob suspects them, not that they confess lying or accuse him of rejecting truthful people. Wahidi, Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Bursevi, Alusi, and others emphasize love and distrust; Tabarsi adds jealousy and resentment. Ibn Kathir says they acknowledged his earlier warning. Study Quran links his suspicion to 12:11–12 and notes Genesis instead says he believed them. Asad says envy led him to infer harm but 12:83 shows he was unsure Joseph had died. [29 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(السدي); ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(السدي,محمد بن إسحاق); THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق؛ أبو العباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الزجاج وغيره); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURHAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN(Bḍ,R); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 12:17/q15/p2 They provided no convincing evidence, and the available signs worked against their claim. — Wahidi's Abu Bakr says objective truth alone would not secure belief without signs; Baghawi likewise says no evidence was offered. Razi allows this alternative. Qurtubi says evidence contradicted their accusation; Biqai sees signs of fabrication and Jacob's insight. Islahi calls the story weak. [6 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو بكر); BAGHAWI-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 12:17/q15/p3 Their claim of expected disbelief is a rhetorical excuse that signals their asserted truth or their expectation of rejection. — Tabatabai calls it a formula used when means are exhausted, indirectly claiming truth; Ibn Ashur says it shows they were not seeking credence. Ibn Kathir calls it calculated tact. Biqai says they conceded Jacob's disbelief; Burhan says even manifest innocence would not sway a father favoring Joseph, but their contrived proof betrayed them. [5 notes: TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURHAN]

## 12:17/q16 What is the force of وَلَوْ كُنَّا صَٰدِقِينَ?  [وَلَوْ كُنَّا صَٰدِقِينَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether ṣādiqīn describes their present claim, their general reputation, or a concessive assertion within their lie.
- 12:17/q16/p1 Even if the brothers were normally known as honest, Jacob would doubt them about Joseph. — Mawradi attributes this to Ibn Jarir; Ibn Jawzi and Qurtubi attribute it to Zajjaj, Tabari, and others. Ibn Atiyya says Jacob might have believed them before this disaster but not amid grief and old suspicion, while finding al-Rummani's analogy unsound. Alusi says this avoids their admitting a lie by contrast; Bursevi allows that Jacob regards them as truthful. [6 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(ابن جرير); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الزجاج وغيره); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 12:17/q16/p2 Even if their present account were in fact true, Jacob would not believe it. — Ibn Ishaq is cited by Mawradi and Ibn Jawzi; Abu Bakr in Wahidi says they may believe themselves truthful but need proof. Baghawi says they could be truthful before God yet lack evidence. Abu Hayyan says the clause may limit the truth claim to this incident; Ibn Atiyya says it continues their lie. [10 notes: IBNABIHATIM(محمد بن إسحاق); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن إسحاق); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو بكر); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(المبرد); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن إسحاق); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; NASAFI-FULL]
- 12:17/q16/p3 They are asserting 'although we have told the truth' as part of the invented story. — Mawradi cites Ibn Ishaq's wording; Ibn Atiyya reports Mubarrad's continuation of their assertion and questions an analogy to 7:88 and al-Rummani's account. Qurtubi notes the readings 'even if' and 'even though'. Tabatabai says the excuse indirectly asserts truth. Alusi resists making it a direct confession of actual truth, since contrast would imply they were lying. [5 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(ابن إسحاق); IBNATIYYA-FULL(المبرد); QURTUBI-FULL; TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 12:17/q17 How does the concessive construction وَلَوْ كُنَّا work?  [وَلَوْ كُنَّا صَٰدِقِينَ · grammar]
- 12:17/q17/p1 It is an elliptical concessive or circumstantial clause: Jacob would still not believe them, even under that condition. — Nahhas paraphrases it with wa-in kunna; Tabarsi supplies the omitted result. Samin calls it a circumstantial clause. Ibn Ashur identifies the waw as circumstantial and law as the remotest possible condition; he says no deleted opposite condition or contrary conclusion is implied, unlike a restrictive conditional, and cites Maʿarri's verse and 2:170 and 3:91. Burhan observes that law and lammā take past-form verbs as exceptions to the usual future orientation of condition verbs. [7 notes: NAHHAS; TABRISI; SAMIN-DURR; BURHAN; IBNASHUR-FULL(المعري)]

## 12:17/q18 How are نَسْتَبِقُ and وَتَرَكْنَا related syntactically to ذَهَبْنَا?  [ذَهَبْنَا نَسْتَبِقُ وَتَرَكْنَا · grammar]
- 12:17/q18/p1 Nastabiq is circumstantial, and taraknā is another circumstantial clause, with qad understood by some grammarians. — Samin al-Durr gives both analyses and notes the optional implicit qad. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 12:17/q19 What is the grammatical role of بِـ in بِمُؤْمِنٍۢ?  [بِمُؤْمِنٍۢ · grammar]
- 12:17/q19/p1 It precedes the predicate in a negative clause, a recurrent Quranic construction. — Bint al-Shati lists this verse alongside 2:167, 11:29, 26:114, 11:83, 16:46, 40:56, 14:22, 6:134, 12:44, 12:103, 26:137–138, 27:81, 30:53, 35:22, 37:162, 52:29, and 68:2. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ]

## 12:17/q20 How do the plural pronominal endings in the verse mark their speakers?  [ذَهَبْنَا كُنَّا · grammar]
- 12:17/q20/p1 Plural and dual suffixes use following letters for number and gender rather than the singular vowel alone. — Sibawayh says masculine dual and plural suffixes add mim after alif or waw; -tumā and -tumū retain ḍamma on taʾ and kaf because following material marks number and gender, and no marker beyond dual is added since dual already counts as plural. Feminine plural verb endings avoid a doubled nun unlike -tun or -kunna, to prevent four or five successive vowels. [3 notes: SIBAWAYH]

## 12:17/q21 When can a hearer ask for identification after hearing a plural reference?  [ذَهَبْنَا · grammar]
- 12:17/q21/p1 A definite named person cannot be queried as though unidentified, but an unfamiliar plural group can be. — Sibawayh says asking man after a specifically named Abd Allah is inappropriate; one may instead ask who he is or which man. He reports Arabs asking maʿa manīn after 'we went with them' or mannā after 'I saw him' when the people are not personally known, treating the report like 'I saw a man'. [2 notes: SIBAWAYH(العرب)]

## 12:17/q22 What did the brothers' shirt evidence show about the wolf story?  [فَأَكَلَهُ ٱلذِّئْبُ · links]
- 12:17/q22/p1 They bloodied Joseph's shirt to substantiate the story, but its intact condition disclosed the lie. — Razi says they used a kid's blood; al-Qadi suggests they stripped Joseph before the well to prepare the ruse and says a torn shirt would have made it credible. Razi explains the next verse's 'came with his shirt with blood' as blood covering it. Farra, Mubarrad, Zajjaj, and Ibn al-Anbari read dam kadhib as blood they lied about, a verbal noun for object or agent, comparing 67:30, 68:6, 34:7. Shaʿbi says Joseph's whole story is in his shirt: bloodied here, examined for direction of tearing at 12:26, and later restoring Jacob's sight. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL(الشعبي,الفراء والمبرد والزجاج وابن الأنباري,القاضي)]

## 12:17/q23 Do the brothers' tears establish that their story is true?  [قَالُوا۟ · interpretation]
- 12:17/q23/p1 No; tears can be produced deliberately and so cannot prove a claim. — Qurtubi says some can manufacture tears and others cannot; he reports a sage's saying that tears mingled on the cheeks distinguish real weeping from pretense. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]

## 12:17/q24 Why does the speech begin without a conjunction after the brothers arrive?  [قَالُوا۟ يَٰٓأَبَانَآ · rhetoric]
- 12:17/q24/p1 The dialogue resumes after an implied question, 'What did they say?', which their address to Jacob answers. — Burhan treats the apparent sequence as a resumption with the intervening question omitted. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 12:17/q25 How does this exchange connect with the surrounding story and its larger point?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes trace different connections: the family's developing mistrust and the narrative's broader providential theme.
- 12:17/q25/p1 Jacob's suspicion here recalls earlier distrust and anticipates his refusal of the brothers' later truthful report. — Study Quran points to 12:11–12 and 12:81–83, and to the protective warning at 12:5 and the shirt scene at 12:18; it says their own actions partly cause the later disbelief. Asad says 12:83 reveals hope that Joseph lived, so Jacob inferred harm without certainty of death. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN; ASAD-NOTES]
- 12:17/q25/p2 The Quranic Joseph narrative illustrates God's hidden direction of human affairs. — Asad contrasts Genesis' romantic account with the Quranic lesson that one may dislike what is good and love what is harmful, citing 2:216. In Abu Hamza's report cited by Tabatabai, the eldest brother prevents murder by proposing the well, the sons lie about a wolf, and Jacob grieves while trusting the true dream God showed him. [2 notes: ASAD-NOTES; TABATABAI(علي بن الحسين عليه السلام)]
