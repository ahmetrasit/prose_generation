# 95:5: 15 questions

## 95:5/q01 What does being returned to the lowest of the low mean?  [رَدَدْنَٰهُ أَسْفَلَ سَٰفِلِينَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the descent names an observable bodily reversal, a final place or form of punishment, or corruption of the human's original moral disposition; the following exception is a major test.
- 95:5/q01/p1 Return to decrepit old age, with bodily weakness and sometimes lost reason. — Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿIkrima, Ibrāhīm, Qatāda and others describe strength, beauty, senses, movement, voice and intellect ebbing; 16:70, 22:5 and 36:68 parallel the reversal. Al-Ṭabarī prefers this visible change because even deniers see aging, whereas they deny Hell; it proves resurrection (95:7). Al-Ḍaḥḥāk and al-Kalbī liken the elder to a child. Al-Farrāʾ and Ibn ʿAṭiyya say generic humanity need not imply every individual ages so far. Ibn Qutayba, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī and al-Baghawī compare the weak, ill and children, with senile elders lower still. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Nasafī detail bent backs, white hair, wrinkled skin, weakened senses and gait; Abū Ḥayyān calls that elaboration excessive. Bursawī cites a Persian poem; Corpus Coranicum cites Qurʾanic usage, Arabic poetry and radda against Birkeland's Hell argument. Al-Ḥākim authenticated the Ibn ʿAbbās report on reciters according to al-Durr. Ibn ʿĀshūr objects that bodily aging ill fits the emphatic oath and the sūra's purpose; Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī object that believers also age, making the apparent exception difficult. - 95:5/q01/p2 God casts the unbeliever into Hell or its lowest depths. — Al-Ḥasan, Mujāhid, Abū al-ʿĀliya, Ibn Zayd and others transmit this reading. ʿAlī describes Hell's gates as descending and being filled from below. Al-Wāḥidī, Ibn Kathīr and others cite the connected exception for believers and compare 103:2–3; al-Qushayrī says the general opening then narrows to disbelievers. Al-Nuḥḥās reports al-Ṭabarī's opposing argument: the audience witnesses aging but denies the Fire. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 4:145 for the lowest depth. - 95:5/q01/p3 The person becomes one of Hell's people, hideous in form; some reports specify the form of a pig. — Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Nasafī, al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī connect the ugliness to ingratitude for fine creation. Abū al-ʿĀliya specifically reports a pig form, while other descriptions say the ugliest shape without naming an animal. Al-Ālūsī favors the Judgment Day reading as most contextual. Bursawī says inner traits become outward forms at resurrection; Ibn Burjān adds an animal or vermin form in the intermediate realm and disfigurement afterward. - 95:5/q01/p4 People fall into disbelief, misguidance, and moral or spiritual baseness. — Al-Zajjāj and al-Qurṭubī compare humanity's loss and believers' exception in 103:2–3. Al-Biqāʿī says desire defeats reason, prompting idolatry, injustice and falsehood; rational people thus sink beneath unaccountable beasts and deserve punishment. Quṭb similarly contrasts animals' faithfulness to their nature with humans' capacity to rise even beyond angels, citing the Ascension. Ibn ʿĀshūr locates the baseness especially in belief: idolatry, deifying time and denying the Creator despite 51:21, and lists human sacrifice, infanticide and burning widows among its moral results. Al-Qāshānī depicts the veiled soul imprisoned in nature. Al-Iṣlāḥī invokes choice, innate guidance and consequent divine humiliation. Samarrai also includes the unbeliever's distressed earthly life, citing 20:124 and 22:31. Asad ascribes decline to human acts and omissions; al-Biqāʿī and Ibn ʿĀshūr stress the initial sound disposition. - 95:5/q01/p5 For the disbeliever, decline runs from youth to age and finally to Hell. — Ibn ʿAbbās gives this successive course and adduces a line attributed to ʿAlī using the low expression for a depth by Hell. [2 notes: DURR(ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 95:5/q01/p6 The lowest are idolaters, while the human in the preceding clause is Muḥammad. — Al-Durr explicitly marks this Ibn ʿAbbās report weak; it also calls the four caliphs the following exception and assigns geographical senses to the oaths. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 95:5/q01/p7 Human arrogance is brought low through bodily filth that recalls created dependence. — Al-Qurṭubī's reported explanation compares pretensions to divinity with Pharaoh's claim in 79:24; humiliation restores awareness of God's decree. [2 notes: QURTUBI(أبو العالية); QURTUBI-FULL]
- 95:5/q01/p8 The basest person exploits another person's religion for gain. — In Mālik's report, Rabīʿa approved this answer; Ibn ʿĀshūr applies it to polytheist leaders who secured power by misleading their followers into idol worship. [4 notes: IBNASHUR(مالك); IBNASHUR-FULL(مالك)]
- 95:5/q01/p9 The phrase can embrace age, Hell, moral decline, and distress together. — Samarrai says the wording admits all transmitted readings; the verse endings intentionally carry multiple senses. Elmalılı traces a broad reversal of purity, health, knowledge and dignity into weakness, ignorance, pain, death and Hell. [6 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; ELMALILI]

## 95:5/q02 What does ثُمَّ indicate here?  [ثُمَّ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the particle locates a later event in time, emphasizes the rank difference, or does both.
- 95:5/q02/p1 A delay in time after humanity's excellent creation. — Elmalılı calls this the ordinary sense and says every human must share some measure of the best form before decline. Samarrai includes an interval before the fall. [4 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; SAMARRAI-ASRAR; ELMALILI+]
- 95:5/q02/p2 A difference in rank: reversal from finest form to lowest is astonishing. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says changing an existing state is more remarkable than originating one. Al-Ālūsī and Samarrai also allow rank succession. [5 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMARRAI-ASRAR]
- 95:5/q02/p3 It marks both temporal delay and a vast difference in rank. — Samarrai explicitly joins the interval and the contrast in status. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR]

## 95:5/q03 How does رَدَدْنَٰهُ construe the descent?  [رَدَدْنَٰهُ أَسْفَلَ سَٰفِلِينَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether radda denotes a change of state with two objects, a one-object verb plus circumstantial description, or motion to a place.
- 95:5/q03/p1 Radda means make or render, taking the pronoun and asfal as two objects. — Al-Ālūsī cites a poetic line for the construction. Ibn ʿĀshūr treats it like a verb of becoming and says the word literally means returning something to an owner or place but here figuratively changes its state. [9 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BURSEVI+]
- 95:5/q03/p2 Radda takes one object and asfal describes the resulting condition circumstantially. — Al-Ālūsī proposes state change; al-Samīn allows a circumstantial modifier of the object. Bursawī calls this analysis clearer. [4 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI+]
- 95:5/q03/p3 Radda means returning or placing someone in a location, with asfal naming an omitted place or adverbial destination. — Al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī supply a place; Ibn ʿĀshūr allows an elided ilā or a place adverb, and calls divine agency literal here. Al-Iṣlāḥī calls asfal a place adverb and sāfilīn circumstantial with the object, reflecting a person's inclination to disgrace. [7 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 95:5/q03/p4 Asfal is 'the lowest' and sāfilīn describes those lowly people or ranks. — Ibn ʿĀshūr treats asfal as a comparative or superlative annexed to debased people. Al-Ālūsī finds a masculine rational plural awkward for places; rhyme and personifying places do not convince him, so he suggests inhabitants of descending Hell levels. The Study Quran reports a parsing in which 'lowly' describes the condemned person's abasement. [5 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN(some)]

## 95:5/q04 To whom does the pronoun in رَدَدْنَٰهُ refer?  [رَدَدْنَٰهُ · referent]
turns on: Whether al-insān in 95:4 is a generic human or a specific person, and whether a general opening narrows to unbelievers.
- 95:5/q04/p1 The human being mentioned in the previous verse, used generically for humankind. — Ibn ʿĀshūr explicitly identifies the antecedent and says its two earlier senses carry through. Samarrai says the wording does not specify unbelievers or another group; al-Farrāʾ and others treat the singular human as collective. Some of humanity undergoes senility, not necessarily every person. [8 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; FARRA; QUSHAYRI; IBNATIYYA; ALUSI]
- 95:5/q04/p2 The unbeliever specifically. — Al-Qushayrī says the opening is general but the ending concerns disbelievers; Ibn Kathīr ties the Fire to failure to obey God and follow messengers. The following exception for believers supports this restriction. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports a view restricting al-insān to unbelievers and thus making the exception disconnected. [9 notes: QUSHAYRI; MAWARDI(مجاهد وأبو العالية); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن ومجاهد وقتادة وابن زيد وأبو العالية); TABRISI(الحسن); IBNKATHIR; QURTUBI(مجاهد); ALUSI-FULL(الحسن، مجاهد، أبو العالية، ابن زيد، قتادة); TABATABAI(unnamed view)]
- 95:5/q04/p3 Muḥammad specifically. — This occurs in al-Durr's explicitly weak report, which then identifies the lowest as idolaters and the exception as four caliphs. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]

## 95:5/q05 Why is سَٰفِلِينَ plural after a singular human?  [سَٰفِلِينَ · grammar]
- 95:5/q05/p1 The singular al-insān names the human kind collectively, so plural agreement follows its meaning. — Al-Farrāʾ says singular asfal sāfil would also be grammatical and gives shifts of number at 39:33 and 42:48. Al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Wāḥidī and al-Iṣlāḥī give the same explanation; al-Rāzī's full text also cites 2:106. Al-Ṭabarī notes that this collective sense permits believers to be excepted like 103:2. ## 95:5/q06 How is أَسْفَلَ سَٰفِلِينَ read?  [أَسْفَلَ سَٰفِلِينَ · readings]
turns on: Presence or absence of the definite article in the transmitted wording.
- 95:5/q06/p1 The usual reading has sāfilīn without the definite article. — Abū Ḥayyān and his fuller text attribute the indefinite reading to the majority. Al-Thaʿlabī and al-Baghawī say indefiniteness covers the whole category, unlike a definite reference to a known group; Bursawī reports a contrasting explanation that lack of a generic article specifies decrepitude. [6 notes: ABUHAYYAN(الجمهور وعبد الله); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); THALABI(أهل المعاني); BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; BURSEVI(عين المعاني)]
- 95:5/q06/p2 ʿAbd Allāh or Ibn Masʿūd reads asfal al-sāfilīn with a definite article. — Al-Farrāʾ says the plural still suits collective humanity; al-Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Qurṭubī, al-Samīn, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Ālūsī and Corpus Coranicum transmit the form. Al-Ālūsī's note writes السالفين, while the other reports give السافلين; Bursawī's contrary account of the codex is attached to the indefinite position. [16 notes: FARRA(عبد الله); THALABI(مصحف عبد الله); JISHUMI(ابن مسعود); BAGHAWI(عبد الله); BAGHAWI-FULL(عبد الله); KASHSHAF(عبد الله); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA(ابن مسعود); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن مسعود); QURTUBI(عبد الله); QURTUBI-FULL(عبد الله); ABUHAYYAN(الجمهور وعبد الله); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR(عبد الله); ALUSI-FULL(عبد الله); CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 95:5/q07 What does the root of أَسْفَلَ express?  [أَسْفَلَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the low sense describes place, status or age; Arabic usage permits several extensions.
- 95:5/q07/p1 Literal lower position can extend to debasement, defeat, powerlessness, or decrepit age. — Ibn Jawzī describes downward direction for heavy bodies and contrasts upward direction for light bodies; he records lowest Hell and the lower army (4:145; 8:42), defeated plotters (37:98), and extreme age. Ibn ʿĀshūr says physical lowliness commonly signifies contempt and worst belief; Corpus Coranicum cites 37:98 and 41:29 for figurative subordination but adopts senility here. [10 notes: IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أهل التفسير); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; CORPUSCORANICUM; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL]
- 95:5/q07/p2 The lowly are the weak, sick, helpless, children, and elderly. — Ibn Qutayba derives sāfil like ʿāl from safala and ʿalā; he and later authors use this class to explain the depth of senility. Ibn ʿAbbās in al-Ṭabrisī includes fear and diminished reason. [9 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; THALABI(أهل المعاني); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن قتيبة); WAHIDI-QT; BAGHAWI; RAZI(ابن قتيبة); RAZI-FULL(ابن قتيبة); TABRISI(ابن عباس); BURSEVI(كشف الأسرار)]

## 95:5/q08 Why does God explicitly say that He returns the human low?  [رَدَدْنَٰهُ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the object is moral change through causes or direct placement in the abode of punishment.
- 95:5/q08/p1 It states God's control over both the beginning and the end of human life. — Samarrai says the passive could suggest that a different agent undid God's creation; the active divine subject closes that possibility. [3 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 95:5/q08/p2 For moral decline, God's agency is figurative through chains of causes. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says God creates higher causes and their interactions, which produce direct causes; he compares attributing a city's construction to a prince and shadow movement to God (25:45–46). Asad points to his note on 2:7. [4 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES]
- 95:5/q08/p3 For placement in Hell, God's agency is literal. — Ibn ʿĀshūr contrasts the locative interpretation with his causal account of moral decline: radd places a person where deserved. [2 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 95:5/q09 How does the next verse's exception relate to this descent?  [أَسْفَلَ سَٰفِلِينَ · links]
turns on: Whether believers are spared the destination, spared senility, or keep their reward despite suffering senility; the scope of 'human' also determines connection.
- 95:5/q09/p1 On the Hell or moral-fall reading, believers are a connected exception from the human kind. — Al-Farrāʾ and al-Ṭabarī compare the exception from loss in 103:2–3. Al-Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān and Ibn Kathīr use connectedness to favor the Fire; Ibn ʿĀshūr says believers return from shirk to original fitra. [16 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); FARRA; TAB-FULL(آخرون,الحسن,مجاهد); WAHIDI-BASIT; QUSHAYRI; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن ومجاهد وقتادة وابن زيد وأبو العالية); QURTUBI; ABUHAYYAN(الحسن ومجاهد وأبو العالية وابن زيد وقتادة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحسن ومجاهد وأبو العالية وابن زيد وقتادة); IBNKATHIR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI; STUDYQURAN; IBNKATHIR+]
- 95:5/q09/p2 On the aging reading, the exception is disconnected: believers can age but retain reward and relief from duty. — Al-Ṭabarī says aging affects believers and unbelievers, while earlier righteous work continues to count; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān add that the pen or duty is lifted when reason fails. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī objects that a connected exception would contradict universal aging. [10 notes: TAB-FULL(آخرون); IBNATIYYA(عكرمة وقتادة والضحاك والنخعي); IBNATIYYA-FULL(عكرمة وقتادة والضحاك والنخعي); ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(عكرمة والضحاك والنخعي); QURTUBI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 95:5/q09/p3 Qurʾān reciters are excepted from senility itself. — Ibn ʿAbbās and ʿIkrima say reciters or memorizers do not lose knowledge after knowing it; al-Durr links the claim to 22:5 or 16:70 and reports al-Ḥākim's authentication. [9 notes: TAB-FULL(عكرمة); IBNKATHIR(ابن عباس وعكرمة); DURR(ابن عباس,عكرمة); DURR-FULL(أنس عن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,ابن عباس,عكرمة)]
- 95:5/q09/p4 If 'human' means only the unbeliever, the believers' exception is disconnected. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports this view for Hell or reversal of bodily form. [1 notes: TABATABAI(unnamed view)]

## 95:5/q10 What happens to a believer's deeds and accountability in senility?  [رَدَدْنَٰهُ أَسْفَلَ سَٰفِلِينَ · interpretation]
- 95:5/q10/p1 Earlier righteous deeds continue to earn reward, and lost capacity is excused. — Ibn ʿAbbās says senile people in the Prophet's time were excused yet kept prior reward. Ibrāhīm, ʿIkrima and Qatāda extend the credit for best work done in youth or health; reports say no liability after loss of reason. Al-Thaʿlabī's report from Anas also contrasts children's unrecorded sins and credited good deeds, and says angels continue good credit and omit sins in extreme age. The prophetic report in al-Durr likewise credits good and withholds bad. Al-Dāmaghānī says deeds cease being recorded after decrepitude, while al-Baghawī distinguishes lost present capacity from continuing credit for earlier practice. ## 95:5/q11 What does the visible reversal from youth to age prove?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the argument must use what its addressees already witness or can invoke revelation about the unseen.
- 95:5/q11/p1 It shows God's power to resurrect and prepares the challenge in 95:7. — Al-Ṭabarī argues the audience acknowledges bodily change but denies Hell; the witnessed reversal establishes divine power to restore the dead. Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls senility an instructive sign within the human species. [7 notes: TAB; TAB-FULL; NAHHAS(محمد بن جرير); IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; SAMARRAI-ASRAR]
- 95:5/q11/p2 Visible proof is unnecessary because revelation and prophetic signs make unseen truths evident. — Al-Naḥḥās reports this counterargument against limiting the reasoning to observed aging. [1 notes: NAHHAS(قيل)]

## 95:5/q12 How does this verse fit the sūra and its surrounding passages?  [* · links]
turns on: How the human descent functions in the oath's argument and whether the oath sites themselves exemplify it.
- 95:5/q12/p1 The sūra contrasts humanity's excellent beginning with a fall caused by refusing guidance, while the faithful retain reward. — Al-Biqāʿī links the best human form, the Prophet and the preceding sūras to divergent human ends; without divine guidance a person mistakes the self for perfection. The secure city and preceding solitude mirror excellence and degradation. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the clause central and coordinated with 95:4 under the oath; the subtle inward truth warrants emphatic swearing. Al-Ālūsī relates the preceding sūra's perfected Prophet to human outcomes. Al-Iṣlāḥī says the oaths validate choice and divine retribution; Samarrai says the oath's answer fits sites of revelation and the later judgment argument. The Study Quran parallels 103:2–3. [28 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL(أبو جعفر بن الزبير); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; SAMARRAI-ASRAR; STUDYQURAN]
- 95:5/q12/p2 The oath sites recall earlier demonstrations of divine favor and punishment. — Farāhī reads the Mount of Figs as Jūdī: Adam's broken covenant cost a favor, repentance brought guidance, and at Noah's time wrongdoers drowned while the righteous were saved (7:27; 11:44,48). He reads Jesus' rejected-stone saying as transfer of trust from Israel and Sinai as deliverance from Pharaoh and a law for the oppressed; Abraham's leadership was promised only to faithful descendants (2:124). Al-Biqāʿī relates the oath to a Torah sequence from Sinai, Seir and Paran and a history of ideal creation, law and fall. The Study Quran reports fig leaves and the olive's light as possible foreshadowing. [13 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Imām Farāhī); BIQAI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(others)]

## 95:5/q13 What does the verse teach about human choice and final accountability?  [* · theology]
- 95:5/q13/p1 Humans can squander sound nature through desire and choice; divine judgment must answer the resulting deeds. — Al-Biqāʿī says desire overcomes reason and misused faculties make people culpable; because many die uncompensated, wisdom requires resurrection and enduring punishment. Al-Aqlaishī contrasts reason's ascent with desire's descent. Al-Iṣlāḥī calls this a divine law of free will and reward or humiliation; Asad says human acts and omissions corrupt positive disposition. Ibn al-Zubayr adds that each is enabled for an appointed end and urges sincerity and thanks, citing 32:13. Al-Qushayrī and al-Ṭabrisī connect disbelief to wretchedness after an originally rational, accountable creation. [18 notes: BIQAI(الإمام أبو العباس الأقليشي); BIQAI-FULL(أبو جعفر بن الزبير,الأقليشي); QUSHAYRI; TABRISI; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; QUTB-ZILAL]

## 95:5/q14 How does the verse's wording carry more than one sense?  [* · rhetoric]
- 95:5/q14/p1 Its endings pair meanings, especially old age and Hell for asfal sāfilīn. — Samarrai says al-amīn holds security and trust, ghayr mamnūn lasting and unreproached reward, dīn religion and recompense, and aḥkam wisdom and judgment. Al-Biqāʿī describes iḥtibāk: the first clause leaves evil deeds and punishment implicit, and the exception implies preserving original fitra. ## 95:5/q15 What is reported about the sūra's place of revelation?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Reports differ between the majority Meccan attribution and the minority Medinan attribution.
- 95:5/q15/p1 Most call it Meccan. — Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī report the majority; al-Ālūsī argues that unanimous identification of the secure city with Mecca supports it. The Study Quran calls it likely early-middle Meccan after sūra 85; Asad also places it after 85. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); ALUSI-FULL; STUDYQURAN; ASAD-NOTES]
- 95:5/q15/p2 Ibn ʿAbbās and Qatāda call it Medinan. — Abū Ḥayyān cites both; al-Ālūsī calls the Ibn ʿAbbās report one variant, and the Study Quran notes the minority view. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); ALUSI-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
