# 104:3: 17 questions

## 104:3/q01 What does yaḥsabu mean here?  [يَحْسَبُ · meaning]
- 104:3/q01/p1 He supposes or falsely estimates that wealth has secured immortality. — Bint al-Shāṭi distinguishes literal counting, figurative estimation, planning and divine reckoning; she says the Qurʾānic three-letter verb marks mistaken supposition, comparing the Queen of Sheba's mistake (27:44), 18:9 and 76:19. She surveys rebuking interrogatives at 29:2, 45:21, 47:29, 23:115, 29:4, 2:214, 3:142 and 9:16, and says all thirty-one open-vowel imperfect occurrences warn against misestimation. Al-Sāmarrāʾī distinguishes ḥasaba-yaḥsubu, 'to count,' from ḥasiba, 'to suppose'; Bint al-Shāṭi also notes ḥasab can mean inherited distinction. [8 notes: BINTSHATI; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; QIRAAT-ER]

## 104:3/q02 How is yaḥsabu read?  [يَحْسَبُ · readings]
turns on: The sīn vowel and the attested reciters or dialects; the notes say the sense is unchanged.
- 104:3/q02/p1 With fatḥa on the sīn, yaḥsabu. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports this from al-Aʿraj, Abū Jaʿfar and Shayba; Ibn ʿĀshūr names Ibn ʿĀmir, ʿĀṣim, Ḥamza and Abū Jaʿfar. Sibawayh calls ḥasiba-yaḥsabu sound and more analogical, though among exceptional imperfect vowels; he contrasts the three patterns shared by transitive and intransitive verbs with intransitive faʿula-yafʿulu, adduces al-Farazdaq's yanʿamu, faḍila-yafḍulu and māta-tamūtu, and relays al-Khalīl's judgment of kāda-takādu as irregular. His discussion of hamzated roots compares abā-yaʾbā with qaraʾa-yaqraʾu and ḥasiba-yaḥsabu, marks jabā-yajbā and qalā-yaqlā weakly transmitted, and cites better-attested ʿamara-yaʿmuru and yaḥzuru; he also discusses amara-yaʾmuru, akala-yaʾkulu, waʿadahu-yaʿiduhu and weak ʿaḍaḍtu-taʿaḍḍu by analogy. Al-Sāmarrāʾī calls this form more common in ordinary language. Both vocalizations mean 'supposes,' according to Qiraat-ER. [18 notes: SIBAWAYH(الخليل); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الأعرج وأبو جعفر وشيبة); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL(الجمهور); QIRAAT-ER; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 104:3/q02/p2 With kasra on the sīn, yaḥsibu. — Al-Naḥḥās describes it as the Prophet's dialect on the rare faʿila-yafʿalu pattern. Al-Durr cites Jābir's report of the Prophet's pronunciation, authenticated by Ibn Ḥibbān and al-Ḥākim and cited by Ibn Mardawayh and al-Khaṭīb. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it the majority reading and one of two dialectal forms; Qiraat-ER calls both canonical, with the same sense. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says every mutawātir reading may be recited in prayer and cites al-Jazarī's collection of ten in al-Nashr. [7 notes: NAHHAS; DURR(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL(الجمهور); QIRAAT-ER; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 104:3/q03 How does the yaḥsabu sentence attach to what precedes?  [يَحْسَبُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the clause resumes, supplies the hoarder's circumstance, or implies a satirical question.
- 104:3/q03/p1 It is a new, independent sentence. — Al-Samīn, al-Biqāʿī, al-Ālūsī and Ibn ʿĀshūr allow resumption; Ibn ʿĀshūr says an independent assertion can convey denial and taunt the hoarder. [6 notes: SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 104:3/q03/p2 It is a circumstantial clause describing the hoarder. — Al-Samīn says it can describe the subject of jamaʿa; Ibn ʿĀshūr says it satirically portrays the slanderer's greed. [5 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 104:3/q03/p3 An omitted interrogative makes it a taunting question. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the implicit question expresses mockery or amazement. Neuwirth once proposed inserting an explicit interrogative because four other early Meccan uses have one (75:3, 75:36, 90:5, 90:7) and 83:1–6 is a woe followed by a polemical question; Corpus Coranicum says she later seems to withdraw that emendation. [3 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; CORPUSCORANICUM(Neuwirth)]

## 104:3/q04 What does the anna clause supply to yaḥsabu?  [يَحْسَبُ أَنَّ مَالَهُۥٓ أَخْلَدَهُۥ · grammar]
- 104:3/q04/p1 The anna clause and its governed words fill both objects of yaḥsabu. — This is al-Naḥḥās's syntactic analysis. [1 notes: NAHHAS]

## 104:3/q05 Why repeat mālahu explicitly?  [مَالَهُۥٓ · rhetoric]
- 104:3/q05/p1 Naming wealth where a pronoun could stand reinforces the point. — Al-Ālūsī and his full version give this as emphatic wording. [2 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 104:3/q06 What does akhladahu mean as a verb?  [أَخْلَدَهُۥ · meaning]
- 104:3/q06/p1 It means to cause someone to remain or live forever, or for a very long time. — Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī and al-Ālūsī equate akhlada with khallada; al-Ālūsī allows unlimited or very long duration. Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies the hamza as causative. Bint al-Shāṭi defines khuld as permanent endurance and says Qurʾānic worldly uses express illusion or deny human permanence: 21:34, 21:8, 26:129; she counts 87 root occurrences. Labīd's line says survivors are neither left nor immortal; Neuwirth cites Ṭarafa's Muʿallaqa 54 for the pre-Islamic immortality motif. [12 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(لبيد بن ربيعة); CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 104:3/q07 What time does the past form akhladahu express?  [أَخْلَدَهُۥ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether completed wording projects a presumed future certainty or describes wealth's support up to now.
- 104:3/q07/p1 It presents the hoped-for future as already achieved. — Al-Farrāʾ, al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī compare saying a sinner has perished or entered Hell before the consequence arrives. Al-Ṭabrisī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī say yaḥsabu makes the past verb prospective. Al-Rāzī, al-Bursevī and Ibn ʿĀshūr say the completed form depicts the hoarder's certainty as if survival were guaranteed; al-Ālūsī calls it intensification. Al-Ṭabrisī also says the cause can be described as an accomplished result before death occurs. [18 notes: FARRA; TAB; TAB-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI(أبو بكر بن طاهر); ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 104:3/q07/p2 It retains a past sense: wealth has sustained or prolonged his life so far. — Al-Samīn expressly allows an actual past meaning. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān say wealth has supported and protected his life, so he expects that support to continue; al-Qurṭubī reports the idea that it gave him life already, while also explaining a past-for-future use. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; QURTUBI(قيل)]

## 104:3/q08 Who is the agent and object of akhladahu?  [مَالَهُۥٓ أَخْلَدَهُۥ · grammar]
turns on: The grammatical subject and object of the causative verb; al-Ālūsī dismisses the inversion.
- 104:3/q08/p1 Wealth is the imagined agent and the man is its object. — Ibn ʿĀshūr explains the causative hamza this way; the normal interpretation throughout the commentaries takes wealth as what supposedly preserves its owner. [4 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI(عصام الدين); ALUSI-FULL(عصام الدين)]
- 104:3/q08/p2 The man is the agent and wealth the object: he thinks he can keep his wealth forever. — ʿIṣām al-Dīn proposes this inversion; al-Ālūsī rejects it. He adds that mishap or death will take the money, and reports the saying that a miser's wealth should expect a mishap or an heir. [0 notes: ALUSI(عصام الدين)-; ALUSI-FULL(عصام الدين,قيل)-]

## 104:3/q09 What does the claim that wealth made him immortal mean?  [مَالَهُۥٓ أَخْلَدَهُۥ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the man's claim is literal, an illusion shown by his conduct, an extension or preservation of life, an expectation beyond death, or an ironic allusion to deeds.
- 104:3/q09/p1 He imagines wealth will prevent death and keep him alive in this world. — Muqātil says this lasts until wealth is spent, though neither wealth nor children can immortalize him. Al-Ṭabarī ties the illusion to amassed, counted and withheld money. Al-Zajjāj describes acting like a rich person who never expects death. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Biqāʿī, Quṭb and others link it to trusting money against danger; Quṭb adds imagined escape from God's decree and judgment. Ibn ʿĀshūr says worldly survival is the hoarder's highest wish because he denies an eternal life beyond it. Al-Ālūsī allows a literal delusion caused by arrogance, distraction from judgment, or belief that material causes and wealth control health and safety. Al-Ṭabrisī says he behaves this way despite knowing everyone dies. [36 notes: MUQATIL; TUSTARI(قيل); TAB; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; MAWARDI(السدي); MAWARDI-FULL(السدي); QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); WAHIDI-QT; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI(السدي); QURTUBI-FULL(السدي); BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES(Asad); CORPUSCORANICUM; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 104:3/q09/p2 His wealth gives him long hopes or distracts him, so he lives as if death will never come. — Al-Ḥasan's reading reported by al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī connects this to heedlessness and distant wishes; al-Ḥasan remarks that certain death seems doubtful to people. Al-Bayḍāwī distinguishes attachment, distraction and long hopes. Al-Bursevī and Ibn ʿĀshūr say the claim is figurative, not anyone's real conviction; al-Ālūsī calls it a representational metaphor. Al-Biqāʿī says the sinner turns from God toward fleeting pleasures. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it an emphatic simile satirizing greed. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī links earthly attachment and perceived self-sufficiency to boundless wants, tyranny and superiority (96:6–7). [20 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI; RAZI-FULL(الحسن); BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN(الزمخشري); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(الحسن); ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES(Asad)]
- 104:3/q09/p3 He thinks his wealth will lengthen his lifespan. — ʿIkrima gives the explicit extension of lifespan; the same report recurs through Ibn Abī Ḥātim, al-Māwardī, al-Qurṭubī and al-Durr. [6 notes: IBNABIHATIM(عكرمة); MAWARDI(عكرمة); MAWARDI-FULL(عكرمة); QURTUBI(عكرمة); QURTUBI-FULL(عكرمة); DURR(عكرمة)]
- 104:3/q09/p4 Wealth has sustained and protected his life, so he supposes it will continue to do so. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls wealth the substance and support of life; Abū Ḥayyān says it has maintained and protected him throughout his lifespan. The past form can thus name the support already experienced. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 104:3/q09/p5 The wording alludes to deeds or afterlife striving as the real way to lasting life. — Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Nasafī name righteous deeds leading to abiding bliss; al-Rāzī adds good remembrance in this world, with enduring bliss in the next. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Biqāʿī and al-Ālūsī formulate the afterlife pursuit. Al-Ālūsī calls this reading very far-fetched and notes that another scholar did not treat it as independent. [11 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); ALUSI-; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 104:3/q09/p6 His lasting buildings, planting and landworks show an expectation of prolonged life or remembrance. — Al-Zamakhsharī cites fortified homes, trees and land development; al-Rāzī specifies brick and plaster and adds the wish to be remembered after death. Al-Bursevī adds digging rivers; al-Ālūsī ties all these acts to long hopes. Islahi compares the monuments and palaces of 26:128–29. [8 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 104:3/q09/p7 He thinks wealth will benefit him after death. — Al-Māwardī gives this as a further possibility, separate from lifespan extension and escape from death. [2 notes: MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL]
- 104:3/q09/p8 He loves wealth so much that he thinks losing any of it would kill him. — Al-Rāzī presents this as a third interpretation: the miser guards money as if keeping it were preserving his own life, and calls it plausible for a miser. [2 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL]
- 104:3/q09/p9 He expects money to bring him to an immortal station. — Abū Bakr ibn Ṭāhir gives this explanation; al-Bursevī says the past form pictures survival and safety from death as already secured. Al-Tustarī similarly says 'the abode of permanence.' [3 notes: TUSTARI; SULAMI(أبو بكر بن طاهر); BURSEVI(أبو بكر بن طاهر)]

## 104:3/q10 How does the verse's immortality language work as figurative speech?  [مَالَهُۥٓ أَخْلَدَهُۥ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the sentence reports belief or exaggerates behavior into a claim.
- 104:3/q10/p1 It portrays the hoarder's conduct as though money had made him immortal. — Al-Ālūsī calls it representational metaphor and Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it an emphatic simile; he says nobody actually thinks wealth literally immortalizes. ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī's comparison theory explains the force: 'like a lion' assigns a measured share of a common quality, while 'the lion' maximizes it; the lion's naming rests chiefly on courage. He distinguishes identity between two names from asserted complete likeness; complete likeness borrows force from identity because at different times the viewer might think he sees the same thing. Applying 'he himself' to the lion comparison suppresses differences and heightens the shared quality. [11 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR]
- 104:3/q10/p2 He may literally hold the delusion that money grants immortality. — Al-Ālūsī allows literal belief from arrogance, preoccupation with accumulation, neglect of the coming terrors, or trusting visible causes and wealth for life and health. [2 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 104:3/q11 What does the wealth signify inwardly?  [مَالَهُۥٓ · inward]
- 104:3/q11/p1 It is a hoard of blameworthy morals and low qualities. — Al-Bursevī relays al-Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya: the person treats this hoard as provision for stages of the afterlife and entry into God's presence. Al-Qāshānī contrasts bodily riches, which perish, with enduring knowledge and spiritual virtues; he says ignorance, a vice of the royal faculty, roots all vices and leads to punishment overwhelming the heart. [3 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية); QASHANI]

## 104:3/q12 What reports describe the man's wealth or conduct?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports give distinct narratives and conflicting dinar amounts; no note resolves the amounts.
- 104:3/q12/p1 He boasted his wealth could provision Quraysh without running out, then counted it after a warning and provoked revelation. — Muqātil says he swore he could distribute wealth across Quraysh while alive without depleting it and challenged poverty; when told that its Giver could remove it, he counted his possessions and told Muḥammad that even if all were bread it would not run out. Sūrat al-Humazah was then revealed. [3 notes: MUQATIL]
- 104:3/q12/p2 Al-Akhnas owned four thousand dinars. — Al-Zamakhsharī and Abū Ḥayyān report this amount, while also transmitting a different figure. [3 notes: KASHSHAF(unspecified report); KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN]
- 104:3/q12/p3 Al-Akhnas owned ten thousand dinars. — Al-Zamakhsharī and Abū Ḥayyān also report this amount; the two figures are alternatives. [3 notes: KASHSHAF(unspecified report); KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN]
- 104:3/q12/p4 A rich man told al-Ḥasan he saved thousands against hardship, rulers, calamities and poverty. — Al-Ḥasan replied that the man would leave the money to people who would neither thank him nor excuse him. [1 notes: KASHSHAF(الحسن)]

## 104:3/q13 Whom does this portrayal target?  [* · referent]
turns on: Whether the sketch designates a moral type, contemporary polytheists, or hypocrites.
- 104:3/q13/p1 A type of wealthy, self-reliant sinner who hoards and mocks. — Corpus Coranicum calls him a prototypical sinner whose unfounded trust in money fuels accumulation and antisocial conduct. Asad treats the hoarding attitude as blameworthy alongside slander; the Study Quran says the sūrah concerns torment for hoarders. [3 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM; ASAD-NOTES(Asad); STUDYQURAN]
- 104:3/q13/p2 Actual polytheists who mocked the Messenger and believers. — Quṭb infers this from the severe warning, but considers reports naming particular individuals unreliable. [1 notes: QUTB-ZILAL]
- 104:3/q13/p3 Hypocrites absorbed by money and the pleasures it purchases. — Islahi says their conduct reveals an attachment to this world's pleasures and contradicts belief in the Hereafter. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 104:3/q14 What does this claim teach about wealth and enduring life?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes develop both the false promise of wealth and the conduct it fosters.
- 104:3/q14/p1 Wealth cannot secure permanence; lasting life comes through faith, knowledge, virtue, righteous deeds and afterlife striving. — Al-Rāzī cites ʿAlī: wealth keepers die while scholars remain through time. Al-Qāshānī says bodily riches perish but knowledge and spiritual virtues last. Elmalılı invokes faith in truth, knowledge and righteous action and the preceding sūrah. Ibn ʿĀshūr says hoarding withholds God's due in spending and zakāt. Islahi argues recognition of life's brevity would redirect spending and competition to the Hereafter; he cites Jesus' teaching that the heart follows where one keeps wealth. Bint al-Shāṭi says the Qurʾān reserves real eternity for the afterlife (21:34, 21:8, 26:129). [14 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT; KASHSHAF; RAZI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; QASHANI; BIQAI; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI; BINTSHATI-IJAZ; ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Jesus (sws))]
- 104:3/q14/p2 Attachment to money breeds false self-sufficiency, greed and contempt for others. — Quṭb links counting and savoring money to verbal and gestural mockery; he says Islam forbids such faultfinding. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites 96:6–7 on self-sufficiency producing rebellion. Asad describes material acquisition becoming almost religious and displacing spiritual concerns; Islahi says the direction of one's life reveals whether this world or the next is its destination. [6 notes: QUTB-ZILAL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES(Asad); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 104:3/q15 How does the next rebuke answer the claimed immortality?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether kallā primarily negates his delusion or affirms the following sworn threat.
- 104:3/q15/p1 Kallā declares the claim false and rebukes the hoarder. — ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd Allāh, client of Ghufra, says God's kallā marks the hearer wrong. Al-Biqāʿī says he dies like every creature; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the oath-emphasizing lām promises he will be cast into al-Ḥuṭama. Al-Bursevī also allows a rebuke of the earlier mockery; Ibn ʿĀshūr connects it to failure to spend and pay zakāt. [13 notes: AKHFASH; THALABI(عمر بن عبد الله مولى غفرة); KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI; QURTUBI-FULL(عمر بن عبد الله مولى غفرة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 104:3/q15/p2 Kallā means 'indeed' and introduces the force of an implied oath. — Al-Rāzī says the lām of layunbadhanna answers this implied oath. Al-Bursevī also permits the oath connection. [2 notes: RAZI; BURSEVI]

## 104:3/q16 How does the punishment reverse the man's expectation?  [* · links]
- 104:3/q16/p1 The supposedly permanent rich mocker is thrown into crushing Fire and confined. — Al-Akhfash says both man and wealth are cast into the Crusher. Quṭb sees a measured reversal: a rich scoffer expecting permanence is discarded, crushed in God's Fire, burned in the heart that generated pride, then confined without aid like a tied animal; sound pattern and progression from threat through question to explanation magnify it. Corpus Coranicum says verse 4 counters his imagined immortality through the root h-t-m. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says Ḥuṭama intensifies crushing, may suggest eating, and is explained as God's kindled Fire; the Study Quran compares hoarded gold and silver branding owners in 9:34–35, alongside 70:18. [8 notes: AKHFASH; QUTB-ZILAL; TABATABAI; CORPUSCORANICUM; STUDYQURAN]

## 104:3/q17 How does the claim connect with the sūrah and other scriptural passages?  [* · links]
turns on: The sources trace different connections: the preceding sūrah, the current moral sequence, other woe texts, or a following dual reference.
- 104:3/q17/p1 It identifies the loser of the preceding sūrah and continues warnings against human injustice. — Abū Ḥayyān says this Meccan sūrah explains the loser of 103:2 by depicting a particular sort of loser. Abū Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr relates it to the ungrateful human at 100:6, humanity in loss at 103:2, and the wealth-deluded person here. Elmalılı recalls the prior sūrah's remedy of faith, knowledge and righteous action. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(أبو جعفر بن الزبير); ELMALILI]
- 104:3/q17/p2 The wealth delusion drives the sūrah's hoarding and slander. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says perceived self-sufficiency breeds rebellion and mockery, citing 96:6–7. Quṭb says the Qurʾān's condemnation both directs the mission and reassures abused believers that God sees and punishes the offense. Asad inserts 'Woe unto him' again because verses 2–3 portray an attitude distinct from verse 1's two types. [6 notes: TABATABAI; QUTB-ZILAL; ASAD-NOTES(Asad); CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 104:3/q17/p3 It parallels other woe and wealth passages. — Corpus Coranicum compares vice catalogues after woe at 107:4–7, 83:1–3 and 83:10–11, 52:11–12 and 51:60; 83:1–6 is closest for wealth and a following question. It cites Hebrew Bible Micah 2:1–3 as another prophetic woe speech. Its comparison with 90:6 contrasts hoarding and ostentatious spending as two self-powered immortality delusions. Islahi compares monumental building at 26:128–29. [4 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 104:3/q17/p4 A dual-form recitation can take the following verb to refer to both the hamza and the lamza. — Al-Bursevī reports this grammatical reading and its supporting dual recitation. [1 notes: BURSEVI]
