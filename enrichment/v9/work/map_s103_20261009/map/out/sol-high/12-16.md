# 12:16: 19 questions

## 12:16/q01 What happened to Joseph before the brothers returned?  [وَجَآءُوٓ · reports]
turns on: The reports place the return at different points relative to Joseph's removal, and Abū Ḥayyān questions the evidentiary basis of detailed well narratives.
- 12:16/q01/p1 The brothers cast Joseph into the well and returned without him. — Al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī place their return after the casting. Ibn Abī Ḥātim's al-Suddī says they slaughtered a young sheep and bloodied his shirt. Al-Wāḥidī's Ibn ʿAbbās says they left Joseph naked in the well and bloodied the shirt with a lamb. In ʿAlī b. al-Ḥusayn's report through Abū Ḥamza, the eldest brother persuaded the others to use the well instead of killing him; Jacob later trusted God's truthful dream despite his grief. [5 notes: TAB-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(السدي); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL; TABATABAI(علي بن الحسين عليه السلام)]
- 12:16/q01/p2 They returned after the caravan had departed with Joseph. — Ibn Abī Ḥātim transmits this sequence from Muḥammad b. Isḥāq: they realized Joseph was gone and then came to Jacob crying at night. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(محمد بن إسحاق)]
- 12:16/q01/p3 A detailed narrative has Jacob consent to Joseph's outing, embrace and bless him, and the brothers cast him down once out of sight. — Abū Ḥayyān reports the brothers had Joseph ask leave; Jacob promised permission next morning, and they carried him while still in view. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]
- 12:16/q01/p4 Detailed tales of the casting and Joseph's pleas are unestablished. — Abū Ḥayyān says neither the Qur'an nor a sound hadith establishes such elaborations; tafsīr books may be consulted without treating them as established. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 12:16/q02 What story did the brothers give Jacob for Joseph's absence?  [وَجَآءُوٓ · reports]
- 12:16/q02/p1 They claimed a wolf ate Joseph. — Muqātil adds that they said they had gone hunting, left Joseph to guard their belongings, and anticipated Jacob's disbelief even if they spoke truth. Al-Qushayrī says they concealed their real reason for crying. ʿAlī b. al-Ḥusayn's report says the claim was a lie. Abū Ḥayyān says the omitted exchange is recoverable: Jacob asked where Joseph was and they gave this answer. [4 notes: MUQATIL; QUSHAYRI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; TABATABAI(علي بن الحسين عليه السلام)]

## 12:16/q03 How was Joseph's bloodied shirt produced, and how is its blood described?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reported wording of the later blood description differs by the consonant read.
- 12:16/q03/p1 The shirt's blood was fabricated and not Joseph's. — ʿAbd al-Razzāq transmits Qatāda's statement. Ibn Abī Ḥātim's al-Suddī says they slaughtered a young sheep and smeared its blood on the shirt; al-Wāḥidī's Ibn ʿAbbās calls the animal a lamb and says Joseph was left naked in the well. The animal wording varies. [3 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); IBNABIHATIM(السدي); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس)]
- 12:16/q03/p2 One reported reading calls the blood lying; ʿĀʾisha's variant calls it fresh. — Al-Jishumī reports the usual reading with zāy and ʿĀʾisha's reading with dāl, giving the latter the sense of fresh blood. [1 notes: JISHUMI(عائشة)]

## 12:16/q04 What time does ʿishāʾ denote?  [عِشَآءًۭ · meaning]
turns on: The sources give different boundaries for ʿishāʾ and different estimates of the brothers' arrival within the late day.
- 12:16/q04/p1 It denotes evening or the late part of the day, extending into the first darkness. — Al-Jishumī lists the day's end, first darkness, a broad span from noon to morning, or sunset prayer until night; with fatḥa, the form means food. Al-Ṭabrisī gives late day, early night, or evening prayer. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī say late day or day's end. Bursevī gives late day through midnight. Al-Rāghib, quoted by al-Ālūsī, narrows it to sunset prayer until night prayer and calls the two evening prayers al-ʿishāʾān. [7 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الراغب); TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 12:16/q04/p2 They arrived after the afternoon prayer. — Bursevī quotes Abū al-Layth for this narrower timing. [2 notes: BURSEVI(أبو الليث)]
- 12:16/q04/p3 They arrived at the evening prayer. — Muqātil places the return at the evening prayer. [1 notes: MUQATIL]

## 12:16/q05 How is ʿishāʾ read and explained in the reported variants?  [عِشَآءًۭ · readings]
turns on: The vowel and shortened or diminutive form affect whether the word denotes time, blurred eyes, darkness, or a flame; grammarians contest the proposed plural morphology.
- 12:16/q05/p1 The ordinary reading is ʿishāʾ, an evening time word. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya contrasts the group's reading with al-Ḥasan's; al-Ālūsī prefers the ordinary reading as evening. Al-Samīn prefers its time adverb construal and rejects a plural noun analysis. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 12:16/q05/p2 Al-Ḥasan is reported to read a diminutive ʿashiyyan. — Al-Kashshāf, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī report this form; Ibn ʿAṭiyya cites Abū al-Fatḥ's comparison with a plural whose feminine tāʾ is dropped, illustrated by a line of ʿAdī. Al-Ālūsī reports objections to the proposed deletion and plural pattern. [6 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(الحسن); IBNATIYYA-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الحسن البصري); ALUSI-FULL(الحسن); ALUSI-FULL-]
- 12:16/q05/p3 A short form or ḍamma reading is analyzed as a plural of people whose eyes were clouded by crying. — Ibn Jinnī transmits al-Ḥasan's عشا through ʿĪsā b. Maymūn, deriving it from ʿashāh with feminine ending removed, but Abū al-Fatḥ objects that a day's crying would not blind them. Al-Kashshāf, al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn transmit the clouded-eye or plural explanation, with varying short forms; Ibn al-Jawzī names Abū Hurayra, al-Ḥasan, Ibn al-Ṣumayfiʿ and al-Aʿmash for initial ḍamma. Abū al-Baqāʾ gives alternate derivations; al-Ālūsī reports plural and sound-change analyses, then objections that deletion of tāʾ and the pattern lack license. Ibn ʿAṭiyya explains the wording as a blurred weeping gaze and links Shurayḥ's court comparison. [11 notes: IBNJINNI-MUHTASAB(الحسن); KASHSHAF-FULL(الحسن); IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو هريرة); RAZI-FULL(ابن جني); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء,الحسن البصري); ALUSI-FULL(الحسن); SAMIN-DURR-; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 12:16/q05/p4 A ḍamma reading derives from ʿashwah, darkness or a venture in confusion. — Al-Samīn reports al-Ḥasan's derivation from darkness. Al-Ālūsī reports a proposed plural of ʿashwah that stresses the brothers' deceit. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR(الحسن البصري); ALUSI-FULL]
- 12:16/q05/p5 One proposed construal takes the variant word as a flame, suggesting haste and glee. — Al-Ālūsī reports this alongside the darkness or confusion explanation. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 12:16/q06 How is ʿishāʾ parsed in the sentence?  [عِشَآءًۭ · grammar]
turns on: The grammatical category depends on whether the word is read as the ordinary time expression or an alternative plural form.
- 12:16/q06/p1 It is an adverb of time modifying the brothers' arrival. — Al-Zajjāj states the time adverb analysis; al-Ṭabrisī and Abū Ḥayyān also give it. Al-Samīn explicitly prefers it over a plural noun reading. [4 notes: ZAJJAJ; TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 12:16/q06/p2 In an alternative reading it is a plural noun, with competing derivations. — Abū al-Baqāʾ, reported by al-Samīn, offers morphological derivations including loss of the feminine ending of ʿashāh; Abū Ḥayyān reports plural and diminutive possibilities for al-Ḥasan's forms. Al-Samīn rejects the plural as the proper parse of the received wording. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR-]

## 12:16/q07 Why did the brothers return in the evening?  [عِشَآءًۭ · interpretation]
turns on: The notes propose concealment and persuasive effect, travel time, or prevention of a search as the motive for arriving late.
- 12:16/q07/p1 Darkness made their false excuse easier and concealed signs of their deception. — Al-Thaʿlabī gives both ease of presenting the excuse and deliberate delay to deceive. Al-Wāḥidī says darkness strengthened their apparent innocence; al-Baghawī attributes ease of excuse to rhetoricians. Al-Ṭabrisī says they could mislead Jacob more boldly. Ibn al-Jawzī and al-Nasafī stress boldness or concealment; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Biqāʿī cite the proverb about seeking favors at night and avoiding apologies in daylight. Al-Biqāʿī adds Jacob could not read their faces. Al-Kawāshī, via Bursevī, says the excuse would seem more emphatic. Al-Ālūsī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī connect evening with staging the deception and winning trust; a reported view in al-Ālūsī says darkness hid the joy of achieving their aim. [12 notes: THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(الكواشي); ALUSI-FULL(قيل); TABATABAI]
- 12:16/q07/p2 They could not reach home earlier. — Al-Ālūsī presents this as one possibility alongside staged deception. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 12:16/q07/p3 A late return reduced Jacob's chance to search for Joseph. — Iṣlāḥī says they probably returned late at night for this purpose. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 12:16/q08 Was this the evening of the same day or a later day?  [عِشَآءًۭ · reports]
turns on: Whether the report of Joseph's three days in the well and Judah's food is taken to date the brothers' return.
- 12:16/q08/p1 It was the evening of the same day. — Al-Ālūsī reports that some understand the verse this way. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 12:16/q08/p2 It was a later evening. — Al-Ālūsī reports an inference from the account that Joseph remained in the well three days while Judah brought him food. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 12:16/q09 What did Jacob do when his sons arrived and told him about Joseph?  [وَجَآءُوٓ يَبْكُونَ · reports]
turns on: The reports differ on Jacob's first question and whether he wept, demanded the shirt, or fainted afterward.
- 12:16/q09/p1 He heard their weeping, became alarmed, asked whether the flock was harmed, and then asked where Joseph was. — Al-Ṭabarī's al-Suddī adds that Jacob demanded Joseph's shirt, saw its blood and wept. Al-Baghawī and al-Kashshāf report hearing cries or wailing before he came out. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says he wept, cried aloud and requested the shirt after the sheep question. Al-Rāzī, al-Nasafī and the Study Quran report the flock or pasture question; al-Bayḍāwī reports alarm and the question about Joseph. The accounts vary in those added actions. [8 notes: TAB-FULL(السدي); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(Bg, Q, Ṭ)]
- 12:16/q09/p2 After the flock question Jacob collapsed, and Judah checked him for life. — Abū Ḥayyān reports Judah lamenting that they lost their brother and killed their father with grief. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 12:16/q09/p3 Jacob asked whether some affliction or wage had struck them and then asked where Joseph was. — Al-Ālūsī reports this wording of the initial question rather than a question about the flock. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 12:16/q09/p4 On hearing Joseph was dead Jacob fainted and remained unconscious until the next morning. — The Study Quran reports that the brothers poured water on him. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(Q)]

## 12:16/q10 How is yabkūn parsed?  [يَبْكُونَ · grammar]
- 12:16/q10/p1 It is a circumstantial clause describing the brothers as they came. — Al-Naḥḥās states this directly. Al-Ṭabrisī calls Jacob's crying circumstantial in his parsing. Al-Samīn says it means they came weeping; Bursevī likewise makes it circumstantial and glosses it in Persian as deliberate weeping. [4 notes: NAHHAS; TABRISI; BURSEVI; SAMIN-DURR+]

## 12:16/q11 Were the brothers' tears genuine?  [يَبْكُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether visible crying represented real remorse over Joseph or a performance supporting the wolf story.
- 12:16/q11/p1 Their weeping was staged to make a false account seem true. — Ibn Abī Ḥātim's al-Ḥasan calls them lying sinners. Al-Wāḥidī says they used it to seem innocent of the alleged wolf attack. Al-Ṭabrisī, al-Bayḍāwī, Bursevī and al-Ālūsī say the crying was feigned; Bursevī's Persian gloss calls it deliberate, and his quotation from the Mathnawī says the displayed grief concealed envy and malice. Al-Biqāʿī defines crying as tears flowing in sadness yet says that tears can be feigned. Al-Ālūsī says liars imitate sorrow, and another reported explanation says they hid joy at success in darkness. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says they wept to disguise their plot and gain trust. [11 notes: IBNABIHATIM(الحسن); WAHIDI-BASIT; TABRISI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(al-Qushayrī)]
- 12:16/q11/p2 They may have sincerely regretted harming Joseph while hiding the reason for their grief. — Al-Qushayrī allows genuine regret, though they blamed a wolf when speaking to Jacob. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]

## 12:16/q12 Do tears establish that a person's claim is true?  [يَبْكُونَ · interpretation]
- 12:16/q12/p1 Tears do not establish truth, and a judge must decide by the prescribed standard or truth. — Al-Wāḥidī reports Mujālid from al-Shaʿbī: Shurayḥ answered a crying woman by citing the brothers. Al-Kashshāf, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Rāzī, Bursevī and al-Ālūsī recount forms of this court example; al-Rāzī calls the brothers oppressive liars, while al-Kashshāf and Bursevī state the duty to follow the prescribed Sunna. Al-Ṭabrisī and al-Biqāʿī state the general inference. Al-Nasafī and Abū Ḥayyān transmit al-Aʿmash's warning not to believe a weeper; al-Ālūsī gives his stronger quip that even one's father should not be believed. Al-Qushayrī and the Study Quran report that a complete hypocrite can control tears at will. Al-Ālūsī cites a couplet proposing tears crossing the cheek as a way to distinguish genuine from imitated weeping. [14 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(مجالد عن الشعبي); KASHSHAF-FULL(الشعبي); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL(شريح); NASAFI-FULL(الأعمش); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الأعمش); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(شريح); ALUSI-FULL(الأعمش,الشعبي); QUSHAYRI; STUDYQURAN(al-Qushayrī)]

## 12:16/q13 What spiritual condition is shown by a liar's ability to weep at will?  [يَبْكُونَ · inward]
- 12:16/q13/p1 It is a sign of divine abandonment or complete hypocrisy. — Al-Qushayrī calls the enabling of a liar's voluntary tears God's abandonment and reports that fully developed hypocrisy lets a person command his eyes; the Study Quran repeats the hypocrisy report. [3 notes: QUSHAYRI; STUDYQURAN(al-Qushayrī)]

## 12:16/q14 What is omitted in the narrative transition to the brothers' return?  [وَجَآءُوٓ · rhetoric]
turns on: The two reconstructions supply different unspoken stages of the surrounding story.
- 12:16/q14/p1 Jacob granted the brothers' request and sent Joseph with them. — Abū Ḥayyān says this connective clause is omitted because context supplies it. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 12:16/q14/p2 The brothers arrived without Joseph, Jacob asked about him, and they gave their wolf claim. — Abū Ḥayyān supplies this unspoken exchange from the context after their arrival. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 12:16/q15 How is the answer to the preceding 'when they took him away' construction analyzed?  [* · grammar]
turns on: Whether an answer is omitted or an extra wāw permits a later explicit clause to serve as the answer.
- 12:16/q15/p1 The answer is omitted and recoverable from context. — Al-Ṭabrisī says the grave plot they intended is understood after the oath; he reports the Basran rejection of an inserted wāw for lack of evidence or analogy. Abū Ḥayyān reports several Basran estimates for the omitted answer, citing discussions of 37:103 and 39:73. [3 notes: TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 12:16/q15/p2 The answer is explicit in the later speech or 'We inspired him,' with wāw treated as additional. — Al-Ṭabrisī reports the Kūfan analysis of the wāw in 'and they agreed' as inserted and says Kūfans cite poetry; Abū Ḥayyān reports an explicit-answer analysis allowing an extra conjunction in Kūfan usage. Basrans argue against the insertion. [3 notes: TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 12:16/q16 How does the following speech continue without a conjunction?  [* · rhetoric]
- 12:16/q16/p1 An implied question, 'What did they say?', is answered by 'O our father.' — Al-Burhān says the apparent sequence is a resumption answering the hearer's imagined question after their arrival crying. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 12:16/q17 What other nearby circumstantial clauses and attachments are noted?  [* · grammar]
- 12:16/q17/p1 'We were racing' is circumstantial, while 'they did not realize' may attach to the promise or revelation. — Al-Ṭabrisī gives both possible attachments for the latter clause while parsing the neighboring account. [1 notes: TABRISI]

## 12:16/q18 How can recoverable omission and oblique expression serve the passage's style?  [* · rhetoric]
- 12:16/q18/p1 A recoverable object can be omitted for graceful, balanced, deliberately indirect speech. — Ibn Jinnī illustrates object omission with 'two women watering' in 28:23, where their camels are understood, and with poetry. He cites Dhū al-Rumma on speech avoiding both rambling and excessive brevity. He interprets 'the best speech is laḥnan' as deliberate obliqueness rather than grammatical error, supported by the Prophetic phrase 'more adept in his case.' [3 notes: IBNJINNI-MUHTASAB]

## 12:16/q19 What wider point does the Qur'anic Joseph story illustrate?  [* · interpretation]
- 12:16/q19/p1 God directs human affairs beyond what people can fathom. — Asad contrasts the Qur'anic story with Genesis' romantic account and cites 2:216: people can dislike what benefits them and love what harms them. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES]
