# 102:2: 22 questions

## 102:2/q01 What does ḥattā make the endpoint of the rivalry?  [حَتَّىٰ · grammar]
turns on: Whether ḥattā limits the duration of distraction or the extent of what rivals count, and whether a grave visit itself changes them.
- 102:2/q01/p1 The distraction continues through life and ends only at death and burial. — Al-Samīn makes ḥattā the limit of alhākum; Ibn ʿĀshūr says no further action follows death. Elmalılı says death ends competition; Qatada's tribes die off one by one. Zayd b. Aslam transmits in a mursal report the Prophet's explanation that rivalry distracts until death arrives and people visit the graves. [27 notes: SAMIN-DURR; MUQATIL; TAB; TAB-FULL(قتادة); WAHIDI-BASIT(صاحب النظم); WAHIDI-QT; QUSHAYRI; RAZI; QURTUBI(قتادة); IBNKATHIR(قتادة); IBNKATHIR-FULL(قتادة); BURSEVI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI; ELMALILI; ITQAN(زيد بن أسلم); CORPUSCORANICUM+]
- 102:2/q01/p2 The rivalry extends even to counting or praising the dead at the graves; the endpoint is included in the distraction. — Al-Bursawī, al-Ālūsī, and Elmalılı explicitly include the endpoint. Ibn ʿĀshūr instead connects ḥattā to the utmost object counted, graves themselves. [14 notes: FARRA; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; QUSHAYRI; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ELMALILI+]
- 102:2/q01/p3 The visit itself may end worldly greed by awakening the visitor. — Elmalılı allows this as a literal construal, while warning that reflection is not universal. [2 notes: ELMALILI]

## 102:2/q02 Why is 'you visited' in the past tense?  [زُرْتُمُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the past form reports an actual past visit, vividly states a certain future, or addresses people from a later time.
- 102:2/q02/p1 The certain future is expressed as already completed. — Ibn ʿĀshūr compares 16:1; al-Ālūsī and Elmalılı also allow the certainty of death. [5 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 102:2/q02/p2 The address includes people already dead or treats earlier generations as examples for the living. — Al-Rāzī compares the Qur'anic address about earlier prophet-killers in 2:61; al-Ālūsī treats ancestral deaths as the listeners' own. [6 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 102:2/q02/p3 The addressees are already near death, so the grave is spoken of as reached. — Al-Rāzī gives this reply to the objection that future death cannot be past. [3 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL]
- 102:2/q02/p4 God addresses unbelievers on Judgment Day, after their grave visit. — Abū Muslim proposes this; al-Ālūsī calls it astonishing, and Elmalılı says the next warning of future knowledge better fits this life or the grave. [2 notes: RAZI(أبو مسلم); ALUSI(أبو مسلم)-; ALUSI-FULL(أبو مسلم)-; ELMALILI(Ebû Müslim)-]
- 102:2/q02/p5 The completed form presents a past tribal grave visit. — Paret takes the perfect as evidence of a historical episode; Corpus Coranicum favors the general gnomic statement. [0 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM-]

## 102:2/q03 What does zurtum mean in Arabic usage?  [زُرْتُمُ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the phrase retains ordinary physical visiting, serves as the attested death idiom, or invokes the root's wider senses.
- 102:2/q03/p1 It means visiting or seeing a place without remaining there. — Al-Jishumī likens a visit to going to water; Islahi cites a Ḥamāsī poem about revisiting one's land. Bint al-Shāṭi traces z-w-r to inclination and notes Qur'anic uses of ziyārah and tazāwar. [5 notes: JISHUMI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Ḥam āsī poet); BINTSHATI]
- 102:2/q03/p2 In poetry, visiting graves is an idiom for dying. — Al-Wāḥidī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, al-Ālūsī, Elmalılı and Bint al-Shāṭi cite Jarīr on Abū Mālik; al-Zamakhsharī also cites a year-end line, and Elmalılı cites al-Akhṭal. [13 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(جرير); KASHSHAF; RAZI(جرير); RAZI-FULL; ALUSI(جرير); ALUSI-FULL(الشاعر,جرير); ELMALILI(Ahtal,Cerîr); BINTSHATI(الطبري)]
- 102:2/q03/p3 The root can also express deviation or falsehood. — Al-Jishumī notes zūr as forged handwriting; Bint al-Shāṭi relates the sensory idea of turning and cites 18:17, 22:30, 25:4, 25:72 and 58:2. [3 notes: JISHUMI; BINTSHATI]

## 102:2/q06 What does al-maqābir denote and how is it formed?  [ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · meaning]
- 102:2/q06/p1 It is the plural of maqbara or maqbarah, a burial ground containing many graves. — Al-Naḥḥās notes singular vowel variants and Hijazi usage; al-Qurṭubī distinguishes qubūr as plural of qabr and cites poetry and Abū Saʿīd al-Maqburī's name. Ibn ʿĀshūr specifies land with many graves. Al-Qurṭubī distinguishes qabartu, 'I buried', from aqbartu, 'I ordered burial' (80:21). ## 102:2/q07 Why use al-maqābir rather than qubūr?  [ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the plural carries merely a lexical place sense or a distinctive collective image.
- 102:2/q07/p1 It evokes a vast collective cemetery across ages and social classes, fitting the end of accumulation. — Bint al-Shāṭi and her Iʿjāz work prefer this semantic breadth over sound alone; the cadence also fits takāthur. Islahi notes the rhyme and the pity of the compressed journey to death. Al-Qurṭubī and Bint al-Shāṭi say maqābir occurs only here; Bint al-Shāṭi contrasts five uses of qubūr and singular qabr at 9:84. [6 notes: BINTSHATI; BINTSHATI-IJAZ; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BINTSHATI+; BINTSHATI-]
- 102:2/q07/p2 It is treated simply as another word for graves or burial places. — Bint al-Shāṭi argues this misses the collective setting and the swift movement from rivalry to death. [2 notes: QURTUBI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; BINTSHATI-]
- 102:2/q07/p3 Al-maqābir is preferred to al-qubūr for its verbal harmony with the preceding takāthur. — Bint al-Shāṭi's Iʿjāz account names verbal harmony in Sūrat al-Takāthur as the reason for this choice (102:1). [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ+]

## 102:2/q09 Was the sūra revealed in Mecca or Medina?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether the reported Ansar setting and related reports outweigh the common Meccan attribution.
- 102:2/q09/p1 It is Meccan, commonly dated after al-Kawthar. — Ibn Kathīr identifies it as Meccan; Abū Ḥayyān reports broad exegetical agreement, with al-Bukhārī as an exception. Bint al-Shāṭi gives the common sixteenth place in revelation. [5 notes: IBNKATHIR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); ASAD-NOTES; BINTSHATI; STUDYQURAN]
- 102:2/q09/p2 It is Medinan. — Al-Ālūsī prefers this from Ibn Burayda's Ansar occasion, a report from Ubayy, and ʿAlī's grave-punishment report; al-Bukhārī and a minority are also cited for Madinan attribution. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); STUDYQURAN; ALUSI-FULL(أبو بريدة)+]

## 102:2/q10 Does the verse point to punishment in the grave?  [زُرْتُمُ ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · theology]
- 102:2/q10/p1 Yes: the warning of knowledge upon reaching the graves implies punishment there. — Al-Ṭabarī and al-Naḥḥās infer this; ʿAlī reportedly says Muslims doubted grave punishment until this sūra or its coming warning was revealed. Ibn Kathīr reports al-Tirmidhī grading ʿAlī's report gharīb. Al-Qurṭubī gives a view that they see God's punishment there; Elmalılı says hoarders awaken after losing their possessions. ## 102:2/q11 What conduct does the verse condemn when rivalry distracts people?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Which goods are being pursued and whether the competition diverts from what truly matters.
- 102:2/q11/p1 Worldly boasting and accumulation divert people from obedience, faith, reflection and preparation for the afterlife. — Al-Ṭabarī names wealth and numbers; al-Rāzī ranks knowledge and virtue above bodily and external goods, citing Abraham's prayer in 26:83 and 57:20 on worldly rivalry. The range includes wealth, kin, allies, armies and pleasure. Ibn ʿĀshūr specifies neglect of Qur'anic evidence and Islam's call; Asad extends the critique to consumerism, power, social disorientation and environmental damage. In a mursal report transmitted by Zayd b. Aslam, the Prophet specifies obedience as what rivalry in increase distracts people from. [61 notes: TAB-FULL; NAHHAS; THALABI(النبي); QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT; KASHSHAF; RAZI(أبو مسلم,الزبير); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI(قيل); QASHANI; IBNKATHIR(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNKATHIR-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ITQAN(زيد بن أسلم)]
- 102:2/q11/p2 Competition in knowledge, virtue and good deeds may be praiseworthy, whereas the familiar worldly rivalry is condemned. — Al-Rāzī says the definite article identifies the blameworthy sort; competition in obedience may invite imitation, as 93:11 suggests. His example of al-ʿAbbās, Shayba and ʿAlī over good acts is linked to 9:19. [3 notes: RAZI]

## 102:2/q12 What is the effect of leaving the object of distraction unstated?  [* · rhetoric]
- 102:2/q12/p1 The omission widens and intensifies the rebuke to every religious duty displaced by rivalry. — Al-Rāzī lists remembrance, obligations, recommended worship, knowledge, reflection and contemplation; al-Bayḍāwī says the suppressed object is religious concerns, and al-Ālūsī says the ambiguity magnifies blame. Zayd b. Aslam's mursal report gives the Prophet's expansion of the unstated object as obedience. [7 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ITQAN(زيد بن أسلم)]

## 102:2/q13 How does this verse relate to the preceding and following warnings?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the note explains the lead-in from al-Qāriʿah or the transition to this sūra's following threat.
- 102:2/q13/p1 It recalls al-Qāriʿah: worldly numbers and wealth distract from the deeds weighed at Judgment. — Al-Rāzī and Ibn al-Zubayr connect preparation for the Calamity (101:1); Islahi says only deeds count in 101:6–11. Bint al-Shāṭi allows a shared warning tone in muṣḥaf order but rejects a revelation chronology link, since the sūras are separated by thirteen in that chronology. Elmalılı urges reflection on scales and destinations. [7 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BIQAI(أبو جعفر ابن الزبير); ELMALILI; BINTSHATI(النيسابوري); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 102:2/q13/p2 The clause leads to the next 'No indeed' and its afterlife threat. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says kallā rejects their accumulation, sawfa guarantees eventual knowledge, and the omitted object is its evil outcome; Elmalılı also reads the coming rebuke as a warning of what heedlessness will bring. [5 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ELMALILI; STUDYQURAN]

## 102:2/q14 What did the Prophet say endures when death ends rivalry?  [* · reports]
turns on: Which Prophetic saying is used to expose what remains valuable at death.
- 102:2/q14/p1 One's wealth is only what one consumes, wears out, or gives away in charity. — This saying follows recitation in al-Ṭabarī; al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, al-Rāzī and al-Bursawī cite versions. Al-Thaʿlabī's Ibn ʿAbbās report defines takāthur as wrongful accumulation and withholding and names five blessings: food, drink, sleep, shade and health. ## 102:2/q15 What purpose should an actual grave visit serve?  [زُرْتُمُ ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · interpretation]

- 102:2/q15/p1 It should remind visitors of death and the Hereafter, soften the heart and turn them toward good deeds. — The Prophet first forbade visits then permitted them for admonition in reports from ʿAlī, Abū Saʿīd, Ibn Masʿūd and Abū Hurayra; al-Tirmidhī grades Burayda's reminder report ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ. - 102:2/q15/p2 Visits made for boasting, display, or idle touring reverse that purpose and fall under the rebuke. — Al-Rāzī calls their worldly attachment a reversal; ʿAlī says ancestral corpses should prompt humility. ## 102:2/q16 Are grave visits permitted or recommended?  [زُرْتُمُ ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · law]
turns on: How the prohibition, later permission and this verse's rebuke apply to different motives for visiting.
- 102:2/q16/p1 Visits for remembrance are permitted, and some jurists call them recommended; the Prophet's later permission followed an earlier prohibition. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān cite the prophetic change. Elmalılı cites Dürr-i Muhtār for permissibility, including women; several Hanafi manuals recommend visits, and Radd al-Muḥtār recommends weekly visits on specified days. Elmalılı says the nonmutawātir report clarifies the verse's scope but cannot abrogate or restrict it. [29 notes: IBNATIYYA(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNATIYYA-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); RAZI(النبي ﷺ,رسول الله); QURTUBI(أبو هريرة,ابن مسعود,بريدة,بعض أهل العلم); QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم عن ابن مسعود,بريدة,بعض أهل العلم); ABUHAYYAN(رسول الله); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الرسول); ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); ELMALILI(Dürr-i Muhtâr,Ebû Hanîfe,Redd-i Muhtâr,İmdâd, Müctebâ, Bahr ve Redd-i Muhtâr)]
- 102:2/q16/p2 The verse itself rebukes boastful visits, leaving reflective visits outside its condemnation. — Elmalılı defends this distinction against what he considers an overbroad reading of al-Ālūsī; he adds that promoting literal visits as the verse's central aim conflicts with the following rebuke. [9 notes: ELMALILI(Âlûsî); ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); ELMALILI+]

## 102:2/q17 May women visit graves?  [زُرْتُمُ ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · law]
turns on: Whether the curse precedes general permission and how grief, age and mixed gatherings affect visits.
- 102:2/q17/p1 The later general permission includes women as well as men. — Some scholars treat the curse report as predating permission; Dürr-i Muhtār permits women to visit. Al-Qurṭubī permits women when separated from men. [7 notes: QURTUBI(بعض أهل العلم); QURTUBI-FULL(بعض أهل العلم); ELMALILI(Dürr-i Muhtâr)]
- 102:2/q17/p2 Women’s visits are disapproved or restricted, especially where grief or mixed gathering risks harm. — Al-Tirmidhī reports a curse on frequent female visitors and grades it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ; others cite women's lesser patience and greater grief. Al-Qurṭubī allows older women but not young women going out, while allowing any age when separated from men and forbidding tempting mixed gatherings. [9 notes: QURTUBI(أبو هريرة,بعضهم); QURTUBI-FULL(أبو عيسى,أبو هريرة,بعضهم)]

## 102:2/q18 What should a visitor do at a grave?  [زُرْتُمُ ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · law]
- 102:2/q18/p1 Visit with a present heart; greet, pray, recite and reflect, while respecting the graves. — Al-Qurṭubī prohibits walking or sitting on graves and says to approach known graves from the front. Elmalılı cites the Prophet at Baqīʿ, manuals recommending greeting, standing for prayer, reciting available Qur'an and sometimes approaching from the feet; reports vary about where the Prophet stood. He warns against asking the dead for help and urges asking God for both living and dead. [18 notes: QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; ELMALILI(Fethu’l-Kadîr,Şerh-i Lübâb)]

## 102:2/q19 Can acts' reward be dedicated to the dead or the Prophet?  [زُرْتُمُ ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · law]
turns on: Whether reward transfer requires a specific permission in the Prophet's case.
- 102:2/q19/p1 Rewards of recitation, prayer, fasting and charity can reach the deceased. — Ibn ʿĀbidīn allows transfer of rewards; Tatarhāniyya recommends intending charity for all believers without diminishing one's reward. Elmalılı allows recitation and dedication; al-Qurṭubī names prayer and Qur'an as benefits to the dead. [5 notes: QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; ELMALILI(Tatarhâniyye,İbn Âbidîn)]
- 102:2/q19/p2 Dedicating Qur'an recitation's reward to the Prophet requires his specific permission, so blessings and wasīla are preferable. — Elmalılı reports Ibn Taymiyya's proposed restriction; al-Subkī and others reject it, saying no special permission is needed and citing Ibn ʿUmar. [1 notes: ELMALILI(Hafız İbn Teymiyye); ELMALILI(Sübkî ve daha diğerleri)-]

## 102:2/q20 How should remembrance of the dead affect one’s conduct?  [* · interpretation]
- 102:2/q20/p1 The dead's deeds are theirs; ancestral pride is pointless, while their good example can inspire righteous action. — Elmalılı cites 2:141, a report that people are resurrected with those they love, and 2:165 and 3:31 for love directed to God. He says visits can preserve exemplars and awaken preparation for resurrection and judgment; asking the dead for aid is especially unfitting. [12 notes: ELMALILI(İbn Âbidîn); STUDYQURAN(ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib)]

## 102:2/q21 Is the opening an assertion or an interrogative rebuke?  [* · readings]
turns on: Whether the opening carries an explicit interrogative hamza or reads as a statement.
- 102:2/q21/p1 It is a declarative rebuke. — Al-Bursawī calls the clause declarative and connects ḥattā to the continued distraction. [2 notes: BURSEVI; RAZI]
- 102:2/q21/p2 It may be read with an interrogative hamza, a question that asserts the rebuke. — Ibn ʿAbbās reads the opening interrogatively; al-Rāzī cites analogous interrogative readings such as aʾ-andhartahum and aʾ-idhā kunnā ʿiẓāman. [3 notes: KASHSHAF(ابن عباس); RAZI]

## 102:2/q22 How is this sūra’s exceptional merit explained?  [* · sciences]
- 102:2/q22/p1 Its thousand-verse merit has been explained as a sixth of the Qur'an because it addresses return to God. — Ibn al-Milq uses roughly 6,200 verses and al-Ghazālī's six aims; al-Ālūsī says the matter exceeds this calculation. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(ناصر الدين بن الميلق)]
