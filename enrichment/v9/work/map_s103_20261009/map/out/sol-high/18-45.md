# 18:45: 25 questions

## 18:45/q01 Whom does the command to give this parable address?  [وَٱضْرِبْ لَهُم · referent]
turns on: Whether the address is tied to the wealthy opponents in the preceding passage, Meccans more broadly, deniers of the world's end, or all people.
- 18:45/q01/p1 Arrogant wealthy people who preferred their possessions to poor believers. — Muqatil identifies Meccan unbelievers; al-Tabari, al-Tha'labi, al-Qurtubi and al-Tabrisi connect them with the demand to expel devoted poor believers (18:28). Al-Razi specifies wealthy polytheists boasting of wealth and supporters. Islahi describes people blinded by worldly love; Kuranyolu adds pride in sons, denial of monotheism and the claim that only worldly life exists (68:14–15; 45:24). Al-Asamm connects the command to the preceding passage. [10 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; THALABI; JISHUMI(الأصم); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; TABRISI(قيل); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 18:45/q01/p2 The Prophet's people or the people of Mecca generally. — Ibn Abbas says the Prophet's people; al-Asamm relates an identification with the people of Mecca. [2 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); JISHUMI(الأصم)]
- 18:45/q01/p3 People denying that worldly life will end. — Al-Asamm relates this identification among the alternative addressees. [1 notes: JISHUMI(الأصم)]
- 18:45/q01/p4 Everyone living in this world. — Al-Biqa'i says the image applies to worldly life for everyone. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 18:45/q02 What condition of worldly life does the parable portray?  [مَّثَلَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا كَمَآءٍ فَأَصْبَحَ هَشِيمًۭا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the image chiefly represents rapid loss of beauty, the coexistence of pleasure and harm, or the world's remarkable condition.
- 18:45/q02/p1 Its beauty and flourishing rapidly give way to decay and disappearance. — Muqatil connects the fading world with the Hereafter's arrival. Yahya, al-Zajjaj, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, al-Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, al-Tabrisi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Razi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Ibn Kathir, al-Biqa'i, Bursevi, al-Alusi, Elmalili and al-Tabataba'i trace fresh rain-fed growth into dry windblown fragments. Al-Zajjaj, al-Kashshaf, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi and al-Alusi say the vanished growth is as though it never existed. Ibn Ashur maps youth, prosperity, novelty and adornment onto rain, growth and dispersed loss; the shared turn from good to bad makes an abstract state visible. Al-Razi compares 22:5, where earth stirs and swells. Kashifi compares youth to a tree dried by death and hopes blown away. Islahi cites 57:20 on amusement, adornment and rivalry in wealth and children. [33 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; ZAJJAJ; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(الكاشفي); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(الراغب); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 18:45/q02/p2 Its blessings are inseparable from adversity, and joy from grief. — Al-Mawardi gives this as an alternative purpose. Al-Qushayri says worldly pleasures conceal sorrow, sweetness bitterness, gifts trials, and promises fail. Ibn al-Jawzi lists changing fortunes and grief paired with joy as an alternative reading. [3 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD]
- 18:45/q02/p3 The world has a remarkable condition worthy of a parable. — Al-Baydawi allows the world's extraordinary quality as the object of comparison; al-Alusi reports the command as explaining that remarkable condition. [2 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 18:45/q03 Why is worldly life compared specifically to water?  [ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا كَمَآءٍ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether water's own properties supply the likeness or the whole plant cycle does.
- 18:45/q03/p1 Both change location and condition and cannot be retained. — The sages reported by al-Tha'labi and al-Qurtubi say neither remains fixed; al-Burhan adds that water slips from a closed hand. [3 notes: THALABI(الحكماء); QURTUBI-FULL(الحكماء); BURHAN]
- 18:45/q03/p2 Both eventually disappear. — The sages in al-Tha'labi say water vanishes as worldly life ends; al-Qurtubi transmits the same comparison. [2 notes: THALABI(الحكماء); QURTUBI-FULL(الحكماء)]
- 18:45/q03/p3 Contact with either brings unavoidable effects and trials. — The sages say one cannot enter water without becoming wet, just as worldly life exposes one to harms and trials. [2 notes: THALABI(الحكماء); QURTUBI-FULL(الحكماء)]
- 18:45/q03/p4 A sufficient amount benefits, while excess harms or destroys. — The sages in al-Tha'labi and al-Qurtubi contrast useful provision with damaging surplus; al-Burhan repeats this analogy. [3 notes: THALABI(الحكماء); QURTUBI-FULL(الحكماء); BURHAN]
- 18:45/q03/p5 The comparison is to the entire rain, growth, decline and dispersal scene. — Al-Burhan calls this the fuller comparison. Bursevi and al-Alusi explicitly reject restricting the likeness to water alone; Ibn Ashur analyzes the shared passage from prosperity to loss as a composite simile. [5 notes: BURHAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 18:45/q04 How is كَمَآءٍ attached grammatically?  [وَٱضْرِبْ كَمَآءٍ · grammar]
turns on: The valency of اضرِب and whether the kāf starts an independent explanation, supplies a second object, or modifies an omitted verbal noun.
- 18:45/q04/p1 It begins an explanatory clause with an omitted subject: worldly life is like water. — Al-Wahidi and Ibn Atiyya supply the omitted subject. Abu Hayyan and al-Samin transmit Ibn Atiyya's parse. Al-Alusi prefers this fresh explanation and objects that a second object of ḍaraba is awkward. Bursevi explains the comparison in these terms. [6 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية); SAMIN-DURR(ابن عطية); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 18:45/q04/p2 It is the second object of اضرِب: make the likeness resemble water. — Abu Hayyan prefers this parse. Al-Samin prefers it because ḍaraba can mean ṣayyara and credits al-Baqa with the proposal. Al-Baydawi also permits water as a second object. [3 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR+; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 18:45/q04/p3 It modifies an omitted verbal noun: strike a likeness in a manner like water. — Al-Hawfi assumes aḍrib takes one object and supplies ḍarban; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin report him. Al-Alusi rejects this analysis. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحوفي); SAMIN-DURR(الحوفي); ALUSI-FULL(الحوفي)-]

## 18:45/q05 What is the water sent from the sky?  [كَمَآءٍ أَنزَلْنَٰهُ مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ · referent]
turns on: The sources distinguish the water's identity from the range of meanings of السماء.
- 18:45/q05/p1 Rain. — Al-Tha'labi and al-Wahidi identify the water as rain. Al-Biqa'i says it descends by God's power at the needed time. Al-Tabataba'i says rain is named because other water sources begin with it. Al-Samin parses أَنزَلْنَٰهُ as an adjective of water. [5 notes: THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI; SAMIN-DURR]
- 18:45/q05/p2 Sky can mean a cloud or the direction above. — Bursevi gives both possible senses of heaven in the phrase. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 18:45/q06 What happens in فَٱخْتَلَطَ بِهِۦ نَبَاتُ ٱلْأَرْضِ, and what does ب do?  [فَٱخْتَلَطَ بِهِۦ نَبَاتُ ٱلْأَرْضِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the plants mix with one another because of rain or the water itself mixes into the plants, with a corresponding causal or participant sense of ب.
- 18:45/q06/p1 Plants become abundant and intertwine with each other because of the water; ب marks cause. — Al-Mawardi, al-Wahidi, al-Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Bursevi, al-Alusi and Ibn Ashur give the dense-growth account. Ibn Atiyya and Ibn Ashur explicitly call the bā' causal; Ibn Ashur denies that it makes ikhtalaṭ transitive. Al-Samin allows cause and cites al-Zamakhshari. Al-Razi says rain strengthens and entangles kinds of plants. Al-Biqa'i includes water mingling with soil and producing varied dense growth. [14 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 18:45/q06/p2 Water mingles with and saturates the plants until they flourish; ب can mark the other participant in mixing. — Al-Mawardi gives this alternative. Abu Ishaq in al-Wahidi says water penetrates the plants. Al-Asamm, the Kashshaf's alternative, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, al-Samin and al-Alusi describe saturation; al-Kashshaf, al-Razi and al-Samin say either of two mixed things can be grammatically described as mingling with the other. Al-Samin also allows transitivity for ب. [12 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); JISHUMI(الأصم); KASHSHAF-FULL(قيل); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 18:45/q07 Why does the wording make vegetation mix with water?  [فَٱخْتَلَطَ بِهِۦ نَبَاتُ ٱلْأَرْضِ · rhetoric]
- 18:45/q07/p1 The reversal emphasizes abundant water and luxuriant growth. — Al-Baydawi and al-Alusi observe that ordinary phrasing would make water mix with plants; the verse makes water seem the dominant mass. Al-Tabataba'i says the wording highlights rain's role in forming the vegetation. [3 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 18:45/q08 Why mention earth after sky?  [ٱلسَّمَآءِ ٱلْأَرْضِ · rhetoric]
- 18:45/q08/p1 The paired words create antithesis. — Ibn Ashur explicitly identifies the sky and earth contrast. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 18:45/q09 Does أَصْبَحَ mean became or literally became by morning?  [فَأَصْبَحَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether morning is an actual temporal detail or أَصْبَحَ functions as a general verb of becoming.
- 18:45/q09/p1 It means became, with no necessary morning time. — Ibn Atiyya cites a line by Rabi' b. Dab' for a resultant state; al-Samin cites poetry too. Al-Alusi says the verse describes eventual drying. Ibn Ashur calls this common usage; al-Tabataba'i denies a morning restriction. [7 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الربيع بن ضبع); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 18:45/q09/p2 It may mean that the plant became dry by morning. — Al-Samin notes that calamities commonly strike in the morning. Al-Alusi reports the literal-morning account as motivated by heavenly calamities arriving at night, but says only eventual drying is described. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 18:45/q10 What does the فَ before أَصْبَحَ convey?  [فَأَصْبَحَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the conjunction marks swift change directly or telescopes an unspoken period of growth.
- 18:45/q10/p1 It conveys how quickly flourishing growth vanishes. — Al-Alusi reports this reading of the immediate sequence. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 18:45/q10/p2 It resumes the account after an omitted period of flourishing. — Al-Alusi reports an explanatory fā' with a time interval understood. Al-Mawardi notes that the water's absence before drying is implicit for concision. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL]

## 18:45/q11 What does هَشِيمًا mean and how is it formed?  [هَشِيمًۭا · meaning]
- 18:45/q11/p1 Dry, broken or crumbled vegetation and other fragile matter. — Ibn Qutayba derives it from hashama, breaking, and relates Hashim's name. Al-Tha'labi transmits Ibn Abbas's dry, al-Dahhak's broken and al-Akhfash's crumbled glosses. Al-Mawardi says drying and breaking, with a poem comparing dead bodies to dried plants; water's disappearance is tacit. Al-Wahidi compares crumbling bread and Hashim's name. Al-Jishumi adds broken hollow things; al-Kashshaf gives hashimah as singular. Ibn Atiyya compares tharid and 54:31. Ibn al-Jawzi transmits al-Farra's formerly moist and al-Zajjaj's dry definitions. Al-Razi cites an anonymous line about crushing bread. Al-Qurtubi lists dry wood and extended uses for generosity, weakness, affection, camel milking and crumbled bread; Ibn al-Ziba'ra's verse and the account of Hashim feeding Mecca after famine explain his name. Abu Hayyan and al-Samin report the former-moist definition; al-Samin calls hashim the singular of hashimah. Al-Alusi permits intensive passive or plural of hashimah; Ibn Ashur gives fa'il with passive force; Bursevi says the root breaks something soft. [28 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; THALABI(ابن عباس,الأخفش,الضحاك); MAWARDI-FULL(الشاعر); WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن عباس والضحاك); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الفراء); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(عبد الله بن الزبعرى); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزجاج وابن قتيبة,الفراء); SAMIN-DURR(الزجاج وابن قتيبة); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(الراغب)]

## 18:45/q12 What action does تَذْرُوهُ describe?  [تَذْرُوهُ ٱلرِّيَٰحُ · meaning]
turns on: The glosses emphasize dispersal, turning motion, or lifting; al-Qurtubi says their senses are close.
- 18:45/q12/p1 The wind scatters, casts or blows the plant fragments away. — Al-Farra compares forms from dharā and adhrā and cites al-Mufaddal's poem about casting off; al-Majaz, Ibn Qutayba and al-Tabari give the basic scatter sense, while al-Tabari's anonymous line uses yadhruk for throwing someone from a mount. Abu Ubayda, al-Qutaybi and later transmitters gloss scattering and blowing. Al-Wahidi gives several dialect forms and dispersal; al-Jishumi says tadhriya scatters light objects in every direction. Ibn Atiyya cites 54:31; al-Qurtubi cites a Sibawayh and al-Farra verse about wind blowing a bird off course and says the glosses are close. Abu Hayyan notes dharā and adhrā as paired forms. Bursevi compares winnowing grain; Ibn Ashur says dharw is throwing into the air. [26 notes: FARRA; MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; THALABI(أبو عبيدة,القتيبي); WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(أبو عبيدة,القتيبي); IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو عبيدة وابن قتيبة وابن كيسان وابن عباس,سيبويه والفراء); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو عبيدة); SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(الراغب)]
- 18:45/q12/p2 The wind carries the fragments to and fro or turns and stirs them. — Ibn Abbas and Ibn Kaysan give turning and back-and-forth motion in al-Tha'labi; al-Baghawi reports Ibn Abbas's stirring. Al-Qurtubi treats these as close to scattering. Abu Hayyan, al-Alusi and al-Tabataba'i transmit the back-and-forth gloss; al-Biqa'i combines stirring with dispersal. Al-Nahhas transmits al-Farra's related adhraytu for turning a person on a camel. [9 notes: NAHHAS(الفراء); THALABI(ابن عباس,ابن كيسان); BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(أبو عبيدة وابن قتيبة وابن كيسان وابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن كيسان); BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(ابن كيسان); TABATABAI(الراغب)]
- 18:45/q12/p3 The wind lifts or raises the plant debris. — Al-Akhfash gives this gloss in al-Tha'labi, Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi; al-Wahidi includes lifting among the motions, and al-Samin also reports it. [6 notes: THALABI(الأخفش); WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الأخفش); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(الأخفش)]

## 18:45/q14 How is تَذْرُوهُ read?  [تَذْرُوهُ · readings]
turns on: Attested recitations use related wāw and yā' roots and different verb forms, with named readers and vowels varying among reports.
- 18:45/q14/p1 The common reading is تذروه from the wāw form. — Al-Nahhas labels it the common reading; al-Zajjaj says two other forms are possible in Arabic but not recited. Al-Farra and al-Majaz give variant verb forms with comparable scattering sense. [4 notes: NAHHAS(القراء العامة); ZAJJAJ; FARRA; MAJAZ]
- 18:45/q14/p2 A reading has تذريه, with forms derived from the alternate yāʾ or causative verb. — Al-Farra attributes tadhrihi al-rih to Abdullah; al-Nahhas adds a report from al-Kisa'i. Al-Tha'labi assigns scattering or lifting forms to Talha b. Musarrif; al-Jishumi calls Talha's form a dialect variant. Al-Wahidi and Abu Hayyan cite Ibn Mas'ud. Ibn Atiyya cites Ibn Abbas; al-Kashshaf reports Ibn Abbas's causative form. Ibn al-Jawzi lists Ubayy, Ibn Abbas and Ibn Abi Abla with raised tā', kasra on rā' and silent yā'; Ibn Mas'ud differs with fatḥa on tā'. Al-Samin separates Abdullah's yā' radical from Ibn Abbas's ḍamma tā' causative reading, perhaps derivable from either root. Al-Qurtubi gives Talha and Ibn Mas'ud; al-Alusi calls Ibn Mas'ud's form quadriliteral. [14 notes: FARRA; NAHHAS(القراء العامة); THALABI(طلحة بن مصرف); WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI(طلحة بن مصرف); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبي); QURTUBI-FULL(طلحة بن مصرف وابن عباس); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن مسعود); SAMIN-DURR(ابن عباس,عبد الله); ALUSI-FULL(ابن مسعود)]

## 18:45/q15 Is ٱلرِّيَٰحُ read in the plural or singular?  [ٱلرِّيَٰحُ · readings]
turns on: Reader attribution and the connotation of singular versus plural wind.

Qiraat-Er associates singular rih in classical usage with destructive wind. ## 18:45/q17 What does كَانَ convey about God's power?  [وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ مُّقْتَدِرًا · theology]
turns on: Whether past kāna states an eternal or precreation attribute, or can point to future power without a tense-shifting cue.
- 18:45/q17/p1 His power is timeless or has always existed, including before creation. — Al-Zajjaj prefers Sibawayh and al-Khalil: kāna describes timeless power, and Arabic past tense cannot mean future or present without a shifting particle. Al-Nahhas reports some saying always and prefers Sibawayh's explanation that observed power was already God's. Al-Wahidi reports al-Zajjaj that power was not newly acquired; Ibn Atiyya transmits Sibawayh's rationally recognized prehuman power and al-Hasan's precreation state. Al-Tabrisi follows al-Zajjaj and Sibawayh. Al-Biqa'i calls divine power eternal, not newly acquired. Al-Nahhas reports Abu Ishaq's rejection of an unsignalled future sense. [9 notes: NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق,قوم); WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن,سيبويه); TABRISI(الزجاج); BIQAI-FULL; NAHHAS(سيبويه)+; ZAJJAJ(سيبويه والخليل)+]
- 18:45/q17/p2 He was able to bring all things into being before they existed. — Al-Hasan gives the precreation explanation in al-Zajjaj, al-Wahidi and al-Tabrisi; Ibn Atiyya also reports him. It specifies the pre-existing scope of divine ability. [4 notes: ZAJJAJ(الحسن); WAHIDI-BASIT(الحسن); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن); TABRISI(الحسن)]
- 18:45/q17/p3 The past form may mean He will be or is and will be powerful. — Al-Zajjaj reports the kā'in wa-yakūn interpretation but argues it conflicts with ordinary tense usage. Al-Nahhas reports the future sense. Al-Tabrisi reports using past power to point toward future events. [2 notes: NAHHAS(قوم); TABRISI(قيل); ZAJJAJ(سيبويه والخليل)-; NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق)-]

## 18:45/q18 What does God's being مُقْتَدِرًا encompass here?  [وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍۢ مُّقْتَدِرًا · theology]
- 18:45/q18/p1 Power over all things, especially creating, sustaining, ending and restoring life. — Muqatil includes resurrection; al-Tabari applies power to ruining gardens and worldly wealth. Al-Zajjaj and al-Wahidi name origination and annihilation; al-Wahidi argues from God's producing and ending vegetation. Al-Kashshaf, al-Qurtubi, al-Nasafi, Ibn Kathir, Bursevi, al-Alusi and al-Biqa'i name creation and destruction; al-Qurtubi and al-Biqa'i include restoration or enlivening. Al-Razi adds nurturing before abolition. Al-Thalabi and al-Baghawi state universal power. Yahya and Elmalili conclude God can do all things. Islahi sees power in both producing and decaying worldly luxuriance. [22 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 18:45/q19 What work does the closing power clause do in the parable?  [وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍۢ مُّقْتَدِرًا · rhetoric]
- 18:45/q19/p1 It concludes from the plant's creation and decay to God's universal power. — Ibn Ashur calls it parenthetical: God produces things and their opposites and orders causes of decay after causes of persistence; its universal wording gives the conclusion. Abu Hayyan likewise says the scattering illustrates power over creation and extinction. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 18:45/q20 How does this parable relate to other Quranic parables and verses?  [* · links]
turns on: The cited verses illuminate different parts of the same growth-and-loss image.
- 18:45/q20/p1 It condenses or parallels the water-and-vegetation parable of 10:24. — Al-Wahidi calls this a shortened version and points to his fuller explanation in Yunus; Ibn al-Jawzi says the comparison is elaborated there. Bursevi calls it a condensation. Ibn Kathir also cites 10:24. [6 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 18:45/q20/p2 Other verses also picture temporary growth that withers or worldly adornment that passes. — Ibn Kathir cites 39:21 and 57:20 plus an authentic hadith calling the world sweet and green. Al-Alusi cites 87:4–5, where pasture becomes dark dry debris. Islahi uses 57:20 to explain worldly amusement and rivalry. Study Quran cites 39:21 and 57:20; al-Razi cites 22:5 for the rain-fed earth stirring and swelling. [5 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN; RAZI-FULL]

## 18:45/q21 How does the verse connect with the surrounding garden story and its aftermath?  [* · links]
turns on: The sources look either backward to the garden story or forward to the contrast with lasting deeds.
- 18:45/q21/p1 It extends the lesson of the ruined garden and the failure of pride in wealth. — Al-Tabari links the address to 18:28 and garden boasting to 18:36. Bursevi says the brother's admonition failed because the listener's heart hardened and ended in regret. Study Quran says verses 32–44 show dependence on divine beneficence and the garden's final barren plain. Al-Nahhas transmits Abu Ishaq's allowance of the preceding verse's ʿuqbā on the pattern of bushrā and a poetic tazlaq line from Sibawayh and al-Mufaddal; these are lexical details in the nearby garden context. [7 notes: TAB-FULL; BURSEVI; STUDYQURAN; NAHHAS(أبو إسحاق,سيبويه والمفضل)]
- 18:45/q21/p2 The next lesson contrasts passing ornaments with enduring righteous deeds. — Ibn Atiyya says righteous work remains in the afterlife while worldly beauty and power fade. Islahi says ornaments do not accompany people to the next world; Kuranyolu contrasts transient wealth and children with enduring righteous deeds. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 18:45/q22 What conduct does the parable call for toward worldly goods?  [مَّثَلَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the ethical emphasis is avoiding pride and misplaced reliance, limiting consumption, or using worldly life without attachment.
- 18:45/q22/p1 Do not boast in or rely on passing wealth; seek the enduring afterlife and righteous deeds. — Al-Tabari warns against pride in wealth and urges work for the Hereafter. Al-Razi says inevitable decline makes delight in worldly beauty unreasonable. Ibn Atiyya, Bursevi and al-Alusi turn the cycle toward reliance on lasting deeds rather than adornment; Bursevi uses Saadi's images of aging, a worm-eaten royal shroud and returning spring. Al-Biqa'i adds worldly toil and anxieties. Islahi says luxury, status and possessions engross the affluent; Kuranyolu says wealth and children pass and condemns using blessings for arrogance or pressure against the poor. [12 notes: TAB-FULL; RAZI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(الشيخ سعدي); ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 18:45/q22/p2 Use sufficient worldly provision with contentment, avoiding harmful excess. — The sages' water comparison distinguishes a useful measure from destructive surplus. Al-Qurtubi reports a saying to take from the world like still water, where little suffices and much intoxicates, and a Muslim report that success is submission, sufficiency and contentment. [4 notes: THALABI(الحكماء); QURTUBI-FULL(الحكماء,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 18:45/q22/p3 Worldly life can be gain for the discerning when used without attachment. — Wahb's account in Bursevi contrasts discerning gain with ignorant heedlessness and says prophets and saints lived in the world without clinging to it. [1 notes: BURSEVI(وهب)]

## 18:45/q23 Which prophetic sayings are cited about living in the world?  [ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا · reports]
turns on: The reports highlight sufficiency and contentment or the world's initial attractiveness.
- 18:45/q23/p1 Take from the world like still water: a little suffices and much intoxicates. — Al-Qurtubi reports this as the Prophet's reply to a man asking to be among the successful. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 18:45/q23/p2 Success belongs to one who submits, receives sufficiency and is made content. — Al-Qurtubi attributes this prophetic report to Muslim. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 18:45/q23/p3 The world is sweet and green. — Ibn Kathir mentions an authentic hadith alongside related Quranic parables. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL]

## 18:45/q24 What inward meaning is drawn from the rain and vegetation?  [كَمَآءٍ نَبَاتُ ٱلْأَرْضِ · inward]
turns on: The inward reports map the image to the lower self, the growth of faith, and the role of grace in spiritual ascent.
- 18:45/q24/p1 Divine care joins spirit to body, while prophetic teaching sows faith in the heart. — Bursevi makes water and earth stand for spirit and body; prophets and saints plant faith and monotheism in fertile hearts through invitation, nourished by the Law. He cites 14:24; the good word and action raise the spirit toward divine nearness (35:10). [2 notes: BURSEVI]
- 18:45/q24/p2 The spirit's outcome depends on God's grace and attraction. — Bursevi says God may leave the spirit in animal-like corporeality or draw it to the highest nearness, where angels prostrate. He cites Jami that years of a seeker's effort alone do not attain the beloved without attraction. [2 notes: BURSEVI(المولى الجامي)]
- 18:45/q24/p3 Worldly adornment is what the lower self continually inclines toward. — Muhammad b. al-Fadl, as transmitted by al-Sulami, calls the world the lower self's close companion, drawing it through inward and outward decoration. [1 notes: SULAMI(محمد بن الفضل)]

## 18:45/q25 How is مُقْتَدِرًا connected to the reported gloss of مُقِيت?  [مُّقْتَدِرًا · links]
- 18:45/q25/p1 A transmitted gloss gives مُقِيت the sense capable, echoing مُقْتَدِرًا here. — Bint al-Shati says muqit occurs only in 4:85 and its root relates to sustenance, alongside aqwāt in 41:10; she cites a reported Quraysh usage meaning qādir and a supporting line by al-Zubayr b. Abd al-Muttalib. [2 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ]
