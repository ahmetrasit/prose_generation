# 41:34: 21 questions

## 41:34/q01 What do the good deed and the evil deed denote?  [ٱلْحَسَنَةُ ٱلسَّيِّئَةُ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the nouns denote general moral classes, the religious opposition in context, particular ethical traits, or an inward contrast; some reported pairings differ in wording and attribution.
- 41:34/q01/p1 Good and evil conduct or deeds generally, differing in worth, effects, degree, and outcome. — Tabari relates believers' obedience to deniers who drown out the Quran (41:26). Baydawi names recompense and outcome; Biqai adds intention and effort; Elmalılı adds internal degrees; Ibn Ashur includes invitation and pardon; Islahi stresses preaching and the Hereafter. Alusi doubts that narrow reported pairings are intended. [12 notes: TAB-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî)]
- 41:34/q01/p2 Faith or Islam contrasts with unbelief, shirk, or disobedience. — Tabari states the final contrast as faith and obedience versus association and disobedience; Ibn Abbas is transmitted for faith versus shirk. Tabrisi reports Islam versus unbelief among several alternatives. [5 notes: TAB-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL]
- 41:34/q01/p3 The good is saying 'There is no god but God'; the evil is polytheism. — Ibn Abbas is named by Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, and Alusi for this precise declaration. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 41:34/q01/p4 The good and evil are rival invitations to monotheism and associationism. — Al-Kalbi is named by Abu Hayyan and Alusi; the contrast concerns calls addressed to others. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الكلبي); ALUSI-FULL(الكلبي)]
- 41:34/q01/p5 Forbearance is good and obscenity is evil. — Al-Dahhak is cited repeatedly; Qurtubi instead transmits knowledge versus obscenity from him, a distinct version. [4 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(الضحاك); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الضحاك); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الضحاك); ALUSI-FULL(الضحاك)]
- 41:34/q01/p6 Knowledge is good and obscenity is evil. — Qurtubi attributes this version to al-Dahhak. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الضحاك)]
- 41:34/q01/p7 Love of the Prophet or his family is good; hostility toward them is evil. — Ali is the reported authority; Abu Hayyan and Alusi include the Messenger himself alongside his family. [4 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); QURTUBI-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(علي); ALUSI-FULL(علي)]
- 41:34/q01/p8 Gentleness or tact is good and harshness is evil. — Ibn Isa says gentle accommodation; Qurtubi lists tact; Abu Hayyan lists leniency. Tabrisi reports this among other contrasting traits. [5 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عيسى); TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); ALUSI-FULL]
- 41:34/q01/p9 Patience is good and aversion, anger, or flight is evil. — Mawardi pairs patience with aversion; Ibn al-Jawzi transmits flight versus patience; other reports give patience versus anger. These are related examples but the flight report reverses the cited order. [5 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الماوردي); BAGHAWI-FULL(الفراء); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); ALUSI-FULL]
- 41:34/q01/p10 Pardon is good and retaliation or injury is evil. — Ibn Umayr names forgiveness against retaliation; Qurtubi and Tabrisi include this among illustrative pairs. [3 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عمير); TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 41:34/q01/p11 Good character contrasts with bad character and blameworthy traits. — Al-Baqli, reported by Bursevi, calls for replacing base traits with praiseworthy ones, especially forbearance. [1 notes: BURSEVI(البقلي)]
- 41:34/q01/p12 Taqiyya is good and disclosure is evil. — Tabrisi reports this from Abu Abd Allah. [1 notes: TABRISI(أبو عبد الله)]
- 41:34/q01/p13 Good arises from the heart and leads to paradise and angels; evil arises from the self and leads to hell and devils. — Qashani gives the inward origins and destinations of each. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 41:34/q02 What is the function of the second lā in the contrast?  [وَلَا ٱلسَّيِّئَةُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether lā is syntactically added for emphasis, supplies a reciprocal negation, or distinguishes instances within each generic noun; Samin disputes an objection that conflates the last with the first.
- 41:34/q02/p1 It is an extra particle reinforcing the denial that good and evil are equal. — Akhfash compares 57:29 and 75:1; Tabari reports Basran grammarians and cites 75:2 while recording an objection about 75:1; Zajjaj, al-Farra, and later commentators adopt or report emphasis. Samin notes that istawā needs two complements and compares 35:21 and 40:58. Alusi compares 35:21; Samarrai notes 35:19–22 and adds differences within each class. [16 notes: AKHFASH; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(الفراء); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT(النحاة)]
- 41:34/q02/p2 It meaningfully negates the reciprocal comparison: neither good equals evil nor evil equals good. — Akhfash illustrates with Abdullah and Zayd; Tabari says each side is unequal to the other; Ibn Atiyya and Ibn Ashur understand an ellipsis in both directions. Jishumi reports this as an alternative to emphasis. [6 notes: AKHFASH; TAB-FULL; JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 41:34/q02/p3 It meaningfully denies equality within the classes of good deeds and evil deeds, whose instances vary in degree. — Zamakhshari is reported by Samin; Bursevi cites al-Kashshaf and ranges from removing harm to greater good and from minor to major sin; Alusi says this yields answering the worse with the best; Elmalılı and Samarrai also see internal variation. Samin rejects the objection that this analysis still treats lā as extra. [6 notes: SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; SAMIN-DURR+; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ)-]

## 41:34/q03 Why use the abstract nouns for good and evil?  [ٱلْحَسَنَةُ ٱلسَّيِّئَةُ · rhetoric]
- 41:34/q03/p1 The nouns portray goodness and evil at their full extent and lead into ethical counsel. — Ibn Ashur contrasts the abstract nouns with nouns naming the doers. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 41:34/q04 How does the opening contrast connect to the surrounding passage?  [وَلَا تَسْتَوِى ٱلْحَسَنَةُ وَلَا ٱلسَّيِّئَةُ · links]
turns on: Whether 41:34 is attached chiefly to 41:33's praise or reaches back to the opponents' obstruction in 41:26; Ibn Ashur records both connections.
- 41:34/q04/p1 It continues the praise of the caller to God in 41:33 by contrasting the believer's good state and the disbeliever's evil. — Ibn Ashur compares 40:58; Tabrisi contrasts the opponents' disruption in 41:26 with Muhammad's call in 41:33, saying his practice confirms his message. Ibn Ashur also sees 41:30's divine promise exposing the gulf from the deniers. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; TABRISI]
- 41:34/q04/p2 It resumes the disbelievers' obstruction of Quran recitation and consoles the Prophet, preparing his gentle response. — Ibn Ashur points to 41:26 and 41:5; Asad identifies scurrilous objections and hostile criticism and compares 13:22; Kuranyolu's Razi cites the closed hearts and noise during recitation. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî)]
- 41:34/q04/p3 It gives the practical response to hostility that may confront a caller to God. — Abu Hayyan says to convey good gently and tactfully; Tabatabai says it follows the praise of inviting to God and teaches the best way to make an impact. Elmalılı makes the best call with righteous action the remedy; Islahi relates conduct to preaching's goals. [6 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; TABATABAI; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 41:34/q04/p4 The imperative is either the consequence of the contrast or the conclusion of the preceding argument; it is independent in either case. — Ibn Ashur states the two possible links and preserves its independent sentence status. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 41:34/q05 What should one do when told to repel with what is best?  [ٱدْفَعْ بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the command is read as a broad practice or a particular form of response; the greeting reports are treated by Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan as a beginning of the broader practice.
- 41:34/q05/p1 Answer injury, insult, anger, and ignorance with patience, forbearance, pardon, and kindness. — Ibn Abbas gives patience when angry, forbearance toward ignorance, pardon when wronged; Tabari compares 23:96. Razi says repeated restraint shames offenders; Umar says answer another's disobedience to God with one's own obedience. Abu Firas's poem praises continual pardon. Qurtubi transmits Ali's advice to Qanbar and two poems on silence frustrating a fool, plus Mahmud al-Warraq's advice by relative standing. Bursevi cites the Prophet's practice of ties, pardon, and good treatment; Ibn Ashur notes his Quranic character and Torah description. Study Quran compares 23:96 and 29:46 and cites hadith on ties, giving, pardon, and no reciprocal harm. [58 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); ZAJJAJ; MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس); QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن عباس,الفراء); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو بكر الصديق,ابن عباس,علي بن أبي طالب,محمود الوراق); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; QASHANI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو فراس الحمداني,ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR-FULL(عمر); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(البقلي,رسول الله); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî,Zemahşerî,İbn Atıyye); STUDYQURAN(IJ,IJ, Ṭs,IK,Ṭs); ABUHAYYAN-FULL+]
- 41:34/q05/p2 Greet the opponent with peace upon meeting. — Mujahid and Ata are repeatedly named. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan say greeting starts the wider virtue but does not exhaust it; Ata also speaks of answering harm with peace. [9 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(مجاهد); TAB-FULL(عطاء); MAWARDI-FULL(عطاء); IBNATIYYA-FULL(مجاهد وعطاء); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(عطاء); QURTUBI-FULL(مجاهد وعطاء); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(مجاهد وعطاء); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(İbn Atıyye)]
- 41:34/q05/p3 Meet falsehood or error with truth. — Tabrisi includes this with forbearance and pardon; Tabatabai does likewise; Study Quran attributes it to Ṭs. [3 notes: TABRISI; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(Ṭs)]
- 41:34/q05/p4 Return a good deed, praise, or material generosity to the offender, beyond mere forgiveness. — Nasafi contrasts praise with insult and a good act with a bad act; Biqai, Bursevi, Alusi, Elmalılı, and Kuranyolu's Zamakhshari distinguish benevolence from pardon. Bursevi's Greatest Imam anecdote says praise induced an abuser to praise him. Ibn Atiyya includes generosity in paying and collecting debts. [8 notes: NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(الإمام الأعظم); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Zemahşerî); IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 41:34/q05/p5 Turn away from the offender's harm or refrain from replying. — Mujahid recommends turning away; Ali, as quoted by Qurtubi, recommends silence to Qanbar, and Qurtubi adduces poems that silence most frustrates an insulting fool. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(مجاهد); QURTUBI-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب)]
- 41:34/q05/p6 Shake hands affectionately and sincerely when meeting. — Ibn al-Arabi applies the verse this way, citing a report that handshaking removes rancor; al-Bara's Prophetic report says two Muslims clasping hands have sins fall away. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(ابن العربي,البراء بن عازب)]
- 41:34/q05/p7 Repel one's own impulses toward vice by practicing virtue. — Study Quran explicitly gives this inward application; Biqai includes harm from oneself as well as from others; Qashani contrasts the heart with the lower self and warns that evil for evil grows. [3 notes: STUDYQURAN; BIQAI-FULL; QASHANI]

## 41:34/q06 What object is omitted after 'repel'?  [ٱدْفَعْ · grammar]
- 41:34/q06/p1 The omitted object is evil, supplied by the preceding contrast. — Ibn Ashur compares 13:22 and 23:96; Study Quran notes the absence of an expressed object before listing anger, error, ignorance, and wrongdoing as possible instances. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN(IJ)]

## 41:34/q07 What does 'that which is best' add to the command?  [بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ · rhetoric]
- 41:34/q07/p1 It urges the finest available good response, greater than simple pardon, and intensifies the command. — Baydawi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi, and Alusi say the implied question and answer stress the matter; one who can do the best can readily do a lesser good. Nasafi compares two good responses. Ibn Ashur says anger makes retaliation natural, so the superlative encourages virtue; Study Quran notes 'better' can mean more beautiful or virtuous. Alusi says the comparator is general and omitted. [10 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]

## 41:34/q08 What are the scope and limits of the command to respond well?  [ٱدْفَعْ بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ · law]
turns on: The extent to which personal pardon remains best when God's limits or greater harms are at stake, and whether the Prophet's handshaking of Jafar establishes a general practice.
- 41:34/q08/p1 It directs the Prophet and community toward patience and pardon during personal injury and preaching, while preserving God's limits and avoiding greater harm. — Ibn Ashur says the Prophet completed moral excellence and cites Aisha: he did not avenge himself except for God's inviolable limits; the community is encouraged and 42:40 promises reward. Study Quran says do not surrender to an enemy or accept falsehood, and avoid returning harm if that will not cause greater harm; it cites 13:27, 23:96, 28:54. Islahi says proportionate retaliation remains permitted under 16:125–126, but forgiveness better serves preaching and reform. [7 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 41:34/q08/p2 Patience is appropriate while truth is being conveyed; after active destructive rejection, taking opponents to task can be better or necessary. — Islahi says the Meccan opponents urged to forgive were later punished by the Companions at God's direction after conclusive communication of truth. Ibn Ashur says greater excellence may require foregoing a particular act of clemency. [3 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 41:34/q08/p3 Handshaking is an applicable social practice. — Ibn al-Arabi cites the saying that it removes rancor; Sufyan argues the Prophet's greeting of Jafar applies to the community, opposing Malik's restriction. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(ابن العربي,مالك وسفيان)]
- 41:34/q08/p4 The Prophet's greeting of Jafar was special, so handshaking should not be generalized from it. — Qurtubi transmits Malik's opposition and Sufyan's contrary reasoning in the same note. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(مالك وسفيان)]

## 41:34/q09 Was the command to respond well abrogated?  [ٱدْفَعْ بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ · sciences]
turns on: Whether later fighting supersedes this imperative or the best response varies with the situation, while pardon remains morally commended.
- 41:34/q09/p1 The sword or fighting verse abrogated the instruction, although forbearance and good company remain recommended. — Ibn al-Jawzi reports exegetes citing 9:5; Qurtubi holds abrogation but preserves overlooking, forbearance, and good company as recommended. [2 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(المفسرون); QURTUBI-FULL]
- 41:34/q09/p2 The instruction remains a conditional moral norm alongside permissible or necessary retaliation. — Islahi cites 16:125–126 for lawful proportional retaliation and better patience, then distinguishes the stage of conveying truth from active attacks. Ibn Ashur preserves action for God's limits; Study Quran allows avoiding greater harm. [6 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]

## 41:34/q10 What result can the better response have on an enemy?  [فَإِذَا ٱلَّذِى بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَهُۥ عَدَٰوَةٌۭ كَأَنَّهُۥ وَلِىٌّ حَمِيمٌۭ · interpretation]
turns on: How literally ka-annahu presents friendship: an actual change, an outward resemblance with uncertain sincerity, or a usual partial result that can deepen.
- 41:34/q10/p1 The enemy can become a close, affectionate ally or friend through kindness. — Muqatil says restraint and pardon can transform opposition; Tabari, Baghawi, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Ibn Kathir, Biqai, Bursevi, Tabatabai, and others affirm the social change. Qashani adds remorse and expulsion of Satan; Biqai pictures practical aid and compares hot water cleansing dirt. Jurjani adduces the Prophetic saying that hearts love their benefactors and cites this verse as a Quranic parallel. Islahi grounds the change in human responsiveness to persistent goodwill; Muhammad won sincere companions, though hardened hearts remained unmoved. [26 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; JURJANI-ASRAR(النبي); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; QASHANI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(البقلي); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Zemahşerî); STUDYQURAN(IK); QURTUBI-FULL+]
- 41:34/q10/p2 The enemy ordinarily only appears like an intimate friend in outward behavior; true intimacy may remain absent. — Qushayri denies full sincerity; Ibn Atiyya explains the simile ka-annahu; Abu Hayyan and Alusi report him. Ibn Ashur says affection enters and hostility recedes but ordinarily falls short of full friendship. Alusi qualifies that this is only a usual minimum: hostility can sometimes disappear altogether, citing a poem. [6 notes: QUSHAYRI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عطية); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 41:34/q10/p3 It quickly yields visible kindness and may win religious opponents into affectionate solidarity. — Tabrisi describes an enemy in religion resembling an ally in faith and kinship. Ibn Ashur treats the sudden-result clause as the command's practical benefit; Qashani lists quieted hostility, remorse, guidance, and becoming close. [4 notes: TABRISI; IBNASHUR-FULL; QASHANI]

## 41:34/q11 What do walī and ḥamīm mean here?  [وَلِىٌّ حَمِيمٌۭ · meaning]
- 41:34/q11/p1 Walī is an ally or supporter, and ḥamīm is a close relative or intimate friend. — Qatada says close ally or relative; Ikrima separates friend from near relative. Ibn Atiyya adds care and protection; Ibn Ashur says the two qualities jointly oppose enmity. Damghani gives close relative or intimate friend for ḥamīm. [8 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB-FULL(قتادة); ZAJJAJ; MAWARDI-FULL(عكرمة); DAMGHANI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 41:34/q12 How does the sudden-result clause work grammatically?  [فَإِذَا ٱلَّذِى بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَهُۥ عَدَٰوَةٌۭ كَأَنَّهُۥ وَلِىٌّ حَمِيمٌۭ · grammar]
turns on: Whether ka-annahu walī functions as a circumstantial clause or the predicate of the relative subject, and how idhā is governed.
- 41:34/q12/p1 The relative phrase is subject, sudden idhā is predicate, and ka-annahu walī is circumstantial. — Samin presents this analysis as making the enemy present in the state of an intimate friend. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]
- 41:34/q12/p2 The relative phrase is subject and ka-annahu walī is its predicate; idhā attaches to resemblance or has no governor. — Samin reports the two subanalyses of sudden idhā. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 41:34/q13 What does the sudden idhā convey?  [فَإِذَا · rhetoric]
- 41:34/q13/p1 It conveys the rapid appearance of kindness's effect on an enemy. — Ibn Ashur identifies the prefixed particle as suddenative and ties its force to the command's promised benefit. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 41:34/q14 Why describe the enemy as 'the one between you and whom there is enmity'?  [ٱلَّذِى بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَهُۥ عَدَٰوَةٌۭ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the expression's force lies in general eloquence or in its open range of hostilities; the readings can coexist.
- 41:34/q14/p1 The fuller relative expression is more eloquent than simply saying 'your enemy'. — Alusi calls it more expressive despite its length; Tabatabai reports a view that also calls it concise. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 41:34/q14/p2 The expression leaves enmity indefinite in kind, strength, and duration, implying stronger kindness for entrenched hostility. — Ibn Ashur draws this range from the relative construction. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 41:34/q15 What occasion or person is said to underlie the verse?  [ٱلَّذِى بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَهُۥ عَدَٰوَةٌۭ · reports]
turns on: Whether the verse's transformation fits Abu Sufyan's biography, especially whether his later closeness came from kindness or conversion; Abu Jahl is a separate reported addressee.
- 41:34/q15/p1 It concerned Abu Sufyan, whose hostility softened through the Prophet's marriage tie with Umm Habiba and later Islam. — Muqatil or Muqatil ibn Hayyan is transmitted; Jishumi names Ramlah, Umm Habiba; Baghawi and Bursevi add Islam and kinship; Alusi calls him later close to Sunnis and remarks on his grandson Yazid. Qurtubi prefers this report because the verse depicts a former enemy becoming intimate; Elmalılı cites him as an example. [9 notes: JISHUMI(مقاتل); BAGHAWI-FULL(مقاتل بن حيان); QURTUBI-FULL(مقاتل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL+; JISHUMI-; IBNASHUR-FULL-]
- 41:34/q15/p2 It concerned Abu Jahl's harm to Muhammad, for which the Prophet was told to show patience and pardon. — Muqatil gives Abu Jahl and adds seeking refuge from Satan; Mawardi and Qurtubi transmit this account. Qurtubi considers the Abu Sufyan account stronger. [3 notes: MUQATIL; MAWARDI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الماوردي)]
- 41:34/q15/p3 No sound occasion is established by the Abu Sufyan report; the verse concerns kindness toward religious opponents generally. — Jishumi says the verse does not support Muqatil's account and cites Abu Sufyan's hostile remarks and incitement against Abu Bakr as evidence he was no true ally. Ibn Ashur accepts later friendliness but says conversion, rather than this verse's kindness, explains the change; he distinguishes Umar and Hind as conversion examples. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL+; JISHUMI+]

## 41:34/q16 What further effects or rewards are attached to patient good conduct?  [* · interpretation]
- 41:34/q16/p1 It protects against Satan, humbles an adversary, and can make the offender ashamed. — Ibn Abbas is transmitted by Tabari, Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, and Kuranyolu; Razi and Kuranyolu say persistent patience shames offenders into leaving misconduct. Ali's counsel to Qanbar says silence pleases God and frustrates Satan. [8 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس,علي بن أبي طالب); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî,İbn Atıyye)]
- 41:34/q16/p2 It earns esteem and afterlife reward and improves the success of preaching. — Bursevi contrasts the believer's restraint with opponents' evil; Kuranyolu's Razi says good merits worldly respect and reward; Islahi says the forgiving course benefits reform and Muhammad's character drew decent opponents into sincere companionship. [5 notes: BURSEVI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 41:34/q16/p3 It quiets hostility, brings remorse, expels Satan, and guides toward paradise. — Qashani says good establishes the responder in the other's heart and reflects divine mercy. He attributes to the Prophet an ungraded saying that the Creator, if visible, would appear as forbearance. [2 notes: QASHANI(النبي عليه السلام)]

## 41:34/q17 What inward discipline is read from the verse?  [* · inward]
- 41:34/q17/p1 Serve God by enduring His servants for His sake, replacing base traits with patience, pardon, and generosity. — Qushayri says not to avenge oneself and links high ranks to hardship; al-Baqli, via Bursevi, stresses transforming blameworthy traits. Qashani traces good to the heart and evil to the lower self. Study Quran applies repelling to one's own impulses. [5 notes: QUSHAYRI; BURSEVI(البقلي); QASHANI; STUDYQURAN]
- 41:34/q17/p2 The closer one is in divine service, the more serious even slight lapses become. — Ibn Ata, cited by Sulami, says ignorant people's grave sins may be overlooked while the truthful are caught for glances and distractions. [1 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء)]
- 41:34/q17/p3 Spiritual love admits different shares and an unsated perfection. — Bursevi cites Ibn al-Farid on wasting life without tasting its wine; Yahya ibn Muadh says love's cup intoxicated him, while Abu Yazid replies that the strong drink without exhausting or being sated even after seven seas. [3 notes: BURSEVI(ابن الفارض,يحيى بن معاذ الرازي)]

## 41:34/q18 How is the verse used in broader arguments about proof, rhetoric, and restraining harm?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: These are distinct uses and surrounding reflections in the cited sources, rather than rival explanations of a single phrase.
- 41:34/q18/p1 The verse expresses the widely recognized inference that beneficence wins affection; eloquence can make such implications immediately accessible. — Jurjani compares the command to the Prophetic maxim about hearts loving benefactors. In Dalail he distinguishes inference from fixed lexical meaning and says eloquence shortens the route from expressed to implied meaning, illustrating indirect expression through metonymy, metaphor, and representation with three cited poetic lines. [8 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR(النبي); JURJANI-DALAIL]
- 41:34/q18/p2 A likeness can be directly sensed or require interpretation; removing doubt makes a proof manifest like the sun. — Jurjani contrasts a cheek and rose alike in redness with an argument and the sun; doubt acts as a mental curtain, and a clear proof leaves denial to defective reason or obstinacy. Interpretive likenesses range from easy to subtle. Qushayri says revealed signs remove excuse from those seeking God. [6 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR; QUSHAYRI]
- 41:34/q18/p3 Argument about subtle rhetorical judgments needs a receptive listener and may leave uncertainty after extended reflection. — Jurjani says critics trust their own taste; persuasion requires willingness to reconsider. He uses Abu Nuwas to show certainty near but unsettled. Bint al-Shati reports this argument, says Jurjani separated rhetoric study from Quranic study, and cites Abu Nuwas's poem of suspicion displacing certainty. [6 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(أبو نواس,عبد القاهر الجرجاني)]
- 41:34/q18/p4 Some harmful defiance must be restrained by force when counsel fails. — Jurjani cites a poetic line about honor and bloodshed as a rational truth confirmed by divine commands, law, and Prophetic practice; tyrants are checked by steel, as dirt must be cleared from a well and illness from a body. [3 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR]
- 41:34/q18/p5 The passage urges following evidence rather than leaders and seeking refuge against human and jinn devils. — Jishumi draws the evidentiary lesson; Razi connects 41:34 and 41:36 with 7:199–200 to show prophets continually sought protection; Muqatil also commands Muhammad to seek refuge from Satan. [3 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL; MUQATIL]

## 41:34/q20 What reports illustrate the Prophet's and believers' practice of this command?  [* · reports]
- 41:34/q20/p1 The Prophet modeled pardon, generosity, and maintaining ties, while his followers answered abuse without revenge. — Ibn Ashur transmits Aisha on his Quranic character and refusal of personal revenge, and a report through Iyad, Ibn Mardawayh, Jabir, and Ibn Jarir connecting 7:199 to Gabriel's instruction to maintain kinship, give to the deprived, and pardon the wrongdoer. Bursevi and Study Quran transmit similar sayings; Qurtubi gives Abu Bakr's forgiving reply and Ali's advice to Qanbar. The report of the Greatest Imam says praise reversed an abuser's speech. [10 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; BURSEVI(الإمام الأعظم,رسول الله); STUDYQURAN; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو بكر الصديق,ابن عباس,علي بن أبي طالب)]
- 41:34/q20/p2 Affectionate, sincere handshaking between Muslims removes sins or rancor. — Al-Bara ibn Azib reports the Prophet saying sins fall away when two Muslims clasp hands; Ibn al-Arabi cites a saying that handshaking removes rancor, with Malik and Sufyan differing on generalizing the Prophet's greeting of Jafar. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(ابن العربي,البراء بن عازب,مالك وسفيان)]
