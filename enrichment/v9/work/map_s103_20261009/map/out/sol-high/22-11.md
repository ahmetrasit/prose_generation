# 22:11: 24 questions

## 22:11/q01 Why say 'from the people' rather than use a pronoun?  [وَمِنَ ٱلنَّاسِ · rhetoric]
- 22:11/q01/p1 The fresh noun evokes the group's instability and vacillation. — Al-Biqāʿī ties the wording to their precarious worship. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 22:11/q02 How does the definite class noun in 'from the people' work?  [ٱلنَّاسِ · grammar]
- 22:11/q02/p1 The article can mark a class applicable to each member. — Sībawayh illustrates definite class names through epithets, stars, days, place names and duals. A common epithet may become an individual's name; al-Khalīl distinguishes named persons from descriptive definite names. Definite known pairs differ from arbitrary pairs. Fixed places retain definite names, while personal name pairs are indefinite; lost derivations do not change the rule. [11 notes: SIBAWAYH(الخليل)]

## 22:11/q03 Who is the conditional worshipper, and what occasion is reported?  [مَن يَعْبُدُ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ · reports]
turns on: Whether the verse is tied to a particular migrant or incident, a group of conditional converts, hypocrites, or weak converts; Ibn ʿĀshūr objects that concealed disbelief cannot explain reassurance after conversion.
- 22:11/q03/p1 Bedouin or other Arab migrants judged Islam by health, children, animals, harvests, charity and wealth, remaining during prosperity and leaving after misfortune. — Muqātil specifies Asad ibn Khuzayma and Ghaṭafān; reports vary between one newcomer and groups, foals and horses, sons versus daughters or miscarriages, fever, drought and delayed alms. Ibn Kathīr cites Bukhārī for Ibn ʿAbbās's individual report. Ibn ʿĀshūr treats this as evidence for a Medinan occasion; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ notes two routes. Ibn Jurayj adds nearby villagers; some feared loss of Jewish allies' protection if Muhammad failed. - 22:11/q03/p9 The ʿUrayniyyūn exemplify the wavering converts. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says they migrated, disliked Medina, recovered with charity-camel milk and urine, then killed a shepherd and stole camels before capture and execution; Ibn ʿAṭiyya also invokes them. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس ومجاهد وقتادة); IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 22:11/q04 What does worshipping God 'on an edge' mean?  [يَعْبُدُ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ · interpretation]
turns on: The literal edge image can point to doubt, one-sided conditional worship, mere outward profession, or a specific inward dependence on reward; several sources explicitly connect these explanations.
- 22:11/q04/p1 It is worship based on doubt or weak conviction, liable to collapse. — Mujāhid, Qatāda and others give the contextual gloss 'doubt'; al-Wāḥidī distinguishes it from the literal edge. Weak grasp of evidence means a small objection can unsettle one. The edge or cliff and army flank image represents lack of firm footing; gratitude in ease and patience in hardship would stabilize worship. [50 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(مجاهد); MAJAZ; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB-FULL(مجاهد); ZAJJAJ; THALABI(بعض أهل المعاني,مجاهد); MAWARDI-FULL(علي بن عيسى,مجاهد); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,أبو الفتح,أبو عبيدة,أكثر المفسرين,ابن الأعرابي وأحمد بن يحيى,ابن اليزيدي عن أبي زيد,المبرد,بعض أهل المعاني,مجاهد، والسدي، وقتادة، وأبو إسحاق، وأبو زيد، وابن الأعرابي); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(مجاهد); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(علي بن عيسى,مجاهد); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو عبيدة,مجاهد وقتادة); QURTUBI-FULL(مجاهد); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو عبيد,ابن عطية,ابن عيسى,الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL(آخرون,مجاهد وقتادة); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN(R,Ṭ); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 22:11/q04/p2 It is worship on one side only: when worldly wishes are met, not in adversity. — Ibn Qutayba calls it one way or creed; al-Azharī prefers the prosperity/adversity pair, and al-Wāḥidī says worship in both shows acceptance of the Creator's direction. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ says the following clauses explain the image. The soldier or cliff stays for booty and flees at danger. Ibn ʿĀshūr notes that the worship remains directed to God alone, as 22:12 contrasts it with calling on others. [11 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; MAWARDI-FULL(ابن كامل,الحسن); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن قتيبة); QURTUBI-FULL; TABATABAI; ELMALILI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الأزهري)+]
- 22:11/q04/p3 It is outward worship by the tongue without the heart. — Al-Ḥasan says religion has tongue and heart and this confession lacks the second; others describe a marginal or verbal Muslim whose worship serves immediate aims. [11 notes: THALABI(الحسن); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الحسن); JISHUMI(الحسن); BAGHAWI-FULL(الحسن); TABRISI(الحسن); QURTUBI-FULL(الحسن); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحسن); ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN(R)]
- 22:11/q04/p4 It is a wager resting on one's own pledged effort and hoped-for reward. — Al-Wāsiṭī likens the worshipper to someone resting on a staked pledge; other inward accounts describe expecting payment proportional to deeds and failing to perceive God's favor in enabling worship. [3 notes: SULAMI(الواسطي,بعضهم)]
- 22:11/q04/p5 It is ambivalent service that tries to keep God and former deities together. — Iṣlāḥī says such people obey only agreeable directives, seek benefits from ancestral deities during calamity, and try to stand with both God and Satan; genuine monotheism requires total submission. His 'edge' is distance from full entry into Islam, with 2:208 and 4:176 cited. [7 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 22:11/q04/p6 It is worship with a single aim of worldly gain. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ says religion is used for gain, while Kur'an Yolu portrays a person seeking a god who grants wishes. [3 notes: TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 22:11/q05 What does the word ḥarf literally denote here?  [حَرْفٍ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the root image is physical boundary or a transferred sense of mode; Abū al-Fatḥ disputes derivation from inclination.
- 22:11/q05/p1 An edge, side, margin, end or boundary. — Al-Wāḥidī and Jishumī cite the sword's edge and related turning aside; Abū al-Fatḥ derives the root from sharpness or limits and adduces pungent food or plants and distancing; another derivation is inclination from the center. Linguists also use it for a letter as part of a word; al-Rāghib calls letters edges joined together. [8 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو الفتح الموصلي); JISHUMI; TABRISI; NASHR(أهل اللغة); BURSEVI(الراغب)]
- 22:11/q05/p2 It can mean a face, aspect, way or condition. — Ibn Qutayba uses one way or school of thought; al-Dānī adduces this verse as an analogy for a linguistic mode in the seven aḥruf tradition. [3 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن قتيبة); NASHR(أبو عمرو الداني)]

## 22:11/q06 How should one answer the challenge 'on which letter do you worship'?  [يَعْبُدُ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ · theology]
- 22:11/q06/p1 One worships God alone without partner. — Muqātil gives this reply to a questioner. [1 notes: MUQATIL]

## 22:11/q07 How is the seven-aḥruf tradition related to the word ḥarf here?  [حَرْفٍ · readings]
turns on: Whether ḥarf means content aspect, language, or a transmitted reading form, and whether the cited reports are sound.
- 22:11/q07/p1 Seven aḥruf are seven modes or aspects, including kinds of content or divine names, distinct from permitted variant recitations. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya describes paths including permission and prohibition; Ubayy's prophetic report permits examples among divine names but not swapping punishment for mercy. Ibn al-Ṭayyib treats them as aspects of names. Al-Bāqillānī and Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr distinguish content categories from variant readings. Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr calls Ibn Masʿūd's report weak; al-Bayhaqī's disconnected chain is called good though Abū Salama never met Ibn Masʿūd. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says any original name-substitution permission was later abrogated and is now impermissible. [12 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن مسعود,القاضي ابن الطيب,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); BURHAN(ابن عبد البر,البيهقي,القاضي أبو بكر)]
- 22:11/q07/p2 The aḥruf mean seven distinct languages. — A reported view takes the number literally as languages; al-Qāḍī Abū Bakr objects that ʿUmar, Ubayy, Hishām and Ibn Masʿūd shared one language yet disputed readings. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(قوم); IBNATIYYA-FULL(القاضي أبو بكر بن الطيب)-]
- 22:11/q07/p3 Every word that has variants must possess seven forms, some unattested. — One group's reasoning is that otherwise the seven-aḥruf hadith would not be fulfilled; missing forms may simply have no transmitted report. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(قوم)]
- 22:11/q07/p4 It suffices if one or two words have seven forms. — Another group says this fulfills the hadith's wording. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(قوم)]
- 22:11/q07/p5 Seven aḥruf are seven linguistic modes or vocalic allowances. — Al-Dānī uses ḥarf as a face or aspect, or a part naming the whole reading because a single letter may change. Ibn Qutayba says replacing a lifelong dialect is difficult, so God permitted latitude. [3 notes: NASHR(أبو عمرو الداني,ابن قتيبة)]
- 22:11/q07/p6 The seven-content-category explanation is invalid. — Aḥmad ibn Abī ʿImrān and al-Ṭaḥāwī say it absurdly collapses all content into one category; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says categories are not letters and rulings cannot change; al-Māwardī notes the report concerns substitution of a letter, not a legal verse with a parable. [3 notes: BURHAN(أحمد بن أبي عمران,ابن عطية,الماوردي)]

## 22:11/q08 What do the faʿla noun forms cited alongside this verse indicate?  [فِتْنَةٌ · grammar]
- 22:11/q08/p1 Faʿla can name a manner or kind of act, and its regular use can name one occurrence; lexical exceptions must be recognized. — Sībawayh distinguishes qatlah and ṭaʿmah for kinds from one event; the direct verbal base supplies the regular single-occurrence form because augmented verbal nouns are not uniformly productive. Ityāna and similar established nouns rarely mark one act. Ghazāh and ḥijjah are lexical one-event nouns; wind names are lexical kinds, while shidda, shaʿra and duriya are unrelated lexical forms. [6 notes: SIBAWAYH]

## 22:11/q09 What does the good that befalls him comprise?  [خَيْرٌ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether 'good' names material circumstances alone or also the convert's inference about religious truth.
- 22:11/q09/p1 Worldly ease: health, livelihood, fertility, wealth, sons and increased livestock. — Mujāhid says prosperity; Qatāda adds crops, comforts and pleasures. Al-Wāḥidī includes crops and says the pronoun in 'by it' refers to this good. The reports list horses, foals, sons and charity; Bursawī and al-Ālūsī distinguish outward settlement from believers' inner steadiness. [23 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); YAHYA-SALLAM(قتادة); ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB-FULL(قتادة,مجاهد); ZAJJAJ; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI(أبو جعفر); DURR-FULL(قتادة,مجاهد); STUDYQURAN(al-Ṭabarī)]
- 22:11/q09/p2 Favorable circumstances are interpreted as proof the new religion is true. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says converts reason that former gods have failed to punish them, while a reverse outcome leads to resentment. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 22:11/q10 What does being reassured by that good mean?  [ٱطْمَأَنَّ بِهِۦ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the assurance is mere conditional settling or an asserted certainty.
- 22:11/q10/p1 He becomes settled and content in Islam while prosperity continues. — Mujāhid glosses it as becoming established; al-Māwardī says it may strengthen faith or settle him in his position. Bursawī says the firmness is outward, not the believer's inner assurance. Al-Wāḥidī identifies the pronoun's antecedent as the good. [17 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); YAHYA-SALLAM(قتادة,مجاهد); TAB-FULL; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI(الكاشفي); ALUSI-FULL; DURR-FULL(قتادة,مجاهد)]
- 22:11/q10/p2 He claims certainty because desired pleasures are coming. — Qatāda says abundant crops and comfort induce a declaration of certainty, which collapses under hardship. [1 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(قتادة)]

## 22:11/q11 What does fitna mean here, and what may it include?  [فِتْنَةٌ · meaning]
turns on: The word can name the concrete adverse event or its function as a test; the sources say prosperity too can test.
- 22:11/q11/p1 A test or calamity, commonly illness, drought, constrained livelihood or loss affecting person, family or property. — Mujāhid adds punishment; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Ṭabrisī gloss test. Al-Rāzī observes that blessing also tests, although ordinary Arabic calls burdensome events fitna. Bursawī says hardship can bring nearness through patience. Al-Biqāʿī says even a small affliction exposes insecurity. The reported migrant faced fever, a daughter, loss of wealth or a delayed alms gift. [22 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); TAB-FULL(مجاهد); ZAJJAJ; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,مجاهد); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI(أبو جعفر); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 22:11/q11/p2 It is a test of whether hardship overturns profession of faith. — Iṣlāḥī says the path to God includes trials; meeting them with submission strengthens faith and multiplies reward, while despair loses both worlds. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 22:11/q12 Why use 'fitna' for hardship rather than call it evil?  [فِتْنَةٌ · rhetoric]
turns on: One explanation concerns moral wording, another the smallness suggested by grammatical form.
- 22:11/q12/p1 Hardship is not inherently evil, and 'fitna' names a burdensome test without moral blame. — Al-Rāzī records Muqātil's question about the contrast with 'good'; Arabic often reserves fitna in this context for what feels hard, although blessings also test. Bursawī says patient acceptance can raise one's rank. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL(مقاتل); BURSEVI]
- 22:11/q12/p2 The feminine phrasing suggests even a small affliction is enough. — Al-Biqāʿī connects the wording with exposure of hidden instability in bodily or financial trouble. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 22:11/q13 What does 'turns back on his face' convey?  [ٱنقَلَبَ عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِۦ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the turn refers primarily to apostasy, physical flight or emotional reversal; al-Rāzī addresses how a hypocrite could 'turn back'.
- 22:11/q13/p1 He abandons Islam and returns to disbelief or former idol worship. — Mujāhid, Abū Isḥāq and others gloss apostasy; al-Mubarrad says 'face' may be his former direction. Al-Baḥr suggests ʿalā can bear the sense ʿan, citing 11:6. Bursawī's Kashifī says he goes back the way he came. Al-Biqāʿī calls it a complete return; al-Rāzī answers the objection that a hypocrite was never inwardly Muslim by saying public praise reverses into denunciation. [33 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(مجاهد); ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(مجاهد); ZAJJAJ; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,المبرد); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(مجاهد); BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(مجاهد); BURSEVI(الكاشفي,بحر العلوم); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI(أبو جعفر)]
- 22:11/q13/p2 The image depicts flight, defeat or anxiety when reassurance vanishes. — Al-Ālūsī offers fleeing or rushing and reports al-Kashf's defeat metonym and another anxiety explanation. Al-Māwardī allows flight to one's people in fear. [3 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 22:11/q13/p3 The face stands for the entire person. — Asad explicitly calls it metonymy. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES]

## 22:11/q14 How is the clause 'he lost this world and the next' connected syntactically?  [خَسِرَ ٱلدُّنْيَا وَٱلْءَاخِرَةَ · grammar]
turns on: The case and clause relation depend on whether the loss reports a new event, describes the turn, or restates it.
- 22:11/q14/p1 The past-tense clause can begin a fresh statement. — Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn list a new clause; al-Ālūsī also lists independent speech. On this analysis the prior clause may complete the opening subject. [4 notes: NAHHAS; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR(العامة); ALUSI-FULL]
- 22:11/q14/p2 The past-tense clause can be circumstantial without qad. — Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn permit this even without qad, and the participial reading supports it. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR(العامة,مجاهد والأعرج وابن محيصن والجحدري); ALUSI-FULL]
- 22:11/q14/p3 The past-tense clause replaces or restates 'turned back'. — Ibn Jinnī calls it a replacement clause and cites 25:68–69 and poetry; Abū al-Faḍl and al-Samīn permit the same, while al-Ṭabrisī calls it appositional restatement. [6 notes: NAHHAS; IBNJINNI-MUHTASAB; TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR(العامة); ALUSI-FULL]

## 22:11/q15 Which readings of 'lost this world and the next' are reported?  [خَسِرَ ٱلدُّنْيَا وَٱلْءَاخِرَةَ · readings]
turns on: The variant changes verb to participle and case; the participle may be circumstantial or nominative, with accompanying case differences.
- 22:11/q15/p1 The common reading has the past verb khasira and accusative 'the Hereafter'. — Al-Farrāʾ says it has the same core sense as khāsir; al-Ṭabarī says all regional readers except Ḥumayd al-Aʿraj use it. Jishumī calls it the reading of the seven readers. [7 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL; JISHUMI(يعقوب); TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR(العامة)]
- 22:11/q15/p2 Khāsir is an accusative active participle in a circumstantial phrase, usually with genitive 'the Hereafter'. — Reports name Mujāhid, Ḥumayd, al-Aʿraj, Ibn Muḥayṣin, al-Jaḥdarī, Ibn Muqsim and others; Yaʿqūb's Rūḥ and Zayd are transmitted for it. Al-Samīn notes accusative ākhira is also possible if dunyā is accusative and gives a poem for loss of tanwīn at a consonant junction. Ibn Muḥayṣin is also transmitted with accusative. The phrase means turning away while already losing both worlds; no pause falls at wajhihi. [19 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL; NAHHAS(مجاهد وحميد); IBNJINNI-MUHTASAB(مجاهد وحميد بن قيس); THALABI(حميد الأعرج ويعقوب); JISHUMI(يعقوب); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(مجاهد وحميد والأعرج); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو رزين العقيلي وأبو مجلز ومجاهد وطلحة بن مصرف وابن أبي عبلة وزيد عن يعقوب); QURTUBI-FULL(مجاهد وحميد بن قيس والأعرج والزهري وابن أبي إسحاق ويعقوب); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(مجاهد وحميد والأعرج وابن محيصن وقنب والجحدري وابن مقسم); SAMIN-DURR(مجاهد والأعرج وابن محيصن والجحدري); NASHR(ابن مهران عن روح وزيد عن يعقوب); ALUSI-FULL(مجاهد وحميد والأعرج وابن محيصن وقنب والجحدري وابن مقسم)]
- 22:11/q15/p3 A nominative khāsir can be the subject replacing a pronoun or predicate of omitted 'he'. — Abū Ḥayyān reports al-Zamakhsharī's favorable subject parse; al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī include the omitted-subject predicate, and al-Ālūsī mentions tajrīd for emphasis. Al-Nasafī ascribes a nominative variant to Rūḥ and Zayd. [5 notes: NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(مجاهد وحميد والأعرج وابن محيصن وقنب والجحدري وابن مقسم)]

## 22:11/q16 What is the loss of this world and the Hereafter?  [خَسِرَ ٱلدُّنْيَا وَٱلْءَاخِرَةَ · interpretation]
turns on: The notes differ on whether worldly loss means missed hoped-for goods, actual bereavement or death, legal and social exclusion, or moral waste.
- 22:11/q16/p1 He misses the worldly benefit he sought and loses afterlife reward through unbelief or apostasy. — Al-Ṭabarī says uncertain worship fails to secure worldly aims and leads to Fire; Yahyā says his cherished worldly life passes and he has no share beyond. Al-Wāḥidī, Ibn Kathīr and Study Quran connect missed goods to rejection of faith. Kashifī adds destruction of deeds. Al-Bayḍāwī says apostasy removes worldly protection and nullifies deeds. [21 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(مجاهد); ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB-FULL; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; DURR-FULL(قتادة); BURSEVI(الكاشفي); TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(Ṭ); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 22:11/q16/p2 He loses worldly goods or loved ones without acceptance, then forfeits the reward of patience. — Abū Ḥayyān identifies property and bereavement; Bursawī prefers loss of family in hardship and Hell after abandoning religion. Iṣlāḥī says ordinary worldly loss could be compensated in the Hereafter if met with submission. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BURSEVI+]
- 22:11/q16/p3 He loses worldly standing, spoils, legal protection and social eligibility, and loses reward in the next life. — Al-Naḥḥās and al-Qurṭubī mention divine denunciation, no spoils or praise and no reward. Al-Rāzī enumerates honor, testimony, leadership, judgment and protection of property and blood; al-Bayḍāwī mentions lost protection. [5 notes: NAHHAS(مجاهد وحميد); MAWARDI-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(مجاهد وحميد بن قيس والأعرج والزهري وابن أبي إسحاق ويعقوب); BAYDAWI-FULL]
- 22:11/q16/p4 His worldly loss is death or separation, while his next-life loss is punishment or hypocrisy. — Al-Nasafī says death and eternal fire; al-Māwardī and al-Ṭabrisī say separation and hypocrisy. Bursawī prefers worldly family loss with loss of reward and Hell. [5 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI+]
- 22:11/q16/p5 Worldly loss is abandoning obedience and wasting time; afterlife loss is accumulated liabilities or complacency in Paradise. — Al-Sulamī transmits both distinct inward accounts: one links disobedience to claims against the person, the other wasted time to contentment in Paradise. Bursawī repeats the first. [3 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم); BURSEVI(بعضهم)]
- 22:11/q16/p6 Sin itself can narrow provision, so the person gains no worldly recovery and loses the afterlife. — Al-Biqāʿī cites 5:66 for provision and a report that sin may deprive a person of it, without naming that report's transmitter. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 22:11/q16/p7 The loss is manifest failure of the self and family on Resurrection Day. — Muqātil connects it to 39:15. [1 notes: MUQATIL]

## 22:11/q17 What does 'that is the manifest loss' add?  [ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ ٱلْخُسْرَانُ ٱلْمُبِينُ · rhetoric]
- 22:11/q17/p1 It emphatically identifies the preceding two-world loss as unmistakable ruin. — Al-Ālūsī says no other description fits; Asad calls it the most obvious loss. Al-Wāḥidī says the verb khasira supplies the noun khusrān; al-Ālūsī says the distant demonstrative signals extremity, though it may also point to an unexpressed referent. [9 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES]

## 22:11/q18 How does this verse fit its surrounding passage?  [* · links]
turns on: How broadly the unit is drawn and whether emphasis falls on rhetorical transition, the surrounding sūra, or 22:12.
- 22:11/q18/p1 It moves from open polytheist disputants to opportunists who profess religion for advantage. — Iṣlāḥī says this group tries to maintain ties with God and Satan, unlike the openly argumentative polytheists, and that two-faced groups appeared among idolaters and People of the Book after Islam's rise. [3 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 22:11/q18/p2 The wider passage contrasts sincere submission and conditional worship and ends with believers' and polytheists' fates. — Iṣlāḥī connects monotheism and the Hereafter to creation, cosmic signs and divine attributes. Study Quran describes a contrast of misguided and righteous through a garden parable and differing final recompense. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 22:11/q18/p3 The following verse shows the worship of God in 22:11 is worship of God alone before a turn to other powers. — Ibn ʿĀshūr appeals to the contrast with invoking others in 22:12; Iṣlāḥī says alleged other deities bring more harm than benefit and only God governs both. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 22:11/q19 What theological lesson is drawn from conditional worship and trials?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether the moral focus is steadfastness, inward intention, God's justice, or associating other powers with Him.
- 22:11/q19/p1 Steadfast worship means gratitude and patience, while conditional service exposes self-interest and loss. — Al-Thaʿlabī says gratitude and patience would cure instability. Sahl al-Tustarī portrays the believer as charging ahead through monotheism, prophetic emulation and supplication; a reported saying from Zayd ibn Aslam says obedience admits one to Paradise and disobedience is refusal. Iṣlāḥī says God alone controls benefit and harm, and trials met with submission increase faith and reward. [10 notes: TUSTARI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); THALABI(بعض أهل المعاني); BAGHAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 22:11/q19/p2 Appetite or confidence in one's own deeds can masquerade as worship. — Tustarī says immediate pleasure makes a person content and deprivation drives him toward disbelief. Al-Sulamī reports serving the self for reward, demanding recompense, and overlooking divine favor in the ability to worship; fear of worldly disgrace produces disgrace beyond. [5 notes: TUSTARI; SULAMI(الواسطي,بعضهم,يحيى بن معاذ)]
- 22:11/q19/p3 God does not wrong servants by punishing them for their sins. — Al-Thaʿlabī defines injustice as transgressing what one owns and says God does not punish without sin. [1 notes: THALABI]
- 22:11/q19/p4 This warning applies whenever Muslims revere the Qur'an ceremonially but fail to live by its guidance. — Iṣlāḥī applies it to public and private obedience and funerary use of the Qur'an; Kur'an Yolu calls it a recurring type in every age and place. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(rivayetler)]
- 22:11/q19/p5 Claims to serve God while associating other powers with Him are baseless. — Iṣlāḥī says the hypocrite seeks God and Satan at once and that deities pursued for benefit cause more harm than good. [5 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 22:11/q20 What general rules about interpreting the Qur'an do the cited notes give?  [* · sciences]
turns on: The distinction is between unsupported opinion and learned interpretation anchored in revelation.
- 22:11/q20/p1 Interpretation dependent on prophetic text or established evidence cannot be asserted by uninformed opinion. — Al-Ṭabarī cites prophetic warnings reported from Ibn ʿAbbās with varying wording and no grading, Abū Bakr's refusal to speak without knowledge, and 7:33. Even a lucky correct guess remains culpable conjecture. [5 notes: TAB-FULL(أبو بكر الصديق,ابن عباس)]
- 22:11/q20/p2 The Prophet was charged with explaining revelation, and learned people may interpret it with care. — Al-Ṭabarī cites the mission to explain revelation and Ibn Masʿūd's ten-verse learning practice. He rejects an inference that the Prophet explained only a few verses: the ʿĀʾisha report has an unknown transmitter, Jaʿfar ibn Muḥammad al-Zubayrī. Early reluctance reflects fear of error, like juristic caution, although completed religion grounds rulings in text or indication. [6 notes: TAB-FULL(عائشة,عبد الله بن مسعود)]

## 22:11/q21 What literary and grammatical examples are supplied in the source excerpts?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: These are distinct literary or grammatical examples present in the supplied notes, rather than competing explanations of one verse word.
- 22:11/q21/p1 Sībawayh gives idiom and ellipsis examples. — Layta shiʿrī is lightened through frequent use, as proverbs may preserve heavy forms. After good news, haniʾan mariʾan is accusative with an omitted verb, such as 'may it be established for him' or 'may it gladden him'. [2 notes: SIBAWAYH]
- 22:11/q21/p2 Al-Bāqillānī criticizes decorated verse whose imagery and place-names outstrip its meaning. — He finds al-Buḥturī technically polished yet weakened by lightning supposedly lighting underground Batn Wajrah, padding with landmarks, and a pilgrimage stanza with sweet language but little substance. He rejects a reading of a fresh lightning flash from the beloved's direction because it damages the poem's order. [5 notes: BAQILLANI]
- 22:11/q21/p3 Al-Jurjānī analyzes the visual comparison of joined lām and alif and the merit of a poetic image. — He describes letters touching in the middle, reads al-Mutanabbī's intent as close compression rather than literal embrace, and says a judge's distinction about possible borrowing does not affect the image's hyperbolic thinness. [4 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR(القاضي)]
- 22:11/q21/p4 Al-Jurjānī restricts technical metaphor to resemblance-based transfer for hyperbole. — Al-Āmidī casually calls majlis for its occupants metaphor, but al-Jurjānī calls this contiguity; al-Āmidī's formal list supports the narrower sense. The original sense remains like a lender's claim when a man is called lion, sea or moon. [5 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR(الآمدى)]
- 22:11/q21/p5 A rare light reading of الدواب is rejected as weak. — Ibn Jinnī says al-Zuhrī alone is reported to lighten the doubled bāʾ, with weak analogy and transmission; lengthened alif compensates for adjacent identical consonants. [2 notes: IBNJINNI-MUHTASAB(الزهري)]
- 22:11/q21/p6 A source identifies several phrases as examples of rare Qur'anic vocabulary. — Al-Zarkashī lists them in the study of gharīb al-Qurʾān without further verse-specific detail in these notes. [1 notes: BURHAN]
- 22:11/q21/p7 A cited observation contrasts a withholding rich person with his anxious, bequest-making behavior when ill. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī supplies the comparison. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT]

## 22:11/q22 Why and how was the Kashshāf commentary undertaken?  [* · sciences]
- 22:11/q22/p1 Demand from learned readers, earlier dictation, travel to Mecca and a patron's eagerness led its author to undertake the work in old age. — Al-Zamakhsharī describes requests from learned, eloquent people of justice; their proposed title; his hesitation over his era's weak rhetorical learning; his earlier openings and Baqara dictation in question-and-answer form; renewed demand on travel; and Abū al-Ḥasan ʿAlī ibn Ḥamza ibn Wahhās's eagerness, even to travel to Khwarazm. [7 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL]

## 22:11/q23 What is said about the time and character of Sūrat al-Ḥajj's revelation?  [* · sciences]
turns on: The dating traditions disagree on the Makkan and Madinan portions.
- 22:11/q23/p1 One view calls it Madinan except verses 52–55. — Study Quran attributes this to Ibn Kathīr. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(IK)]
- 22:11/q23/p2 Another calls it Makkan except verses 19–22. — Study Quran attributes this to al-Qurṭubī. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(Q)]
- 22:11/q23/p3 Most authorities treat the sūra as containing both Makkan and Madinan material. — A tradition says it came by night and day, at home and on journeys, in war and peace, with abrogating and abrogated as well as clear and allegorical verses. Ibn ʿĀshūr says verse 11 apparently came in Medina. [3 notes: STUDYQURAN(Q); IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 22:11/q24 What virtues accompany the exhortation to sincere conduct?  [* · inward]
- 22:11/q24/p1 Humility, forbearance, gentleness, fidelity and truthfulness cultivate sound relations and character. — Bursawī says they bring affection, supporters, won hearts, brotherhood and completed virtue, and prays to leave base traits for good ones. [1 notes: BURSEVI]
