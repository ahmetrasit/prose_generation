# 51:52: 18 questions

## 51:52/q01 What does “likewise” compare?  [كَذَٰلِكَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the demonstrative recalls the preceding rejection or anticipates the following statement.
- 51:52/q01/p1 The present rejection of Muhammad repeats earlier peoples’ rejection of their messengers and their sorcery or madness charges. — Al-Zajjāj and Abū Isḥāq call it the same recurring state; al-Bayḍāwī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī say the following clause explains the comparison. Al-Ālūsī links it to discordant talk at 51:8. [19 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(أبو جعفر بن الزبير); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 51:52/q01/p2 The matter is simply so: the following statement about earlier peoples supplies what “likewise” anticipates. — Al-Ālūsī says the omitted-subject construction points forward; al-Naḥḥās gives the same nominal sense. [2 notes: NAHHAS; ALUSI-FULL]

## 51:52/q02 How is “likewise” construed grammatically?  [كَذَٰلِكَ · grammar]
turns on: The case of the kāf and whether a verb after negative ما can govern it.
- 51:52/q02/p1 It is nominative, the predicate of an omitted subject: “the matter is so.” — Al-Naḥḥās states this option; al-Qurṭubī links it to a warning to associators; al-Samīn calls it stronger; al-Ālūsī says it points forward. [5 notes: NAHHAS; QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 51:52/q02/p2 It is accusative, qualifying an omitted verbal noun or expressing a comparison with earlier messengers. — Al-Naḥḥās relates it to Quraysh’s predecessors; al-Qurṭubī applies that reading to disobedient monotheists. Makkī makes it an adjective of an omitted verbal noun; al-Samīn says أتى after negative ما cannot govern it. [4 notes: NAHHAS; QURTUBI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(مكي)]
- 51:52/q02/p3 Attachment to the following verb under negative ما, or to the later speech verb, is syntactically untenable. — Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Samīn say what follows negative ما cannot govern an earlier phrase; al-Ālūsī also rejects attachment to the speech verb. [3 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(مكي); ALUSI-FULL]

## 51:52/q03 Who are “those before them” and “them”?  [ٱلَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِم · referent]
turns on: Whether earlier communities as a whole or their unbelievers alone are meant.
- 51:52/q03/p1 They are earlier communities before Quraysh or the Meccan unbelievers. — Al-Ālūsī explicitly calls them Quraysh’s predecessors. Al-Wāḥidī’s Wajīz says earlier peoples of Mecca. [6 notes: NAHHAS; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 51:52/q03/p2 The phrase may be narrowed to unbelievers among those predecessors. — Al-Ālūsī records this proposal but finds it unconvincing. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 51:52/q04 Does “any messenger” literally include every prophet and every person’s response?  [مِّن رَّسُولٍ · interpretation]
turns on: How broadly the indefinite messenger and plural speakers range, especially Adam, law-preserving prophets, and believers.
- 51:52/q04/p1 The statement describes every earlier messenger as facing the accusation from his people. — Al-Ṭabrisī explains sorcerer as crafty and madness as obscured understanding. Biqāʿī qualifies that at least some in each generation said it. [12 notes: WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL(أبو جعفر بن الزبير); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES(Asad); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 51:52/q04/p2 It concerns messengers, while law-preserving prophets who followed an earlier messenger are outside the claim. — Al-Rāzī invokes prophets who affirmed prior law and Adam as objections, then distinguishes those following an earlier messenger; al-Ālūsī and Kur’an Yolu report his distinction. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî)]
- 51:52/q04/p3 The communal charge expresses a widespread or majority response, not every individual’s words. — Al-Rāzī notes some believed; omission of believers serves consolation. Al-Ālūsī reports this solution and says its extension to objections about particular messengers is weak; Kur’an Yolu also reports it. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî)]
- 51:52/q04/p4 It concerns messengers sent directly to an existing people with a message. — Al-Ālūsī reports that this excludes mere reaffirmers of earlier law; Adam lacked a community beyond Eve. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 51:52/q04/p5 It concerns descendants of Adam, excluding Adam himself. — Al-Ālūsī reports the proposal and objections to it. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 51:52/q04/p6 The law-preserving prophet distinction is insufficient. — Al-Ālūsī says reports and verses also call those prophets messengers; it also leaves Adam, sent without being accused. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL; RAZI-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام)-]

## 51:52/q05 How does the arrival clause relate to “they said”?  [مَآ أَتَى مِّن رَّسُولٍ إِلَّا قَالُوا۟ · grammar]
turns on: The clause relation and implied speech construction are distinct compatible analyses.
- 51:52/q05/p1 An omitted pronoun before “sorcerer” supplies the predication: “he or this is a sorcerer.” — Al-Zajjāj supplies huwa before sāḥir; al-Ālūsī reports a predicate construal alongside direct quotation of the labels. [2 notes: ZAJJAJ; ALUSI-FULL]
- 51:52/q05/p2 “Any messenger” is the subject of “came,” and “except they said” describes the circumstance of his arrival. — Al-Samīn explicitly makes the latter clause circumstantial for those before them. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 51:52/q06 What does “sorcerer” mean in the accusation?  [سَاحِرٌ · meaning]
- 51:52/q06/p1 It means a sorcerer, also rendered spellbinder. — Asad says spellbinder translates literal sorcerer. Al-Ṭabrisī adds crafty; Bursevī says opponents dismissed miracles as magic. [4 notes: TABRISI; BURSEVI; ASAD-NOTES(Asad)]

## 51:52/q07 How does “or” relate the two charges?  [أَوْ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the alternatives belong to different speakers, express doubt, or are quoted as alternative labels.
- 51:52/q07/p1 It distributes accusations among speakers: some said sorcerer, some madman, some both. — Abū Ḥayyān cites Noah’s people using madness alone (26:27); Biqāʿī and al-Ālūsī also give this reading. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 51:52/q07/p2 It can mark doubt between incompatible allegations. — Biqāʿī observes sorcery presupposes cleverness while madness implies its opposite. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 51:52/q07/p3 It can present two quoted alternatives rather than a single predicate. — Al-Ālūsī reports this construal alongside 'he is a sorcerer.' [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 51:52/q08 What does “madman” mean in the accusation?  [مَجْنُونٌ · meaning]
- 51:52/q08/p1 It can mean mentally ill, or someone believed to be possessed or under a jinn’s control. — Kur’an Yolu gives both senses with 2:61, 7:157, 7:184, and 15:6; al-Ṭabrisī describes obscured understanding; Asad translates madman. [4 notes: TABRISI; ASAD-NOTES(Asad); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]

## 51:52/q09 Why did people make these accusations?  [سَاحِرٌ أَوْ مَجْنُونٌ · interpretation]
turns on: Which cause or analogy the source emphasizes; these accounts can coexist.
- 51:52/q09/p1 They rejected messengers who challenged their desires and customs, dismissing signs as sorcery and warnings about resurrection or punishment as madness. — Biqāʿī names desired customs; Bursevī contrasts miracles and resurrection; Iṣlāḥī calls the labels stock excuses for signs and warnings. [3 notes: BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 51:52/q09/p2 Their response arose from ignorance or varied defects of understanding and character. — Al-Nasafī names ignorance. Al-Bāqillānī says Arabic fluency alone cannot explain rejection: opponents varied in ignorance, stubbornness, envy, ingratitude, and weak reasoning, whereas poets Labīd, Kaʿb, and Ḥassān accepted Islam. [2 notes: NASAFI-FULL; BAQILLANI]
- 51:52/q09/p3 Their accusations echoed Pharaoh’s attempt to discredit Moses. — Study Quran cites 51:39 and records further parallels to Muhammad’s accusers at 10:2, 52:29, 15:6–7, 23:70, 34:8, and 68:51. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 51:52/q10 How does the following question about mutual counsel link to this accusation?  [* · links]
- 51:52/q10/p1 Its pronoun refers to the accusation implied by “they said,” and its question marvels at the recurrence across generations. — Al-Samīn says earlier and later generations appear to have passed the same charge to one another. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 51:52/q11 What purpose does this recurring pattern serve here?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the verse is read as consolation, instruction, warning, or a lesson; these purposes can coexist.
- 51:52/q11/p1 It consoles Muhammad: earlier messengers faced the same denunciation, so he should not grieve. — Al-Rāzī says believers are omitted for consolation. Iṣlāḥī says the Prophet is neither uniquely opposed nor negligent; al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, Elmālili, and Study Quran stress the parallel. [8 notes: RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 51:52/q11/p2 It calls the Prophet to persevere, turn from wrongdoers, and remind receptive hearers. — Iṣlāḥī says God will support him and his companions and that he should continue resolutely despite fabricated stories. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 51:52/q11/p3 It warns Quraysh that rejection may bring the fate of Pharaoh’s followers. — Study Quran cites 51:39. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]
- 51:52/q11/p4 Earlier communities’ stories provide lessons for reflective readers. — Al-Jishumī gives this reason for recounting the narratives. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 51:52/q12 How does the verse connect with its surrounding passage and other verses?  [* · links]
turns on: Which neighbouring theme or scriptural parallel is foregrounded.
- 51:52/q12/p1 The same accusations against Muhammad were made by Meccan unbelievers. — Muqātil is reported by al-Wāḥidī; Study Quran lists sorcery, possession, and other charges. [4 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; STUDYQURAN]
- 51:52/q12/p2 It resumes the opening account of deniers’ discordant speech and their denial of resurrection, before warning of the promised day. — Al-Ālūsī relates it to 51:8; Ṭabāṭabāʾī describes the closing section’s return to opening themes. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 51:52/q12/p3 It parallels Pharaoh’s denunciation of Moses and other accounts of prophetic rejection. — Study Quran points to 51:39 and to 15:11, 34:34, 36:30, 43:7, and 43:23; it also cites accusations against Muhammad at 10:2, 52:29, 15:6–7, 23:70, 34:8, and 68:51. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 51:52/q13 What theological implications are drawn from messengers’ rejection?  [* · theology]
turns on: Different theological points attached to the rejection and reception of revelation.
- 51:52/q13/p1 God sends messengers amid widespread ignorance for guidance, while leaving belief uncoerced because forced or desperate belief is unacceptable. — Al-Rāzī makes both points. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 51:52/q13/p2 God acts and rules as He wills. — Al-Rāzī closes the discussion with this Sunni formulation. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 51:52/q13/p3 What God decrees is good, while evil lies in created things that can both benefit and harm. — Al-Rāzī reports this from some people, comparing fire and water, whose inherent forces also harm the poor. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(بعض الناس)]
- 51:52/q13/p4 Understanding revelation depends on God-given favor and intellect. — Al-Bāqillānī calls the Qur’an a guiding light and cites 39:9, 42:52, 2:26, 22:31, and 4:98; he urges the understanding reader to thank God and the doubter to continue rhetorical study, citing 20:114 and 23:97–98. [2 notes: BAQILLANI]

## 51:52/q14 What inward reading is given to the passage?  [* · inward]
turns on: The first applies the accusations inwardly; the second expounds nearby monotheistic exhortation.
- 51:52/q14/p1 Rebellious souls in every age reject outward prophets and inward divine inspirations as magic or madness. — The Taʾwīlāt Najmiyya, quoted by Bursevī, attributes this to satanic refusal, pride, defiance, and transgression fed by comfort and wealth. [1 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]
- 51:52/q14/p2 Monotheism requires fleeing to God and cutting attachment to ego, desire, this world, and the next. — The Taʾwīlāt Najmiyya says the true fugitive finds rest with God and warns of painful distance caused by association, citing 4:48. [2 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]

## 51:52/q15 What do the cited grammatical examples say about doubled formulas?  [* · grammar]
turns on: Whether labbayk is single or dual, alongside examples of repeated action; these excerpts do not discuss a word in 51:52.
- 51:52/q15/p1 Ḥanānik expresses repeated mercy through a fixed annexed dual-like form and an omitted verb. — Sībawayh compares subḥāna Allāh and maʿādh Allāh; al-Khalīl says each blessing continues into another; Ṭarafa’s verse illustrates an appeal; Arab usage has subḥāna Allāhi wa-ḥanānihi and the comparison rayḥānahu. [5 notes: SIBAWAYH(الخليل,العرب,طرفة بن العبد)]
- 51:52/q15/p2 Labbayk and saʿdayk express repeated answering and compliance as fixed accusatives. — Sībawayh compares the response 'hearing and obedience'; ḥadhārīk similarly means caution after caution. Arab nominatives samaʿ and ḥanān and accusative samaʿan wa-ṭāʿatan are also reported. [4 notes: SIBAWAYH(العرب)]
- 51:52/q15/p3 Dawālik and ḍarban hādhā dhīka describe reciprocal or successive actions and can be analyzed circumstantially. — ʿAbd Banī al-Ḥasḥās’s line describes one garment torn after another; Sībawayh gives the related phrase and its circumstantial option. [2 notes: SIBAWAYH(عبد بنى الحسحاس)]
- 51:52/q15/p4 Labbayk is a single annexed word like ʿalayk. — Sībawayh reports Yūnus’s analysis. [1 notes: SIBAWAYH(يونس)]
- 51:52/q15/p5 Labbayk is dual like ḥawālayk. — Sībawayh reports al-Khalīl’s evidence from singular ḥanān; some Arabs shorten to lab or ḥawālik, while cited lines resist treating labbayk as a preposition like ʿalayk. [2 notes: SIBAWAYH(الخليل,العرب)]

## 51:52/q16 What do the cited discussions say makes eloquent composition?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: The cited excerpts discuss composition, public challenge, and an unrelated grammatical example; they do not directly gloss 51:52.
- 51:52/q16/p1 Eloquence lies in authored arrangement of meanings and grammatical choices, not copied word sounds. — Al-Jurjānī compares naẓm to arranging colors; examples involve ordering, definiteness, ellipsis, pronouns, and repetition. A reciter reproduces sounds without becoming the poet or choosing his syntax. [8 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL]
- 51:52/q16/p2 A poem’s luster arises from particular choices of indefiniteness, coordination, comparison, condition, circumstance, order, and tense. — Al-Jurjānī notes indefinite sawdad and dahr, the understood subject with fa, repeated comparative kāf, balanced conditionals and circumstantial forms ṣārikhan and mustathīban, placement of idh nabā before takūn, choice of takūn over kāna, and wa-ankara ṣāḥib over wa-ankartu ṣāḥiban. He says the opening pleases the listener. [11 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL]
- 51:52/q16/p3 The Qur’an has no literary counterpart in preserved earlier Arabic speech, poetry, sermon, letter, or sajʿ. — Al-Bāqillānī argues such a precedent would exist if humans could compose one. [1 notes: BAQILLANI]
- 51:52/q16/p4 The public challenge and widely transmitted failure to meet it suffice to establish the miracle. — Al-Bāqillānī reports this objection, then answers that the challenge publicly exposes incapacity, removes doubt, and binds eloquent experts through the Qur’an itself. [1 notes: BAQILLANI(معترض); BAQILLANI-]
- 51:52/q16/p5 The challenge publicly establishes the proof by making incapacity visible. — Al-Bāqillānī gives this reply to the objection that independent proof would diminish the challenge. [1 notes: BAQILLANI]
- 51:52/q16/p6 In a نعم construction, the specified object of praise or blame can be omitted when context supplies it. — Al-Burhān gives this grammatical example. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 51:52/q17 How do the cited discussions explain metaphor?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether metaphor is located in lexical substitution or intended meaning; these examples do not directly gloss ساحر.
- 51:52/q17/p1 A metaphor such as calling a brave man a lion asserts a lionlike quality; the transfer lies in intended meaning. — Al-Jurjānī says الأسد invokes courage and strength. If it merely replaced شجاع as a synonym, it would gain no eloquence; جعلته أسدا assigns a quality as جعلته أميرا assigns office, and two-object جعل works like صيّر. [4 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL]
- 51:52/q17/p2 The account that metaphor simply transfers a word from its lexical meaning is misleading. — Al-Jurjānī rejects this familiar account with الأسد for brave and بحر for generous. [1 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL]

## 51:52/q18 Which closing prayers are reported in the cited material?  [* · reports]
turns on: Different prayer texts appear in an ancillary excerpt, with no direct claim about 51:52.
- 51:52/q18/p1 A prayer asks that one’s life, work, days, and day of meeting God end at their best. — Reported in al-Nashr. [1 notes: NASHR]
- 51:52/q18/p2 A prayer asks for a pure life, peaceful death, and return without disgrace. — Reported in al-Nashr. [1 notes: NASHR]
- 51:52/q18/p3 A prayer asks for the best requests, success, deeds, reward, life and death, firm faith, a heavy scale, accepted prayer, forgiveness, and highest Paradise. — Reported in al-Nashr. [1 notes: NASHR]
- 51:52/q18/p4 A prayer asks for every aspect of good and the highest ranks of Paradise. — Al-Nashr enumerates beginnings, endings, totalities, and inner and outer good. [1 notes: NASHR]
- 51:52/q18/p5 A prayer asks for the best hidden and public deeds and the highest ranks of Paradise. — Reported in al-Nashr. [1 notes: NASHR]
- 51:52/q18/p6 A prayer asks God to raise remembrance, remove burdens, set matters right, purify and illumine the heart, guard chastity, and forgive sins. — Reported in al-Nashr. [1 notes: NASHR]
- 51:52/q18/p7 A prayer asks blessing in faculties, sustenance, character, family, life, death, and work, and acceptance of good deeds. — Al-Nashr specifies hearing, sight, soul, and heart as well. [1 notes: NASHR]
- 51:52/q18/p8 A prayer asks for help remembering, thanking, and worshipping God well. — Reported in al-Nashr. [1 notes: NASHR]
- 51:52/q18/p9 A prayer asks for good outcomes in every matter and protection from worldly disgrace and afterlife punishment. — Reported in al-Nashr. [1 notes: NASHR]
- 51:52/q18/p10 A prayer asks for awe, obedience, certainty, enduring faculties and strength, victory, and protection from hardship and merciless rule. — Al-Nashr specifies protection from religious calamity and excessive worldly concern. [1 notes: NASHR]
