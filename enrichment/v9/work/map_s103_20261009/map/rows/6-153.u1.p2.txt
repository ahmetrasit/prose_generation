6:153/q19 What reports associate these three verses with the Prophet's testament or pledge? [وَصَّىٰكُم بِهِۦ · reports] (turns on: Different reports attach the verses to a sealed testament, a pledge, or a missionary encounter.)
  6:153/q19/p1 They are the Prophet's sealed testament, to be recited from 6:151 through 6:153. — reasons: Ibn Masʿūd's report is graded ḥasan gharīb by al-Tirmidhī and ḥasan by al-Ālūsī. Al-Rabīʿ b. Khuthaym recites or writes these verses when asked for a sealed sheet or advice.
  6:153/q19/p2 The Prophet sought a pledge on the three verses, promising reward for fulfillment and leaving unpunished breaches to God's will. — reasons: ʿUbāda b. al-Ṣāmit reports that worldly punishment expiates a breach, while God may punish or pardon a deferred one.
  6:153/q19/p3 At Minā the Prophet recited these commands to an Arab tribe after inviting them to monotheism, his mission, and his protection. — reasons: The Durr report says he then recited 16:90; Mafrūq praised the morals and Hāniʾ approved the words.
6:153/q20 What does the closing hope for taqwā mean? [لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: The sources dispute how the three endings relate to each duty group and whether the final aim primarily stresses following or avoiding division.)
  6:153/q20/p1 Obey God and fear His punishment by keeping His commands and avoiding prohibitions. — reasons: Mujāhid glosses mindfulness as obedience; ʿAṭāʾ as fear. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk mentions the five prayers guarding against Hell. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn say the complete way protects from the Fire and brings lasting salvation.
  6:153/q20/p2 Taqwā comes from adhering to the one path and refusing divisive alternatives. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī stresses avoidance of division as the verse's main purpose; he objects to Abū Ḥayyān's priority on following the path. He links piety to human nature in 91:7–8, while Iṣlāḥī says divine reminder awakens nature.
  6:153/q20/p3 The endings progress from reason through remembrance to piety. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya says obvious prior prohibitions call for reason and later desire-related ones for recollection; al-Nasafī, al-Biqāʿī, Iṣlāḥī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī describe reflection leading to heed and precaution. Al-Ṭabrisī notes the neighboring tadhakkarūn readings with light or doubled consonant, from tatadhakkarūn by deletion or assimilation.
  6:153/q20/p4 The first duties are obvious, while the second group is harder to delimit. — reasons: Al-Rāzī contrasts five obvious duties with four requiring thought; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects the claim of obscurity, allowing only care over orphan property and measures.
  6:153/q20/p5 The first ending addresses idolaters' ignorance and the second their remembered virtues. — reasons: Al-Quṭb al-Rāzī is quoted through Rūḥ al-Maʿānī; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects it as historically inaccurate and confusing Qurʾānic recollection with ordinary memory.
6:153/q21 How does the verse connect with its neighboring verses and the sūra? [* · links] (turns on: The sources connect the command to the three-verse sequence, Abrahamic and Qurʾānic guidance, and later anti-division warnings.)
  6:153/q21/p1 It gathers the preceding commands and prepares the renewed warning against religious division. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī says prohibitions and orders culminate in the one-way command; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī relates 42:13 and treats the path declaration as prelude to the warning. Ibn ʿĀshūr finds three linked sections: social order, economic relations, and Islam's inclusive principle. Al-Biqāʿī compares 6:126 and the three testament endings.
  6:153/q21/p2 It restates Abraham's religion against Quraysh's invented practices and prepares the surah's closing proclamation. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī traces the passage from Quraysh's invented food taboos through Abraham's duties to 6:155–165; he says Moses' law guided Israelites on the same path and a demanded sign cannot replace prophetic guidance. Kur'an Yolu contrasts baseless polytheist rules with nine duties and the encompassing tenth.
  6:153/q21/p3 It connects to warnings against sitting with disputants and against sectarian division. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī cites 6:68 and 4:140 for avoiding disputants and 6:159 for religious fragmentation; Ibn Kathīr links 42:13, and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī likewise invokes prophetic unity.
  6:153/q21/p4 The Qurʾān's revealed authority and universal prophetic mission underpin the call. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr says messengers remove excuses (4:165), Muhammad is the final universal messenger to humans and jinn (7:158; 6:19), and all reached by the Qurʾān are warned. He cites 11:17 and 68:44–45 on rejection, the 'red and black' report explained by Mujāhid, 41:42 on revelation's integrity, 4:82, 38:29 and 47:24 on reflection, and 3:187 and 3:77 against concealment.
  6:153/q21/p5 The sūra's final verses invite choice while making each person accountable. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says people may follow the straight path or wander, and each bears responsibility for deeds.
6:153/q22 Where were these three verses revealed? [* · sciences]
  6:153/q22/p1 They are an exception revealed in Medina within an otherwise Meccan sūra. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās and Shahr b. Ḥawshab report the exception; Shahr says the sūra came all at once with ranks of angels. Study Quran notes that some early authorities considered some verses possibly Medinan.
6:153/q23 What response to religious innovations is drawn from the warning? [وَلَا تَتَّبِعُوا۟ ٱلسُّبُلَ · law] (turns on: The reports range from counsel to preserve Sunnah through social and legal sanctions to severe eschatological warnings.)
  6:153/q23/p1 Follow transmitted Sunnah and avoid innovations, excessive dispute and sectarian desires. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī cites Ibn Masʿūd's rebuke of pebble-counting devotional circles: good intent does not validate an act. ʿUmar b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz advises simple inherited religion; Abū al-ʿĀliya urges the original practice, which al-Ḥasan confirms. Sahl says public disputation spread innovation; Ibn Masʿūd warns against hair-splitting. Al-Tustarī cites a severe no-repentance saying.
  6:153/q23/p2 Persistent company with innovators shares their judgment; avoid sitting with them even to dispute. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī cites 6:68 and 4:140 and attributes the rule to Aḥmad, al-Awzāʿī and Ibn al-Mubārak; he compares ʿUmar b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz's treatment of companions of drinkers.
  6:153/q23/p3 Innovation can block repentance or Paradise and is spiritually worse than an ordinary sin. — reasons: Tustarī's attributed report says repentance is narrowed or barred for innovations. Sahl cites a report barring innovators from Paradise; Sufyān calls innovation dearer to Iblīs since its followers do not repent; al-Awzāʿī relates Iblīs's strategy. Al-Fuḍayl warns that love of an innovator harms deeds and heart.
  6:153/q23/p4 Some sectarian doctrines and conduct are treated especially severely. — reasons: Sahl rejects prayer behind or marriage with the Muʿtazila and lists their disputed doctrines; al-Shaʿbī gives a fiery etymology of people of desires. Al-Qurṭubī recalls 72/73 sect reports, a proposed extra anti-scholar sect, and Rāfiʿ's warning about people equating Iblīs with God in creative power.
6:153/q24 What is the rhetorical force of the repeated testament and appeal to piety? [ذَٰلِكُمْ وَصَّىٰكُم بِهِۦ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُونَ · rhetoric] (turns on: The rhetorical emphasis may fall on comprehensive obedience or on the closing anti-division warning.)
  6:153/q24/p1 The three repeated charges strengthen acceptance, with reason, recollection and piety marking a progression. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī stresses the three verse endings; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls them linked sections, and al-Nasafī and Iṣlāḥī explain the mental sequence. Al-Ālūsī says the comprehensive path warrants emphatic repetition.
  6:153/q24/p2 The path declaration leads to the prohibition on division, which explains the final piety clause. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī contrasts this discourse focus with Abū Ḥayyān's emphasis on following the comprehensive path.
6:153/q25 What other word-choice and simile observations are cited alongside these notes? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: The cited material ranges from composite image syntax to other terms for travel and number choice.)
  6:153/q25/p1 A composite simile makes a combined scene a single image, while independent similes remain separate. — reasons: Al-Jurjānī contrasts Bashshār's dust and flashing swords with separate comparisons; Ruʾba's markings and al-Buḥturī's horse and cloud also depend on combined appearance. He argues Bashshār's conjunction keeps the image attached, compares wa with maʿa, and cites the undivided she-camel and calf conditional.
  6:153/q25/p2 Sulūk can mean traveling along a path, broader than merely entering. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī contrasts Moses' usluk in Qasas, with its repeated journeys from the chest to Midian and back, with adkhil in Naml, where dakhal forms occur five times and the story opens at the fire; he cites 27:7, 27:12, 28:7, 28:11, 28:22, 28:29, 28:32 and 71:19–20.
