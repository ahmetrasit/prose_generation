## THE CURRENT MAP
18:46/q01 What do wealth and sons signify in worldly life? [ٱلْمَالُ ٱلْبَنُونَ زِينَةُ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the focus is their attractiveness, their practical support, or their testing and danger.)
  18:46/q01/p1 They are attractive but transient worldly adornments, unable by themselves to provide lasting benefit or indicate divine favor. — reasons: Muqatil contrasts their fleeting beauty with afterlife reward. Al-Tabari, al-Tha'labi, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Ibn Kathir, al-Biqa'i, al-Alusi, Ibn Ashur, Tabataba'i, Islahi, Kuran Yolu, and Study Quran stress that they perish and cannot furnish the grave. Study Quran cites 18:34, 17:6, 18:7–8; Ibn Kathir cites 3:14 and 64:15. Al-Tabari illustrates rich men boasting over Salman, Khabbab, and Suhayb. Ibn Ashur cites Tarafa's poem as evidence of Arab pride in property and noble sons.
  18:46/q01/p2 Wealth supplies beauty, benefit and livelihood; sons supply strength, defense, family continuity and public status. — reasons: Al-Mawardi and al-Qurtubi distinguish wealth's beauty and benefit from sons' power and protection. Islahi explains Arabian tribal backing and displays of numbers. Study Quran calls them status and security. Al-Biqa'i adds that either may benefit if used to please God, or harm and disappoint.
  18:46/q01/p3 Wealth and children are a test and may even become enemies or a means of distance from God. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi cites 64:15 and 64:14. Ibn Kathir cites 64:15. Bursevi invokes verses on apparent increase and a divine report, with 23:55–56, saying abundance may distance a believer whereas restriction brings nearness.
18:46/q02 Why is wealth named before sons? [ٱلْمَالُ ٱلْبَنُونَ · rhetoric]
  18:46/q02/p1 Wealth is the more fundamental, immediately needed ornament and support. — reasons: Al-Alusi says it benefits parents and children at all times, while sons aid only parents; wealth sustains an individual and can adorn without sons, whereas sons without wealth may bring distress.
18:46/q03 What does zīna mean here and in related Qur'anic uses? [زِينَةُ · meaning]
  18:46/q03/p1 It is what beautifies something and makes it desirable; here it includes worldly display or luxury. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi defines adornment by its attraction to the self and distinguishes its Qur'anic senses: general beauty (2:212, 3:14, 67:5), jewelry (20:87), luxury or wealth (10:88 and this verse), and a public retinue such as Qarun's finery (28:79).
18:46/q04 How does the singular zīna predicate wealth and sons? [ٱلْمَالُ ٱلْبَنُونَ زِينَةُ · grammar]
  18:46/q04/p1 Zīna is a verbal noun used for the two concrete subjects, by emphasis or with an omitted description. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya notes that a description must be supplied, though the excerpt ends before giving it. Al-Samin says they are themselves adornment emphatically, or things that adorn. Abu Hayyan permits an omitted possessor or taking the pair as abundance. Al-Alusi says the verbal noun is transferred to the adorned thing. Al-Qurtubi permits singular and dual predicates.
18:46/q05 Is zīna read in another number? [زِينَةُ · readings]
  18:46/q05/p1 A rare reading has dual zīnatā al-ḥayāh. — reasons: Al-Samin says its alif drops in pronunciation before the next consonant cluster, making the form resemble an accusative singular; al-Qurtubi says the dual is grammatically possible.
18:46/q06 Why are wealth and sons called adornment of worldly life? [زِينَةُ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا · rhetoric]
  18:46/q06/p1 The genitive confines their adornment to this quickly decaying life. — reasons: Al-Alusi allows either ornaments belonging specifically to worldly life or ornaments found in it; if that life rapidly decays, its ornaments decay even sooner. Ibn Atiyya says the preceding parable devalues the life they adorn.
18:46/q07 Whose boasting does the verse address? [ٱلْمَالُ وَٱلْبَنُونَ · referent]
  18:46/q07/p1 It rebukes rich unbelieving leaders who boasted over poor believers. — reasons: Al-Tabari names Uyayna and al-Aqra against Salman, Khabbab, and Suhayb; al-Tha'labi, al-Wahidi, and al-Qurtubi name Uyayna and his peers. Al-Baghawi calls the leader Utba. Abu Hayyan, al-Razi and Ibn Ashur identify wealthy polytheists; Kuran Yolu calls them wealthy Meccans rejecting monotheism. The poor believers' worship is the lasting contrast.
18:46/q08 What occasion is reported for this passage? [* · reports]
  18:46/q08/p1 The passage beginning with the command to recite revelation is linked to Uyayna and al-Aqra's treatment of poor worshippers. — reasons: Al-Tabari says the section from 18:27 through this verse was revealed about them; al-Suddi relays Khabbab's account beginning with the order not to drive away those calling on God morning and evening (6:52).
18:46/q09 What are the enduring righteous deeds? [ٱلْبَٰقِيَٰتُ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتُ · referent] (turns on: Whether named devotions define the entire phrase or exemplify the general class; al-Tabari stresses unrestricted wording, while Sa'id's rejection and al-Alusi's reading of 'these are' support restriction.)
  18:46/q09/p1 Every act or word of obedience whose reward God preserves, including the named forms of worship and remembrance. — reasons: Al-Tabari prefers the unrestricted wording because Scripture and Prophetic reports do not exclude other works; the report says four phrases are among the deeds. Al-Zajjaj, al-Tabrisi, al-Qurtubi and the reported choice of Ibn Jarir agree. Ibn Abbas lists prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, charity, emancipation, jihad and kinship; al-Tha'labi adds testimony, forgiveness and blessings on the Prophet. Al-Razi includes deeds leading to knowledge, love and service. Al-Baydawi names Ramadan fasting and good speech; Kuran Yolu includes faith, moral conduct, enjoining good and kindness. Tabataba'i treats specific reports as examples. Al-Alusi reports al-Khafaji's inclusion argument but questions whether Prophetic 'these are' permits it.
  18:46/q09/p2 The five prescribed daily prayers. — reasons: Ibn Abbas, Sa'id ibn Jubayr, Amr ibn Shurahbil, Ibrahim, Abu Maysara, Masruq and Ibn Mas'ud are named in various chains. Al-Tha'labi and al-Durr connect them with 11:114, where good deeds erase bad ones. Yahya also reports a combined view attributed to Ibn Abbas, taking prayers and formulas together.
  18:46/q09/p3 The four formulas of glorifying, praising, affirming God's oneness and magnifying Him. — reasons: Muqatil and several transmitters cite Prophetic identifications; Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, Ikrima, Dahhak, Hasan and Qatada also give this list. Al-Ghazali, as quoted by al-Razi, reads them as ascending knowledge: freedom from defect, source of good, oneness, and greatness beyond reason. Abu Hurayra's report says they are 'among' the enduring deeds. Ibn al-Jawzi also reports an Ali formula substituting reliance for glorification or praise; it varies slightly. Reports on these formulas' merits appear separately below.
  18:46/q09/p4 The four remembrance formulas together with lā ḥawla wa-lā quwwata illā bi-llāh. — reasons: Uthman, Ibn Umar, Sa'id ibn al-Musayyib, Ata, Muhammad ibn Ka'b and Prophetic reports give the fivefold list. Some transmitted lists vary: Abu Sa'id's list omits explicit praise in al-Tha'labi; Ibn Umar and Ali lists also vary. Sa'id explicitly rejects prayer, fasting, almsgiving and pilgrimage as the specific answer. Abraham's Paradise planting report supports inclusion of hawqala; Salim challenges then accepts Muhammad ibn Ka'b's addition. Ibn Atiyya, al-Qurtubi and Abu Hayyan call this the majority interpretation. Abd al-Haqq graded one report sound.
  18:46/q09/p5 Good speech or words of goodness. — reasons: Ibn Abbas is reported through al-Awfi and others; al-Razi cites 22:24 about the people of Paradise. Al-Wahidi says good speech is itself remembrance. Al-Baydawi includes it alongside worship.
  18:46/q09/p6 Sincere intentions or actions done purely for God's approval. — reasons: Ibn Ata and al-Hasan say intentions make actions accepted and raised. Al-Sulami also reports sincere acts; al-Qushayri requires truthfulness and absence of self-seeking; Qatada and al-Biqa'i emphasize seeking God's face. Al-Alusi reports love, knowledge, intimacy, sincere tawhid and turning from others as an inward form.
  18:46/q09/p7 Righteous daughters, or reward for raising daughters well. — reasons: Ubayd ibn Umayr reads a contrast with sons; al-Qurtubi cites Aisha's poor mother and daughters, a report of daughters pleading for their father at Hell, and Qatada's account of the daughter promised at 18:81 who bore twelve prophetic sons. Bursevi cites a Prophetic shield-from-Fire promise for good care. A bereaved parent may count a child as an enduring reward, as al-Durr's burial and bereavement reports illustrate.
  18:46/q09/p8 Love of the Prophet's family. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi transmits Abu Abd Allah telling al-Husayn ibn Abd al-Rahman that their love is among the enduring deeds; Tabataba'i reports it as an instance.
  18:46/q09/p9 Night prayer specifically. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi reports this from Abu Abd Allah.
  18:46/q09/p10 Divine unity itself. — reasons: Al-Sulami reports Ja'far al-Sadiq: tawhid remains as long as its professing believer remains.
  18:46/q09/p11 Sincere counsel to other people. — reasons: Al-Sulami reports Yahya ibn Mu'adh's identification.
18:46/q10 Why are the deeds called enduring and righteous? [ٱلْبَٰقِيَٰتُ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتُ · interpretation]
  18:46/q10/p1 Their reward or fruit remains with God after worldly benefits pass away. — reasons: Al-Tabari says each righteous act remains for its doer. Al-Kashshaf, al-Tabrisi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Ibn Ashur, Asad (also citing 19:76), Study Quran (19:76), and Tabataba'i say God preserves its reward forever. Al-Alusi says the reward, rather than the transient act itself, endures.
