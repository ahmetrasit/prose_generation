6:151/q12 What forms of association does the prohibition cover? [أَلَّا تُشْرِكُوا۟ بِهِۦ شَيْـًۭٔا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether shirk denotes worship and belief alone or includes obedience and inward attachment.)
  6:151/q12/p1 It forbids worshipping or assigning any partner to God, including idols, celestial bodies, jinn, and attributed children. — reasons: Razi relates the four groups to Abraham's argument in 6:74, 6:76; Abu Hayyan and Study Quran enumerate the same partners; Qashani reads serving whim and Satan as idolatry.
  6:151/q12/p2 It also forbids obeying another person in disobedience to God. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi gives this as a second sense; Elmalili and Qashani connect illicit religious decrees with unauthorized authority.
  6:151/q12/p3 Inward association includes recognizing anything besides God in the heart. — reasons: Bursevi describes the inward form and quotes a Persian verse urging exclusive unity.
6:151/q13 Why is the ban on association placed first? [أَلَّا تُشْرِكُوا۟ بِهِۦ شَيْـًۭٔا · rhetoric]
  6:151/q13/p1 It is the gravest prohibition and foundation of sound belief and reform. — reasons: Biqai says removing vice precedes virtue; Ibn Ashur says belief opens success; Tabatabai calls shirk the greatest injustice, citing 4:116; Elmalili counts nine prohibitions with tawhid as foundation; Kuranyolu calls unity Islam's first condition.
  6:151/q13/p2 It directly counters the preceding polytheist errors and invented prohibitions. — reasons: Razi's four groups comprise idol, star, jinn and child-attribution worshippers; Qashani says whims had ruled lawful and forbidden.
6:151/q14 What consequences or assurances are attached to avoiding shirk? [أَلَّا تُشْرِكُوا۟ بِهِۦ شَيْـًۭٔا · theology]
  6:151/q14/p1 The duty holds even under severe torture. — reasons: Ibn Abi Hatim and Ibn Kathir report Ubadah's seven counsels and warnings even if burned, cut or crucified.
  6:151/q14/p2 Dying without shirk leaves a person eligible for Paradise and forgiveness of lesser sins. — reasons: Ibn Kathir cites Abu Dharr's Gabriel report, a sacred report promising forgiveness with hope, 4:48 and 4:116, and Ibn Masud; the reports explicitly mention adultery, theft and wine.
6:151/q15 What does kindness to parents require? [وَبِٱلْوَٰلِدَيْنِ إِحْسَٰنًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: The minimum legal prohibition and the full positive extent of iḥsān.)
  6:151/q15/p1 It requires positive care, honor, obedience within God's bounds, gentle speech and fulfillment of their rights. — reasons: Mawardi and Ibn Abi Hatim list rights and truthful speech; Qurtubi adds freeing them from bondage and avoiding domination; Alusi reports Ibn Abbas's ban on harsh replies, looks and raised voice; Ibn Kathir cites 17:23, 31:15, 2:83, and deeds loved by God; Elmalili forbids obeying parents in shirk.
  6:151/q15/p2 Merely refraining from mistreatment is insufficient; the command also prohibits harming or neglecting parents. — reasons: Baydawi and Bursevi emphasize the positive wording; Razi, Nasafi, Biqai, Ibn Ashur, Elmalili and Islahi derive a ban on neglect or scolding. Ibn Kathir notes a variant 'wa-waṣṣā rabbuka' reinforcing the imperative.
  6:151/q15/p3 Filial service is spiritually transformative. — reasons: Bursevi reports a holy man's service to his mother leading to meeting al-Khidr and cites the Persian depiction of Paradise beneath mothers' feet.
6:151/q16 Why does parental kindness immediately follow God's oneness? [أَلَّا تُشْرِكُوا۟ بِهِۦ شَيْـًۭٔا وَبِٱلْوَٰلِدَيْنِ إِحْسَٰنًۭا · rhetoric]
  6:151/q16/p1 Parents are the proximate cause of existence and care, after God as true cause; gratitude and social order require honoring them. — reasons: Jishumi, Tabarsi, Razi, Qashani, Biqai, Bursevi, Alusi and Study Quran cite nurture and parental blessing; Tabatabai says family mercy supports procreation, kinship and society; Ibn Kathir and Study Quran cite repeated pairings with worship. Abu Hayyan points readers to the related explanation in 2:83.
  6:151/q16/p2 Disobedience to parents is the greatest sin after shirk. — reasons: Qashani links ignorance of parents' rights to ignorance of God's; Bursevi makes the ranking explicit; Tabatabai calls it perhaps the greatest thereafter.
6:151/q17 How does إِحْسَٰنًۭا function grammatically? [وَبِٱلْوَٰلِدَيْنِ إِحْسَٰنًۭا · grammar]
  6:151/q17/p1 It is an accusative verbal noun standing for an omitted command to show kindness. — reasons: Nahhas calls it a verbal noun; Wahidi coordinates an understood 'do kindness' with not associating; Ibn Atiyya calls it an absolute object; Qurtubi and Ibn Ashur supply the command. Asad reports commentators' agreement that the command is implied.
6:151/q18 Why is الوالدين used for parents? [ٱلْوَٰلِدَيْنِ · rhetoric]
  6:151/q18/p1 Commands about goodness, prayer and care consistently use الوالدين rather than الأبوين. — reasons: Samarrai adduces 17:23 and 4:36 alongside this verse.
6:151/q19 What child-killing practice and scope does this prohibition address? [وَلَا تَقْتُلُوٓا۟ أَوْلَٰدَكُم · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the immediate Arab practice exhausts the ruling or illustrates its wider scope.)
  6:151/q19/p1 It addresses pre-Islamic infanticide, especially burying daughters from poverty or shame, while wording includes sons and daughters. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya emphasizes walad includes both; Qurtubi and Ibn Kathir report boys also killed; Thalabi and others mention daughters buried alive; Islahi notes custom and honor; Tabatabai adds fathers' mistaken ownership claim.
  6:151/q19/p2 The wording reaches killing an unborn child for economic reasons. — reasons: Elmalili expressly extends it to abortion; Asad says it may refer to abortions; Kuranyolu says the ban may include unborn children.
  6:151/q19/p3 The prohibition prevents parents from destroying God's creation, descendants and children entrusted to them. — reasons: Bursevi cites 11:6; Elmalili says children have a right to life; Islahi calls parents custodians.
6:151/q20 What does إِمْلَٰقٍ mean? [إِمْلَٰقٍ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the root names a state of want, fear of it, hunger, spending, or ruin.)
  6:151/q20/p1 It means poverty, lack or depletion of property and provisions. — reasons: Majaz compares amlaqa with property vanishing; Mawardi gives insolvency and a bankrupt person's flattery; Jishumi and Qurtubi discuss the related root; Shammar allows intransitive impoverishment or transitive destruction; Aws ibn Hajar's verse witnesses wealth consumed by calamities; Ibn Atiyya speculates about black stones.
  6:151/q20/p2 It can mean fear of poverty, the motive for killing. — reasons: Zajjaj, Nahhas, Ibn Abbas and later sources understand the causal wording this way; Bint al-Shati cites Ibn Abbas's poem about generosity amid poverty.
  6:151/q20/p3 It means hunger in the language of Lakhm. — reasons: Muarrij is cited by Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi and Samin.
  6:151/q20/p4 It means expenditure, spending away property or wasteful extravagance. — reasons: Mundhir ibn Said cites a saying ascribed to Ali; Muhammad ibn Nuaym specifies extravagant spending.
  6:151/q20/p5 It can mean ruin or corruption. — reasons: Shammar says amlaqa can destroy possessions; Razi, Wahidi and Samin transmit this extension.
6:151/q21 What does مِنْ إِمْلَٰقٍ identify, and how does 17:31 differ? [مِّنْ إِمْلَٰقٍۢ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether min identifies existing poverty specifically or a broader fear of deprivation.)
  6:151/q21/p1 It gives actual present poverty as the motive; 17:31 names fear of future poverty. — reasons: Abu Hayyan contrasts present need here with future fear there; Ibn Kathir, Biqai, Samin, Ibn Ashur and Samarrai agree. Bint al-Shati contrasts min with khashyat, citing Bukhari and Abu Ubayda on the root.
  6:151/q21/p2 It can include fear that supporting children will make rich or poor parents destitute. — reasons: Zajjaj and Wahidi use fear; Elmalili says the ban applies rich or poor, and infers that if actual poverty does not excuse killing, future fear is even less an excuse.
6:151/q22 Why are parents' provision and children's provision ordered this way? [نَّحْنُ نَرْزُقُكُمْ وَإِيَّاهُمْ · rhetoric] (turns on: The order of the objects and the force of the fronted divine subject.)
  6:151/q22/p1 Fronting 'We' makes God the exclusive provider and interrupts the ban to remove its excuse. — reasons: Ibn Ashur highlights the subject's placement and a shift from third-person Lord to God's direct voice; Razi, Mawardi, Bursevi, Alusi, Elmalili, Tabatabai and Islahi stress divine provision for both generations.
  6:151/q22/p2 Poor parents are reassured first here; in 17:31, those fearing future poverty hear of children's provision first. — reasons: Abu Hayyan, Samin, Ibn Kathir, Burhan, Biqai, Ibn Ashur and Samarrai connect pronoun order with the different motives; Samarrai says children bring their own provision.
6:151/q23 Does the child-killing ban govern contraception or abortion? [وَلَا تَقْتُلُوٓا۟ أَوْلَٰدَكُم مِّنْ إِمْلَٰقٍۢ · law] (turns on: Whether preventing conception is equivalent to killing existing offspring, and how the ban applies to the unborn.)
  6:151/q23/p1 Withdrawal is prohibited or disliked as hidden infanticide. — reasons: Qurtubi reports an analogy from prevention at origin to killing an existing child; a prophetic description of withdrawal as hidden infanticide is used against it, though others take it as dislike. Hasan and Muhammad ibn al-Muthanna read a permissive report as discouragement.
  6:151/q23/p2 Economically motivated abortion falls under the ban, and many contemporary scholars prohibit abortion
