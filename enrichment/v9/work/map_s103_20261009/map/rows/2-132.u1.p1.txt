2:132/q10 What is reported about Jacob, his sons, and their lives? [وَيَعْقُوبُ بَنِيهِ · reports] (turns on: The biographical reports and the chronology relevant to Jacob hearing Abraham's counsel.)
  2:132/q10/p1 Jacob instructed twelve sons, the tribal ancestors, and later died in Egypt with burial in Canaan beside Isaac. — reasons: Sources list the twelve sons by name. Qurṭubī and Bursevī give Jacob 147 years and Joseph's transfer of his body; Ibn ʿĀshūr names Rebekah as his mother, Israel as his other name and Machpelah as the burial site. Bursevī adds that Esau and Jacob died the same day and were buried together.
  2:132/q10/p2 Jacob gathered his sons after seeing Egyptians worship idols, fire, and cattle. — reasons: Al-Kalbī reports this as the setting for his question about what they would worship after him; Bursevī repeats the Egyptian idolatry background.
  2:132/q10/p3 Jacob lived in Abraham's lifetime. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr cites the promise of Isaac and Jacob at 11:71 and grants of Jacob in 29:27 and 21:72; he also discusses Jerusalem's building and a forty-year interval from the Sacred Mosque, rejecting Ibn Ḥibbān's chronology. Ibn ʿĀshūr agrees.
  2:132/q10/p4 Jacob was born only after Abraham died. — reasons: Qushayrī, as quoted by Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr, uses this against the accusative reading; Ibn Kathīr says this requires sound proof.
2:132/q11 How does يَٰبَنِىَّ introduce the testament, and what is its reading? [يَٰبَنِىَّ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the speech after the testament requires an implied saying verb or is governed by the speech sense of enjoining; a variant supplies an.)
  2:132/q11/p1 An implied verb of saying introduces the quoted address. — reasons: Basran grammarians require 'he said' because a full sentence cannot normally be a direct object; Akhfash and Naḥḥās offer this. If Jacob is accusative the implied verb is singular, while nominative allows dual, according to Ālūsī. Farra, Ṭabarī, Thaʿlabī, Jishumī and others compare Qur'anic speech constructions, including 4:11 and 33:29.
  2:132/q11/p2 No separate saying verb is needed because enjoining itself is a speech act. — reasons: Kufans connect the quotation directly to waṣṣā; al-Ṭabarī treats omitted an as semantic syntax, illustrated by 4:11 and poetry. Al-Farrāʾ rejects the claim that an was intended and elided: such speech clauses may appear with or without it.
  2:132/q11/p3 Ubayy, Ibn Masʿūd and al-Ḍaḥḥāk read 'an yā banī' with an explicit explanatory particle. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ, Thaʿlabī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, Samin and Ālūsī cite the reading. Samin says it supports an explanatory clause rather than a verbal noun; those denying explanatory an call it redundant. Ālūsī notes that it can remove Basrans' need for an implied verb.
2:132/q12 What is the vocative and possessive form of يَٰبَنِىَّ? [يَٰبَنِىَّ · grammar]
  2:132/q12/p1 It means 'O my sons'; the possessive yāʾ is opened to avoid two consecutive quiescent letters. — reasons: Naḥḥās compares 14:22; Qurṭubī transmits his explanation. Thaʿlabī and Baghawī gloss the address.
2:132/q13 Why say 'my sons' in the address? [يَٰبَنِىَّ · rhetoric]
  2:132/q13/p1 The tender kinship address encourages acceptance of the chosen religion. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says it stirs them to Islam and makes departure from God's chosen way irrational.
2:132/q14 Why does إِنَّ take kasra here? [إِنَّ · grammar]
  2:132/q14/p1 It starts the quoted speech conveyed by waṣṣā/awṣā, either directly or after an implicit 'he said.' — reasons: Zajjāj, Abū Isḥāq and Ibn ʿAṭiyya explain the kasra by reported speech. Naḥḥās and Qurṭubī allow an omitted speech verb. The Basran/Kufan analyses of the vocative apply to the ensuing quotation.
2:132/q15 What does ٱصْطَفَىٰ mean, and how was the religion chosen? [ٱصْطَفَىٰ · meaning] (turns on: Whether choice is explained through the word's sense, proofs clarifying the religion, or divine enablement.)
  2:132/q15/p1 It means choosing or selecting the purest religion for them. — reasons: Majāz derives it from ṣafwa, purity; Qurṭubī cites a rajaz line for the form, and Samin traces its alif through yāʾ from a root waw. Jishumī explains selection either as purifying and distinguishing it through proofs or simply as choosing Islam.
  2:132/q15/p2 God's choosing includes establishing proofs, calling them, and enabling their acceptance. — reasons: Rāzī adds clear proofs and protection from other religions; Kashshāf, Nasafī and Ālūsī say God enabled them to follow the finest faith. Biqāʿī calls this mercy that spares a search; Qushayrī and Abū Ḥayyān say the announcement encourages obedience and hope of divine aid.
2:132/q16 What does لَكُمُ contribute? [لَكُمُ · grammar]
  2:132/q16/p1 It means 'for your sake,' without implying the religion is pure only for these addressees. — reasons: Samin glosses lakum as li-ajlikum; Ālūsī says Islam is inherently the purest religion, rather than only pure for them.
2:132/q17 Which religion is meant by ٱلدِّينَ? [ٱلدِّينَ · referent] (turns on: The known historical religion of Islam and an inward account of its divine unity.)
  2:132/q17/p1 It is the known religion of Islam, the upright monotheist way, selected over other religions. — reasons: Ṭabarī says the definite article marks the religion familiar from ancestral counsel. Wāḥidī, Jishumī, Ṭabrisī, Qurṭubī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Samin take the article as referential, rather than generic. Wāḥidī calls it ḥanīfiyya; Qushayrī says legal practices may vary while monotheist foundation stays one. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites 3:19 for inherited Islam; Study Quran distinguishes Islam as Muhammad's proper name and a primordial universal orientation, citing 3:19, 3:85, 5:3 and 6:125.
  2:132/q17/p2 In an inward reading, it is the divine religion of unity with God, beyond a separate self. — reasons: Qāshānī reads the enjoined word as unity and says God's religion and essence are one.
2:132/q18 What does the prohibition فَلَا تَمُوتُنَّ require? [فَلَا تَمُوتُنَّ · interpretation] (turns on: How a command expressed at the involuntary event of death applies to a person's controllable religious state.)
  2:132/q18/p1 Remain in Islam at every moment, so death finds one Muslim. — reasons: Ṭabarī, Zajjāj and many others say death is uncontrollable and its time unknown; the command therefore addresses the lifelong state. Wāḥidī and Jishumī compare 'do not let me see you here.' Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān call it concise admonition that also warns of sudden death. Rāzī says delay may leave one outside Islam at death; Abū Ḥayyān says avoid acts that lead to that end. Kashshāf and Bayḍāwī compare 'die a martyr' and prayer conditioned on humility; the state determines the honor or worth of death.
  2:132/q18/p2 The needed state is inward faith alone when bodily deeds are no longer possible at death. — reasons: Ālūsī reports this proposal, based on a Prophetic supplication for living Muslims and faithful dead, but finds it unpersuasive.
  2:132/q18/p3 An inward reading sees natural death of ignorance replaced by death to self and life through God until bodily death. — reasons: Qāshānī explains the injunction as remaining alive through God.
2:132/q19 What forms and usages of māt are cited? [تَمُوتُنَّ · meaning]
  2:132/q19/p1 Arabic uses mawta, mayta and mamāta, with related expressions for lifelessness and death. — reasons: Wāḥidī transmits al-Farrāʾ's forms and usages for barren land, mass death, selling livestock as against other goods, and a person with a lifeless heart.
2:132/q20 How is فَلَا تَمُوتُنَّ formed grammatically? [فَلَا تَمُوتُنَّ · grammar]
  2:132/q20/p1 It is a prohibitive jussive with emphatic heavy nūn; a wāw is lost at the meeting of two sukūns. — reasons: Naḥḥās and Qurṭubī give the jussive and deletion; Samin derives it from tamūtūnanna, with the indicative nūn dropped and the ḍamma left as a trace.
2:132/q21 Why is the death command worded this way? [فَلَا تَمُوتُنَّ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the wording is a direct prohibition of a state at death or a metaphorical negation of the person.)
  2:132/q21/p1 It makes lifelong constancy urgent by presenting death's uncertain arrival and the disgrace of dying outside Islam. — reasons: Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān stress brevity and unknown timing; Kashshāf, Bayḍāwī and Samin say the wording judges death outside faith as devoid of good. Al-Fāḍil al-Yamanī calls it metaphorically negating the person to negate a state, but Ālūsī objects that negating the person would negate all attributes.
  2:132/q21/p2 The wording metaphorically negates the person to negate one particular state. — reasons: Al-Fāḍil al-Yamanī proposes this analysis; Ālūsī objects that negating a person negates all attributes, rather than just one state.
2:132/q22 What does مُسْلِمُونَ mean here? [مُّسْلِمُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether Muslim describes faith and obedience generally, inward entrustment, or especially good expectation of God at death.)
  2:132/q22/p1 Believers who submit obediently to God and remain sincere monotheists. — reasons: Muqātil explains 'submit' as sincerity. Thaʿlabī, Jishumī, Baghawī and Qurṭubī transmit variants 'believers,' 'sincere,' or 'entrusting themselves to God'; Biqāʿī says wholly surrendered. Wāḥidī reports Ibn ʿAbbās's uncompromising rejection of associating anything with God, even under torture.
  2:132/q22/p2 It means entrusting oneself to God's command at all times. — reasons: Tustarī says continuous surrender and entrustment distinguish those who follow divine command from those left to reasoning; the wording admits a particular or general sense.
