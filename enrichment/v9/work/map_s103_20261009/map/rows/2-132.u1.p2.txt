2:132/q23 What is the grammatical function of وَأَنتُم مُّسْلِمُونَ? [وَأَنتُم مُّسْلِمُونَ · grammar]
  2:132/q23/p1 It is a nominal subject-predicate clause in the circumstantial accusative, within an exception of states. — reasons: Naḥḥās, Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Qurṭubī state the circumstantial analysis; Samin calls it a total exception of states governed by the preceding verb.
2:132/q24 What does the verse teach about final states and human action? [* · theology]
  2:132/q24/p1 Steadfast practice and intention seek a Muslim end; deeds are judged by their endings. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr says people generally die and rise in the condition they followed, and God eases good intentions; he reconciles a sound final-destiny hadith by versions saying contrary deeds only appeared so to people and cites 92:5–10. Abū Ḥayyān explicitly cites the judgment of deeds by endings. Tustarī says piety is required according to capacity under 3:102 and is no license to abandon it.
  2:132/q24/p2 The verse indicates the prophets' faithful descendants and divine selection are fitting to protected prophets. — reasons: Jishumī says Abraham accepted truth when clear, resisted habit and desire, and was chosen for obeying well; the verse indicates prophetic protection from sin and that descendants followed Islam.
2:132/q25 What inward or spiritual lessons are drawn from the testament? [* · inward]
  2:132/q25/p1 Surrender calls for entrusting oneself to God, dying to self, and inward piety that transforms outward life. — reasons: Sulamī links Abraham's charge to his unhesitating submission during his son's sacrifice until ransom. Tustarī reports that reforming the inward reforms the outward; Godfearing people become mercy for wrongdoers, and 49:13 promises honor, nearness, Paradise and success through piety. Qāshānī reads Islam as unity and life through God.
2:132/q26 What counsel for parents and dependents follows from the verse? [* · law] (turns on: Whether the testament belongs specifically to the deathbed or also to other moments of feared loss.)
  2:132/q26/p1 People approaching death should counsel dependents to keep religion, obedience and piety. — reasons: Ṭabrisī states the lesson. Al-Ḥasan says Jacob had no bequest but piety, implying deathbed testament. Ibn ʿĀshūr says counsel is given when loss is feared, especially death or departure. Abū Ḥayyān cites the Prophet's challenge to a father who gifted only one child and wanted equal dutifulness.
  2:132/q26/p2 A testament may also be delivered before another feared loss of religious teaching. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the fathers may have spoken at death, as 2:133 suggests, or at another time.
2:132/q27 What occasion or challenge is reported for the verse? [* · reports]
  2:132/q27/p1 It answered Jews who claimed Jacob enjoined Judaism on his sons at death. — reasons: Bayḍāwī transmits this report; Biqāʿī links the following verses to refuting Jewish and Christian claims that the patriarchs followed their later religions.
2:132/q28 How does this verse connect with its surrounding passage? [* · links]
  2:132/q28/p1 It continues the account of Abraham's submission and care for descendants, then presents the religion transmitted through Jacob against later scriptural claims. — reasons: Jishumī says the sequence moves from concern and prayers for descendants' welfare to religion. Biqāʿī places the testament after Abraham's leadership and before refutation of later Jewish and Christian identifications. Abū Ḥayyān says the prophets' call continues and praises the discourse's coherence, comparing the challenge at 17:88. Elmalılı says it clarifies Israel's brothers and Islam's value; Study Quran traces submission from 2:128 onward.
  2:132/q28/p2 The 2:133 account of Jacob's own address supports understanding him as another testator. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr explicitly adduces the next verse, and Ibn ʿĀshūr cites it as evidence for a possible deathbed setting.
2:132/q29 What other reports about Abraham's era accompany the discussion? [* · reports]
  2:132/q29/p1 Chronologies and genealogies are reported with differing figures and textual lineages. — reasons: Thaʿlabī transmits a prophetic report of eight thousand prophets, half from Israel. Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān date Abraham's death about 2,600 years before Muhammad, while noting a Jewish chronology 400 years shorter. Biqāʿī gives the Torah lineage through Terah and Noah and notes Luke's extra Cainan.
  2:132/q29/p2 The Torah account traces Abraham's migration, covenants, children and descendants. — reasons: Biqāʿī reports Terah leaving Ur for Haran, God's call to Abram at seventy-five, travel to Canaan and Egypt, Lot's separation and rescue, a star-numbered progeny and promised land, the four-hundred-year enslavement prediction, Hagar and Ishmael, Abraham's renaming and covenant at ninety-nine, circumcision, Sarah and Isaac, Ishmael's blessing and twelve princes, and Hagar's later wilderness ordeal. He gives ages eighty-six at Ishmael's birth, thirteen at his circumcision, and day eight for Isaac's. He reconciles a Torah exile account with the sound report of nursing Ishmael at the House by possible miraculous travel. He and earlier scholars read the promises about blessing through Abraham and Ishmael's hand as prophecies of Muhammad.
2:132/q30 What does the name ٱللَّهَ involve in lexical analysis? [ٱللَّهَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the divine name is derived and what function al- has.)
  2:132/q30/p1 The article replaces a deleted hamza from ilāh. — reasons: Al-Itqān reports Sībawayh and al-Fārisī: the article's fixed form and hamza's cut pronunciation support the analysis.
  2:132/q30/p2 The article marks glorifying definiteness added to a form from lāh. — reasons: Al-Itqān reports this alternative.
  2:132/q30/p3 The al- is inseparable from the name and is not definiteness. — reasons: Al-Itqān attributes this to a group.
  2:132/q30/p4 The form grows from written hāʾ, possessive lām, and exaltatory al-. — reasons: Al-Itqān attributes this derivation to some.
  2:132/q30/p5 The name is indivisible and has no prior derivation. — reasons: Al-Itqān attributes this to al-Khalīl and others.
2:132/q31 How are feminine and diminutive forms illustrated beside the address 'my sons'? [يَٰبَنِىَّ · grammar]
  2:132/q31/p1 Sībawayh drops an added feminine tāʾ in diminutives and substitutes original feminine hāʾ; some Arabic usage varies. — reasons: He exemplifies bint → bunayya and ukht → ukhayya; other Arabs say hunayha for hunt and hunayya for hun, with hāʾ substituting for yāʾ as in dhih.
2:132/q32 What do comparisons of smooth and subtle expression illustrate in the accompanying material? [* · rhetoric]
  2:132/q32/p1 Smooth wording is clear and easy on the tongue; a subtle comparison asks the reader to infer a deeper relation. — reasons: Jurjānī distinguishes ordinary praise of transparent, familiar diction from a refined ring image: Kaʿb described al-Muhallab's sons as day guardians and night cavalry, united like a ring without a discernible end. The image needs discernment beyond immediate hearing.
2:132/q33 What broader remarks on Qur'anic letters accompany the material? [* · sciences]
  2:132/q33/p1 Common opening letters can represent the whole and carry articulatory significance. — reasons: Al-Burhān says alif, lām, mīm are common and span deep throat, tongue tip and closed lips; the testimony of faith similarly entails other duties. It also contrasts tāʾ's five articulatory qualities with sīn and hāʾ.
2:132/q34 How do the accompanying recitation discussions distinguish stopping and pausing? [* · sciences] (turns on: Whether a brief sakt requires specific transmission or can clarify an ending more generally.)
  2:132/q34/p1 Verse fragments should not be left unfinished; qaṭʿ ends recitation at verse end, waqf pauses for breath and resumption, and sakt interrupts briefly without breath. — reasons: Al-Nashr and al-Itqān transmit Ibn Abī al-Hudhayl's report that predecessors disliked abandoning part of a verse; al-Jazarī and al-Suyūṭī infer the Companions. Waqf may occur at verse ends or inside verses, never inside a word or joined spelling; a basmala accompanies a new sura. Earlier scholars used the terms interchangeably, later ones distinguished them. Hamza, al-Ashnānī, al-Kisāʾī, Ibn Ghalbūn, Makkī, Ibn Shurayḥ, Qutayba, al-Dānī and al-Jaʿbarī give varying descriptions of sakt's shortness.
  2:132/q34/p2 Sakt is allowed only where transmitted recitation supports an intended meaning. — reasons: Al-Itqān reports Ibn al-Jazarī's preferred restriction.
  2:132/q34/p3 Sakt may be made at verse endings while continuing, to clarify meaning. — reasons: Al-Itqān reports this alternative and notes that some understand a transmitted hadith accordingly.
2:132/q35 What other word-form observations occur in the accompanying material? [* · grammar]
  2:132/q35/p1 Tanwīn may replace an added verse-final lengthening letter; structural alteration is another kind of word change. — reasons: Al-Itqān cites Ibn Hishām as an alternative to Zamakhsharī's analysis of verse-end tanwīn; it also illustrates structural alteration with Ṭūr Sīnīn and an original Sīnā.
2:132/q36 What levels of Islam and righteousness are distinguished in the accompanying discussion? [* · theology]
  2:132/q36/p1 Islam and faith have levels; Abraham's request for righteousness may concern a higher rank. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī says earlier reports describe the first level. He cites 21:72, 2:130, 29:27 and 16:122 for Abraham's request to join predecessors and God's answer in the Hereafter; he suggests the higher company could be Muhammad and his purified family, citing 7:196.
