  10:31/q08/p1 Life first denotes the principle of knowledge and power in animals and also includes vital processes in plants. — reasons: Tabatabai starts with animal life and extends the inquiry to plants; he reports narrower accounts of animals alone or animals and plants emerging from nonliving matter, with dead parts separated from living organisms.

10:31/q09 Why is the emergence of living from dead and dead from living phrased this way? [يُخْرِجُ ٱلْحَىَّ مِنَ ٱلْمَيِّتِ وَيُخْرِجُ ٱلْمَيِّتَ مِنَ ٱلْحَىِّ · rhetoric]
  10:31/q09/p1 The reversal of opposites displays divine power and heightens wonder. — reasons: Abu Hayyan calls it the production of opposites from one another. Biqai sees a move from deficiency to completion. Ibn Ashur calls the reciprocal wording a riddle and antithesis, with no hidden allusion such as one might find in 3:27. Elmalili reasons that opposed transformations reveal active power beyond nature.
10:31/q10 How is al-mayyit read in both occurrences? [ٱلْمَيِّتِ ٱلْحَىِّ · readings]
  10:31/q10/p1 The light form mayt drops gemination of ya, including the accusative mayta. — reasons: Qiraat-ER records the reduction in both forms; some grammarians distinguish mayt as already dead from mayyit as dying or recently dead.
10:31/q11 What does managing the affair include? [يُدَبِّرُ ٱلْأَمْرَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the question asks for the ultimate source of governance or permits a subordinate cosmic manager.)
  10:31/q11/p1 God alone determines and carries out the affairs of all creation. — reasons: Mujahid says He decides and executes; Tabari includes heaven, earth, what is in them and people; Wahidi includes this world and the next. Jishumi names day, night, seasons, life and death. Razi adds upper and lower worlds and spirits and bodies. Ibn Kathir says no one can overturn His judgment and cites 55:29 for creatures' dependence. Ibn Atiyya says this governance rests on complete eternal knowledge, without thought or change. Elmalili stresses ordering the universe by command; Tabatabai says all delegated-seeming management ultimately ends with God.
  10:31/q11/p2 God initiates and continually renews the changing turns of cosmic affairs. — reasons: Sulami reports this from an unnamed source and from al-Wasiti; al-Wasiti says it defeats claims of independent agency based on one's own acts and work.
  10:31/q11/p3 A cosmic pole may manage the world and hold up the sky by God's permission. — reasons: Alusi reports some mystics appealing to 79:5 for a nondivine manager; some claim the pole and God differ only in consideration because of the pole's perfect nearness. Critics call this unity of being, rejected by most theologians and some Sufis including al-Imam al-Rabbani. Alusi argues that comparison with angels or Jesus acting by permission fails because the question asks for the ultimate source.
10:31/q12 Why does the question about managing the affair follow the specific signs? [وَمَن يُدَبِّرُ ٱلْأَمْرَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the coordination is described chiefly as generalization after specifics or as emphatic joining of related expressions.)
  10:31/q12/p1 It generalizes from particular powers to God's unlimited governance. — reasons: Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Biqai, Alusi and Ibn Ashur describe this widening. Alusi says all affairs originate with God and return to Him though humans cannot know their details; Ibn Ashur compares 51:21–22. Suyuti says naming a particular first emphasizes it, and rejects denial of the general-after-particular construction. Study Quran points to 10:3.
  10:31/q12/p2 The coordinated expressions can also serve emphasis through conjunction of near synonyms. — reasons: Burhan reports Zamakhshari grouping the governance question with the provision question as coordinated expressions. Burhan says waw can join synonyms or near synonyms to intensify a statement, particularly differently worded pairs, and cites 3:146, 20:112, 20:77, 74:22, 12:86, 74:28, 4:171, 20:107, 43:80, 5:48, 17:52, 33:67, 33:12 and 35:35.
10:31/q13 Who answers 'Allah,' and why? [فَسَيَقُولُونَ ٱللَّهُ · referent] (turns on: Whether the forecast is restricted to the actual polytheist audience or also states what any thoughtful person must recognize.)
  10:31/q13/p1 The polytheists, particularly the Meccans or Quraysh, admit God performs these acts. — reasons: Muqatil names Quraysh; Razi, Ibn Atiyya, Ibn Kathir, Biqai, Alusi, Tabatabai and others say the admission is unavoidable or immediate because the signs are plain. Qushayri says their words are conjecture without inward conviction. Razi says they sought idols' nearness or intercession while knowing idols cannot help or harm. Asad says they assigned divine or semidivine qualities to intermediaries (7:172); Islahi adds supposed favored delegates with powers over sustenance and children. Study Quran reports R's comparison to 29:61, 29:63, 31:25, 39:38, 43:87, 39:3 and 10:18 and says many saw Allah as head of a pantheon.
  10:31/q13/p2 Any honest and reflective rational person would answer God, even if the polytheists did not. — reasons: Qurtubi says creation of heaven and earth is near self-evident to reason; if the idolaters deny the premise, the proof still reaches a thoughtful person.
10:31/q14 How does the first fa connect the command to ask with their answer? [فَسَيَقُولُونَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the condition is inherent in the command or supplied in the grammar.)
  10:31/q14/p1 The command to ask itself functions as a condition: if he asks, they will answer. — reasons: Ibn Ashur compares 17:51–52; without the command-as-condition sense, the fa would be absent, as in 23:84–85. He says supplying an explicit conditional is close in meaning but differs as a grammatical rather than semantic estimate.
  10:31/q14/p2 An implied conditional can be supplied before the answer. — reasons: Ibn Ashur reports this from grammarians and compares command constructions in 14:31 and 17:53; he sees little difference in sense from treating the command itself as conditional.
10:31/q15 What is the syntax of Allah in their answer? [ٱللَّهُ · grammar]
  10:31/q15/p1 Allah is the subject of an omitted predicate: God alone performs these acts. — reasons: Alusi gives the ellipsis and says the polytheists reply without hesitation because the evidence cannot be denied.
10:31/q16 What does the final question ask them to do? [أَفَلَا تَتَّقُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether taqwa is a rebuke calling for fear and abandonment of shirk, a call to heed the warning, a specific practice, or a question about ability.)
  10:31/q16/p1 Fear God's punishment and abandon associating partners with Him. — reasons: Muqatil, Tabari, Wahidi, Jishumi, Baghawi, Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, Razi, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Abu Hayyan, Ibn Kathir, Biqai and others derive this from their admission that only God performs the acts. Qurtubi includes retribution here and hereafter. Alusi says the interrogative rebukes their actual failure and implies 'you know this, yet do not fear.' Study Quran reports Z linking it to their error and coming punishment, with 7:65, 23:23, 23:32, 23:87 and 26:11. Islahi adds fear of false attributions and denial of God's attributes.
  10:31/q16/p2 Take warning and stop. — reasons: Ibn Jawzi reports this from Ibn Abbas; Abu Hayyan offers it as an alternative to fearing punishment.
  10:31/q16/p3 Guard against Hell through the five daily prayers. — reasons: Ibn Abi Hatim reports this specific gloss from al-Dahhak.
  10:31/q16/p4 The question may ask whether they are able to fear God. — reasons: Ibn Abi Hatim reports the alternative gloss from Mujahid.
10:31/q17 How are the fa and omitted object in the final rebuke understood? [فَقُلْ أَفَلَا تَتَّقُونَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the omitted complement is a single object, as Alusi insists, or the analysis implies two.)
  10:31/q17/p1 The fa draws the rebuke from their admission, with a further explanatory fa and an understood object of fear. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says 'then say' follows their answer and the last fa draws its consequence. He takes God as the unspoken object; Alusi takes God's punishment as the one omitted object and supplies the premise 'you know this, yet do not fear.' Alusi rejects the implication in al-Qadi's analysis that tattaqun governs two objects.
  10:31/q17/p2 The construction might imply that tattaqun governs two objects. — reasons: Alusi ascribes this apparent implication to al-Qadi but rejects it: here the verb governs one omitted object.
10:31/q18 What overall proof do the verse's questions establish? [* · theology]
  10:31/q18/p1 Their acknowledged dependence on God's exclusive powers proves His sole lordship and right to worship, and rebukes shirk. — reasons: Zajjaj says only God can provide, make senses and life, and govern. Ibn Atiyya calls the proof inescapable; Qurtubi says reason recognizes a creator even if idolaters resist. Razi says the argument follows an exposure of idolatry; Abu Hayyan calls its evidence undeniable. Biqai and Jishumi also find a proof of resurrection. Tabatabai says it meets idolaters' belief in separately managed domains and proves all management ends with God, which entails exclusive worship. Asad says intermediaries empty belief in one God of spiritual value. Islahi says the admitted creator and giver of life must be sole Lord, defeating denial of resurrection too. Study Quran's introduction adds false gods cannot create or guide.
