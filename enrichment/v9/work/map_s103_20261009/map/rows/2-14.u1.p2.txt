2:14/q25 What exactly are they mocking? [إِنَّمَا نَحْنُ مُسْتَهْزِءُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the immediate target is people or the faith they profess.)
  2:14/q25/p1 Muhammad and his Companions or believers, through a false display of faith. — reasons: Ibn Abbas, Ibn Atiyya, al-Tabrisi and Bursevi identify the targets. Al-Wahidi says the outer show differs from inward belief; al-Tabari's Ibn Jurayj report says hypocrites claim fellowship when believers prosper then mock privately. The declarers seek to assure leaders of continued loyalty.
  2:14/q25/p2 Islam itself, whose validity they reject while claiming loyalty to Judaism. — reasons: Al-Nasafi makes the object Islam and says ridicule confirms disbelief; al-Qurtubi says the term means denying the summons to belief.
2:14/q26 How is the hamza of مستهزئون read or softened? [مُسْتَهْزِءُونَ · readings] (turns on: The realization or deletion of hamza and the preceding vowel, especially at pause.)
  2:14/q26/p1 The hamza is pronounced or softened to an intermediate sound following Sibawayh. — reasons: Al-Nahhas and al-Samin describe a sound between hamza and wāw; Ibn Atiyya notes ḍamma over wāw and Abu Ali's report of softening; al-Tabrisi explains relevant vowel environments, and Abu Hayyan says most readers follow Sibawayh.
  2:14/q26/p2 The hamza becomes a full yāʾ: مستهزيون. — reasons: Al-Akhfash argues from preceding kasra and analogy with السفهاء; Ibn Jinni says the ḍamma remembers the original hamza. Ibn Yazid rejects the analogy because السفهاء requires a wāw but this form does not. Al-Tabrisi says al-Akhfash rejects intermediate sound as unparalleled.
  2:14/q26/p3 The hamza is omitted, yielding مستهزون with a raised zāy. — reasons: Al-Nahhas says the replaced-hamza form matches the codex; Abu Jaʿfar deletes dammated hamza after kasra before wāw. Hamza's pause form is reported by al-Samin and al-Nashr; Ibn al-Jazari defends it as strong against al-Sakhawi and al-Fasi, distinguishing weak deletion that retains kasra. Qiraat-ER calls omission streamlined and common; al-Tabrisi mentions unspecified readers.
  2:14/q26/p4 Deletion with retained kasra before wāw is weak. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari contrasts this with Hamza's sound pause performance.
2:14/q27 What other hamza substitution rules are adduced with this reading? [مُسْتَهْزِءُونَ · readings]
  2:14/q27/p1 Hamza may be transferred or replaced according to several route-specific patterns. — reasons: Al-Nashr details al-Asbahani/Warsh variants in فبأي, disputed بأيكم المفتون, Abu al-Ala's شانيك, al-Hudhali's لنبوينهم, al-Azraq's لئلا, Ibn Wardan's المنشئون, and disputed scope for نبيوني and related forms. Ibn Mihran omits al-Asbahani's replacement. The treatise also lists transfer, uniform ḍamma, and selective transfer before final hamza, al-Ala's extra alif, Ibn Malik's unattested recitational pattern, and Kufan analogies; al-Farra, Abu Zayd and al-Zajjaj cite yāʾ-based verb forms including يستهزون.
2:14/q28 Who spoke the professions in the reported incident? [قَالُوٓا۟ ءَامَنَّا · referent] (turns on: Whether the speakers are Ibn Ubayy's circle, Jewish hypocrites, or hypocrites generally.)
  2:14/q28/p1 Ibn Ubayy spoke for his companions. — reasons: Elmalili says the earlier singular wording of the incident supports this representation.
  2:14/q28/p2 Jewish hypocrites, including People of the Book, were among the speakers. — reasons: Al-Tabari transmits reports of Jews who professed belief and then consulted privately, including Jews who first believed and became hypocrites; al-Hasan says hypocrites and some People of the Book; al-Durr reports Jewish hypocrites professing the companions' religion.
  2:14/q28/p3 Hypocrites generally are the speakers. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi identifies the verse's subjects simply as hypocrites; Abu Hayyan reports a general occasion view.
2:14/q29 What occasion of revelation or incident is reported for this verse? [* · reports] (turns on: Competing incident reports and al-Alusi's assessment of their chains.)
  2:14/q29/p1 Ibn Ubayy praised Abu Bakr, Umar and Ali publicly, then urged his companions to imitate his deceit; Muslims informed the Prophet and the verse descended. — reasons: Ibn Abbas reports the scene through al-Thaʿlabi, al-Wahidi, Ibn al-Jawzi and al-Durr; Baydawi, Bursevi, Elmalili and Study Quran retell it. Details vary: Ali rebukes him; followers praise his maneuver; he calls the Companions fools; one version has him assure Saʿd b. ʿUbada of support and then urge ancestral religion. Al-Alusi says he knows no sound occasion report and rejects the al-Suddi al-saghir chain as fabricated.
  2:14/q29/p2 Jewish men professed the Companions' religion, then reassured their own leaders of continued loyalty and mockery. — reasons: Ibn Abbas reports this through al-Tabari and al-Durr; another report makes them hypocrites among the People of the Book.
  2:14/q29/p3 These verses concern hypocrites generally, without a specific incident. — reasons: Abu Hayyan reports this exegetical view; al-Alusi says no sound specific occasion is known.
  2:14/q29/p4 God described a people before they existed, revealing unseen knowledge. — reasons: Abu Hayyan reports this third account.
2:14/q30 What other people and incidents illustrate the hypocrites' circle? [* · reports]
  2:14/q30/p1 Ibn Ubayy headed a circle including named associates; Qazman and al-Jadd b. Qays illustrate outwardly deceptive conduct. — reasons: Al-Biqaʿi recounts Qazman's fierce fighting at Uhud followed by confession of tribal zeal and suicide; al-Jadd's request to avoid temptation is linked to 9:49; Ibn Ubayy's boast is linked to 63:8. Wadiʿa, Malik, Suwayd and Daʿis are associated with his group and 59:11.
2:14/q31 What legal weight should an outward profession of Islam have when hypocrisy is suspected? [قَالُوٓا۟ ءَامَنَّا · law] (turns on: Whether concealed disbelief can be proved and punished despite an outward Islamic profession.)
  2:14/q31/p1 A judge treats the outward profession as Islam and does not execute on concealed disbelief or private knowledge. — reasons: The Maliki account says the Prophet's restraint shows a judge cannot rule from private knowledge, with 33:60–61 applying to open hypocrisy; Qatada gives the same threshold. Al-Shafiʿi, Ahl al-Raʾy and al-Tabari spare an accused zindiq who denies the charge and professes Islam. Shafiʿi's school says outward Islam erases prior wrongs, citing 63:1; Hanbali and hadith authorities cite a prophetic report requiring testimony and prayer and the hope that descendants would believe. Al-Tabari says God alone judges hidden states. Ibn Atiyya also says the Prophet avoided seeming to kill his companions.
  2:14/q31/p2 A present-day zindiq may be executed on testimony without an offer of repentance. — reasons: Malik equates him with the Prophet's hypocrite; Ismaʿil al-Qadi says two witnesses would have led to executions in named cases. Ibn Atiyya counters that their reports implied rather than explicitly proved disbelief; the Malikis answer that 63:1 accused hypocrites generally but not individuals.
2:14/q32 What does this conduct teach about hypocrisy and company? [* · interpretation]
  2:14/q32/p1 It condemns religious hypocrisy, ostentation, mockery of truth, and allegiance to unbelief. — reasons: Al-Jishumi states these offenses; Elmalili says contemptuous mockery of faith entails unbelief and that the verse compresses hypocrites' psychology. Ibn Hisham compares secret confidants apart from God at 9:16 with their hidden allegiance. Kuran Yolu says guidance depends on education and influences, and warns about satanic helpers among humans and jinn.
2:14/q33 How does this verse connect with the surrounding description of hypocrites? [* · links]
  2:14/q33/p1 It details their dealings with believers and unbelievers and introduces their concealed conduct. — reasons: Baydawi and Bursevi say it prepares the account of hypocrisy rather than repeating prior wording; al-Alusi says it adds guile and situational motive. Ibn Ashur links the clauses to 2:12–13 and 2:8: meeting believers prepares for the main disclosure when they withdraw; the paired clauses independently enumerate their failings.
2:14/q34 What inward reading is given of their divided allegiance? [* · inward]
  2:14/q34/p1 A weak innate light permits contact with believers, while stronger acquired darkness pulls them to unbelievers. — reasons: Al-Qashani says they belittle luminous people because darkness dominates and only light recognizes light's worth; al-Mathnawi couplets cited by Bursevi commend loving and accompanying people of heart, whose worth God knows.
2:14/q35 How is God's later 'mockery' related to theirs? [* · links]
  2:14/q35/p1 God repays their mockery by exposing or turning their deception against them. — reasons: Al-Biqaʿi says they receive outward Muslim treatment while tasting humiliation in both worlds; al-Harali says the imperfect suggests continuing recompense with room for repentance. Kuran Yolu says God knows their hidden state, informs the Prophet, and their own traps make them objects of ridicule.
2:14/q36 What adjacent recitation and parsing discussions accompany this verse? [* · readings] (turns on: The notes include several neighboring forms under the same cited verse heading.)
  2:14/q36/p1 Adjacent passive forms admit kasra, hinted ḍamma, and other dialectal forms, with readers differing. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi reports al-Kisai and Yaʿqub's initial ḍamma in listed forms, Nafiʿ and Ibn Amir in subsets, and the remainder's kasra; he explains vowel movement and retained trace of original form. Abu Hayyan contrasts Quraysh/Kinana kasra with other tribes' ḍamma and a third unattested wāw dialect.
