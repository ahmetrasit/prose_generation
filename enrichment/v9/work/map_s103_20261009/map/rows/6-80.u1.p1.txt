6:80/q11 How is the final yāʾ of هَدَىٰنِ read? [هَدَىٰنِ · readings] (turns on: Which reader or transmission is followed, and whether the word is connected or paused.)
  6:80/q11/p1 Some retain the speaker's yāʾ in connection, pause, or both, with transmission-dependent variation. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid says Abu Amr and some Nafi transmissions retain it in connection but omit it at pause; Jishumi includes Abu Jafar, Nafi, Abu Amr and Yaqub among retainers. Nasafi reports Abu Amr retaining at both. Nashr reports Yaqub and a Qunbul route retaining at both, while Abu Jafar and Abu Amr omit in connection. Qiraat-ER describes full yāʾ at pause or retention only in connection.
  6:80/q11/p2 Other transmissions omit the yāʾ in connection and pause, or only at pause, because context or kasra supplies it. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid lists Qalun, Warsh, Musayyibi and others as omitting throughout, though he also names Nafi transmissions retaining in connection. Nahhas says kasra indicates the omitted yāʾ and nūn compensates, while retention is sound. Ibn Ashur specifies Qalun's omission in both and compares 2:186 for omission at pause.
6:80/q12 Is the dāl of هَدَىٰنِ inclined by imāla? [هَدَىٰنِ · readings] (turns on: Whether the yāʾ root warrants imāla or the surface form governs pronunciation.)
  6:80/q12/p1 Al-Kisa'i inclines the dāl toward kasra. — reasons: Ibn Khalawayh, Ibn Atiyya and Ibn Jawzi ground imāla in the final yāʾ of the root, still relevant after the suffix; Qiraat-ER says the sound changes without a change of meaning.
  6:80/q12/p2 The other readers give fatḥa or tafkhīm according to the surface form. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid contrasts them with al-Kisa'i; Ibn Khalawayh gives the phonetic rationale.
6:80/q13 What harm did the people threaten Abraham with? [وَلَآ أَخَافُ مَا تُشْرِكُونَ بِهِۦٓ · reports] (turns on: The reports differ over the threatened affliction and whether the divine punishment was future or already alleged.)
  6:80/q13/p1 Their gods would afflict him with madness or insanity. — reasons: Ibn Jurayj explicitly reports the threat of madness; Thaʿlabi, Wahidi, Baghawi and others include it among the possible afflictions.
  6:80/q13/p2 Their gods would inflict leprosy, disease, other illness, or calamity for abandoning, mocking, or damaging them. — reasons: Tabari names leprosy; Thaʿlabi and Ibn Atiyya give leprosy or illness, Abu Hayyan leprosy or disease. Alusi lists abandonment, mockery and damage as motives; Ibn Ashur infers a threat from the otherwise unmotivated denial of fear, comparing Hud in 11:54. Islahi calls threatened wrath a basis of polytheistic fear.
  6:80/q13/p3 They claimed a deity had already afflicted him or turned him from their worship. — reasons: Tabatabai compares their possible charge with Hud's people at 11:52–55; Abu Hayyan, Alusi and Ibn Kathir also compare Hud's confrontation and threat.
6:80/q14 Why does Abraham not fear what they associate with God? [وَلَآ أَخَافُ مَا تُشْرِكُونَ بِهِۦٓ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the focus is the idols' intrinsic powerlessness or divine control over every apparent effect.)
  6:80/q14/p1 Idols and celestial objects cannot independently hear, understand, benefit, harm, or defend themselves. — reasons: Muqatil and Tabari stress carved wood and images without perception; Tabrisi notes a broken idol cannot defend itself and a setting star is created. Razi says fear properly concerns a capable agent. Ibn Kathir says if idols could act, they should plot and hasten harm. Biqai contrasts divine power to guide and harm with the idols' inability to harm or benefit. Abu Hayyan notes mā can include things or persons.
  6:80/q14/p2 Any apparent effect through idols, talismans, or stars ultimately comes from God. — reasons: Razi rejects making talismanic effects independent: even stellar powers are created by God. Qashani says harm from created things comes from Him. Tabatabai says events in God's ordered realm are prevented or permitted by Him, so hypothetical fear would affirm His lordship; Islahi stresses trust amid trials.
6:80/q15 What does مَا تُشْرِكُونَ بِهِۦ refer to, and how is it constructed? [مَا تُشْرِكُونَ بِهِۦ · grammar] (turns on: The grammatical category of mā and whether bihī returns to God, mā, or an implied cause.)
  6:80/q15/p1 Mā is relative or indefinite descriptive, denoting the things or persons they associate; the object of 'associate' is understood. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, Samin, Alusi and Ibn Ashur give this analysis. The pronoun may refer to God, with bāʾ marking what receives the association, or to mā, with bāʾ causal or meaning that by which they associate. Burhan's other examples show relative mā in worship and other clauses.
  6:80/q15/p2 Mā can be a verbal noun: Abraham denies fearing the act of associating partners with God. — reasons: Samin makes bihī refer to God and supplies an omitted object; Alusi also lists the verbal-noun analysis.
  6:80/q15/p3 An implied 'cause' may stand before the pronoun. — reasons: Abu al-Baqa proposes this addition; Samin says it is unnecessary.
6:80/q16 How does وَلَآ أَخَافُ relate syntactically to the preceding words? [وَلَآ أَخَافُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the no-fear clause is a new assertion or a circumstantial qualification.)
  6:80/q16/p1 It is an independent assertion of confidence, coordinated with the disputation statement or God's guidance. — reasons: Samin describes an independent sentence answering the idol threat. Ibn Ashur says coordination with guidance is clearer, since the opponents already knew Abraham did not fear idols.
  6:80/q16/p2 It is a circumstantial clause of Abraham in the question, or nested under 'He guided me'. — reasons: Samin says the nested analysis needs an implied subject before the present verb after wāw.
6:80/q17 How does the exception إِلَّآ أَن يَشَآءَ رَبِّى شَيْـًۭٔا limit Abraham's denial of fear? [إِلَّآ أَن يَشَآءَ رَبِّى شَيْـًۭٔا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether illā resumes with fear of God, excepts a time or state of harm through idols, or qualifies the very feeling of fear.)
  6:80/q17/p1 It is disconnected: he fears only harm or punishment his Lord independently wills, not the idols. — reasons: Zajjaj and Wahidi mention punishment for sin; Razi additionally allows worldly trials or lost blessings. Ibn Atiyya, al-Hawfi, Baghawi, Tabari's reported reading, and Ibn Ashur treat the clause as 'but I fear God's will'; Ibn Ashur prefers this for its rebuke of the idolaters. Al-Hasan likewise allows sin or independent harm. Ibn Jawzi calls the clause a fresh start, and Damghani compares 7:188 and 10:49. Tabatabai reports but objects that a general fear shifts too far from the partners.
  6:80/q17/p2 It is connected: they are not feared at any time or in any state unless God wills harm through or by empowering them. — reasons: Zamakhshari supplies an omitted span of time; Abu al-Baqa supplies circumstances. Tabrisi prefers the empowering sense because any bestowed power would prove creation and God's sole right to worship. Baydawi, Nasafi, Bursevi and Biqai allow harm through them only by divine power. Razi lists reviving and empowering idols as possible. Tabatabai rejects attributing benefit or harm to partners and says their createdness would not refute worshippers who already grant it.
  6:80/q17/p3 It can mean that God does not will Abraham to fear the idols, or wills a test of his fear. — reasons: Qurtubi gives the non-willing of fear; Alusi gives a disconnected form in which he fears God's willing that he fear. Study Quran describes divine testing. Asad translates the wording as 'unless my Sustainer wills a thing.'
6:80/q18 What grammatical uses of إِلَّا are relevant here? [إِلَّآ · grammar] (turns on: The notes distinguish the verse's resumed clause from ordinary exception and other attested uses of illā.)
  6:80/q18/p1 Here illā can introduce a fresh, disconnected clause resembling an exception. — reasons: Ibn Jawzi's reported exegetes classify 6:80 with 7:188, 7:89, 92:20, 88:23 and 95:6 as istiʾnāf; Damghani compares 7:188 and 10:49. Nahhas explicitly calls the exception disconnected.
  6:80/q18/p2 Illā is fundamentally the most common exception particle. — reasons: Abu Zakariya lists comparable nouns siwā, ghayr, bayd, mayd; verbs laysa, lā yakūn, ʿadā; and particles ḥāshā, khalā. Reported exegetes illustrate ordinary Quranic exceptions at 25:69, 29:14 and 43:67.
  6:80/q18/p3 In other Arabic uses it may mean 'and' or 'other than'. — reasons: A poem about brothers and two stars illustrates the waw sense; exegetes cite 21:22 and 44:56 for the ghayr sense. These are background classifications, not claimed as the sense in 6:80.
6:80/q19 How is شَيْـًۭٔا parsed? [شَيْـًۭٔا · grammar] (turns on: Whether shayʾan names what is willed or intensifies the act of willing.)
  6:80/q19/p1 It is the direct object of 'wills': God may will some thing or harm. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya states this; Abu Hayyan permits it; Samin records it as the alternative to his preferred cognate analysis.
