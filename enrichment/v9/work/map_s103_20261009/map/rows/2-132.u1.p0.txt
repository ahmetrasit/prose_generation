## THE CURRENT MAP
2:132/q01 What does enjoining by waṣṣā or tawṣiya mean? [وَوَصَّىٰ · meaning]
  2:132/q01/p1 It is beneficial admonition or a covenant urging a person toward obedience or pious action. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān defines waṣiyya as an admonitory instruction; Jishumī calls it a command to obey. Bayḍāwī and Ālūsī derive its sense from joining the recommender's act to the recipient's, opposed to separation; Ālūsī allows speech or sign and times other than death. Ibn ʿĀshūr says it is stronger than an ordinary order because loss is feared, especially at death or travel; he cites the Prophet's farewell-like counsels to al-ʿIrbāḍ and Muʿādh, and the counsel against anger. Bursevī says it may benefit religious or worldly life. Ibn Abī Ḥātim reports Ibn ʿAbbās that Abraham counseled adherence to God's religion, Islam; Rāzī says the designation 'testament' is more emphatic than an order because concern for religion intensifies as death approaches.
2:132/q02 How is وَوَصَّىٰ read, and do its forms differ in force? [وَوَصَّىٰ · readings] (turns on: Whether the two Arabic verb forms merely alternate or the doubled form carries intensity, repetition, or a religious use.)
  2:132/q02/p1 Nāfiʿ and Ibn ʿĀmir read awṣā with alif; others read geminated waṣṣā; both convey enjoining, while some give waṣṣā greater force or repetition. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ, Thaʿlabī, Baghawī, Rāzī, and others associate awṣā with Medinan/Syrian codices; Abu Jaʿfar and Ibn ʿAbbās are also named. Ibn Mujāhid and al-Fārisī specify readers. Al-Fārisī and Ṭabrisī support Form II by tawṣiya and Form IV by 4:11–12 and waṣiyya; the root denotes joining plant/house parts. Zajjāj, Māwardī, Ibn al-Jawzī and others say gemination intensifies or repeats counsel. Qirāʾāt-ER describes awṣā as formal bequeathing. Thaʿlabī and Qurṭubī report codex variants; Abū Ḥayyān relays Thaʿlab's twelve further Medinan/Iraqi differences. Kashshāf also locates wa-awṣā in the codices of the Hijaz and Syria.
  2:132/q02/p2 The two forms are synonymous, with no necessary contrast in frequency or force. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ says both are sound and common; Naḥḥās rejects a forced multiplicity because it makes the geminated reading less apt, comparing karramnā/akramnā. Jishumī, Ṭabrisī, Qurṭubī, Baghawī and Ālūsī treat them as equivalent; Baghawī compares anzala/nazzala. Sāmarrāʾī proposes a separate tendency: waṣṣā for religion and awṣā for worldly inheritance, while 19:31 is a religious exception; he compares karrama/akrama.
2:132/q03 What does the feminine pronoun in بِهَآ refer to? [بِهَآ · referent] (turns on: Which feminine antecedent the context supplies: the explicit religion, the nearer declaration, or an implied feminine notion.)
  2:132/q03/p1 It refers to Abraham's previously mentioned religion or way, the milla. — reasons: Zajjāj cites the surrounding mention of Abraham's way at 2:130. Jishumī prefers the explicit antecedent as broad enough to include the other suggestions; the Qāḍī, Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī likewise prefer its explicitness and breadth. Rāzī's Qāḍī says it encompasses all that secures afterlife success; Ālūsī links it to prophets transmitting true religion.
  2:132/q03/p2 It refers to Abraham's declaration 'I have submitted to the Lord of the worlds,' understood as a feminine word or sentence. — reasons: Zamakhsharī cites 2:131 and 43:28, where it is called an enduring word; Ibn ʿAṭiyya prefers this nearer antecedent. Samin corrects an attribution by noting Zamakhsharī also gave it. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the declaration condenses the religion's meaning.
  2:132/q03/p3 It refers to the confession of God's oneness, 'There is no god but God.' — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās and Muqātil call this the word of sincerity; Ṭabrisī connects it to the enduring word of 43:28. Thaʿlabī and Baghawī report the same identification.
  2:132/q03/p4 It refers to the testament or command itself, including the later instruction to die Muslim. — reasons: ʿIkrima and Zajjāj identify the matter of counsel; Abū ʿUbayda, Abū Ḥayyān, Samin and Biqāʿī allow the testament or the subsequent charge as an implied feminine antecedent.
  2:132/q03/p5 It refers to implicit obedience or Abraham's chosen quality. — reasons: Al-Mufaḍḍal supplies obedience and adduces Arabic idiom and poetry, including 53:33; Abū Ḥayyān and Samin list obedience, and Biqāʿī allows the chosen quality.
2:132/q04 Why does Abraham address his sons? [بَنِيهِ · rhetoric]
  2:132/q04/p1 A father's special care and his sons' readiness make them fitting recipients; addressing all of them shows the religion's priority. — reasons: Jishumī and Ṭabrisī say he also invited everyone; Rāzī says no son was excluded and no other bequest competed with religion. Abū Ḥayyān says the sons already followed Islam, so the counsel seeks their afterlife safety, is unrestricted in time and place, and ends with a strong warning. Bursevī says a sound leader's family helps make the community sound; he and Rāzī note the urgency of a father’s care.
  2:132/q04/p2 He passed on a precious religion to successors so they would not deviate from it. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says people of truth guard hard-won teaching through succession; Biqāʿī notes that descendants transmitted the testament in turn, especially Jacob.
2:132/q05 How many sons of Abraham are reported, and who were they? [بَنِيهِ · reports] (turns on: Different transmitted genealogical counts and lists.)
  2:132/q05/p1 Four sons are counted, including Ishmael, Isaac, Midian and Mada'in/Medan. — reasons: Muqātil counts four in the opening note; Ibn al-Jawzī names them, and Elmalılı repeats the four-name list as one report.
  2:132/q05/p2 Eight sons are counted: Ishmael, Isaac, and six by Qantura/Keturah. — reasons: Thaʿlabī, Baghawī, Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, Bursevī and Ibn ʿĀshūr name the mothers Hagar, Sarah, and Qantura. Lists of six vary (Midian, Midan, Zamran and other forms); Qurṭubī says Qantura married Abraham after Sarah's death. Ibn ʿĀshūr says only Midian's line receives much Torah detail, including Moses' refuge and Jethro/Reuel; Abū Ḥayyān says only Ishmael and Isaac left enduring lines.
  2:132/q05/p3 Other counts are fourteen, twelve, or twenty-four sons. — reasons: Bayḍāwī lists fourteen beside four and eight; Ālūsī attributes a twelve-son list to al-Itqān, while Elmalılı reports twenty-four.
2:132/q06 What background is given about Ishmael and Isaac? [بَنِيهِ · reports] (turns on: Different age and sacrifice reports in the historical background.)
  2:132/q06/p1 Ishmael was Hagar's son, older than Isaac, and was taken to Mecca as an infant. — reasons: Qurṭubī prefers infancy over reported ages two and fourteen; historians cited by Abū Ḥayyān say he was fourteen years older than Isaac and eighty-nine when Abraham died. Qurṭubī gives Ishmael's death at 137 or 130, Isaac's at 180, and says Isaac was buried beside Abraham; Abū Ḥayyān concurs on the latter. Qurṭubī lists Isaac's descendants as Romans, Greeks, Armenians and Israelites.
  2:132/q06/p2 Reports put Ishmael at two or fourteen when brought to Mecca. — reasons: Qurṭubī transmits both reported ages and separately favors infancy.
  2:132/q06/p3 The son offered for sacrifice was Ishmael. — reasons: Qurṭubī reports this identification while favoring the Isaac identification.
  2:132/q06/p4 The son offered for sacrifice was Isaac. — reasons: Qurṭubī calls this the more correct identification and says he will discuss it under al-Ṣāffāt.
2:132/q07 How is Abraham's name read or adapted? [إِبْرَٰهِۦمُ · readings]
  2:132/q07/p1 Ibrāhāmu is a canonical pronunciation with long ā in the final syllable. — reasons: Qirāʾāt-ER says the adaptation affects pronunciation and lineage resonance rather than syntax; Ibn Khālawayh notes Arabic adaptation of foreign names.
2:132/q08 How does وَيَعْقُوبُ attach to the sentence, and who receives the counsel? [وَيَعْقُوبُ · grammar] (turns on: The case of Yaʿqūb and whether coordination, ellipsis, or object attachment governs him.)
  2:132/q08/p1 Nominative Jacob is coordinated with Abraham: each charged his own sons. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī rejects making the two patriarchs' charges different; Ibn ʿAbbās and Qatāda report the shared testament. Ṭabrisī and Samin prefer coordination because it needs no ellipsis; Naḥḥās explicitly parses both as nominative subjects. Ibn Kathīr cites the next account at 2:133 for Jacob's own charge.
  2:132/q08/p2 Nominative Jacob begins a new clause with an understood 'he charged' or 'he said'; his own sons then hear the address. — reasons: Akhfash prefers this analysis because Abraham's earlier clause already implies speech. Jishumī, Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān and Samin report it; Samin notes that on this parse the quoted 'my sons' belongs to Jacob.
  2:132/q08/p3 Accusative Jacob is coordinated with 'his sons' and was among those Abraham enjoined, as a grandson. — reasons: Akhfash offers object coordination; ʿAmr b. Fāʾid and Ismāʿīl b. ʿAbd Allāh are named as readers. Abū Ḥayyān says 'sons' can include a grandson. Qushayrī objects that Jacob was neither a son nor alive to hear it; Ibn Kathīr disputes the chronological objection using 11:71, 29:27 and 21:72.
  2:132/q08/p4 Jacob is an implicit agent of the enjoining. — reasons: Ālūsī records this analysis only to call it remote.
2:132/q09 How is the name Yaʿqūb explained? [وَيَعْقُوبُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether an Arabic birth/descendant etymology can apply to a foreign proper name.)
  2:132/q09/p1 He was named for following his twin Esau out of the womb while holding Esau's heel. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās is repeatedly cited. Bursevī adds a womb dialogue: Esau demanded to emerge first; Jacob yielded for their mother's safety, and their dispositions later diverged. Qurṭubī and Ālūsī doubt the etymology or report; Samin argues a foreign proper name cannot be derived as though Arabic.
  2:132/q09/p2 The name relates to Jacob's many descendants. — reasons: Thaʿlabī and Baghawī report this alternative; Samin rejects Arabic
