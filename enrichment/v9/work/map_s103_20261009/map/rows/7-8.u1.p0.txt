## THE CURRENT MAP
7:8/q01 What does weighing mean in this verse? [ٱلْوَزْنُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the text and transmitted reports require a physical instrument, and what supplies a just measure for deeds.)
  7:8/q01/p1 A real weighing with a created balance determines the fate of deeds and people. — reasons: Al-Tabari appeals to Prophetic reports, the ordinary sense, and lack of any rational or transmitted obstacle. Zajjaj and Ibn al-Anbari give sound reports, apparent wording, and broad scholarly agreement priority. Ibn Atiyya cites the visible balance in a Prophetic report; al-Amidi objects that justice itself cannot be heavy or light. Al-Qurtubi cites reports against a purely figurative reading. Al-Tabrisi considers this possible despite preferring justice.
  7:8/q01/p2 Weighing is just judgment, exact reckoning, or recompense; balance language conveys fairness figuratively. — reasons: Mujahid, al-Dahhak, and al-A'mash transmit the justice/judgment gloss. Zajjaj and al-Wahidi note ordinary figurative usage, including a poet's 'scale' for a disputant; Ibn Atiyya cites Abu Talib. Al-Razi argues that transient acts cannot be physically weighed and an omniscient God's weighing would serve no purpose; al-Tabrisi cites Arabic usage. Ibn Ashur says the mode remains uncertain and language of the unseen approximates it. The Study Quran connects the image to fair commercial measures and cosmic balance.
  7:8/q01/p3 The measure is the truth proper to each deed; good has weight in proportion to its truth, while false deeds lack weight. — reasons: Al-Tabatabai argues that a prayer, alms, or speech must meet its own truth standard, rather than comparing two piles. Quranic parallels 23:102–103 and 101:6–10 pair good with heaviness, evil with lightness; 21:47 requires a just criterion even for mixed deeds. The book discloses truth in each act (39:69; 45:29). Prophets and successors embody the measure of right belief and action.
  7:8/q01/p4 Weighing judges deeds relative to their due reward or punishment, with complete fairness. — reasons: Al-Baydawi describes comparison with recompense; Ibn Ashur specifies exact dues and allows divine pardon or raised rank. Kuran Yolu says quantity and quality both count, with increased reward or forgiven sin consistent with 6:160; Elmalili pictures final closing of credit and debt.
7:8/q02 What does the noun ٱلْوَزْنُ mean in Arabic? [ٱلْوَزْنُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the noun denotes physical measurement or an established extension to assessment and equivalence.)
  7:8/q02/p1 It is the verbal noun of weighing: measuring or comparing things to determine amount or relative measure. — reasons: Al-Tabari gives وزن, وزنا, وزنة. Jishumi and al-Tabrisi describe comparing two objects; Jishumi adds extension to prosody and measured speech. Al-Raghib, reported by al-Alusi, defines determining measure, usually with a balance. Sībawayh distinguishes verbal-noun وزن in the accusative from concrete adjectival وزن in وزن سبعة.
  7:8/q02/p2 It can denote figurative equivalence, worth, or measured expression. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi explains poetic measured speech and mental equivalence. Al-Razi and al-Wahidi cite a poet's measure for every opponent; Ibn Ashur cites al-Ra'i's weighing for deliberation and expressions light on the tongue but heavy in the scale.
7:8/q03 How are ٱلْوَزْنُ يَوْمَئِذٍ ٱلْحَقُّ parsed? [ٱلْوَزْنُ يَوْمَئِذٍ ٱلْحَقُّ · grammar] (turns on: Which term supplies the predicate, how the day phrase attaches, and whether الحق is adjective, predicate, appositive, or verbal noun.)
  7:8/q03/p1 ٱلْوَزْنُ is subject, يَوْمَئِذٍ its predicate, and ٱلْحَقُّ qualifies the weighing. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya, Razi, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, Samin, and Alusi give this analysis. Alusi prefers its sense and the freedom of adverbs; Abu al-Baqa objects to separation from the noun, but Samin replies that the relative clause is already complete, while noting a predicate problem on one reconstruction.
  7:8/q03/p2 ٱلْحَقُّ is the predicate; يَوْمَئِذٍ is a temporal modifier of the weighing. — reasons: Al-Wahidi explicitly gives subject and predicate. Al-Asbahani objects that a definite verbal noun rarely governs an adverb but cites 4:148 as a precedent. Samin calls a broad-object analysis of the day weak.
  7:8/q03/p3 ٱلْحَقُّ is an accusative verbal noun while يَوْمَئِذٍ supplies the predicate. — reasons: Al-Farra, reported by Jishumi, and Qurtubi mention this alternative. Samin treats hypothetical accusative reading discussion as unnecessary given the fixed wording.
  7:8/q03/p4 ٱلْحَقُّ is an appositive to the pronoun implied in the adverb, or a predicate of an omitted subject. — reasons: Makki and al-Isfahani give the appositive analysis; Alusi calls it sound but favors the first parse. Abu al-Baqa also suggests an omitted subject or that الوزن predicates an omitted subject; Alusi finds these strained.
7:8/q04 What does يَوْمَئِذٍ refer to and how is its ending explained? [يَوْمَئِذٍ · grammar]
  7:8/q04/p1 It is the Day of Resurrection when nations and messengers are questioned and deeds recounted; tanwīn replaces the omitted clause after إذ. — reasons: Nasafi, Samin, and Ibn Ashur supply the elided questioning clause, referring to 7:6–7. Samin attributes the compensatory tanwin to the majority and notes al-Akhfash disagrees.
7:8/q05 What does ٱلْحَقُّ say of the weighing? [ٱلْحَقُّ · meaning] (turns on: Whether truth qualifies the fairness of the process or names the standard by which each deed has weight.)
  7:8/q05/p1 It is just, exact, sound, and proportionate, without injustice. — reasons: Mujahid glosses it as justice. Biqai says the result matches truth without excess. Ibn Ashur says justice if figurative, even proportion if a balance is depicted; verbal-noun form intensifies reality. Ibn Ashur and Kuran Yolu allow forgiveness and enhanced reward within justice.
  7:8/q05/p2 It is the truth present in a deed that gives that deed weight. — reasons: Al-Tabatabai argues that each act has its fitting measure of truth, and that acts without that truth are weightless; the book reveals its measure.
7:8/q06 Is there a reported alternate wording or reading of ٱلْحَقُّ? [ٱلْحَقُّ · readings]
  7:8/q06/p1 A reading substitutes ٱلْقِسْطُ, 'justice'. — reasons: Al-Alusi records the substitution while also citing al-Raghib's definition of weighing.
7:8/q07 What is placed in the balance? [ٱلْوَزْنُ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · referent] (turns on: Whether nonbodily acts can have physical weight, and how reports about deeds, records, and persons relate.)
  7:8/q07/p1 Deeds themselves are weighed, sometimes embodied as beautiful good acts and ugly bad acts. — reasons: Al-Suddi and others simply say deeds. Ibn Abbas reports forms; Ibn Kathir cites traditions depicting Qur'an, suras, and righteous acts visibly. Some theologians propose accidents becoming bodies; Qurtubi rejects that mechanism, whereas others deem it sound.
  7:8/q07/p2 Written records of deeds are weighed, with God giving them heaviness or lightness. — reasons: Al-Tabari describes records in opposed pans. Ibn Umar argues that actions are not bodies. The ninety-nine sin-scrolls and small testimony card supply repeated evidence; Qurtubi prefers this account, as do reports attributed to most exegetes. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan say God creates weight in records; al-Qurtubi adds the believer's delivered record.
  7:8/q07/p3 Persons themselves are weighed; physical bulk does not secure spiritual weight. — reasons: Ubayd ibn Umayr repeatedly reports a huge eater and drinker lighter than a mosquito or fly wing; al-Baydawi and Ibn Ashur cite the Prophetic report. Ibn Kathir adds Ibn Masud's thin legs outweighing Uhud. Al-Alusi notes the report does not explicitly establish that persons enter the pans and asks about the other pan.
  7:8/q07/p4 Visible signs of good and bad deeds appear in the two pans. — reasons: Al-Mawardi and al-Jubba'i propose that the signs display relative deeds to people.
  7:8/q07/p5 Deeds, records or forms, and their human agents can all be weighed on separate occasions. — reasons: Ibn Kathir explicitly reconciles the three reports in this way. Biqai leaves the mode open, including direct disclosure to hearts.
  7:8/q07/p6 Only the final outcomes of deeds are weighed. — reasons: Wahb ibn Munabbih says God grants a good or evil ending according to His will.
7:8/q08 What sort of instrument or figure is the balance? [ٱلْوَزْنُ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · theology] (turns on: Whether reports describe a literal afterlife object, approximate its reality, or use balance as a name for another criterion.)
  7:8/q08/p1 A familiar physical balance has a beam or tongue and two pans. — reasons: Amr ibn Dinar, Ibn Abbas, Hasan, and majority reports describe it. Baghawi reports an east-to-west span; Ibn Salam places it facing the Throne with pans toward Paradise and Hell; Aisha and Salman describe vast pans. Ibn Atiyya and Ibn Ashur question reports detailing its size or form.
  7:8/q08/p2 The balance is God's just criterion or disclosure of deeds, so its afterlife reality need not resemble a worldly object. — reasons: Al-Tabatabai interprets bodily-balance narrations as accessible depictions and says the Imam denies only worldly physical weight. Ibn Ashur leaves form unknown; Kuran Yolu says its manner and instrument are known to God.
  7:8/q08/p3 The book recording deeds is itself the 'balance'. — reasons: Zajjaj and Qurtubi separately report this identification.
