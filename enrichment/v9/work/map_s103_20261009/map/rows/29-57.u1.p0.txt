## THE CURRENT MAP
29:57/q01 Who is included in 'every soul'? [كُلُّ نَفْسٍۢ · referent]
  29:57/q01/p1 Every soul or living being, human or otherwise, is included; no place or person escapes death. — reasons: Mawardi says every living being dies. Bursevi explicitly includes humans and other beings; the generic indefinite nafs conveys universality. Elmalılı says whoever and wherever; Yahya al-Sallam cites 23:15, 55:26 and 39:30. Durr's report about Ali's question makes the verse answer the suggestion that prophets might remain after other creatures die.
29:57/q02 What does 'tasting death' mean? [ذَآئِقَةُ ٱلْمَوْتِ · meaning] (turns on: Whether tasting names death's certainty, its felt bitterness, or a literal small portion experienced through bodily contact.)
  29:57/q02/p1 Every soul inevitably dies, wherever it is. — reasons: Mawardi offers this as one explanation. Yahya al-Sallam cites 23:15, 55:26 and 39:30; Elmalılı stresses both place and person. Tabatabai calls tasting a metaphor for certainty; Alusi says the participle stresses certainty. Wahidi, Baghawi, Jishumi and others apply the inevitability to migration fears.
  29:57/q02/p2 Every soul experiences death's bitterness, anguish and separation. — reasons: Mawardi offers the pangs and severity as an alternative to simply dying. Kashshaf and Nasafi compare this experience with tasting bitter food; Raghib, reported by Bursevi, allows tasting to mean experiencing little or much and includes the pain of separation. Alusi treats the expression as a metaphor comparing death to bitterness. Qurtubi's cited elegy laments death and the loss of former loved ones and neighbors.
  29:57/q02/p3 Tasting can be literal: the soul undergoes a small portion of death through contact with the body. — reasons: Bursevi reports this alternative, taking tasting as a small amount taken by the mouth.
29:57/q03 What is the inward sense of the soul's death and return? [نَفْسٍۢ ٱلْمَوْتِ إِلَيْنَا تُرْجَعُونَ · inward] (turns on: Whether the inward focus is the effacement and revival of selfhood or the self's attachment to God through death.)
  29:57/q03/p1 The self dies to independent selfhood under divine witnessing, then is revived and established in truth. — reasons: Junayd, reported by Sulami, says witnessing the Real effaces the self, removes its defects, and God revives it through witnessing Him.
  29:57/q03/p2 The self must abandon attachment to itself and attach to God; death then becomes return to Him, not annihilation. — reasons: Razi cites 28:88 for everything besides God perishing, 3:169 for martyrs alive with God, and a Prophetic saying that believers move from one abode to another rather than die. Bursevi says only those disengaged from ordinary attachment know the spirit's appearance in and departure from the body.
29:57/q04 How does bodily death occur? [نَفْسٍۢ ٱلْمَوْتِ · theology]
  29:57/q04/p1 The connecting animal spirit ceases, so the soul involuntarily leaves the body and bodily life ends. — reasons: Bursevi describes a human as body, soul and a subtle vapor between them called the animal spirit; life lasts while their connection is viable. He calls its extinction and the soul's departure formal death, and says only those fully detached know how the spirit enters and departs.
29:57/q05 Why express death with a participle? [ذَآئِقَةُ · rhetoric]
  29:57/q05/p1 The participle emphasizes that death is certain. — reasons: Alusi contrasts the participle dhāʾiqa with a finite verb of tasting.
29:57/q06 How is 'tasting death' read and constructed? [ذَآئِقَةُ ٱلْمَوْتِ · readings] (turns on: Whether dhāʾiqa governs al-mawt as a construct or is nunated and takes an accusative object.)
  29:57/q06/p1 Dhāʾiqat al-mawt is read as a genitive construction. — reasons: Jishumi states the construct reading and allows another construction; his separate note about ka-ʾayyin glosses that neighboring word as 'how many.'
  29:57/q06/p2 Dhāʾiqatun al-mawta is read with nunation and accusative 'death'. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and Alusi attribute this reading to Abu Haywa (Abu Hayyah in Abu Hayyan's note); Jishumi says a nonconstruct reading is possible.
29:57/q07 What does 'then' indicate? [ثُمَّ · rhetoric]
  29:57/q07/p1 It marks delay in time or rank between death and return. — reasons: Alusi gives both temporal and ranked delay.
29:57/q08 What does returning to God mean? [إِلَيْنَا تُرْجَعُونَ · interpretation]
  29:57/q08/p1 After death people are raised and brought to God's judgment for recompense according to deeds. — reasons: Bursevi derives turjaʿūn from rajʿ and glosses it 'you are returned'; he, Wahidi, Jishumi, Baghawi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Nasafi, Biqai and Alusi specify judgment and reward or punishment. Yahya al-Sallam places the return on Judgment Day; Abu Hayyan at Resurrection; Elmalılı mentions resurrection and presentation before God. Biqai stresses return to God alone, not another. Razi links dying for God's cause to recompense; Muqatil links it to the migrants' eternal rooms and rivers.
29:57/q09 How is the verb 'you are returned' read? [تُرْجَعُونَ · readings] (turns on: The initial consonant changes direct address to third person, and vowel pattern changes passive return to active returning; the notes give differing transmitter attributions and contextual explanations.)
  29:57/q09/p1 It is read in direct address with tāʾ and passive voice: 'you are returned'. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid and Farisi name the other readers, including Hafs from Asim; Ibn Atiyya, Ibn al-Jawzi, Abu Hayyan and Nashr call it the majority reading. Qurtubi says the address fits God's believing servants. Ibn Khalawayh notes the shift of person. Bursevi gives the passive lexical sense of the written form. Samin compares the second-person forms at 30:11.
  29:57/q09/p2 It is read with third-person yāʾ, passively referring to people: 'they are returned'. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid and Farisi report it in some Asim transmissions; Baghawi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Baydawi, Samin and Nashr specify Abu Bakr from Asim, and Qurtubi also names Sulami. Ibn Atiyya adds Abu Hatim from Abu Amr. Farisi and Qurtubi say the third person follows 'every soul'; Jishumi says it refers to everyone. Qiraat-Er describes the shift from address to narration. Samin compares 30:11. Some source notes specify person without voice.
  29:57/q09/p3 It is read in the active second-person form: 'you return'. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and Alusi attribute it to Ali. Nasafi and Nashr attribute an active addressed form to Yaqub; Nashr specifies an open tāʾ and a kasra on jīm. Qiraat-Er says the Form I makes the audience the grammatical agent.
  29:57/q09/p4 A further third-person reading uses active voice: 'they return'. — reasons: Samin reports this additional reading without naming its transmitter.
29:57/q10 How does the reminder of death and return address migration? [* · links] (turns on: The sources emphasize either the impossibility of escaping death by staying home or the promised recompense after death; both serve the migration context.)
  29:57/q10/p1 Death is inevitable wherever one lives, so fear of death or livelihood should not delay migration and obedience. — reasons: Muqatil connects the warning to migrants without money or livelihood in Medina who trust God; Wahidi, Jishumi, Baghawi, Tabarsi and Ibn al-Jawzi say remaining among polytheists cannot prevent death. Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi mention fear of hunger or death on leaving Mecca, and Qurtubi recalls 3:185. Razi adds grief at leaving family and home; Abu Hayyan and Kuran Yolu specify livelihood fears about Medina. Biqai says obedience does not shorten life nor sin prolong it; Kuran Yolu and Abu Hayyan say death comes at its appointed time. Nasafi says certainty makes leaving home easier. Ibn Kathir recounts the first migration to Abyssinia under the Negus, then the Prophet's and other Companions' migration to Medina.
  29:57/q10/p2 Death leads to God's lasting reward, so migration and endurance for His sake will not be lost. — reasons: Muqatil associates patient, trusting migrants with eternal rooms and rivers; Razi says dying in God's cause earns recompense on return. Ibn Ashur says the verse eases persecution even unto death by recalling eternal reward and hints at future jihad. Ibn Atiyya says return reduces worldly fears.
29:57/q11 What conduct does the verse urge beyond the immediate fear of migration? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The notes draw moral force from inevitable death as preparation for judgment, detachment from worldly life, or deterrence from sin and confusion after prophetic death.)
  29:57/q11/p1 Prepare for the afterlife through worship, obedience and sincere devotion to God. — reasons: Kashshaf says the death verse follows the command to seek a place fit for worship and urges utmost preparation for requital. Baydawi and Alusi likewise require preparation. Elmalılı says flight cannot avoid return, so worship God alone and meet Him sincerely even under coercion. Study Quran relates preparation through worship to 29:56. Tabatabai contrasts preparation with fleeting adornment. Bursevi says the final outcome makes exile from idolatry, sin or unchangeable innovation easy; Alusi urges exclusive worship and migration.
  29:57/q11/p2 Recognize worldly life as a transient waystation rather than prefer it to the lasting afterlife. — reasons: Jishumi presents this as an alternative explanation. Alusi says worldly life does not last and the afterlife is for recompense; Tabatabai warns against fleeting adornment. Qurtubi's elegy warns against attachment to worldly beauty and asks where former loved ones and neighbors went.
  29:57/q11/p3 Let the reminder deter sins and prevent disorientation when a prophet dies. — reasons: Mawardi offers both functions of mentioning death.
