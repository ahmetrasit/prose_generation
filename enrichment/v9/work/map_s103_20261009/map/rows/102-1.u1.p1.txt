102:1/q10 What occasion of revelation is reported? [* · reports] (turns on: The differing transmitted reports identify distinct communities, and some describe a literal graveyard count while others describe dying during rivalry.)
  102:1/q10/p1 Banū ʿAbd Manāf and Banū Sahm of Quraysh competed in numbers and notables, then counted the dead. — reasons: Muqātil says Sahm exceeded ʿAbd Manāf by three households; al-Kashshāf and Abū Ḥayyān report ʿAbd Manāf led among the living but Sahm among the dead because of larger pre-Islamic numbers. Al-Wāḥidī attributes it to Muqātil and al-Kalbī; Qurṭubī adds Ibn ʿAbbās. Study Quran calls it best attested.
  102:1/q10/p2 Two Ansari clans, specified as Banū Ḥāritha and Banū al-Ḥārith in some reports, boasted over the living and dead. — reasons: Ibn Burayda and Abū Burayda transmit this; Elmalılı notes only this report clearly has physical visits to graves and points to a Medinan setting. Al-Suyūṭī and al-Ālūsī use it to favor Medina.
  102:1/q10/p3 Jews or the People of the Book boasted that their groups outnumbered others. — reasons: Qatāda's report says the boast persisted until they died astray; Shaybān's version broadens it to the People of the Book. Study Quran locates the Jews in Medina.
  102:1/q10/p4 It descended concerning merchants. — reasons: ʿAmr b. Dīnār swears to this report, transmitted by Qurṭubī.
  102:1/q10/p5 Its occasion is broad: competing clans died one after another while boasting over numbers. — reasons: Qatāda's account names no specific tribe and says the rivals' members died until all reached the graves; this explains the endpoint as death rather than a graveyard tally.
102:1/q11 How does the following grave visitation complete this verse? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the next clause describes literal counting at graves, death in figurative language, or a visit that prompts reflection.)
  102:1/q11/p1 The rivals counted or named dead ancestors at the graves to extend a contest begun among the living. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf calls 'visiting' sarcastic; Abū Ḥayyān calls it a mocking depiction. Elmalılı stresses this misuse of graves is the source of rebuke; Ibn ʿĀshūr allows the endpoint to be the object of rivalry, even tombs.
  102:1/q11/p2 The rivalry continued until death and burial; 'visiting graves' figuratively means dying. — reasons: Rāzī says the clause can mean they forgot the grave until reaching it. Al-Kashshāf cites two poetic examples of visiting graves for dying; al-Bayḍāwī, Ibn Kathīr, and others read the life wasted in worldly pursuit. Elmalılı says death certainly ends competition and the following rebuke favors this reading.
  102:1/q11/p3 A literal visit may briefly soften hearts and end greed, but the rivals may instead boast at graves. — reasons: Rāzī reports a view that hardened hearts retain only a little religious feeling at graves, comparing scant gratitude at 7:10 or 2:83. Elmalılı says seeing death or graves can awaken receptive hearts, but not universally, and also records that the clause can attach to takāthur or ilhā.
  102:1/q11/p4 The grave is a temporary visit before return to one's true home. — reasons: ʿUmar b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz argues a visitor must return home, implying life after burial.
102:1/q12 What does the verse teach about worldly rivalry and its consequences? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The sources emphasize either spiritual loss and judgment or its further social and material consequences.)
  102:1/q12/p1 Pursuit of passing gains makes people heedless of lasting felicity and accountability. — reasons: Rāzī ranks inner knowledge and virtue above bodily health and external wealth; he cites Abraham's prayer for wisdom at 26:83 and notes people sacrifice wealth to relieve bodily pain. Al-Qāshānī says sensory pleasure wastes innate purity and intellect. Al-Biqāʿī traces neglect to desire and love of ease; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says ingratitude meets questioning and punishment.
  102:1/q12/p2 Unchecked acquisition damages moral vision, happiness, social justice, and the environment. — reasons: Asad says exclusive pursuit erases moral limits, destabilizes persons and societies, harms the natural environment, and brings frustration and confusion in technological society. Kur'an Yolu adds injustice from personal to international life through economic, political, and communication power. Islāḥī says wealth desire remains insatiable despite changing family norms and rising living standards.
102:1/q13 Is every form of rivalry for increase blameworthy? [ٱلتَّكَاثُرُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether context and the article restrict the condemned rivalry to worldly goods, or whether the verse's unrestricted wording should remain open.)
  102:1/q13/p1 No: rivalry in knowledge, obedience, virtue, and sincere good deeds is commendable. — reasons: Rāzī distinguishes genuine felicity from passing goods; 9:19's report of boasts about pilgrimage water, Kaʿba key, and fighting disbelief illustrates religious claims. 93:11 allows recounting God's favor to inspire others. Elmalılı cites commands to race in good at 2:148, 5:48, 83:26, while warning that sincerity and quality matter more than sheer quantity (57:23, 4:36, 5:100). Al-Biqāʿī reasons from an ordered universe and human injustice to a wise Creator's resurrection and judgment, corroborated by creation and revelation.
  102:1/q13/p2 The verse's wording is not restricted to a predefined class of blameworthy increase. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ rejects adding a limiting category to the unqualified noun; she still reads the address as covering people distracted by worldly adornments, including wealth and children.
102:1/q14 What is the Prophet's teaching about the wealth people call their own? [ٱلتَّكَاثُرُ · reports] (turns on: The reports offer related admonitions: what wealth is retained, how desire persists, and what accompanies a person after death.)
  102:1/q14/p1 A person truly keeps only what is consumed, worn out, or given away for lasting reward; the rest is left behind. — reasons: The Prophet says this after reciting the sūra in reports from Ibn al-Shikhkhīr and Abū Hurayra; al-Ṭabarī treats it as evidence that wealth is one object of takāthur. Al-Āḥnaf says a coin becomes one's own when spent in gratitude or reward, and a poem contrasts keeping and spending. Elmalılı cites Tirmidhī's ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ grading; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it one fitting admonition, not an exhaustive gloss.
  102:1/q14/p2 Greed for wealth persists and asks for still more, until earth fills the human mouth. — reasons: Anas's report says one valley of gold leads to desire for two and God accepts repentance. Ibn Kathīr adds the sound report that old age leaves greed and hope intact; Ubayy's report of a saying once thought Qurʾān gives variants of two valleys and a third.
  102:1/q14/p3 At death family and wealth return while deeds remain with the person. — reasons: Anas reports the Prophet's triad of family, wealth, and deeds.
102:1/q15 What did Ubayy report about the saying on Adam's desire for wealth? [* · reports] (turns on: The reports vary in valley count and in whether they describe prior treatment of the saying as Qurʾān or only its admonitory content.)
  102:1/q15/p1 People had treated the saying as Qurʾān until this sūra was revealed. — reasons: Versions mention one or two valleys of gold, desire for another, a belly filled only by earth, and divine acceptance of repentance. Ibn al-ʿArabī calls the report sound and neglected by commentators. Ibn ʿĀshūr says 'we' could mean Muslims generally and does not fix a Medinan date.
  102:1/q15/p2 The saying belongs to the broader prophetic warning against endless acquisition. — reasons: Anas's version asks for a second valley after a first, while Ubayy's variations ask for a third; Study Quran cites it as a parallel to takāthur.
102:1/q16 Does this sūra establish punishment in the grave? [* · theology]
  102:1/q16/p1 Yes: ʿAlī says uncertainty about grave punishment ended when this passage was revealed. — reasons: Naḥḥās takes the verse as indicating grave punishment; ʿAlī's report is transmitted by al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Abī Ḥātim, al-Durr, al-Suyūṭī, and Ibn Kathīr. Ibn Kathīr cites al-Tirmidhī's gharīb judgment; al-Itqān and al-Ālūsī also use the report in dating the sūra.
102:1/q17 Was the sūra revealed in Mecca or Medina? [* · sciences] (turns on: The rival Quraysh and Ansari occasion reports, Ubayy's wording, ʿAlī's grave-punishment report, and the fit of the sūra's threats to a Meccan audience.)
  102:1/q17/p1 It is Meccan. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya says he knows no disagreement; Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān report exegetical consensus, while recording al-Bukhārī's contrary label. Ibn ʿĀshūr prefers Mecca because the severe warning suits polytheists and the Quraysh occasion; he rejects Ubayy's 'we' as proof of Medina, since it could mean Muslims generally. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ and Asad call it early Meccan.
  102:1/q17/p2 It is Medinan. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan says Medina in Jishumī; al-Bukhārī is cited against the exegetes by Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān. Al-Suyūṭī and al-Ālūsī prefer Medina, using the Ansari occasion; al-Ālūsī adds Ubayy's saying and ʿAlī's grave-punishment report, arguing the latter is mentioned in Medinan material. Ibn ʿĀshūr contests Ubayy's dating inference.
  102:1/q17/p3 Its place of revelation is disputed. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī states the dispute without choosing.
