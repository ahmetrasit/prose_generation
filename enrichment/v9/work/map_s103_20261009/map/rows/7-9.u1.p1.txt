7:9/q07 Can the scales balance equally, and where do mixed or sinful believers fit? [خَفَّتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · theology] (turns on: Whether equal totals exist, whether the binary address includes mixed believers, and whether success means immediate or eventual deliverance.)
  7:9/q07/p1 The two outcomes exhaust people; no one has equal good and bad totals. — reasons: Razi explicitly denies an equal group. Ibn Ashur says the passage's purpose addresses believers with good and polytheists devoid of it, leaving mixed believers aside.
  7:9/q07/p2 Equal deeds place a person among the people of the Heights. — reasons: Ibn Abbas says a single deed or grain can change the outcome; Alusi reports identifying the omitted equal group with those in 7:46.
  7:9/q07/p3 The passage leaves mixed believers outside its immediate purpose. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says they are treated elsewhere; Alusi reports a reply putting them among people with mixed deeds in 9:102 and notes God's promised fulfillment.
  7:9/q07/p4 Sinful believers may first have deeds weighed without tawhid, then tawhid tips the balance when they leave Hell. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya reports this answer twice to the question how a successful heavy-scaled sinner can enter Hell; the testimony of divine oneness is added after an initial weighing.
  7:9/q07/p5 A sinful believer's eventual release from Hell itself counts as success despite earlier discipline. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya gives this answer twice; the question arose because heavy scales and temporary Hell seemed in tension.
  7:9/q07/p6 Faith's testimony can rescue a believer whose good deeds appear light. — reasons: Al-Wahidi attributes this to Ibn Abbas and reports a prayer card. Razi contrasts prayer card and testimony card, and reports al-Qadi's qualification that testimony must be joined to its duties. Ibn Atiyya says testimony of divine oneness must tip a person's balance.
7:9/q08 What is the purpose of weighing if God already knows? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · theology] (turns on: How a visible weighing relates to God's knowledge, justice, public proof, and the criterion of judgment.)
  7:9/q08/p1 It makes judgment and justice visible, establishes proof, and ends excuses. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi compares a truthful record (45:29); Ibn Atiyya likens recording and limb testimony; Ibn al-Jawzi says it displays fate and deeds. Abu Hayyan cites questioning, admission, limbs, prophets and angels. Study Quran links 7:6 and says the scale reassures people; Z says it rebukes wrongdoers. Razi reports increased joy or shame. Ibn al-Jawzi says God reports deeds to messengers and nations despite already knowing them; Ibn Abbas says the placed record speaks of the deeds.
  7:9/q08/p2 It tests faith during life and distinguishes the saved from the doomed hereafter. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi and Ibn al-Jawzi state both purposes; the public caller reports lasting felicity or misery.
  7:9/q08/p3 The measure reveals how much truth each deed contains and assigns proportional recompense. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i says the placed book identifies each deed's truth (39:69; 45:29); without a criterion mixed deeds would be judged arbitrarily.
  7:9/q08/p4 Weighing is unnecessary as a physical way for God to discover deeds. — reasons: Razi argues deeds cannot physically be weighed and a display adds no information; Imam al-Sadiq says God knows every amount already. Al-Tabataba'i clarifies this denies worldly physical weight, while embodied deeds and manifest justice remain possible.
7:9/q09 What do the reported scales and attendants look like? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · reports] (turns on: The reported form, scale, location and agents of the Judgment balance; Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan question detailed shape reports.)
  7:9/q09/p1 A balance has a tongue or pointer and two pans. — reasons: Ibn Abbas, Hasan and the majority describe the instrument. Ibn Atiyya says reports of more precise dimensions and shape are unreliable.
  7:9/q09/p2 Gabriel oversees the scale and settles wrongs between people. — reasons: Hudhayfa says victims receive the oppressor's good deeds; if none remain their sins transfer to him. Ibn Atiyya calls Gabriel custodian.
  7:9/q09/p3 One vast scale faces the Throne, with pans toward Paradise and Hell, large enough for the heavens and earth. — reasons: Ibn Salam gives the location, size and Gabriel's presence; Aisha's report likens the pans to heaven and earth, with a swordlike path; Salman transmits a similar account. Al-Tabataba'i treats such imagery as accessibility for simple hearers.
  7:9/q09/p4 A caller or angel announces lasting bliss or misery when each balance is weighed. — reasons: Ubayd Allah ibn al-Ghayzar sets it amid the crowded Resurrection; Ibn al-Iyzar places the caller beside the scale; Anas has an angel announce the result.
7:9/q10 What does the card that outweighs ninety-nine records bear? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · reports] (turns on: Which words the rescuing card bears in the distinct reports.)
  7:9/q10/p1 It bears the testimony that there is no deity but God and Muhammad is His messenger. — reasons: Ibn Umar and Abd Allah ibn Amr transmit ninety-nine vast sin scrolls against a tiny card; Ibn al-Jawzi names Ahmad and al-Tirmidhi, and Ibn Kathir reports al-Tirmidhi graded it sound. Bursevi explains a card as a price tag sewn to clothing.
  7:9/q10/p2 It bears the believer's blessings upon the Prophet, who produces and identifies the card. — reasons: Al-Wahidi gives this further report with a fingertip-sized card; Razi contrasts it with the more widespread testimony-card account.
7:9/q11 When do people fail to remember one another at Resurrection? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · reports] (turns on: Which stages of the Resurrection reports include; the accounts can describe more than one stage.)
  7:9/q11/p1 At the balance, until each learns whether it is heavy or light. — reasons: Aisha's report says people are barefoot and unclothed and occupied with records and weighing, citing 80:37; Qatada's report also names the balance.
  7:9/q11/p2 When records fly, until each sees where the book lands. — reasons: Aisha's continuation cites 69:19; Qatada's report also names this stage.
  7:9/q11/p3 At the bridge over Hell, while hooks and thorns detain people. — reasons: Aisha's continuation and Qatada's report name the bridge.
7:9/q12 What do light scales lead to? [خَفَّتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ خَسِرُوٓا۟ أَنفُسَهُم · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the result is described as eternal punishment, a deed-total outcome, or wasted human capacity.)
  7:9/q12/p1 They lead to ruin, Hell, and lasting misery. — reasons: Ibn Abbas gives an unbeliever's ugly deeds and Fire; Ibn Atiyya says the good-deed pan rises and the lost remain eternally in Hell; al-Tabrisi and al-Wahidi identify punishment. Callers announce lasting wretchedness.
  7:9/q12/p2 They register punishment according to the excess of bad over good. — reasons: Jabir and Ibn Abbas state the prevailing side determines Paradise or Hell; one deed can tip it.
  7:9/q12/p3 They show a person has wasted the capacity and sound nature that could have brought benefit. — reasons: Al-Baydawi and Alusi speak of original disposition, while Qashani describes exchanging capacity for worldly debris and ego aims.
7:9/q13 What does it mean to lose themselves? [خَسِرُوٓا۟ أَنفُسَهُم · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the loss stresses destroyed capital or disposition, punishment, or the lost Paradise home.)
  7:9/q13/p1 The self is one's chief capital, lost like a merchant's investment. — reasons: Jishumi, al-Haddadi and al-Biqa'i use the capital image. Ibn Ashur explains loss as the opposite of profit and cites 6:20 and 2:16.
  7:9/q13/p2 They destroy their own selves or capacity for benefit through punishment and bad action. — reasons: Ibn Abbas says arriving at punishment; Ibn Atiyya and al-Tabrisi describe destruction and eternal Hell. Baydawi and Alusi say they waste innate disposition; Qashani says worldly aims replace original capacity.
  7:9/q13/p3 They lose their home in Paradise as well as themselves. — reasons: Ibn Abbas's report names both losses.
7:9/q14 What are 'Our signs' here? [بِـَٔايَٰتِنَا · referent] (turns on: How broadly the signs are identified, and whether the Imam report gives a general sense or one example.)
  7:9/q14/p1 They are God's proofs, indications, commands and prohibitions. — reasons: Tabari calls them proofs requiring affirmation; Ibn Atiyya adds commands and prohibitions, and Nasafi calls them proofs.
  7:9/q14/p2 They include what Muhammad brought from God. — reasons: Muqatil identifies denial of the Qur'an's divine origin; Ibn Abbas via al-Wahidi and al-Tabrisi name Muhammad's message.
  7:9/q14/p3 Denial of the Imams is a concrete instance of wronging the signs. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i reports this interpretation but explicitly treats it as one instance within the wider sense.
7:9/q15 What does 'wronging Our signs' mean? [بِـَٔايَٰتِنَا يَظْلِمُونَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether injustice is glossed as denial, broader refusal to give signs their due, or a reported specific instance.)
  7:9/q15/p1 It means denying or disbelieving the signs, suppressing their evidence and refusing to follow them. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba notes the several senses of zulm; Tabari says rejecting proofs despite their validity. Ibn Ashur says denial suppresses established truth; Bursevi calls it placing denial where belief belongs.
  7:9/q15/p2 It means persistently putting the signs in the wrong place or failing to give them their due. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i and Elmalili emphasize continued misplacement; Ibn Ashur reads the imperfect as repeated past denial. Qashani takes ego display and concealing divine qualities as this injustice.
  7:9/q15/p3 It can include denying the Imams as a particular case. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i says a report supplies this instance of a broader meaning.
