6:151/q24 What are the فَوَٰحِش and their apparent and hidden forms? [ٱلْفَوَٰحِشَ مَا ظَهَرَ مِنْهَا وَمَا بَطَنَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the plural remains general or is narrowed to sexual vice, bodily versus inward acts, or reported examples.)
  6:151/q24/p1 They refer especially to sexual transgressions, public prostitution and private illicit lovers. — reasons: Muqatil, Ibn Abbas and others describe Jahiliyya tolerating secret adultery while condemning public brothels; Alusi prefers this from the list's sequence and analogy to lost offspring; Elmalili includes zina, sodomy and preliminaries, while Tabatabai says public and secret sexual acts.
  6:151/q24/p2 All grave sins and shameful conduct are banned, whether displayed or concealed. — reasons: Jishumi favors the broad reading; Razi, Ibn Atiyya, Tabarsi, Ibn Ashur, Kuranyolu and Study Quran argue the plural and unqualified wording preclude restriction to zina; Ibn Ashur lists public anger and slander, private adultery and theft; Razi and Study Quran connect private restraint to sincere God-consciousness. Zajjaj explicitly includes shirk, child-killing and inventing religious prohibitions among indecencies.
  6:151/q24/p3 Public forms are visible bodily acts; hidden forms are inward beliefs, desires, intentions or heart-sins. — reasons: Tabarsi prefers this as broadest; Mawardi and Ibn Jawzi report beliefs of the heart, Qashani includes desire and intended vice, and Islahi warns of inner vice nourished through senses and thought; Tustari distinguishes limbs from persistence and inward love of sin.
  6:151/q24/p4 Apparent indecencies are forbidden marriages, while hidden indecency is adultery. — reasons: Reports differ in examples: marrying mothers or daughters; fathers' wives or two sisters; Ali ibn al-Husayn has father's wife. Ibn Atiyya rejects exhaustive specification; Tabatabai calls them examples only.
  6:151/q24/p5 Outward indecency is wine; inward indecency is adultery. — reasons: Dahhak's gloss is repeated by Mawardi, Jishumi, Baghawi, Ibn Jawzi and Thalabi, whose hidden gloss is truncated.
  6:151/q24/p6 Public indecency is wronging others; hidden indecency includes adultery and theft. — reasons: Ikrima is transmitted by Ibn Abi Hatim and Durr.
  6:151/q24/p7 The phrase includes specific wrongs such as asking people for things or causeless divorce after miscarriage or childbirth. — reasons: Ibn Abi Hatim transmits a Prophetic report on asking and Yahya ibn Jabir on divorce after miscarriage; Durr reports asking and divorce after bearing a child, preserving the report variants.
  6:151/q24/p8 Outward indecency is ostentation; inward indecency is false spiritual claims or action intended for others besides God. — reasons: Sulami reports Harith al-Muhasibi on other-directed intention and another authority on display versus false claims.
  6:151/q24/p9 One referent is naked circumambulation of the House. — reasons: Ibn Abi Hatim reports Muhammad ibn Qays's addressees and Zuhri's interpretation of Jahiliyyah nakedness.
  6:151/q24/p10 The Prophet named adultery, theft and wine as punishable indecencies. — reasons: Durr gives this report, wider than a zina-only gloss.
6:151/q25 How is مَا ظَهَرَ مِنْهَا وَمَا بَطَنَ attached grammatically? [مَا ظَهَرَ مِنْهَا وَمَا بَطَنَ · grammar] (turns on: The appositional syntax and the semantic division it expresses.)
  6:151/q25/p1 The phrase names public conduct and private conduct, both subject to the ban. — reasons: Abd al-Razzaq reports Ma'mar; Qatada and Ibn Abbas use overt and secret conduct; Kashshaf and others compare 6:120.
  6:151/q25/p2 It is an accusative apposition or inclusive substitute for al-fawāḥish. — reasons: Zajjaj, Nahhas, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Nasafi and Samin agree; Samin also permits partitive min and says the second minhā is elided.
6:151/q26 Why say 'do not approach' indecencies? [وَلَا تَقْرَبُوا۟ ٱلْفَوَٰحِشَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the warning is framed as avoiding outward means or inward beginnings.)
  6:151/q26/p1 It intensifies the ban to include preliminaries, occasions and means leading to commission. — reasons: Biqai says prohibition of proximity is stronger; Alusi warns approach invites commission; Ibn Ashur calls closeness metaphorical because sins lack a literal place; Elmalili extends it to sexual preliminaries; Kuranyolu requires avoiding conditions that lead to sin.
  6:151/q26/p2 It calls for uprooting inward vice before it becomes action. — reasons: Islahi says vice starts in the heart, grows through senses and thoughts, and can grip a person even when unacted.
6:151/q27 Why does God forbid open and hidden indecencies? [وَلَا تَقْرَبُوا۟ ٱلْفَوَٰحِشَ مَا ظَهَرَ مِنْهَا وَمَا بَطَنَ · theology] (turns on: The notes supply divine jealousy and moral-social consequences as distinct grounds.)
  6:151/q27/p1 Both forms corrupt the person or society, regardless of whether people see them. — reasons: Razi says secret sin after public restraint values people's censure over God's judgment; Tabatabai says permissive sexual wrong erodes stigma, lineage and family life; Study Quran reports the public/private piety contrast.
  6:151/q27/p2 Divine jealousy rejects both public and secret sexual wrongdoing. — reasons: Ibn Kathir cites Ibn Masud, Sa'd ibn Ubada and Abu Hurayra reports; he notes Abu Hurayra's route is transmitted only by Ibn Mardawayh, though the chain has a parallel.
6:151/q28 What inward meanings are drawn from the ban on indecencies? [وَلَا تَقْرَبُوا۟ ٱلْفَوَٰحِشَ مَا ظَهَرَ مِنْهَا وَمَا بَطَنَ · inward] (turns on: Different accounts of the heart's vice and proper discipline.)
  6:151/q28/p1 Inward vice can be intention, desire, ostentation or serving another than God. — reasons: Sulami gives Harith al-Muhasibi's intention gloss; Qashani includes desire and concealed prohibited acts; Bursevi includes illicit looking and acts that distance one from truth.
  6:151/q28/p2 Outward vice belongs to the limbs; inward vice includes persistence, cravings and ready willingness to sin. — reasons: Tustari says loving an unavailable sin is persistence; Sahl counts craving even lawful food and extending one's present state into tomorrow as persistence.
  6:151/q28/p3 The heart's obligatory worship is stillness joined to knowledge and certainty. — reasons: Tustari defines this inward duty; he also says prophets can repent from outward acts and therefore can have irrelevant thoughts.
6:151/q29 Who is the protected ٱلنَّفْسَ? [ٱلنَّفْسَ ٱلَّتِى حَرَّمَ ٱللَّهُ · referent] (turns on: Whether inviolability is restricted to believers or includes covenant-protected others.)
  6:151/q29/p1 It is any inviolable life, including Muslims and protected non-Muslims under covenant or treaty. — reasons: Thalabi, Baghawi, Ibn Atiyya and many later authors make the scope explicit; Qurtubi uses generic al-nafs and 70:19–22, 103:1–3; Alusi excludes hostile combatants and rejects Ibn Jubayr's restriction; Ibn Ashur says the generic singular encompasses persons.
  6:151/q29/p2 It is specifically the life of a believer. — reasons: Said ibn Jubayr is reported by Ibn Abi Hatim and Durr; Alusi objects that covenant-protected lives also count.
6:151/q30 What does حَرَّمَ ٱللَّهُ convey about the soul? [ٱلنَّفْسَ ٱلَّتِى حَرَّمَ ٱللَّهُ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the wording primarily names the prohibited act or sanctifies the person.)
  6:151/q30/p1 Its killing has been forbidden from ancient times; attaching the ban to the person emphasizes sanctity. — reasons: Ibn Ashur traces the norm to Adam and explains the metonymy; Kuranyolu also calls life inviolable from Adam onward.
  6:151/q30/p2 It may mean God has made the soul itself inviolable, like a sacred precinct. — reasons: Ibn Ashur cites 5:1, 27:91 and a hadith on Medina's sanctuary; Tabatabai likewise offers revealed protection from loss of life or rights.
