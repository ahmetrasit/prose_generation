## THE CURRENT MAP
9:6/q01 Who is the polytheist eligible to seek protection? [أَحَدٌۭ مِّنَ ٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the indefinite 'one' specifies universal coverage and how the preceding fighting order bounds it.)
  9:6/q01/p1 Any individual polytheist covered by the fighting order, including a person from a hostile or treaty breaking group after the grace period. — reasons: Ibn Ishaq identifies those ordered fought; Ibn Abbas calls him treatyless; Ibn Ashur says 'one' makes coverage universal even after a broken pact, citing 5:2 and the ban on answering betrayal with betrayal. Other authors specify the expired sacred months.
  9:6/q01/p2 The phrasing includes any polytheist who seeks to hear revelation or has a sound reason to visit. — reasons: Mujahid says whoever comes to hear the Prophet and revelation; al-Biqa'i calls it a limited middle course after general restrictions; Ibn Ashur stresses the individual noun and hypothetical conditional.
9:6/q02 How is 'if one of the polytheists seeks protection' parsed? [وَإِنْ أَحَدٌۭ مِّنَ ٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ ٱسْتَجَارَكَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the nominative is governed by a recovered verb or is an initial subject; why in permits the construction.)
  9:6/q02/p1 A hidden verb raises ahad, and istajāraka explains it; conditional in is normally followed by a verb. — reasons: Al-Akhfash calls this the more analogical analysis; al-Zajjaj, Ibn Atiyya, Razi, al-Baydawi and others reject a bare subject after in. Al-Farra says in uniquely tolerates separation from its jussive verb; 4:176 and poetry illustrate it. Sibawayh calls in the mother of conditionals.
  9:6/q02/p2 Ahad can be a nominal subject after the conditional, with a following verbal predicate; the implied verb is only an analytical device. — reasons: Al-Akhfash adduces noun-initial constructions after an, including poems 'ʿāwid Harāʾa', 'lā tajzaʿī', and 'atajzaʿ'; Abu Ali answers al-Zajjaj by invoking Sibawayh and an underlying verb in the response. Ibn Ashur appeals to generic indefiniteness and conditional generality.
  9:6/q02/p3 The special grammar of in is not explained by saying it occurs only in conditions. — reasons: Ibn Yazid notes that in can mean mā, be extra, or be lightened from inna; its ambiguity remains distinctive. Al-Qurtubi transmits the same objection to Sibawayh.
9:6/q03 Can a noun stand before the jussive conditional response? [وَإِنْ أَحَدٌۭ مِّنَ ٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ ٱسْتَجَارَكَ فَأَجِرْهُ · grammar] (turns on: How to parse the poetic accusative and whether the response has an implied fa.)
  9:6/q03/p1 An accusative object may intervene, but a nominative subject may not. — reasons: Al-Kisa'i cites poetry with accusative al-khayr after a jussive conditional, reasoning that the response verb recalls its own subject.
  9:6/q03/p2 Without fa or an equivalent jussive, a nominal answer is invalid; the cited accusative is adjectival. — reasons: Al-Farra rejects al-Kisa'i's exception, parses al-khayr as an adjective of al-ayyām and reads taʿqub as feminine.
9:6/q04 What force do the indefinite ahad and fronted conditional have? [وَإِنْ أَحَدٌۭ مِّنَ ٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ · rhetoric]
  9:6/q04/p1 They foreground any individual seeker and strengthen the protection rule. — reasons: Sibawayh, as reported by Razi, sees fronting the important object as protecting blood despite the fight command. Ibn Jinni finds breadth in conditional in and allows ahad to mean one individual, citing 4:128 and 4:176. Al-Burhan adds expected occurrence and special identification; Ibn Ashur says the rare hypothetical precludes an excuse that access was denied.
  9:6/q04/p2 The doubled explanatory construction gives emphasis. — reasons: Al-Burhan says an object or subject mentioned first and resumed by a pronoun or explanatory verb has greater force than ordinary order, citing 17:100, 76:31, 7:30, 82:1, and 84:1.
9:6/q05 What does istajāraka mean? [ٱسْتَجَارَكَ · meaning]
  9:6/q05/p1 To ask for shelter, security or a protector. — reasons: Al-Jishumi relates it to taking someone into one's neighborhood; Asad invokes the Arabian duty to protect neighbors. Ibn Ashur calls neighboring a metaphor from safe residence and says ajāra means securing rather than literally making a neighbor. Al-Tha'labi adduces Arabic usage and poetry.
9:6/q06 For what purposes does the verse require or allow safe conduct? [ٱسْتَجَارَكَ فَأَجِرْهُ · law] (turns on: Whether hearing God's speech defines the seeker's reason for asking or the host's purpose in granting refuge.)
  9:6/q06/p1 Protection to hear and examine Islam is obligatory; other purposes can also be protected according to their circumstances and Muslim interests. — reasons: Qurtubi calls religious inquiry the verse's apparent case and other grants matters of public interest. Elmalili distinguishes obligatory religious protection from context dependent worldly requests. Ibn Kathir lists missions, trade, peace, truce and tribute; Wahidi lists trade, envoys, and property retrieval. Ibn Ashur allows reasonable motives and says discussion of Islam will occur during a temporary stay. Malik would return an ambiguous enemy encountered on a road to safety, and Ibn al-Qasim applies the same protection to a coastal trader expecting peaceful sale. Ibn Ashur cites the Prophet's prohibition on betraying a betrayer.
  9:6/q06/p2 The request itself is general, while hearing God's word is the grantor's purpose. — reasons: Elmalili prefers no restriction of the seeker to religious needs; Alusi says Ali's reported 'need' may be general, although he identifies its immediate case as religious.
  9:6/q06/p3 The verse specifically protects a request for religious inquiry or a comparable religious need. — reasons: Tabataba'i denies that the verse establishes general asylum for unrelated reasons; Shaykh al-Islam ties the request to the stated end of hearing. Some interpreters read Ali's 'need' religiously.
9:6/q07 Who may grant effective safe conduct? [فَأَجِرْهُ · law] (turns on: Which persons can bind the community by a pledge and how to treat an ambiguous pledge.)
  9:6/q07/p1 The ruler, a free Muslim, a slave, a free woman, and a fighting capable minor may grant it. — reasons: Qurtubi says the ruler acts for the public benefit and all accept a free man's pledge; he prefers the majority's permission for a slave, citing the hadith that even the lowest Muslim grants protection, and says lack of a war stipend is immaterial. Abu Hayyan lists the Awza'i, Thawri, Shafi'i, Ahmad, Ishaq, Muhammad ibn al-Hasan, Abu Thawr, Dawud and the known Maliki view; a fighting boy belongs to the defenders.
  9:6/q07/p2 A slave's or woman's pledge needs the imam's review or approval; some deny the slave's pledge. — reasons: Abu Hanifa and another Maliki view deny a slave's effective pledge; Ibn Habib refers it to the imam. Ibn al-Majishun alone conditions a free woman's pledge on imam approval, which Abu Hayyan calls anomalous.
  9:6/q07/p3 Even an ambiguous pledge by a child or insane person may protect the entrant pending safe return. — reasons: Wahidi's jurists treat a minor's or insane person's promise as ambiguous safe conduct; Elmalili reports protection for lawful religious and trade entrants even under such a guarantee.
9:6/q08 What does the command 'grant him protection' require? [فَأَجِرْهُ · law]
  9:6/q08/p1 Secure the seeker from killing and attack while he hears the message, then preserve his safety until return. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj specifies protection from killing; al-Nahhas removes him from the kill order. Al-Dahhak says maintain him during the stay. Al-Biqa'i requires protection against anyone who might harm him; Kuranyolu stresses absence of fear and pressure.
  9:6/q08/p2 A treatyless arrival can either be received or returned safely. — reasons: Aṭāʾ's report leaves the two practical dispositions open.
9:6/q09 How long may the protection last? [فَأَجِرْهُ حَتَّىٰ يَسْمَعَ · law] (turns on: Whether a fixed four month interval governs or the ruler and circumstances determine the stay.)
  9:6/q09/p1 It continues through genuine inquiry and until the visitor reaches safety; its exact duration is set by circumstances or the ruler. — reasons: Razi says custom governs, continuing while truth seeking is sincere but not for a deceptive delay. Alusi prefers the ruler's judgment. Ibn Kathir reports four months permissible, a full year disallowed, and disagreement between. Ibn Ashur says the return duty can take long.
  9:6/q09/p2 Four months is the stipulated respite. — reasons: Alusi reports al-Nisaburi calling four months the sound Shafi'i view; Ibn Kathir reports its permissibility.
9:6/q10 What does it mean for the seeker to 'hear' God's speech? [يَسْمَعَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether physical recitation is enough and how much teaching suffices for understanding.)
  9:6/q10/p1 He must hear and understand the Quran, its proofs, commands and prohibitions; a non Arabic speaker needs comprehension. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says hearing stands for comprehension because the ear leads to it. Tabataba'i makes intelligibility rather than Arabic sound decisive for non Arabs; Alusi and Elmalili say an eloquent Arab may understand from hearing. Al-Tabrisi requires time to consider; Abu Hayyan calls the Quran the greatest miracle.
  9:6/q10/p2 The amount heard may be the whole Quran or the essential message of this surah. — reasons: Study Quran reports both thresholds without choosing; Maʾwardi gives the corresponding scopes of 'speech'.
