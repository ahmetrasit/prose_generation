104:3/q10 How does the verse's immortality language work as figurative speech? [مَالَهُۥٓ أَخْلَدَهُۥ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the sentence reports belief or exaggerates behavior into a claim.)
  104:3/q10/p1 It portrays the hoarder's conduct as though money had made him immortal. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī calls it representational metaphor and Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it an emphatic simile; he says nobody actually thinks wealth literally immortalizes. ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī's comparison theory explains the force: 'like a lion' assigns a measured share of a common quality, while 'the lion' maximizes it; the lion's naming rests chiefly on courage. He distinguishes identity between two names from asserted complete likeness; complete likeness borrows force from identity because at different times the viewer might think he sees the same thing. Applying 'he himself' to the lion comparison suppresses differences and heightens the shared quality.
  104:3/q10/p2 He may literally hold the delusion that money grants immortality. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī allows literal belief from arrogance, preoccupation with accumulation, neglect of the coming terrors, or trusting visible causes and wealth for life and health.
104:3/q11 What does the wealth signify inwardly? [مَالَهُۥٓ · inward]
  104:3/q11/p1 It is a hoard of blameworthy morals and low qualities. — reasons: Al-Bursevī relays al-Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya: the person treats this hoard as provision for stages of the afterlife and entry into God's presence. Al-Qāshānī contrasts bodily riches, which perish, with enduring knowledge and spiritual virtues; he says ignorance, a vice of the royal faculty, roots all vices and leads to punishment overwhelming the heart.
104:3/q12 What reports describe the man's wealth or conduct? [* · reports] (turns on: The reports give distinct narratives and conflicting dinar amounts; no note resolves the amounts.)
  104:3/q12/p1 He boasted his wealth could provision Quraysh without running out, then counted it after a warning and provoked revelation. — reasons: Muqātil says he swore he could distribute wealth across Quraysh while alive without depleting it and challenged poverty; when told that its Giver could remove it, he counted his possessions and told Muḥammad that even if all were bread it would not run out. Sūrat al-Humazah was then revealed.
  104:3/q12/p2 Al-Akhnas owned four thousand dinars. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī and Abū Ḥayyān report this amount, while also transmitting a different figure.
  104:3/q12/p3 Al-Akhnas owned ten thousand dinars. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī and Abū Ḥayyān also report this amount; the two figures are alternatives.
  104:3/q12/p4 A rich man told al-Ḥasan he saved thousands against hardship, rulers, calamities and poverty. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan replied that the man would leave the money to people who would neither thank him nor excuse him.
104:3/q13 Whom does this portrayal target? [* · referent] (turns on: Whether the sketch designates a moral type, contemporary polytheists, or hypocrites.)
  104:3/q13/p1 A type of wealthy, self-reliant sinner who hoards and mocks. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum calls him a prototypical sinner whose unfounded trust in money fuels accumulation and antisocial conduct. Asad treats the hoarding attitude as blameworthy alongside slander; the Study Quran says the sūrah concerns torment for hoarders.
  104:3/q13/p2 Actual polytheists who mocked the Messenger and believers. — reasons: Quṭb infers this from the severe warning, but considers reports naming particular individuals unreliable.
  104:3/q13/p3 Hypocrites absorbed by money and the pleasures it purchases. — reasons: Islahi says their conduct reveals an attachment to this world's pleasures and contradicts belief in the Hereafter.
104:3/q14 What does this claim teach about wealth and enduring life? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The notes develop both the false promise of wealth and the conduct it fosters.)
  104:3/q14/p1 Wealth cannot secure permanence; lasting life comes through faith, knowledge, virtue, righteous deeds and afterlife striving. — reasons: Al-Rāzī cites ʿAlī: wealth keepers die while scholars remain through time. Al-Qāshānī says bodily riches perish but knowledge and spiritual virtues last. Elmalılı invokes faith in truth, knowledge and righteous action and the preceding sūrah. Ibn ʿĀshūr says hoarding withholds God's due in spending and zakāt. Islahi argues recognition of life's brevity would redirect spending and competition to the Hereafter; he cites Jesus' teaching that the heart follows where one keeps wealth. Bint al-Shāṭi says the Qurʾān reserves real eternity for the afterlife (21:34, 21:8, 26:129).
  104:3/q14/p2 Attachment to money breeds false self-sufficiency, greed and contempt for others. — reasons: Quṭb links counting and savoring money to verbal and gestural mockery; he says Islam forbids such faultfinding. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites 96:6–7 on self-sufficiency producing rebellion. Asad describes material acquisition becoming almost religious and displacing spiritual concerns; Islahi says the direction of one's life reveals whether this world or the next is its destination.
104:3/q15 How does the next rebuke answer the claimed immortality? [* · links] (turns on: Whether kallā primarily negates his delusion or affirms the following sworn threat.)
  104:3/q15/p1 Kallā declares the claim false and rebukes the hoarder. — reasons: ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd Allāh, client of Ghufra, says God's kallā marks the hearer wrong. Al-Biqāʿī says he dies like every creature; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the oath-emphasizing lām promises he will be cast into al-Ḥuṭama. Al-Bursevī also allows a rebuke of the earlier mockery; Ibn ʿĀshūr connects it to failure to spend and pay zakāt.
  104:3/q15/p2 Kallā means 'indeed' and introduces the force of an implied oath. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says the lām of layunbadhanna answers this implied oath. Al-Bursevī also permits the oath connection.
104:3/q16 How does the punishment reverse the man's expectation? [* · links]
  104:3/q16/p1 The supposedly permanent rich mocker is thrown into crushing Fire and confined. — reasons: Al-Akhfash says both man and wealth are cast into the Crusher. Quṭb sees a measured reversal: a rich scoffer expecting permanence is discarded, crushed in God's Fire, burned in the heart that generated pride, then confined without aid like a tied animal; sound pattern and progression from threat through question to explanation magnify it. Corpus Coranicum says verse 4 counters his imagined immortality through the root h-t-m. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says Ḥuṭama intensifies crushing, may suggest eating, and is explained as God's kindled Fire; the Study Quran compares hoarded gold and silver branding owners in 9:34–35, alongside 70:18.
104:3/q17 How does the claim connect with the sūrah and other scriptural passages? [* · links] (turns on: The sources trace different connections: the preceding sūrah, the current moral sequence, other woe texts, or a following dual reference.)
  104:3/q17/p1 It identifies the loser of the preceding sūrah and continues warnings against human injustice. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says this Meccan sūrah explains the loser of 103:2 by depicting a particular sort of loser. Abū Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr relates it to the ungrateful human at 100:6, humanity in loss at 103:2, and the wealth-deluded person here. Elmalılı recalls the prior sūrah's remedy of faith, knowledge and righteous action.
  104:3/q17/p2 The wealth delusion drives the sūrah's hoarding and slander. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says perceived self-sufficiency breeds rebellion and mockery, citing 96:6–7. Quṭb says the Qurʾān's condemnation both directs the mission and reassures abused believers that God sees and punishes the offense. Asad inserts 'Woe unto him' again because verses 2–3 portray an attitude distinct from verse 1's two types.
  104:3/q17/p3 It parallels other woe and wealth passages. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum compares vice catalogues after woe at 107:4–7, 83:1–3 and 83:10–11, 52:11–12 and 51:60; 83:1–6 is closest for wealth and a following question. It cites Hebrew Bible Micah 2:1–3 as another prophetic woe speech. Its comparison with 90:6 contrasts hoarding and ostentatious spending as two self-powered immortality delusions. Islahi compares monumental building at 26:128–29.
  104:3/q17/p4 A dual-form recitation can take the following verb to refer to both the hamza and the lamza. — reasons: Al-Bursevī reports this grammatical reading and its supporting dual recitation.
