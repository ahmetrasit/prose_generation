## THE CURRENT MAP
39:10/q01 Why is the Prophet told to say “My servants”? [قُلْ يَٰعِبَادِ · rhetoric]
  39:10/q01/p1 God's own address honors and reassures believers while strengthening the command. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says the possessive promises nearness against opponents. Bursawī says the Prophet repeats God's very words. Ālūsī calls it direct divine exhortation; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the command marks importance and elevates believers. Asad supplies “God speaks” to clarify the possessive.
  39:10/q01/p2 The command follows praise of the devout in the preceding verse to strengthen their resolve. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr reads an implied “say to the believers” after 39:9 and notes the vocative yā is elided.
39:10/q02 How is يَٰعِبَادِ read and written? [يَٰعِبَادِ · readings] (turns on: Whether this verse has a pronounced possessive yāʾ, or reports of it belong to 39:53.)
  39:10/q02/p1 The possessive yāʾ is omitted in this verse. — reasons: Ibn Mujāhid reports Qunbul from Ibn Kathīr with kasra on dāl and no yāʾ, as with other readers; al-Nasafī says most omit it. Bursawī says the kasra suffices. Ibn ʿĀshūr says all ten omit it at pause and liaison, following the consonantal text and distinguishing 39:53.
  39:10/q02/p2 A possessive yāʾ is read in at least some circumstances. — reasons: Ibn Mujāhid says Abū ʿAmr separates at an āya ending but joins عباد الذين with yāʾ. Ibn ʿAṭiyya attributes open yāʾ to most, silent yāʾ to Abū ʿAmr, ʿĀṣim and al-Aʿmash, and deletion in liaison to others. Ibn ʿĀshūr rejects this attribution as confusion with 39:53.
39:10/q03 Why is the shorter form يَٰعِبَادِ used? [يَٰعِبَادِ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the deletion is explained by the size of the addressed group or the sufficiency of the kasra.)
  39:10/q03/p1 The shorter form suits the smaller group addressed here. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī relates written length to group size, contrasting longer ʿibādī at 39:53 and 39:17; he says additions and deletions are contextual across passages.
  39:10/q03/p2 The omitted yāʾ is sufficiently signaled by the preceding kasra. — reasons: Bursawī links the spelling to grammatical economy while stressing the honor of God's possessive address.
39:10/q04 Who are the believing servants addressed? [يَٰعِبَادِ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ · referent] (turns on: Whether the address is read in its historical setting, broadly of monotheists, or inwardly.)
  39:10/q04/p1 Jaʿfar ibn Abī Ṭālib and companions preparing to migrate to Abyssinia. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās is cited by al-Qurṭubī and Ibn ʿĀshūr; Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Baghawī, Abū Ḥayyān, and Ālūsī report the same occasion, with variants in whether the group resolved to depart or had migrated.
  39:10/q04/p2 Believers who affirm God's unity and are pressed by persecution. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī calls them monotheists. Ibn ʿĀshūr says their established faith survives pressure that could force outward disbelief; Iṣlāḥī describes oppressed poor Muslims in Mecca.
  39:10/q04/p3 The spiritually elect servants, freed from all attachment but God. — reasons: Bursawī's Kashf al-Asrār calls them those preferring God's pleasure to ego desire; Bursawī describes seekers moved by longing and love, illustrates self-abasement through a Prophetic saying and Abū Yazīd's striking wish concerning Hell. Ālūsī echoes longing and exclusive seeking.
39:10/q05 What does “fear your Lord” require? [ٱتَّقُوا۟ رَبَّكُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether taqwā is explained as outward obedience, the deepening of existing faith, or inward detachment.)
  39:10/q05/p1 Obey God, avoid sins, and remain firm in reverent responsibility. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī and al-Baghawī give obedience and avoidance; al-Naḥḥās, al-Ṭabrisī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī and al-Nasafī clarify avoidance of punishment or prohibitions. Bursawī adds persistence; Kuranyolu calls taqwā careful responsibility and cites 7:26 and 6:160.
  39:10/q05/p2 Deepen existing faith and sincerity despite hardship. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says basic belief can coexist with incomplete piety, and God needs nothing from obedience. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the already pious may weaken under pressure, so the command prepares migration. Kuranyolu names faith, piety and excellence as conditions of a good Muslim life.
  39:10/q05/p3 Use God as protection from everything besides Him and remove the veil of attachment. — reasons: Bursawī describes escape from separation and joy in divine beauty; al-Baqlī makes taqwā stripping the self of attachment until it stands unveiled.
39:10/q06 Does the command to believers imply grave sin removes faith? [ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ ٱتَّقُوا۟ رَبَّكُمْ · theology] (turns on: Whether grave sin cancels faith and its reward or faith remains despite sin.)
  39:10/q06/p1 No: faith remains even when a believer has not fulfilled taqwā. — reasons: Al-Rāzī argues that commanding believers to add God-consciousness presupposes faith with sin; exhortation is stronger if they remain believers when they fall short.
  39:10/q06/p2 Avoidance of major sins preserves faith's reward; grave sin cancels it. — reasons: Al-Qāḍī says the command protects faith and its recompense from nullification. Al-Rāzī rejects the implication.
39:10/q07 What is the iʿrāb of ٱتَّقُوا۟? [ٱتَّقُوا۟ · grammar]
  39:10/q07/p1 Its tāʾ replaces an original wāw. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās gives the morphological observation, repeated by al-Qurṭubī.
39:10/q08 What does doing good, أَحْسَنُوا۟, entail? [أَحْسَنُوا۟ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether excellence is described by outward acts, the Prophetic definition of worship, or an inward realization.)
  39:10/q08/p1 It includes faithful obedience, righteous conduct, and kindness to others. — reasons: Al-Baghawī calls the doers believers acting well; al-Qushayrī says excellence is doing all one can; al-Wāḥidī specifies monotheists who obey; al-Jishumī includes generosity and inviting others to religion; al-Ṭabrisī says virtuous acts and kindness. Ibn ʿĀshūr relates them to the God-fearing in 39:9 and says the relative phrase embraces all with the quality. Bursawī stresses sincerity and the testimony of faith; a bad deed cannot earn good recompense.
  39:10/q08/p2 Iḥsān is worshipping God as though seeing Him, aware that He sees the worshipper. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān reports the Prophetic definition; Bursawī and Ālūsī invoke the Gabriel report, with Bursawī adding that witnessing brings full sincerity and guards against ostentation and shirk. Ibn ʿĀshūr relates this to the devout of 39:9.
  39:10/q08/p3 Inward excellence is seeking God alone and witnessing His beauty. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī speaks of effacing attributes and taking on divine attributes; Najm al-Dīn says sincere seekers ask no good from others and find God; al-Khujandī's poem makes seeking the key to the treasure; al-Baqlī describes unveiling in heart and spirit. Ālūsī gives exclusive seeking.
39:10/q09 What good is promised to those who do good? [لِلَّذِينَ أَحْسَنُوا۟ فِى هَٰذِهِ ٱلدُّنْيَا حَسَنَةٌۭ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether “in this world” locates good deeds or the reward, and whether the promise is confined to one realm.)
  39:10/q09/p1 Paradise is the good reward for goodness done in this world. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī, Muqātil, al-Wāḥidī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Jishumī, al-Rāzī and many later sources locate reward in the Hereafter. Al-Rāzī argues the indefinite good is majestic and lasting, that unbelievers share health, and that the restrictive wording excludes shared worldly benefits (43:33); he cites the saying that worldly life is a believer's prison and unbeliever's garden. Al-Qushayrī makes the same worldly-benefits objection in al-Qurṭubī. Al-Ālūsī's opening exposition treats the indefinite as magnification; Bursevi adds vision of God.
  39:10/q09/p2 Worldly health, safety, sufficiency, and well-being are the good. — reasons: Al-Suddī is cited by al-Ṭabarī, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Māwardī, al-Jishumī, al-Baghawī and others. Al-Rāzī reports a saying naming security, health and sufficiency as inexhaustible blessings. Abū Ḥayyān adds success and divine protection; al-Ṭabrisī adds thanks and reputation. Al-Ālūsī describes this as an immediate small share, but objects that unbelievers too receive health, citing 41:50; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects the proposed attachment to ḥasana.
  39:10/q09/p3 Goodness includes reward in this world and the Hereafter. — reasons: Al-Māwardī offers worldly reward besides final recompense; al-Qurṭubī replies that grateful believers receive both; Ibn Kathīr states both. Al-Biqāʿī prefers breadth across both realms. Ālūsī later prefers both, with inward and outward action; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī lists peace of mind, sound soul and freedom from dependence here, lasting delight there. Kuranyolu prefers the unrestricted promise; Study Quran likewise allows health and peace here and good beyond.
  39:10/q09/p4 The worldly good is praise, good reputation, kind words, or God's guardianship. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās includes God's friendship, praise and good names; al-Māwardī proposes praise with next-life recompense. Al-Jishumī and al-Ṭabrisī cite praise and gratitude; al-Qurṭubī reports a good reputation; al-Ālūsī says God's guardianship is distinctive to believers.
  39:10/q09/p5 The worldly good is victory, spoils, divine support, or relief from enemies. — reasons: Al-Naqqāsh says victory and spoils; Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports al-Suddī's victory and support gloss, though he prefers the Hereafter. ʿAṭāʾ, reported by Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī, ties relief from enemies to emigrating to Medina; al-Qurṭubī includes victory and booty.
  39:10/q09/p6 Inwardly the good is finding God, seeing His enduring Face, and divine unveiling. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī calls divine vision the good of those witnessing; Najm al-Dīn and Ālūsī describe finding God. Al-Baqlī distinguishes worldly inspirations from immersion in eternity and abiding in the attributes hereafter.
