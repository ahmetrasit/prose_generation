## THE CURRENT MAP
90:13/q01 What does فك mean? [فَكُّ · meaning]
  90:13/q01/p1 It is releasing something from a binding restraint or another person's hold. — reasons: Wahidi's authorities explain separation by breaking a chain, untying a knot, releasing a pledge, or opening a seal. Razi cites al-Akhtal's line about fetters; Abu Hayyan and Alusi cite poetry about a captive. Ibn Ashur stresses removal from a possessor and the effort of pulling something from a resistant holder. Bursevi compares separating one thing from another.
  90:13/q01/p2 The verbal noun is fakk or fakāk, with fatḥa rather than kasra in fakāk. — reasons: Al-Farra gives the vocalization; Wahidi, Razi, and Alusi transmit it. Alusi also explicitly names fakk as a verbal noun.
90:13/q02 Why is the person called a رقبة? [رَقَبَةٍ · rhetoric]
  90:13/q02/p1 The neck stands for the whole person, especially a slave or captive whose neck is bound. — reasons: Mawardi likens enslavement to a captive's neck restraint and cites Hassan ibn Thabit. Qurtubi says bondage binds the neck; Ibn Ashur calls it part for whole and says it is the first shackled part noticed. Elmalili likewise says part for whole; Bursevi compares 'head' or 'back' for a mount. Samarrai says it evokes distress.
90:13/q03 What does فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ ask a person to do? [فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether 'neck' denotes an enslaved or captive person, a broader external restraint, or the person's own moral and inward bondage.)
  90:13/q03/p1 Emancipate an enslaved person. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, Tabari, Baghawi and others give the ordinary sense. Tabarisi says removal of the restraining status establishes liberty. Qutb says direct release and aid yield the same freedom. Ibn Ashur calls this a basic legal preference for freedom. Elmalili treats a single slave as the simplest case.
  90:13/q03/p2 Rescue a captive from captivity. — reasons: Mawardi distinguishes captivity from manumission, citing Hassan's verse for releasing captives. Ibn Atiyya says ransom is one person's undertaking a captive's release. Qurtubi and Abu Hayyan include captive release.
  90:13/q03/p3 Help pay the price of another person's freedom, including a contracted slave. — reasons: Wahidi states contribution to the price. Razi and Nasafi include a contracted slave's payment; al-Bara's report distinguishes outright manumission from helping. Biqai says 'release' covers even a small contribution. Alusi reasons that if full manumission is included, helping is included all the more.
  90:13/q03/p4 Release a person from other restraints, such as debt, retaliation, oppression, illness or social subjugation. — reasons: Wahidi compares a pledge and seal; Kashshaf includes another obligation, and the reported saying specifies retaliation or debt. Biqai lists captivity, injustice, debt and illness. Asad adds economic, social and political exploitation; Samarrai contrasts broad fakk with the narrower taḥrīr of expiation and applies it to oppressed peoples.
  90:13/q03/p5 Free one's own soul from sins through repentance and obedience. — reasons: Ikrima gives repentance. Mawardi and Qurtubi prefer avoiding sins and doing good deeds, saying the emancipation hadith does not exclude it. Al-Jubba'i speaks of accepting the burden of obedience. Elmalili connects each person's captivity to deeds and cites 52:21.
  90:13/q03/p6 Free oneself through worship that leads to Paradise and release from the Fire. — reasons: Razi calls this the greater freedom. Alusi reports it but finds the reading unlikely because later deeds would become particulars following a general statement. Study Quran reports it separately from repentance.
  90:13/q03/p7 Free the inward self from ego, greed, desire, and self-regard. — reasons: Al-Wasiti names the self, one's deeds, seeing one's own merit and seeking closeness; al-Qasim names greed's humiliation. Qushayri describes release from desire and people through a guide, and surrender of personal planning. Qashani speaks of detaching the heart from natural inclinations, or cultivating virtues when detachment fails.
90:13/q04 How is فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ read? [فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ · readings] (turns on: The transmitted vocalization and whether fakk and the feeding word are nouns or finite verbs, with further case variants in the following phrase.)
  90:13/q04/p1 As nominal fakku raqabatin with nominal iṭʿām: 'freeing a neck or feeding'. — reasons: Reported for most Medinan, Kufan and Syrian readers, including Nafi, Asim, Ibn Amir and Hamza; Ibn Ashur also names Abu Jafar, Yaqub and Khalaf. Some notes give nominative fakk and iṭʿām with genitive raqaba. Al-Farra reports Hasan and Ali with the nouns. Abu Ubayd and Akhfash prefer it because it directly defines the obstacle; Akhfash, Abu Hatim and Abu Ubayd also favor it.
  90:13/q04/p2 As past verbs fakka raqabatan aw aṭʿama: 'he freed a neck or fed'. — reasons: Reported for Ibn Kathir, Abu Amr and al-Kisai, and in some accounts Hasan, Abu Raja and Ali. Abu Amr and Tabari argue it aligns verbs with the subsequent thumma kāna; al-Farra also prefers this coordination in some reports. Tabari accepts both sound readings. The verb has an implicit subject from the earlier human being and raqaba is its accusative object. Abu Amr's transmissions show small attachment differences.
  90:13/q04/p3 As mixed noun and verb forms, with reported differences over the case of raqaba and coordination of feeding. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya reports some Successors' fakki raqabatin and an accusative form followed by nominal iṭʿām. Abu Hayyan and Alusi report construct fakk raqabatin with past aṭʿama, coordinated by verbal sense. These are distinct from the two common complete patterns.
  90:13/q04/p4 Related readings put dhā masghaba in the accusative as the fed person, rather than describing the day. — reasons: Hasan, Abu Raja and Ali are named in differing reports. Hasan has nominal iṭʿām; on this parse yatīman is apposition or adjective to the hungry person. The common dhī describes the day by metaphor. Tabarisi also gives an anomalous construction. Samīn explains the omitted feeding agent by a Basran grammatical rule.
90:13/q05 How does the phrase explain the steep obstacle grammatically? [فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the obstacle is directly defined by the deed, requires an implied 'crossing', or is explained by finite verbs.)
  90:13/q05/p1 The nominal phrase directly defines the obstacle by figuratively identifying a hard deed with an ascent. — reasons: Tabari likens this to the Qur'anic explanation of Hāwiya as fire at 101:10–11; Farisi notes explanatory syntax. Alusi and Ibn Ashur say metaphor removes the need for an inserted noun. Tabatabai rejects supplying 'crossing'. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan supply an implicit explanatory question; Ibn Ashur supplies 'it is'.
  90:13/q05/p2 The nominal construction needs an implied 'crossing' so that an action explains an action. — reasons: Farisi says without implied iqtihām the pass would be equated literally with emancipation, citing explanatory constructions at 104:5–6 and 101:10–11. Samīn similarly supplies iqtihām. Alusi allows this if the reports describing a real ascent are sound.
  90:13/q05/p3 The finite verbs replace or explain 'he crossed the obstacle', with the intervening question parenthetical. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says the verbal reading needs no implied question. Farisi argues an explanatory clause need not copy a nominal predicate and cites 69:4 and 3:59. Nasafi and Ibn Ashur treat the verbs as apposition or explanatory replacement; Ibn Ashur also allows repeated negative force. Tabari connects the verbs to thumma kāna.
  90:13/q05/p4 The noun reading can be related to later verbs by an elided an. — reasons: Farra and Tabari cite a poetic line of Tarafa where overt an in a second clause permits it to be understood earlier. Nahhas objects that this would demand the accusative and an cannot vanish without a substitute.
90:13/q06 How does the negation before this verse apply to freeing and feeding? [فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the preceding clause is straightforward negation or a rebuking omitted question.)
  90:13/q06/p1 One lā covers the coordinated deeds, although Arabic often repeats it. — reasons: Farra compares 75:31. Ibn Ashur says 'crossed' entails freeing and feeding, or the later faith clause supplies the second negation; any context preventing ambiguity suffices, as al-Hariri illustrates. Alusi says the negation repeats in sense through the definition. Baydawi says lā suits the multiple intended repeated deeds better than lam.
  90:13/q06/p2 The expression can be a rebuking question with an omitted interrogative particle. — reasons: Ibn Ashur prefers asking why the boaster spent on gambling and wine but did not spend on liberation and famine relief.
90:13/q07 What is the steep obstacle that this deed crosses? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the ascent is the morally difficult deed itself or a reported place beyond this life.)
  90:13/q07/p1 It is the difficult moral ascent of freeing, feeding, faith and other righteous deeds. — reasons: Majaz and Qatada identify its practical acts. Baydawi calls the mountain pass a metaphor requiring self-discipline. Alusi and Ibn Ashur identify the hard deed with ascent. Bint al-Shati rejects allegory because the Qur'an itself answers with these deeds; Wahidi objects to the obviousness of a merely physical Hell obstacle. Samarrai identifies release and feeding as the answer.
  90:13/q07/p2 It is a steep, slippery mountain in Hell. — reasons: Ibn Umar and Hasan are reported with this location. Abu Raja reports 7,000 years up and 7,000 down; Alusi requires sound Prophetic evidence before treating this as exegesis.
  90:13/q07/p3 It is Hell itself. — reasons: Ibn Abbas is reported with this identification; Hasan also identifies the obstacle as Hell in Durr. Alusi withholds acceptance without a sound Prophetic report.
