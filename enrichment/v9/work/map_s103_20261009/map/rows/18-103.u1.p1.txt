18:103/q10 Why does the verse attach the strongest loss to deeds? [بِٱلْأَخْسَرِينَ أَعْمَٰلًا · rhetoric]
  18:103/q10/p1 The passage joins lost striving to supposedly skillful action, and deeds frame the surrounding discussion. — reasons: Sāmarrāʾī says this is the Qur'an's sole attribution of loss to work and compares 18:30; Ibn Kathīr says they follow a way not approved by God while expecting acceptance.
18:103/q11 What makes these people's work a loss? [بِٱلْأَخْسَرِينَ أَعْمَٰلًا · interpretation] (turns on: The notes differ on whether failure lies in unbelief, mistaken evaluation of evil acts, corruption of good acts, or the aim toward which effort is directed.)
  18:103/q11/p1 They labor expecting gain or reward, but their worldly effort comes to nothing while they suppose it good. — reasons: Yaḥyā reports al-Suddī's description; Thaʿlabī and Baghawī compare a failed trader; Ṭabrisī says the disbelieving Jews and Christians thought their worship virtuous; Ibn Kathīr says the work is invalid under an approved religious way though they think it accepted; Study Quran connects futility to denial of God's signs and meeting Him, citing 7:147.
  18:103/q11/p2 Disbelief or corrupt worship voids even acts good in themselves, although their doers expect reward. — reasons: Qurṭubī names corrupt belief, with disbelief specific here; al-Irshād gives charity and freeing slaves as intrinsically good examples; Ālūsī relays the contrast with intrinsically bad acts; Jishumī says worship of others leads to the Fire; Ibn ʿĀshūr says idol patrons are useless.
  18:103/q11/p3 They treat intrinsically bad acts, including shirk, as good worship. — reasons: Ālūsī relays this contrast from Irshād al-ʿAql al-Salīm; Kur'an Yolu says polytheists believe idols draw them near God and deem shirk obedience, yet their deeds receive no value in either life; Biqāʿī details varied forms of object worship.
  18:103/q11/p4 Good deeds can be ruined by boastfulness, reproaching beneficiaries, or worship for display. — reasons: Abū Bakr al-Warrāq specifies demanding thanks and performing worship for reputation; Māwardī mentions boasters; Qurṭubī names ostentation as a possible cause of nullification.
  18:103/q11/p5 Choosing worldly gain over God's pleasure and the afterlife redirects effort toward a different kind of work. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu contrasts aims; Māwardī identifies those seeking this world and abandoning the next.
18:103/q12 What duty or warning does the question teach? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The notes draw both a duty to examine one's striving and a warning of its final consequence.)
  18:103/q12/p1 One must pursue truth seriously, since earnest effort can still go astray. — reasons: Jishumī expressly draws this duty from 'shall We inform you'; Qurṭubī warns that people can think nullified work good.
  18:103/q12/p2 Those whose deeds fail through these evils face Hell. — reasons: Yaḥyā links the question to Hell prepared for unbelievers; Jishumī says worshippers of others exhaust themselves and end in the Fire; Kur'an Yolu says the offenders described here will be punished in Hell.
18:103/q13 How is the lām of هَلْ pronounced before نُنَبِّئُكُم? [هَلْ نُنَبِّئُكُم · readings] (turns on: The reading disagreement turns on lām assimilation before nūn and the scope of Hishām's transmitted routes.)
  18:103/q13/p1 Al-Kisāʾī assimilates the lām into the following nūn. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī reports that al-Kisāʾī assimilates lām of hal or bal before all eight listed consonants, including this nūn; al-Naḥḥās says assimilation is possible at a distance because the articulation points are close.
  18:103/q13/p2 Ḥamza and Hishām make the lām distinct before this nūn. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās judges Ḥamza's reading more correct on Sībawayh's concern about lām-to-nūn assimilation. Ibn al-Jazarī says Ḥamza assimilates before tāʾ, thāʾ, and sīn but not this nūn; Hishām shows lām before ḍād and nūn and assimilates before six other consonants, which the author calls the sound majority view.
  18:103/q13/p3 Hishām's assimilated readings differ by transmission route. — reasons: Some confine his assimilation to al-Ḥalwānī; al-ʿAlāʾ, al-Dānī, and al-Hudhalī give both al-Ḥalwānī and al-Dājūnī; al-Dānī and al-Hudhalī explicitly report complete assimilation without a dispute.
18:103/q14 What pronunciation principles are recorded with the recitation material? [* · readings] (turns on: These are distinct pronunciation and madd topics in the cited material.)
  18:103/q14/p1 Each letter's point of articulation and qualities must be learned in isolation and in context. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī says practice makes precision natural and neighbouring letters alter sounds. He compares hamza with hāʾ, ʿayn with ḥāʾ, ghayn with khāʾ, jīm with shīn and yāʾ, ḍād with ẓāʾ, ṭāʾ with dāl and tāʾ, ẓāʾ with dhāl and thāʾ, and ṣād with zāy and sīn; he specifies the relevant differences in voicing, stoppage, openness, emphasis, frication, spreading, and istiṭāla.
  18:103/q14/p2 Madd lengths follow the stronger operative cause and vary with continuation, pause, and reader. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī says lexical madd before hamza outranks semantic madd; at a stop on words like yashāʾ qasr is excluded, and a reciter using full madd in continuation cannot use tawassuṭ. Warsh keeps full madd on al-samāʾ; al-Azraq's pause options on yastahziʾūn, muttakiʾīn, and al-maʾāb track connected reading; Warsh's examples raʾā aydiyahum, jāʾū abāhum, and al-sūʾā an kadhdhabū allow three pause options, while barāʾ and āmīn al-bayt stay full.
  18:103/q14/p3 Madd levels may be counted in alifs, but the count describes a continuum; temporary madd allows three lengths. — reasons: Al-Itqān reports al-Hudhalī's five-alif sixth level for Ḥamza and six-alif seventh level for Warsh; Ibn al-Jazarī calls such level counting verbal because small increases create the next level. For temporary madd, the author allows extension, intermediate length, or shortening for every reciter.
18:103/q15 What occasion of revelation is reported for the losers? [* · reports] (turns on: The competing reports name different groups; one is challenged by the description of unbelief.)
  18:103/q15/p1 The verse was revealed concerning monks and priests confined in monasteries. — reasons: Jishumī attributes the report to ʿAlī.
  18:103/q15/p2 The verse was revealed concerning the people of Harūrāʾ. — reasons: Jishumī attributes the report to Abū ʿAlī; al-Aṣamm objects that the following language calls them unbelievers though they are Muslims, while Jishumī would accept an authentic report and permits kufr for denial of some signs.
18:103/q16 How does this warning fit the surrounding sūra and verses? [* · links] (turns on: The cited links emphasize different elements of the surrounding account.)
  18:103/q16/p1 It turns prior warnings about idolatrous reliance and failed perception into an account of futile works and leads toward the close of the sūra. — reasons: Yaḥyā places it after Hell is prepared for unbelievers; Biqāʿī connects heedlessness of remembrance to this warning; Ibn ʿĀshūr links idol patrons and their hoped-for favor to futile effort; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the six verses conclude themes of worldly seduction, protectors besides God, spiritual blindness, and bad ends before 18:110.
  18:103/q16/p2 The surrounding account also affirms Gog and Magog and the trumpet blast as signs of the Hour. — reasons: Jishumī records these earlier passage themes.
18:103/q17 How does the term for greatest loss relate to other Qur'anic passages? [بِٱلْأَخْسَرِينَ · links] (turns on: The comparative explanations turn on which offences and emphatic wording each passage contains.)
  18:103/q17/p1 Hūd's offenders are worse than Naḥl's because they obstruct others as well as themselves. — reasons: Sāmarrāʾī compares 11:22, 16:109, and 27:5, observing the added obstruction of God's path.
  18:103/q17/p2 Hūd uses stronger emphasis than Naml because it adds charges of obstructing God's path and lying against God. — reasons: Sāmarrāʾī compares 11:18–22 with 27:4–5. He reports grammarians' analyses of lā jarama as an oath or as 'truly/rightfully'; either reinforces the Hūd assertion.
18:103/q18 What report about works and the Qur'an accompanies this verse? [* · reports]
  18:103/q18/p1 The Qur'an appears as a fragrant companion to one who acted by it, comforts him, intercedes, and secures his honor. — reasons: Kaʿb says it identifies itself as the person's good deeds, recalls his thirsty days and wakeful nights to God, and he is crowned and robed.
18:103/q19 How does the following alladhīna clause attach to this question? [* · grammar] (turns on: The following relative word can be supplied with a nominative predicate or construed as an accusative of censure.)
  18:103/q19/p1 Alladhīna may be nominative as the predicate of an omitted 'they are'. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān gives this parse; the next clause can describe or stand in apposition to alladhīna.
  18:103/q19/p2 Alladhīna may be accusative for censure. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān gives this alternative; the following clause can be its description or apposition.
18:103/q20 Which groups does Saʿd report as having strayed? [* · reports]
  18:103/q20/p1 Monastic ascetics and the Kharijites strayed, and God caused their hearts to stray. — reasons: Al-Durr transmits this as a second report from Saʿd alongside his separate identification of Jews and Christians.
