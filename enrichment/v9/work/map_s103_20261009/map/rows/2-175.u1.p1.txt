2:175/q07 Is the exclamatory aṣbara a verb or a noun, and how does its complement work? [أَصْبَرَهُمْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the fixed exclamative pattern has verbal inflection and a direct object or nominal behavior and an object-like complement.)
  2:175/q07/p1 It is a past-tense verb, with the following accusative as its direct object. — reasons: Al-Wahidi says its form moved from triliteral to quadriliteral, its fixed final fatḥa marks a verb, and the object is genuinely acted upon; the poetic diminutive mā aḥaysana merely imitates a noun. Basrans cite agreement, fatḥa, and attached accusative pronoun. Al-Samin prefers this parsing; Abu Hayyan states its effect on the object.
  2:175/q07/p2 It is a noun or comparative form, with the following accusative an object-like complement. — reasons: Kufans cite noun-like diminutives; al-Razi says an underlying verb would change a middle wāw to alif, allow an intervening adverb, and not be restricted to bare triliteral roots. He rejects the Basran subject paraphrase: it loses wonder, is uninformative, treats mā as an unsuitable indefinite subject, and would make the maker God require 'who,' not 'what.' Attached accusatives also occur with laʿalla and layta; aṣbara can be a comparative noun. Abu Hayyan records the resulting change in the complement's status.
2:175/q08 What can the root ṣ-b-r and the form aṣbara mean here? [أَصْبَرَهُمْ · meaning] (turns on: Which extension of restraint and which force of the hamza the context requires.)
  2:175/q08/p1 Ṣabr is restraint of the self, hence patience in hardship and, in another Qur'anic use, fasting. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi explains ṣābir as restraining panic, Ramadan as the month of patience, and Qur'anic uses in 3:17, 14:21, 38:44, and 2:45. Ibn al-Anbari alternatively connects its name to bitterness like the plant ṣabr.
  2:175/q08/p2 Ṣabara can mean to confine or restrain someone, as with a tied animal used as a target. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi notes the prohibited shooting of a bound animal called maṣbūra; al-Mubarrad cites that prohibition and a poem for confinement, but Ibn Atiyya challenges the poem's vowels, reading aṣbarahā as a past form of ṣabara.
  2:175/q08/p3 Aṣbara can mean to command patience or make someone patient, through the causative hamza. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya allows both senses for an interrogative reading; Abu Hayyan insists it means 'make patient' and rejects al-Mubarrad's unsupported equation with simple ṣabara. Al-Tabrisi cites al-Hutay'a's poem for subjecting someone to something disliked.
2:175/q09 How can an exclamation or wonder be attributed to God? [فَمَآ أَصْبَرَهُمْ · theology] (turns on: Whether the exclamation is assigned to human observers or its divine sense is restricted to magnification.)
  2:175/q09/p1 It directs human hearers to marvel or tells them the state merits wonder; God does not experience ignorance or astonishment. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi, Ibn al-Anbari as reported by Ibn al-Jawzi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, and Bursawi give this account. Al-Tabrisi adds rebuke and reproach; Shurayh rejected a reading of 'I wonder' in 37:12 on the same ground.
  2:175/q09/p2 Divine wonder means magnification alone, whereas human wonder may include ignorance of its cause. — reasons: Al-Razi separates the two elements of ordinary wonder; al-Nakha'i compares divine laughter, mockery, and cunning, which likewise do not bear human meanings.
2:175/q10 What does 'the Fire' stand for in this phrase? [ٱلنَّارِ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether Fire names punishment and its worldly causes figuratively or evokes the future Fire directly.)
  2:175/q10/p1 It stands for its punishment or for deeds and causes leading to it. — reasons: Al-Tabari says Fire replaces 'its punishment' and compares ellipsis in praise of Hatim and Antara; al-Samin says 'deeds of Hell' is understood. Bursawi calls Fire the cause by metonymy; al-Tabrisi and al-Kisai/Qutrub understand the deeds of Hell's people.
  2:175/q10/p2 It is the actual future Fire, presented as if already visible. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says certain future torment is imagined as present, comparing Zuhayr and Malik ibn al-Rayb's poetry and 102:5–6.
2:175/q11 What ethical or theological lessons are drawn from their choice? [* · theology] (turns on: The notes draw distinct moral, theological, and embodied-deed consequences from the same choice.)
  2:175/q11/p1 Their unbelief is their own act, and culpable deeds merit punishment; knowing sin is graver. — reasons: Al-Jishumi cites the verse against the Jabriyya, says curse and punishment follow the actor's actions, and says knowledge increases guilt and punishment.
  2:175/q11/p2 Scholars are warned against hiding truth for gain, status, favors, office, luxury, or leadership. — reasons: Bursawi says greed, envy, and desire are an inner fire that devours good deeds; al-Alusi says the worldly motive explains the severe warning.
  2:175/q11/p3 Concealment and its worldly price become embodied as fire and punishment. — reasons: Al-Tabatabai says deeds take embodied form: choosing a small price becomes eating fire, then exchanging guidance and forgiveness for error and punishment.
2:175/q12 How does the verse connect with the following account of the Book and division? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the connection explains the punishment, identifies the disputants, or extends the warning to disputes over the Qur'an.)
  2:175/q12/p1 The next verse explains the condemnation: God revealed the true Book, and those who differed over it are in deep division. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi cites 2:176; Bursawi says rejection or concealment of true scripture warrants torment. Abu Hayyan traces the sequence from disbelievers' deafness and blindness through lawful food and necessity to this condemnation, which intensifies from their choice to their opposition to the Book.
  2:175/q12/p2 The people divided over scripture are Jews and Christians, in deep enmity. — reasons: Al-Suddi identifies the parties and characterizes their schism.
  2:175/q12/p3 The distant schism warns against disputing the Qur'an and God's signs. — reasons: Abu al-Aliya cites 40:4, where only unbelievers dispute God's signs.
2:175/q13 What rhetorical figures are associated with this verse or its imagery? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: The notes name different rhetorical devices or illustrative imagery.)
  2:175/q13/p1 It exemplifies mumāthala or tamthīl, an allusive metaphor whose chosen words exemplify the intended meaning. — reasons: Al-Baqillani says Qudama calls it tamthil and sets it against irdaf.
  2:175/q13/p2 Hell's speech, sight, and rage are vivid personifying images like poetry about a corner kissing a footprint and a minbar walking. — reasons: Al-Baqillani cites 50:30, 25:12, and 67:8 for the Hell images.
  2:175/q13/p3 Muṭābaqa means mentioning a thing with its opposite, such as night and day or black and white. — reasons: Al-Baqillani attributes the common definition to al-Khalil, al-Asmai, and Ibn al-Mutazz.
2:175/q14 What do the nearby descriptions of God's treatment and painful punishment mean? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The notes discuss different adjacent expressions rather than one disputed reading.)
  2:175/q14/p1 God's not speaking to them marks His anger. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj compares a person refusing to speak to someone through anger.
  2:175/q14/p2 God sends no angels to greet them, or does not let them hear His speech as the righteous do. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj reports these two alternatives in the preceding verse's description.
  2:175/q14/p3 God's not purifying them means He does not praise them, so they are punished. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj glosses wa-lā yuzakkīhim.
  2:175/q14/p4 Alīm means painful or aching, describing intense pain. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj distinguishes muʾlim as intensified pain. Bint al-Shati cites poetry about pain and sleeplessness and notes painful punishment in sixty-eight Qur'anic verses, including 2:10 and 9:61.
  2:175/q14/p5 Kindling a beacon means displaying the light of truth to its seeker. — reasons: Bint al-Shati reports Ibn al-Athir's gloss of Ali's phrase.
2:175/q15 What orthographic principles are illustrated by nearby Qur'anic spellings? [* · sciences] (turns on: The notes give distinct general and symbolic explanations for varying alif spellings.)
  2:175/q15/p1 Mushaf copies differ in spelling some names; omitted letters can motivate retaining another alif. — reasons: Al-Burhan lists Harut, Marut, Haman, and Qarun; Dawud retains alif after omission of waw, and most copies likewise retain alif in Isra'il after deleting ya.
  2:175/q15/p2 Sound masculine and feminine plurals generally omit alif, except when followed by hamza or a doubled letter. — reasons: Al-Burhan states the orthographic rule.
  2:175/q15/p3 Retaining alif may reflect a lower, visible worldly aspect, while omitting it reflects a higher spiritual aspect. — reasons: Abu al-Abbas supplies the principle, comparing 38:75 and 114:4. Al-Burhan applies it to al-firash and al-ta'am, the People of the Book's and believers' food in 5:5, the higher food of 5:75, and intensified shutting of spiritual doors in 12:23 versus concrete doors in 12:25; some contrasts are subtler than others.
