  28:29/q30/p1 The journey marks approach to divine intimacy and prophetic light, shared with his family. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭāʾ reads completion as the end of a period of love and travel as nearing divine beneficence. Jaʿfar says sacred lights and intimacy enveloped Moses, God spoke, granted his request, and secured him from fear; Abū Bakr ibn Ṭāhir stresses the pure place and familiarity.
  28:29/q30/p2 The journey figures spiritual perfection and inward movement of every faculty. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī reads the shorter term as perfection, family as faculties, mount as secret, fire as Holy Spirit, and blessed spot as perfected heart. Najm al-Dīn reads detachment from family, property and both worlds, then successive lights until lordship appears; he says frozen human qualities warm by love or divine attraction, supported by Kamāl al-Khujandī's verse on a heart burned by love.
  28:29/q30/p3 God ordered an apparently ordinary need to lead Moses to speech and prophethood. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī says God arranges causes for decreed events: Moses feared a trial, but fire and then light led to hearing 'Lord of the worlds.' Maymūn compares Sheba's queen finding Islam on a worldly journey and Moses finding divine speech while seeking fire.
28:29/q31 What virtues and guidance does the Midian-to-fire episode illustrate? [* · interpretation]
  28:29/q31/p1 Moses' and Shuʿayb's virtues prepare the revelation scene and invite moral imitation. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr lists generosity, aid to the distressed, compassion, modesty, gratitude, chastity, fidelity, hospitality, protection and gentleness. He says Meccan polytheists could compare Moses' premission virtue with Muhammad's, while Muslims should emulate prophetic character.
28:29/q32 What is reported about Moses' staff before the journey? [* · reports] (turns on: The accounts differ over its origin and chain of custody.)
  28:29/q32/p1 The staff came from Paradise through Adam and Gabriel to Moses. — reasons: ʿIkrima says Gabriel took it when Adam died and gave it to Moses at night; another report says Gabriel brought the myrtle branch from Paradise as Moses headed for Midian.
  28:29/q32/p2 Prophets inherited it until Shuʿayb gave it to Moses. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī records this transmission route.
  28:29/q32/p3 An angel deposited it with Shuʿayb, whose daughter could bring no other staff. — reasons: Al-Suddī recounts her repeated unsuccessful attempts and her father's gift of this staff to Moses.
  28:29/q32/p4 His father-in-law gave it to protect the flock. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī also records this functional origin story.
28:29/q33 What happened around Shuʿayb's farewell? [وَسَارَ بِأَهْلِهِۦٓ · reports]
  28:29/q33/p1 He grieved at Moses' departure, prayed, and recovered strength and sight. — reasons: Būrsawī reports Moses' longing to see his mother, aunt, Aaron and sister; Shuʿayb invoked Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob and Joseph, Moses said amen, and God restored him.
28:29/q34 What adjacent grammatical points are recorded with this verse's source material? [* · grammar]
  28:29/q34/p1 After ḥattā, the verb's case depends on whether the journey causes the event; coordination depends on repeating ḥattā. — reasons: Sībawayh contrasts entry or baggage arrival caused by travel with sunrise, dawn or hearing a call not caused by it; exhaustion can result from travel. With a repeated ḥattā, a second clause can be raised, as Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates; one shared ḥattā cannot raise 'sun rises' or make a second subjunctive follow a raised first verb. Coordinated 'I traveled and he traveled until we enter' means a joint journey.
  28:29/q34/p2 At the following call, min marks origin and min al-shajarah may appose the valley bank. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī says the call came from the valley's bank beside the tree; the tree grew on the bank, so min al-shajarah is an inclusion apposition. He cites 43:33 as a parallel.
28:29/q35 What is said about the later command involving Moses' wing and fear? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the later expression denotes a physical hand movement, self-control metaphor, or a purported sleeve sense of rahb.)
  28:29/q35/p1 He was told to tuck his hand under his arm after recoiling from the serpent. — reasons: Ibn Khālawayh explains fear of the serpent and calming by pressing hand to ribs. Al-Zamakhsharī calls the hands wings by bird analogy; the tucked right hand both calms fear and displays another miracle without a shameful pose. He reconciles 20:22 by taking the right hand as one wing and the left as the other, with distinct aims of white-hand display and concealing fear.
  28:29/q35/p2 He was commanded to steady himself, as a frightened bird spreads its wings. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī explains the metaphor through a bird returning its wings to normal; he cites ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz telling an embarrassed scribe to draw in his wing and calm down.
  28:29/q35/p3 Rahb could mean a sleeve or pocket rather than fear. — reasons: Ibn Khālawayh says a nonquiescent hāʾ is compatible with Arabic vowel reduction. Al-Zamakhsharī rejects the Himyaritic sleeve claim for weak attestation, poor contextual fit, and the unsupported sleeveless-garment story; he records four vowel patterns of the fear sense.
28:29/q36 What readings and word derivations are noted for the later signs? [* · readings]
  28:29/q36/p1 Al-buqʿah, rahb and dhānika have reported variant vocalizations. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī gives ḍamma or fatḥa for buqʿah, four vowel patterns for rahb meaning fear, and light dual dhānika from dhāka versus doubled dual from dhālika.
  28:29/q36/p2 Burhān and sulṭān name illuminating proof. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī derives burhān's brightness from a fair woman's description, treats n as extra by comparison with abraha, and links sulṭān to salīṭ, oil, because proof gives light.
28:29/q37 How does the Torah account of Moses before the fire relate to the Qur'anic account? [* · links] (turns on: Whether specific Torah details can be reconciled with Qur'anic narration or indicate textual alteration.)
  28:29/q37/p1 The Torah recounts Israel's oppression, Moses' rescue and flight, and his Midian marriage; details differ from the Qur'an. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī quotes Jacob's seventy descendants in Egypt, Pharaoh's forced labor and infanticide, midwives Puah and Shiphrah, Moses' basket, Pharaoh's daughter and naming, his killing of an Egyptian, escape, Reuel's seven daughters, marriage to Zipporah and son Eliezer. He says a claim that both disputants were Hebrews should be reconciled with the Qur'an or deemed alteration; al-Zamakhsharī's identification of the Qur'anic two among seven daughters does not prove the seven. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī likewise summarizes Qur'anic birth, flight and marriage, and says the Qur'an selects episodes for guidance rather than all detail.
  28:29/q37/p2 The Torah's history and its account of Aaron's calf conflict with Qur'anic presentation. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the Torah was altered, relating Nebuchadnezzar's 588 BCE temple destruction and burning, Cyrus's 538 BCE return permission, and Ezra's rewriting. He quotes Aaron's alleged collection of earrings and casting of the calf, says Qur'anic passages indirectly criticize it, and lists other differences: two Hebrew disputants, Aaron casting the staff, omission of seventy men and revival, and stone tablets.
28:29/q38 How is Moses' wider prophetic story and standing described? [* · interpretation]
  28:29/q38/p1 He is a highly honored prophet whose mission and story span many Qur'anic passages. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī counts 166 explicit names in 34 sūras and many signs; he recounts the winter fire and commission, the later journey with the righteous servant at 18:60–82, and Moses' rank as sincere, chosen, guided, righteous, near in private speech and recipient of Torah as criterion, guidance, light and admonition. God links Moses and Aaron in covenant, favor and peace at 33:7, 37:114–122 and 42:13; Moses asks Aaron's aid at 20:29–35 and Aaron defends his stewardship at 7:142–150.
28:29/q39 What is reported about emphatic lengthening of lā in adjacent reading material? [* · readings]
  28:29/q39/p1 Emphatic lā is lengthened moderately in several negative formulas. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī attributes it to emphasis and hamza, says supported lengthening is preferable, and reads a moderate degree because the non-hamza cause is weaker. He cites Hamza routes through Ibn Sawwār, Sibt al-Khayyāṭ, Ibn Fāris, al-Khuzāʿī, Khalaf, Sulaym and Khallād, plus Ḥafṣ through Hubayra for lā rayba. Al-Nawawī favors lengthening lā ilāha illā Allāh to aid reflection; two weak devotional reports promise divine vision or erasure of sins.
28:29/q40 How is the report that only four companions collected the Qur'an understood? [* · sciences] (turns on: What 'collected' means and whether Anas's naming four was exhaustive.)
  28:29/q40/p1 It does not exclude the Qur'an's collective preservation by others. — reasons: Al-Suyūṭī rejects the heretical exclusion because distributed memorization yields tawātur. Al-Qurṭubī says Anas named four from familiarity or immediate recollection. Al-Bāqillānī says the wording is nonexclusive or only four met a narrower criterion.
  28:29/q40/p2 The four uniquely collected particular forms, channels or timing of the Qur'an. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī proposes all readings and modes, both abrogated and surviving recitation, direct reception from the Prophet, publicly known teaching, writing together with memorization, or completed memorization before the Prophet died.
  28:29/q40/p3 Collection can mean hearing, obeying and acting on the Qur'an. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī gives this as an eighth answer; Abū al-Dardāʾ calls the true collector one who hears and obeys.
