39:10/q10 How does فِى هَٰذِهِ ٱلدُّنْيَا attach syntactically? [أَحْسَنُوا۟ فِى هَٰذِهِ ٱلدُّنْيَا حَسَنَةٌۭ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the prepositional phrase modifies the act, the reward, or deliberately allows both.)
  39:10/q10/p1 It modifies أَحْسَنُوا۟, placing the doing of good in this world. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī, al-Naḥḥās and others report this; al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī and al-Nasafī favor it with Paradise as reward. Al-Samīn says an omitted qualifier magnifies ḥasana. Bursawī parses ḥasana as subject and li-lladhīna as predicate. Ibn ʿĀshūr says this also urges action before opportunity passes; Ālūsī's primary exposition agrees.
  39:10/q10/p2 It locates or describes حَسَنَةٌۭ, making worldly good the direct promise. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī and al-Naḥḥās report this parse; al-Zamakhsharī says a preceding phrase can specify location despite not being a following adjective. Al-Samīn makes it a fronted adjective with an omitted circumstantial element. Al-Biqāʿī calls a new subject-predicate worldly clause too narrow; Ālūsī records al-Suddī and al-Zamakhsharī, yet criticizes a circumstantial modifier of an indefinite subject and al-Akhfash's weak analysis. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects the attachment.
  39:10/q10/p3 Its central placement permits both worldly and afterlife senses. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī prefers a broad reading. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the placement is deliberate and allows multiple meanings; the indefinite reward can magnify either otherworldly essence or worldly quality.
39:10/q11 What does the word حَسَنَةٌۭ contribute to the promise? [حَسَنَةٌۭ · rhetoric]
  39:10/q11/p1 Its indefiniteness magnifies the promised good. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī's view, via al-Samīn, supplies an omitted “great”; Ālūsī says the indefinite magnifies Paradise. Ibn ʿĀshūr says it can magnify afterlife reward intrinsically or worldly good qualitatively, with 2:201 as a comparison.
  39:10/q11/p2 It stands for a good condition, with a noun elided. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr compares the transfer of adjective to noun in 2:201 and the inverse expression in 42:40.
  39:10/q11/p3 The fronted لِلَّذِينَ draws attention to the recipients of divine kindness. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr connects the word order to an invitation to become God-fearing doers of good.
39:10/q12 What does أَرْضٌ mean in Arabic usage? [وَأَرْضُ ٱللَّهِ وَٰسِعَةٌ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the question concerns the ordinary root sense or its particular Qur'anic geographical uses.)
  39:10/q12/p1 Earth is named for breadth; related expressions extend the root to expansiveness. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī calls earth the familiar broad expanse. Ibn al-Sikkīt says a sore “arḍ” when it spreads; Ibn Fāris says any broad thing is arḍ and arīḍ describes a man suited to good. The same lexicon records termite-eaten wood, a wool or hair carpet, approaching someone, a foreigner, lush land, and Ibn ʿAbbās's distinction between earth's and one's own trembling.
  39:10/q12/p2 Across Qur'anic passages, arḍ can denote Paradise, Mecca, Medina, or Syria. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī cites Paradise at 21:105 and 39:74, Mecca at 4:97 and 13:41, Medina at 4:97, 4:100, 17:76 and 29:56, and Syria at 7:137 and 21:71; he includes 39:10 among Medina examples.
39:10/q13 What does “God's earth is vast” refer to here? [وَأَرْضُ ٱللَّهِ وَٰسِعَةٌ · referent] (turns on: Whether the land is a worldly destination, the Garden, provision from the earth, or inward spiritual space.)
  39:10/q13/p1 The broad worldly earth offers other places where believers can worship safely. — reasons: Mujāhid explains leaving idol worship; al-Ṭabarī says leave polytheist territory; Ibn ʿAbbās ties it to leaving Mecca and 4:97; al-Rāzī and al-Nasafī cite prophets and the righteous as precedents. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says nearby lands fit the migration story; al-Rāzī and Ālūsī prefer this because subsequent patience fits migration. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the obvious fact an indirect exhortation like a proverb, with 4:97 explicit. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī takes the historically Meccan pressure as particular but wording general.
  39:10/q13/p2 It is Paradise or the broad Garden sought through righteous deeds. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās reports the Garden; al-Thaʿlabī reports Muqātil; Ibn ʿĪsā, Abū Muslim and al-Ṭabrisī offer it to encourage goodness. Abū Muslim cites 39:74 and 3:133 and the movement from piety to Paradise. Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it unsupported, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī remote, and Kuranyolu weak.
  39:10/q13/p3 It designates Medina, the destination of migration. — reasons: Muqātil identifies Medina; ʿAṭāʾ likewise calls it the migration land or Medina, with relief from enemies as the good; Ibn al-Jawzī cites this verse among Medina uses.
  39:10/q13/p4 Its breadth signals abundant provision or fruitful lands available to believers. — reasons: Al-Māwardī prefers this because the clause presents a favor; al-Jishumī reports fruitful lands; al-Qurṭubī finds provision plausible and recalls Sufyān al-Thawrī's advice to move to cheaper bread.
  39:10/q13/p5 Inwardly it is the expanded self or heart, or limitless divine presence. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī describes the contented self freed by certainty; al-Baqlī says the Real expands the heart. The Najmī interpretations and Rūmī say no unveiling is a final arrival; Ālūsī calls it the limitless domain of majesty and beauty.
39:10/q14 What practical migration does the spacious earth call for? [وَأَرْضُ ٱللَّهِ وَٰسِعَةٌ · law] (turns on: Whether migration is the concrete response to persecution, a move for provision, or a broader departure from conditions that impede goodness.)
  39:10/q14/p1 Leave a place where religion cannot be practiced or sin is forced or openly practiced. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj cites 4:97 for leaving idol pressure; al-Naḥḥās and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr extend flight to sinful settings. Al-Qushayrī and later exegetes say persecution cannot excuse abandoning worship. Bursawī cites a report promising Paradise for migration to protect faith and distinguishes that motive from worldly moves to a more sinful place. Iṣlāḥī says move if Makkah makes worship impossible.
  39:10/q14/p2 Migration may include moving to land with cheaper living or better provision. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī draws this from al-Māwardī's provision reading and quotes Sufyān al-Thawrī on cheaper bread.
  39:10/q14/p3 The enduring sense includes spiritual migration from evil and changes in work or place. — reasons: Asad sees the capacity always to do good and cites 4:97; Kuranyolu ranges from changing jobs to changing countries when one cannot live one's values or improve conditions.
39:10/q15 What migration setting lies behind the verse? [وَأَرْضُ ٱللَّهِ وَٰسِعَةٌ · reports] (turns on: Whether the historical allusion concerns the first migration to Abyssinia or departure toward Medina.)
  39:10/q15/p1 It alludes to Jaʿfar and companions' migration to Abyssinia. — reasons: Al-Baghawī and Ibn ʿAṭiyya report the group preparing or enduring trials and emigrating; al-Qurṭubī and Ibn ʿĀshūr attribute the identification to Ibn ʿAbbās. Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī repeat it. Ibn ʿĀshūr dates the Abyssinian move to year five before the later Medinan migration, with 83 men and 19 women excluding children, under a just king; he also recounts Abū Bakr's interrupted attempted departure and says the Prophet remained to establish Islam in Mecca until Medina was ready.
  39:10/q15/p2 It urges leaving Mecca for Medina or another safe land. — reasons: Muqātil names Medina; Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Rāzī make Mecca the point of departure. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the reassurance eases leaving home and 4:97 makes migration explicit; Iṣlāḥī describes Quraysh pressure and a land that would receive expelled Muslims.
39:10/q16 How is the spacious-earth clause linked to the promise around it? [وَأَرْضُ ٱللَّهِ وَٰسِعَةٌ · links] (turns on: Whether the aside addresses practical worship and migration or climate and bodily welfare.)
  39:10/q16/p1 It removes the excuse that homeland prevents excellence and prepares the special reward for patient migrants. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī calls it an aside and argues the next clause distinguishes migrants, whose sacrifices follow prophets and righteous people; he prefers this to a climate-and-health connection, citing 4:97 and 29:56 and the nearer attachment rule. Al-Biqāʿī says all land lies under God's power. Ibn ʿĀshūr sees an indirect call that consoles those leaving home.
  39:10/q16/p2 It meets concerns about climate and bodily health while contrasting immediate worldly good with later full reward. — reasons: Al-Kashf calls this explanatory connection finer than a restrictive circumstantial parse; Ālūsī acknowledges its merit but prefers the obedience-and-migration connection.
