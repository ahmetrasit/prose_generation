6:151/q32 How does إِلَّا بِٱلْحَقِّ qualify killing grammatically? [إِلَّا بِٱلْحَقِّ · grammar]
  6:151/q32/p1 It qualifies the killer's circumstance, the cause of killing, or an understood kind of killing. — reasons: Samin allows a circumstantial qualifier or an adjective of an omitted killing; Alusi extends it to all circumstances, causes or kinds; Ibn Ashur says the preposition marks circumstance or cause.
6:151/q33 Why specify unlawful killing after banning indecencies and infanticide? [وَلَا تَقْتُلُوا۟ ٱلنَّفْسَ ٱلَّتِى حَرَّمَ ٱللَّهُ · rhetoric]
  6:151/q33/p1 The specific ban emphasizes murder's gravity and allows an explicit rightful exception. — reasons: Jishumi, Tabarsi, Razi, Abu Hayyan and Samin make this rationale; Ibn Kathir notes the repetition; Tabatabai reports it as a proposal, while Biqai says child-killing is prohibited repeatedly.
  6:151/q33/p2 Its violation destroys public security and the structure of society. — reasons: Tabatabai gives this consequence; Ibn Ashur says this verse's first set preserves life and ends mutual killing.
6:151/q34 What inward meaning is given to killing and life here? [وَلَا تَقْتُلُوا۟ ٱلنَّفْسَ · inward]
  6:151/q34/p1 The deeper death is dying in pursuit of God, while one killed in His path remains alive with Him. — reasons: Bursevi joins this reflection to an anecdote in which a dead, handsome youth told Abu Saʿid al-Kharraz that lovers live after death, moving from one home to another.
6:151/q35 What does ذَٰلِكُمْ وَصَّىٰكُم بِهِۦ refer to and mean? [ذَٰلِكُمْ وَصَّىٰكُم بِهِۦ · interpretation] (turns on: The referent and nuance of the testament formula, and a proposed lexical distinction.)
  6:151/q35/p1 It points collectively to the preceding five commands and calls them God's firm, compassionate charge. — reasons: Nahhas allows 'that is the command' or 'God clarified and commanded'; Ibn Abi Hatim reports Ibn Abbas glossing the testament as God's religion; Ibn Atiyya cites poetry; Razi, Abu Hayyan and Alusi explain the tender presentation; Ibn Ashur says singular demonstrative can refer to a set.
  6:151/q35/p2 The wording affirms a decisive command and renewed covenant. — reasons: Qurtubi parses the attached address pronouns as vocative, subject pronoun as God; Samin allows demonstrative as subject with verbal predicate or accusative under an omitted verb; Alusi calls the nominal form emphatic.
  6:151/q35/p3 Waṣṣā and awṣā both mean enjoin, though one later distinction assigns the former to religion and latter to worldly matters. — reasons: Jishumi equates them; Samarrai contrasts religious counsel with worldly inheritance and cites 19:31 as an exception.
6:151/q36 Why end with لَعَلَّكُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ? [لَعَلَّكُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: The immediate sense of reasoning and why this ending differs from the next two.)
  6:151/q36/p1 Reason lets people understand duties, discern benefit and harm, restrain themselves and comply. — reasons: Mawardi gives understanding or rational avoidance of punishment; Jishumi, Tabarsi, Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, Razi and Alusi stress grasping benefit and self-restraint; Samin calls reason the basis of responsibility; Tabatabai says these five are apparent once passions no longer obscure judgment.
  6:151/q36/p2 The first ending suits obvious prohibitions heedlessly violated; later endings concern remembrance and piety. — reasons: Qutb al-Razi connects heedlessness with reason and familiar duties with memory; Imam Razi instead distinguishes obvious first duties and subtler later duties; Alusi stresses restraint in mostly negative first set; Biqai and Islahi see a progression through reason, remembrance, then taqwa.
  6:151/q36/p3 Laʿalla expresses hope from the human hearer's perspective. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says God wants reason to distinguish benefit from harm, while hope belongs to the audience's viewpoint.
6:151/q37 How do these commandments form the verse group and connect to the sūra? [* · links] (turns on: How the five duties relate to the next two sections and to the preceding controversy.)
  6:151/q37/p1 The list mixes positive and negative duties, and each form implies the opposite. — reasons: Kashshaf, Nasafi, Biqai, Elmalili, Ibn Ashur and Islahi explain why commands appear under an opening about prohibitions; Tabatabai says recitation reproduces revelation's mixed form. Tabarsi says forbidding shirk and commanding its avoidance are equivalent.
  6:151/q37/p2 The three sections set social, economic and religious order, each sealed by God's testament. — reasons: Ibn Ashur distinguishes belief and family in the first, orphan property and justice in the second, and the straight path in the third; Biqai says the repeated closure strengthens acceptance; Islahi links Abraham's and Moses' guidance. Kashshaf explains the fatha reading of the straight-path clause in 6:153 with an omitted causal lām and says the kasra reading conveys the same cause, supported by 72:18.
  6:151/q37/p3 They restate Abraham's religion and expose Quraysh's neglect beneath invented food taboos. — reasons: Islahi describes 6:145–154 as a reply to idolaters who claimed God ordained their way; 16:90 supplies a parallel criterion of justice and goodness; Kuranyolu identifies nine principal duties plus the straight path.
  6:151/q37/p4 The first verse begins with wrongs apparent to sound reason: shirk, filial neglect, infanticide, indecency and murder. — reasons: Tabatabai groups the five, and Ibn Ashur says they establish right belief, stable family and protected life.
6:151/q38 What status do these commandments have across revealed scriptures? [* · sciences] (turns on: Whether the label emphasizes universal fixed law or a particular identification with Moses' tablets.)
  6:151/q38/p1 They are decisive, universal and unabrogated commandments, the Mother of the Book. — reasons: Ibn Abbas reports them as the muhkamāt of 3:7, common to all scriptures, with Paradise for observance and Hell for neglect; Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi mention the Ten Commandments identification. Elmalili counts nine listed prohibitions with tawhid as their first foundation. Ibn Kathir reports Ibn Abbas calling them foundations of scripture.
  6:151/q38/p2 They correspond to the ten commandments given to Moses. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi and Abu Hayyan transmit this identification; Itqan and Elmalili give lists including monotheism, false oath, filial duty, murder, adultery, theft, testimony, coveting and Sabbath rules.
6:151/q39 What do reports say about the opening of the Torah? [قُلْ تَعَالَوْا۟ أَتْلُ مَا حَرَّمَ رَبُّكُمْ عَلَيْكُمْ · reports] (turns on: The wording and count of the reported Torah correspondence.)
  6:151/q39/p1 Kaʿb says this opening command or the ten commandments were first in the Torah. — reasons: Thalabi, Ibn Atiyya, Tabarsi, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, Durr, Itqan, Elmalili and Alusi transmit variants: first thing, first verse with basmala, or first ten revealed verses.
  6:151/q39/p2 Some describe them more generally as the ten commandments revealed to Moses. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi and Abu Hayyan present this alongside Ibn Abbas's universal-commandment report.
6:151/q40 What is said of the Prophet's sealed testament and the pledge on these verses? [* · reports] (turns on: Distinct reported uses of the verses as testament, pledge, and prophetic address.)
  6:151/q40/p1 Ibn Masud calls 6:151–153 the Prophet's sealed written testament. — reasons: Ibn Abi Hatim, Ibn Kathir, Durr, Alusi and Tirmidhi transmit it; Tirmidhi grades it ḥasan gharīb, while Alusi says ḥasan.
  6:151/q40/p2 The Prophet took a pledge on the commands: fulfillment is rewarded; a breach may be punished here or left to God's will after death. — reasons: Ibn Abi Hatim gives seven traits and the punishment-or-pardon rule; Ibn Kathir says Hakim graded the extended report authentic while the two Sahihs have shorter pledges; Durr and Alusi repeat the three-verse pledge, with worldly punishment expiating.
  6:151/q40/p3 Al-Rabi ibn Khuthaym treated the verses as a sealed letter or written counsel from the Prophet. — reasons: Thalabi, Qurtubi and Durr report reciting them to a companion and writing them for a questioner.
  6:151/q40/p4 At Mina, the Prophet recited the commandments to a tribe while inviting them to monotheism, messenger-belief and protection. — reasons: Durr names Mafruq and Hani's approval, a following recital of 16:90, a promise concerning Persian lands, and a quotation of 33:45 before the Prophet left with Abu Bakr.
  6:151/q40/p5 At the Farewell Pilgrimage the Prophet named shirk, unlawful killing, adultery and theft as prohibitions. — reasons: Durr transmits this four-item report.
