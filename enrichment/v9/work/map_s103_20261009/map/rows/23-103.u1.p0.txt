## THE CURRENT MAP
23:103/q01 What do the mawazin designate? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · meaning] (turns on: Whether mawazin names a physical instrument, the weighed deeds, or justice, and whether unbelievers' deeds are weighed.)
  23:103/q01/p1 A real balance weighs deeds. — reasons: Al-Razi describes a balance with tongue and two pans, good deeds appearing beautifully and evil deeds uglily. Ibn Atiyya describes unbelief weighed against an empty pan or charitable deeds, and says weighing makes proof tangible in familiar human terms. Alusi notes disagreement over whether unbelievers' deeds are weighed.
  23:103/q01/p2 The scales designate good deeds or the things weighed. — reasons: Al-Razi says valued deeds lead to success, whereas weightless deeds resemble a mirage (24:39). His report from Ibn Abbas calls mawazin the weighed righteous deeds and cites 'no weight' in 18:105. Ibn Atiyya explains the plural by the plurality of deeds. Baydawi and Bursevi invoke 18:105; Alusi allows scales of good deeds. Study Quran refers readers to 101:6-9 for its discussion of the scales.
  23:103/q01/p3 The scales are a metaphor for justice. — reasons: Al-Razi reports this interpretation without an argument here.
  23:103/q01/p4 The scales can designate evil deeds with no weight. — reasons: Alusi gives this as an alternative dependent on whether unbelievers' deeds are weighed.
23:103/q02 Why are their scales light? [خَفَّتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the lightness describes an unfavorable balance of deeds or deeds having no worth at all.)
  23:103/q02/p1 Their good deeds are deficient or outweighed by sins and unbelief. — reasons: Al-Tabari specifies little good and preponderant sin; al-Tabrisi says obedience is deficient and points to 7:9 for mechanics. Ibn Kathir says bad deeds outweigh good. Al-Nasafi calls them weighed down by evil. Ibn Atiyya gives alternatives: unbelief against an empty pan or against charity to kin, which unbelief outweighs. Al-Biqa'i identifies neglected faith-based deeds. Kuranyolu ties reward to faith and good works.
  23:103/q02/p2 Their deeds or beliefs carry no value before God. — reasons: Al-Razi likens worthless deeds to a mirage (24:39); his Ibn Abbas report glosses 'no weight' as no worth (18:105). Baydawi and Bursevi cite 18:105 for unbelievers. Alusi allows evil deeds with no weight if their deeds are weighed.
23:103/q03 Who are the people with light scales? [وَمَنْ خَفَّتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · referent]
  23:103/q03/p1 They are unbelievers, identified by their deficient or worthless deeds. — reasons: Muqatil names unbelievers; Baydawi and Bursevi cite 18:105, which denies them weight. Al-Nasafi also names unbelievers. Ibn Atiyya discusses an unbeliever's unbelief against charity or an empty pan. Other commentators describe the group by light deeds without further identifying them.
23:103/q04 Why is mawazin plural? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · grammar]
  23:103/q04/p1 Because the weighed deeds are multiple. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says the plurality belongs to the things weighed, the deeds; presenting them as weighed makes the proof tangible in terms people know.
23:103/q05 How do the demonstratives and pronouns distinguish the groups? [فَأُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ ٱلَّذِينَ · grammar]
  23:103/q05/p1 The demonstrative points to a group by sense, while pronoun form and demonstratives mark the contrasted groups. — reasons: Alusi explains that a demonstrative can refer to the group in meaning while singular pronouns follow grammatical form. Ibn Ashur says the demonstratives distinguish the two groups by their qualities.
23:103/q06 What does losing their selves mean? [خَسِرُوٓا۟ أَنفُسَهُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: Which loss the trade image identifies: foregone gain, an inherited place, inability to benefit, wasted potential, or disappointed expectation.)
  23:103/q06/p1 They fail to profit and suffer ruin, losing God's mercy and their own benefit. — reasons: Yahya says they failed to gain; al-Tabari specifies their share of mercy. Al-Nasafi says they cheated themselves, while Ibn Kathir calls them failed, ruined, and bankrupt. Study Quran says 'lose their souls' can also be rendered 'lose themselves.'
  23:103/q06/p2 Believers inherit the places the losers forfeited. — reasons: Ibn Abbas, as reported by al-Razi, explains their self-loss as being cheated when believers inherit their places.
  23:103/q06/p3 They cannot benefit from themselves while trapped in punishment. — reasons: Al-Razi reports this alternative explanation.
  23:103/q06/p4 They squander life's time and capacity for perfection through heedlessness and desires. — reasons: Baydawi and Alusi stress wasted time and potential. Bursevi explains khusran as a shortfall in capital; Kashifi calls life capital squandered in heedlessness and appetite. Al-Biqa'i adds pursuit of appetites in the abode of action. Al-Tawilat compares irreversible spoiled potential to an egg that can no longer develop into a chick.
  23:103/q06/p5 Their expected salvation turns into its opposite, defeating hopes of intercession or escape. — reasons: Ibn Ashur traces khusran to failed trade and cites 6:12 and 7:9. He says they expected idol intercession or escape from resurrection but, after rejecting Islam, found the opposite, as if their selves had perished.
23:103/q07 How is anfusahum construed? [خَسِرُوٓا۟ أَنفُسَهُمْ · grammar]
  23:103/q07/p1 Anfusahum is the direct object of khasiru. — reasons: Ibn Ashur gives this grammatical parsing.
23:103/q08 What does abiding in Hell entail? [فِى جَهَنَّمَ خَٰلِدُونَ · theology]
  23:103/q08/p1 Their punishment and residence in Hell are everlasting, without departure or death. — reasons: Muqatil and Yahya explicitly deny death; Yahya and Ibn Kathir deny exit. Al-Razi says the wording plainly establishes everlasting punishment of unbelievers. Al-Biqa'i says Hell's fire never dies. The other commentators state continued residence or enduring Hell.
23:103/q09 How does 'abiding in Hell' attach syntactically? [فِى جَهَنَّمَ خَٰلِدُونَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the whole clause is apposition, whether only fi jahannam replaces an implied verb, or whether khalidun serves as a predicate; the proposed adjectival relation is also challenged.)
  23:103/q09/p1 The whole expression is an apposition to 'they lost themselves.' — reasons: Al-Kashshaf offers this analysis, repeated by al-Razi, al-Nasafi, and al-Samin. Al-Samin specifies that both relative-clause expressions have no syntactic position. Alusi says al-Zamakhshari seems to prefer it. Abu Hayyan calls it unusual because loss results in residence in Hell; Alusi agrees with that objection. Al-Samin says al-Zamakhshari's wording refers to the entire phrase, not only fi jahannam.
  23:103/q09/p2 Khalidun is a second predicate of ulaika or the statement is explanatory. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf, al-Razi, al-Nasafi, al-Samin, and Alusi allow a second predicate. Alusi also offers explanation; Abu Hayyan observes that the apparent syntax makes Hell predicative rather than adjectival. Al-Samin says the follow-up alternatives fit khalidun.
  23:103/q09/p3 Khalidun is the predicate of an omitted subject, hum. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf, al-Razi, al-Nasafi, and al-Samin list this ellipsis; al-Samin supplies hum explicitly.
  23:103/q09/p4 Only fi jahannam is appositional, replacing an implied istaqarru. — reasons: The Shaykh, reported by al-Samin, proposes this because loss leads to settlement in Hell. Al-Samin objects that it leaves khalidun syntactically stranded and misstates al-Zamakhshari's full-phrase analysis.
  23:103/q09/p5 The relative clause describes the demonstrative. — reasons: Abu al-Baqa allows this. Al-Samin questions it because the clause appears to be the demonstrative's predicate; Abu Hayyan likewise says the apparent syntax is predicative, not adjectival.
23:103/q10 Why does ruined spiritual potential lead to lasting Hell? [خَسِرُوٓا۟ أَنفُسَهُمْ فِى جَهَنَّمَ خَٰلِدُونَ · inward]
  23:103/q10/p1 Once spiritual potential is altered, it cannot be restored, so its loss persists. — reasons: Al-Tawilat compares a person to an egg whose potential becomes a chick under the hen's care; once altered and uncared for, it cannot recover. It says a mystic abandoning the path is worse off than one abandoning the legal religion. Bursevi reports that this irreversible alteration accounts for remaining in Hell. Jami's couplet says even Moses and Aaron cannot retrieve one swallowed by earth, nor can a healer reverse fate.
23:103/q11 What does the division by scales imply about final outcomes? [* · theology]
  23:103/q11/p1 The heavy-scaled succeed and the light-scaled are lost; some infer there is no neutral or equal outcome. — reasons: Al-Razi places accounting after the Trumpet and divides the blessed with heavy scales from the wretched with light scales. He reports an inference excluding those entitled to neither reward nor punishment and those with equal weight. Kuranyolu ties salvation to reward earned through faith and good works. An Anas report says an angel announces each person's eternal felicity or misery from the balance's weight, but Ibn Kathir judges its chain weak because Dawud al-Muhabbar is abandoned.
23:103/q12 Does this assignment of punishment establish who creates human acts? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether deserved punishment for sin requires that a person independently create the sinful act.)
  23:103/q12/p1 Punishment for bad deeds would be unjust if God created human acts. — reasons: Al-Razi reports this Mu'tazilite inference from the verse's assignment of punishment to deeds.
  23:103/q12/p2 The inference from desert to independently created human acts fails. — reasons: Al-Razi argues that unmotivated sin is accidental and cannot deserve punishment; if motivated, the motive is not itself the person's act without infinite regress, so the resulting act is compelled and likewise cannot establish desert as the argument assumes.
