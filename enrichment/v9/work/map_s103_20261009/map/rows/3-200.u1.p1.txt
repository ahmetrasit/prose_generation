3:200/q09 What does fearing God require here? [وَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether piety is described in outward duties or inward stations.)
  3:200/q09/p1 Obey God and avoid every prohibition in all affairs. — reasons: Tabari defines piety by commands and limits; Nahhas says it governs every affair, not merely war. Qurtubi requires piety in battle; Alusi says ostentation could corrupt preceding deeds. Ibn Kathir quotes advice to Mu'adh; Abduh links piety to knowledge of what pleases God through scripture, Sunna and early exemplars.
  3:200/q09/p2 Purify the inward self from attachment to other than God. — reasons: Ja'far names gracious intimacy; Baydawi and Bursevi contrast disavowal and avoidance of vice as law, path and reality; Qashani lists display, objection, pride and selfhood at successive stations.
3:200/q10 How does 'perhaps' work? [لَعَلَّكُمْ · rhetoric] (turns on: Hope or purpose.)
  3:200/q10/p1 It urges believers to hope for success while continuing good deeds. — reasons: Zajjaj says 23:1 assures success but the verse addresses human hope and avoidance of acts nullifying work. Ibn Atiyya calls it the human viewpoint; Nasafi says uncertainty prevents complacency.
  3:200/q10/p2 It can state the purpose of the commands. — reasons: Qurtubi allows hope or purpose; Jishumi names reward or escape from invalidated deeds.
3:200/q11 What is the promised flourishing? [تُفْلِحُونَ · meaning] (turns on: Root meaning or concrete promised ends.)
  3:200/q11/p1 Attained reward, worldly victory and happiness in the next life. — reasons: Muhammad ibn Ka'b points to meeting God tomorrow; Ibn Kathir includes both lives, Biqai victory and martyrs' bliss. Jishumi argues against Murji'a denial of acts' role; Tabatabai sees complete happiness in both worlds.
  3:200/q11/p2 Enduring success and everlasting life. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba derives falah from remaining; Dahhak agrees. Nasafi specifies staying with what one loves after hardship.
3:200/q12 How does the closing verse connect to the sūra and its neighbors? [* · links] (turns on: Immediate summary, inter-sūra link or wider sūra context.)
  3:200/q12/p1 It gathers beliefs and duties, renewing resolve after Uhud. — reasons: Razi lists monotheism, justice, prophecy, resurrection, pilgrimage and jihad; Asamm connects many duties. Ibn Ashur locates vigilance after Uhud and before later surprise; Alusi recalls suffering and enemy stratagems. Tabatabai calls this the sura's result.
  3:200/q12/p2 It links al-Baqara's end and al-Nisa's opening. — reasons: Biqai connects 2:286's plea for victory, 2:186's nearness, earlier scripture, and a close echoing the opening. Alusi notes taqwa at both sura boundaries and Uhud links (3:172; 4:84,88); Elmalili sees further instruction on family and society.
  3:200/q12/p3 The preceding sūra treats theology, Mary and Jesus, People of the Book, and Uhud. — reasons: Study Quran notes the name from 3:33; interpretation's levels at 3:7; Mary, Jesus, mixed portrayals of scriptural communities, and Uhud's cowardice, hypocrisy, attachment and afterlife questions.
3:200/q13 What is reported about the sūra's revelation and Najran debate? [* · reports]
  3:200/q13/p1 The first thirty to eighty verses are associated with a Najran Christian visit and debate. — reasons: Christians argued from Jesus's miracles, fatherless birth and plural divine speech; the Prophet answered with his mortality, limited knowledge, bodily needs and inability to sustain creation. A delegate had Byzantine honors; the opening was reportedly revealed afterward.
3:200/q14 What virtues and recitations of Āl ʿImrān are reported? [* · reports] (turns on: Reports differ in reward and Friday timing; Alusi rejects the bridge report.)
  3:200/q14/p1 Friday recitation wins divine and angelic blessings until sunset or sunrise. — reasons: Kashshaf and Alusi give sunset; Baydawi gives sunrise.
  3:200/q14/p2 The two radiant sūras plead for reciters at Judgment. — reasons: Nasafi and Study Quran describe clouds, sunrays or birds interceding.
  3:200/q14/p3 Each recited verse grants safety on the bridge over Hell. — reasons: Kashshaf and Baydawi transmit it; Alusi calls it fabricated and censures transmitters.
  3:200/q14/p4 Reciting the ending at night earns vigil reward; the Prophet recited its final ten at night. — reasons: Alusi transmits Uthman and Abu Hurayra; Kur'an Yolu reports tahajjud practice.
3:200/q15 What is said of al-Nisāʾ after Āl ʿImrān? [* · sciences] (turns on: Date and verse-count traditions.)
  3:200/q15/p1 It is Medinan; one Meccan verse does not classify the whole sūra. — reasons: Alusi cites Aisha's post-Hijra setting, rejects Nahhas's inference from the Kaaba key verse, and quotes Suyuti's distinction.
  3:200/q15/p2 It was revealed around the Hijra. — reasons: Alusi reports an alternative.
  3:200/q15/p3 Its verse counts are Syrian 177, Kufan 176, and other 175. — reasons: Alusi locates the difference in two clause divisions.

3:200/q17 Why does the verse address believers as a collective? [* · interpretation]
  3:200/q17/p1 Human life is intrinsically social, and revelation consciously organizes shared religious life. — reasons: Tabatabai traces marriage and household into clan and rule through strength, wealth and political skill; this early hierarchy encouraged idolatry. Conflict led prophets and books to restore unity (10:19; 2:213; 42:13). Islam uniquely orders communal activity explicitly. Shared origins and kinship (25:54; 49:13; 3:195) give communities a real collective lifespan, knowledge and deeds; group panic and custom can overwhelm individuals. The Qur'an commands brotherhood, reconciliation, cooperation and avoiding division (3:104-105; 6:153,158; 8:46; 5:2). Government and every believer sustain worship, giving and enjoining right.
3:200/q18 Does popularity or historical success decide which social order is true? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether popularity, current power or objective reality determines truth.)
  3:200/q18/p1 No; truth is external reality, and a system may be true before prevailing. — reasons: Tabatabai reports objections from early Islamic decline, Western longevity, and majority-governed modern order. He replies that new systems begin amid hostility, prophetic groups began small, Islam spread rapidly and transformed history, and present weakness is no refutation (3:137). Frequent causal outcomes differ from majority opinion: even skeptics assent to evident reality, while majorities can oppose truth (23:70-71). Law may account for predominant practical benefit (5:6; 2:183). He disputes attributing modern progress to Christianity.
  3:200/q18/p2 The objection takes modern majority rule, durability and social strength as evidence of superiority. — reasons: Tabatabai reports claims that Western order refines conduct and Islamic law cannot now operate; proponents liken majority rule to prevalent natural outcomes.
3:200/q19 How does Islamic social happiness compare with material civilization? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether happiness comprises bodily and spiritual truth or chiefly material satisfaction.)
  3:200/q19/p1 It joins bodily welfare to knowledge of God, virtue and worship. — reasons: Tabatabai says Islamic law uses sound reason, social enforcement and inward education, whereas material civil law follows appetite and regulates mainly visible conduct. He traces material idolatry into collective systems, critiques European domination and class division, and urges judging nations by treatment of outsiders while exempting just individuals from group blame. A society has a common purpose like a body's organs; competition requires law, but excluding morals and theology turns religion into political custom. He attributes communism and world wars to inequality and greed and rejects wealth alone as happiness.
  3:200/q19/p2 Material civil law principally pursues collective enjoyment and majority preference. — reasons: Tabatabai describes its outward restrictions and freedom outside them, but disputes its claim to moral completeness.
3:200/q20 What sustains virtue and endurance without a visible worldly reward? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether divine accountability supports or displaces worldly responsibility.)
