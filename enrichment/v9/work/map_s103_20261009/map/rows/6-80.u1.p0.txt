## THE CURRENT MAP
6:80/q01 What does the people's disputing with Abraham mean? [وَحَآجَّهُۥ قَوْمُهُۥ · meaning] (turns on: Whether reciprocal morphology requires an actual exchange, allows one-sided contention, or identifies who initiated it.)
  6:80/q01/p1 They contended with him, each side advancing an argument or asking for proof. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and al-Rabi gloss it as quarrelling or disputing. Jishumi defines muḥājja as reciprocal presentation and demand for evidence. Ibn Atiyya derives it from ḥujja. Ibn Ashur prefers a real exchange and cites other Quranic debates with Abraham, including 21:52–56 and 26:72–73.
  6:80/q01/p2 The reciprocal verb can describe an argument begun by the people while Abraham listens and then replies, or intensify a one-sided contention. — reasons: Ibn Ashur allows the opponent's reception and response to justify reciprocal morphology even if only one side argues; the people's words are omitted to focus on Abraham's firmness.
  6:80/q01/p3 The reciprocal form makes its grammatical subject the initiator. — reasons: Ibn Arafah applies this to 2:258, distinguishing Abraham's opening speech from Nimrod's opening of the debate; Ibn Ashur says grammarians do not establish this rule.
6:80/q02 What claims did Abraham's people raise in the dispute? [وَحَآجَّهُۥ قَوْمُهُۥ فِى ٱللَّهِ · reports] (turns on: The reports name inherited custom, rival deities, celestial powers, and threats; several sources give more than one.)
  6:80/q02/p1 They defended inherited worship and objected to the oneness of God. — reasons: Razi cites 43:23 and 38:5 for ancestral imitation and rejection of one God; Biqai says they insisted on inherited idols. Study Quran cites 21:53, 43:23 and 38:5. Abu Hayyan and Alusi describe traditional arguments as false.
  6:80/q02/p2 They claimed their gods were better, asked Abraham to describe God, or urged him to worship another god. — reasons: Tabari reports the superiority claim; Ibn Ishaq reports the request to describe God. Ibn Jurayj reports invocation of other gods; al-Durr reports a call to worship one.
  6:80/q02/p3 They argued for celestial bodies, idols, or created things as partners and causal powers. — reasons: Zajjaj and Abu Ishaq name stars, sun, moon, and idols; Qashani includes the alleged causal power of celestial and other created beings. The argument concerns divine oneness or attributes, as Ibn Ashur notes.
  6:80/q02/p4 They threatened him with punishment or injury by their gods as part of their argument. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says they mistook intimidation for proof. Tabatabai says fear of divine anger was their stronger motive for worship; his account has them press Abraham to accept their gods and reject God's lordship. Elmalili says their verbal and practical pressure aimed to win.
6:80/q03 What events or narrative setting surround this exchange? [وَحَآجَّهُۥ قَوْمُهُۥ · reports] (turns on: The notes situate the exchange in different episodes and stages of Abraham's public opposition.)
  6:80/q03/p1 Nimrod's claim to give life and death led Abraham to challenge him to bring the sun from the west. — reasons: Muqatil says Nimrod killed one man and spared another to claim life and death, then was confounded by Abraham's solar challenge.
  6:80/q03/p2 Abraham mocked and damaged idols made by Azar before his people disputed with him. — reasons: Baghawi says Azar gave grown Abraham idols to sell; Abraham mocked their uselessness, struck their heads in a river, and told them to drink. Alusi reports the threat may have followed abandonment, mockery, or damage.
  6:80/q03/p3 He publicly opposed his people after the truth became clear; this report follows his declaration in 6:79. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says the opening address and waw mark a self-contained report following 6:79, with sequence understood. Islahi holds that Abraham's grand words in 6:79–80 were prophetic and occurred after appointment.
6:80/q04 Why are the people's arguments left unstated? [وَحَآجَّهُۥ قَوْمُهُۥ · rhetoric]
  6:80/q04/p1 Their claims are too feeble to recount; the narrative centers Abraham's answer and firmness. — reasons: Biqai points to the clear proofs already given. Kuranyolu says their counterarguments against signs of unity are baseless. Ibn Ashur says their argument is omitted to highlight his steadfastness; he nonetheless identifies real debates elsewhere.
6:80/q05 How is أَتُحَٰٓجُّوٓنِّى read? [أَتُحَٰٓجُّوٓنِّى · readings] (turns on: Whether two nūns are assimilated or one is deleted, and whether the light form is accepted Arabic.)
  6:80/q05/p1 With a doubled nūn, assimilating the indicative and pronoun-related nūns. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid lists Ibn Kathir, Abu Amr, Asim, Hamza and Kisa'i; other lists add the rest apart from the light-reading transmitters. Ibn Ashur says waw is lengthened to separate consonants. Qiraat-ER calls it emphatic. Sibawayh's general rules explain deletion of indicative nūn in other plural emphatic forms.
  6:80/q05/p2 With a single light nūn, one of the two original nūns omitted for ease. — reasons: Nafi and Ibn Amir are commonly named, with variant details for Hisham and Ibn Dhakwan; Jishumi, Ibn Ashur and others include Abu Jafar. Sibawayh cites Medinans and 15:54; ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib's فليني and 39:64 support the sound change. Farisi reports attribution to Ghatafan. Qiraat-ER says the lighter sound softens the tone. Nahhas, Abu Hayyan, Samin and Ibn Ashur defend its Arabic and canonical standing against Abu Amr's or Makki's criticism.
6:80/q06 Which nūn is omitted in the light reading? [أَتُحَٰٓجُّوٓنِّى · grammar] (turns on: The disputed deletion is the first grammatical nūn or the second one before yāʾ.)
  6:80/q06/p1 The second, pronoun-related or protective nūn is omitted; the first inflectional nūn remains. — reasons: Farisi, Abu Ali, al-Akhfash and followers keep the grammatical subject and inflection marker. Abu Ali says the survivor takes kasra beside yāʾ. Al-Akhfash calls the second redundant and heavy, citing laytanī/laytī and poetry. The comparison with dropping one nūn in تبشرون and تأمروني is widely given.
  6:80/q06/p2 The first, indicative or inflectional nūn is omitted; the second remains before the speaker's yāʾ. — reasons: Sibawayh argues the inflectional marker can independently fall; its substitute can too. The remaining nūn already has kasra, avoiding a vowel change. Samin discusses poems, a hadith with negative lā, Abu Amr's readings at 3:160, 6:109 and 2:67, and examples of omitted ḍamma; he records objections that a root may drop while its derivative need not, and that retaining inflection has force. Alusi says the pronoun nūn is not merely protective.
  6:80/q06/p3 The form begins with two nūns, which Arabic may separate, retain, assimilate, or reduce. — reasons: Samin compares 39:64, 15:54, 16:27 and 46:17; Sibawayh's larger account distinguishes singular, dual, masculine plural and feminine forms before emphatic nūn and vowels used to avoid consecutive sukūns.
6:80/q07 What does Abraham's question about God mean? [قَالَ أَتُحَٰٓجُّوٓنِّى فِى ٱللَّهِ · interpretation] (turns on: The force of the interrogative and the sense of the reciprocal verb.)
  6:80/q07/p1 It rebukes their attempt to dispute divine oneness or attributes after God has guided him. — reasons: Zajjaj, Ibn Jawzi, Baydawi and others take fī Allāh as concerning God's unity; Ibn Ashur specifies attributes and oneness rather than God's person. Wahidi and Alusi say the question dismisses an unworthy challenge; Ibn Kathir calls their claims sophistry.
  6:80/q07/p2 The question answers the challenge and closes the debate if the verb is reciprocal; otherwise it is a reply within it. — reasons: Ibn Ashur compares 3:20 and says the rebuke expresses no hope that they will return to Abraham's belief.
6:80/q08 What does فِى ٱللَّهِ attach to? [فِى ٱللَّهِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the quote is syntactically coordinated with the narrative verb or begins independently.)
  6:80/q08/p1 It attaches to Abraham's quoted أتحاجوني, the second verb. — reasons: Abu Hayyan says coordination attaches it to the second verb. Samin reports the Shaykh's rationale: the first verb already has an object pronoun and would need an omitted complement. Samin questions whether coordination is necessary because the speech may simply start a new clause.
  6:80/q08/p2 The quoted question is a new clause, so the attachment need not be explained through coordination of the two verbs. — reasons: Samin finds this more natural than making the earlier ḥājjahu govern 'about God'.
6:80/q09 What guidance had God given Abraham? [وَقَدْ هَدَىٰنِ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether guidance names settled knowledge, the method and proof of unity, or a hypothetical path of discovery.)
  6:80/q09/p1 God guided him to knowledge, truth, and affirmation and worship of divine oneness. — reasons: Tabari, Zajjaj, Thaʿlabi, Wahidi, Zamakhshari, Ibn Atiyya, Tabrisi, Baydawi and others state this. Tabatabai says settled knowledge followed God's showing the kingdom of heaven and earth. The guidance renders further false argument futile, as Razi, Abu Hayyan, Alusi, Ibn Kathir and Ibn Ashur explain.
  6:80/q09/p2 God taught him how to establish proof of divine unity; the act of guidance itself proves God's lordship. — reasons: Alusi reports the proof-making sense. Tabatabai prefers the deeper argument: guidance to reject other gods is divine governance, and a rival deity would not lead him to reject its own divinity. He objects to stopping at 'no further debate' when the context argues.
  6:80/q09/p3 The account describes a hypothetical following of their path followed by recognition of its complete invalidity. — reasons: Alusi reports the suggestion while insisting neither this nor guidance to proof means Abraham had been astray or ignorant of God.
