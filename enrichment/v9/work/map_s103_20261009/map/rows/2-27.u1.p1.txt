God command to be joined? [مَآ أَمَرَ ٱللَّهُ بِهِۦٓ أَن يُوصَلَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the clause names one concrete relation or all commanded connections.)
  2:27/q13/p1 Kinship and family ties, including relatives' rights and kindness. — reasons: Tabari cites 47:22 and maternal relation; Wahidi specifies Quraysh's hostile break with Muhammad. Study Quran cites 4:1, 8:75, 33:6 and notes the Jews' shared ancestry. Bursevi reports warnings and blessings for kinship.
  2:27/q13/p2 The Messenger and believers, whom rejection or hostile alliance cuts off. — reasons: Hasan names the Messenger; Razi mentions alliance with unbelievers. Abu Hayyan objects that a person would be called man rather than ma.
  2:27/q13/p3 Belief in Muhammad and all prophets and scriptures without selective acceptance. — reasons: Thalabi cites 4:150 and 2:285; Ibn Abbas says believers join their faith by distinguishing none; Nasafi says the prophets agree in truth.
  2:27/q13/p4 Profession joined to action, which hypocrites leave apart. — reasons: Abu Hayyan cites 2:8 and hypocrites' tongue and heart; Bursevi reports neglect of known truth.
  2:27/q13/p5 All divinely commanded ties, worship, law, good conduct, and the bond between God and servant. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi include kinship as one part of religion. Baydawi lists believer alliance, prophets, congregational prayer, and any good. Kuranyolu adds unified humanity and proper order; Biqai adds completion of innate gifts.
  2:27/q13/p6 Higher spiritual kin and paths by which the soul journeys to God. — reasons: Qashani names the Holy Spirit, higher principles, and celestial spirits. Najmi interpretation cites 73:8 for spiritual wayfaring and withdrawal toward God.
  2:27/q13/p7 Reconciliation between spouses as one particular bond. — reasons: Kuranyolu reports this alongside kinship.
2:27/q14 How are مَا and أَن يُوصَلَ parsed? [مَآ أَمَرَ ٱللَّهُ بِهِۦٓ أَن يُوصَلَ · grammar] (turns on: The attachment of an yusala and the grammatical status of ma.)
  2:27/q14/p1 An yusala is genitive apposition to the pronoun in bihi; ma is the severed object. — reasons: Akhfash compares 'I passed the people, some of them.' Abu Hayyan cites poetry and prefers this; Baydawi and Alusi find it stronger in wording and force.
  2:27/q14/p2 An yusala is accusative apposition to ma. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya, Mahdawi, Abu al-Baqa, and Bursevi allow it; Abu Hayyan calls it weaker.
  2:27/q14/p3 An yusala gives purpose or aversion: lest it be joined. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Abu al-Baqa allow estimates such as 'disliking that it be joined'; Abu Hayyan rejects them.
  2:27/q14/p4 An yusala predicates an omitted huwa. — reasons: Abu al-Baqa proposes it; Samin calls it remote.
  2:27/q14/p5 Ma is a relative pronoun with 'God commanded it' as its clause. — reasons: Nahhas makes it the object of yaqtaun; Samin and Nasafi give the relative analysis.
  2:27/q14/p6 Ma is indefinite and qualified by its following clause. — reasons: Abu al-Baqa allows it; Abu Hayyan argues that an unrestricted indefinite cannot ground such severe blame.
  2:27/q14/p7 Ma can be a verbal noun despite the returning pronoun. — reasons: Samin attributes this only to Abu al-Hasan and Ibn al-Sarraj.
2:27/q15 What is an amr? [أَمَرَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether command is only verbal or also names the act or affair.)
  2:27/q15/p1 A superior's verbal request for an act, though context can yield permission, threat, challenge, or creation. — reasons: Jishumi and Tabarsi give request as the core; Baydawi links affairs with intended purposes. Mu'tazilites require superiority, Abu al-Husayn only claimed superiority; Pharaoh's words in 7:110 challenge that distinction.
  2:27/q15/p2 The word can also name an act or affair, not just speech. — reasons: Jurists cite Pharaoh's command in 11:97; Baydawi and Zamakhshari link a 'thing' to what is commanded.
2:27/q16 Does a bare command imply obligation? [أَمَرَ · law] (turns on: The default legal force of the command form.)
  2:27/q16/p1 Usually it implies obligation. — reasons: Jishumi reports the majority while recognizing contextual uses.
  2:27/q16/p2 It indicates recommendation. — reasons: Jishumi reports Abu Ali and Abu Hashim; he rejects suspension of judgment.
2:27/q17 What is corruption on earth here? [وَيُفْسِدُونَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether corruption denotes particular public offenses or all harmful sin.)
  2:27/q17/p1 Sins, idolatry, injustice, and evil deeds. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi specify worship of other than God and following desire. Kuranyolu includes material and spiritual disorder, pollution, unrest, pain, and calamity; Biqai pictures arrested bodily and moral development.
  2:27/q17/p2 Inviting unbelief or obstructing faith in Muhammad and the Quran. — reasons: Ibn Abbas identifies calling to unbelief; Ibn Jawzi reports intercepting migrants. Baydawi adds mockery of truth and damaged world order; Razi cites Pharaoh's fear at 40:26.
  2:27/q17/p3 Intimidating travelers, blocking roads, and creating insecurity. — reasons: Mawardi and others list road violence; Alusi refers to routes blocked to prevent migration.
  2:27/q17/p4 Any sin whose harm extends to others. — reasons: Tabarsi, Razi, and Alusi prefer this scope; Alusi says 'on earth' signals harm beyond the sinner. Razi says observance of law sustains order.
  2:27/q17/p5 Breaking the covenant and fragmenting the ties of social order. — reasons: Tabarsi and Abu Hayyan report covenant breach itself; Baydawi and Kuranyolu connect severance to worldly disorder.
  2:27/q17/p6 Quarrelling and stirring discord. — reasons: Razi reports this distinct account.
  2:27/q17/p7 Spoiling the innate seed of unity by shirk and rejection of prophets. — reasons: Najmi interpretation says faith and deeds should water the seed, citing Sura 103; Qashani describes wasting luminous substance for perishable darkness.
2:27/q18 What does 'losers' mean here? [ٱلْخَٰسِرُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Lexical diminished share versus particular worldly and final consequences.)
  2:27/q18/p1 They forfeit their share of mercy, reward, and lasting good, like traders losing capital. — reasons: Tabari cites Jarir for diminished share and honor. Wahidi adds missed reward and punishment; Qaffal speaks of labor without return. Baydawi, Biqai, Alusi, and Kuranyolu develop loss of eternal good for transient gain.
  2:27/q18/p2 They are destroyed or doomed, ultimately inhabitants of the Fire. — reasons: Tabari says doom is a consequence rather than the lexical definition; Jawhari gives ruin senses. Muqatil connects final loss to 13:25.
  2:27/q18/p3 They lose their allotted home, family, and portion in Paradise to believers. — reasons: Razi cites 23:10–11; Bursevi gives a home, family, and servants for each person.
  2:27/q18/p4 Unbelief nullifies their earlier good deeds and reward. — reasons: Razi applies this to Jews and hypocrites.
  2:27/q18/p5 Immediate pleasures sought through unbelief vanish through fighting or death. — reasons: Razi reports this worldly account.
  2:27/q18/p6 For non-Muslims loss language denotes unbelief; for Muslims it denotes sin or worldly loss. — reasons: Ibn Abbas's transmitted formula says sin in several sources but worldly loss in Tabarsi.
2:27/q19 How is أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْخَٰسِرُونَ parsed? [أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْخَٰسِرُونَ · grammar] (turns on: The grammatical role of hum.)
  2:27/q19/p1 Ulaika is subject, al-khasirun predicate, and hum an emphatic separator. — reasons: Zajjaj calls the separator imad in Kufan terminology; Nasafi stresses emphasis.
  2:27/q19/p2 Hum starts a second nominal clause whose predicate completes the first. — reasons: Zajjaj, Nahhas, and Qurtubi allow the nested clause.
  2:27/q19/p3 Hum is extra; al-khasirun alone predicates ulaika. — reasons: Nahhas reports this; Qurtubi permits redundancy.
2:27/q20 What force does the sequence and diction carry? [* · rhetoric]
  2:27/q20/p1 Escalating acts, recurring verbs, passive joining, and a distant demonstrative intensify the condemnation. — reasons: Abu Hayyan orders breach, broader severance, and broader corruption; imperfect verbs renew a settled fasiq trait. The passive includes ties others must join. Biqai and Alusi read distance from good; Alusi sees exclusive loss in a trade image. Samin notes the echo of 2:5.
2:27/q21 How are the three verbs connected syntactically? [يَنقُضُونَ وَيَقْطَعُونَ وَيُفْسِدُونَ · grammar]
  2:27/q21/p1 Yaqtaun and yufsidun are coordinated with yanqudun in the relative clause. — reasons: Nahhas makes ahd Allah the first verb's object and ma the second's; fī l-arḍ attaches to the third.
2:27/q22 How does this verse connect to its surroundings? [* · links] (turns on: Contextual explanation and verbal parallel.)
  2:27/q22/p1 It describes the fasiqin of the preceding parable and is echoed by covenant breakers in 13:25. — reasons: Ibn Kathir cites 13:19–25. Tabatabai contrasts fulfillers and breakers there. Abu Hayyan links parables to guidance or misguidance. Elmalili connects bad parable interpretation with practical wrongdoing and the next shift to direct address; Baghawi sees a turn to Arab polytheists. Abu Hayyan cites reports that children are born in a natural state and those dying before maturity enter Paradise; Asad connects the innate bond to divine nearness in 50:16 and 13:25.
  2:27/q22/p2 The verse's reversals and final wording echo nearby contrasts and 2:5. — reasons: Abu Hayyan pairs small and great, belief and unbelief, guidance and misguidance, and breach after confirmation; Samin notes the final-clause echo.
2:27/q23 What event occasioned this passage? [* · reports] (turns on: The reports differ on objectors and examples.)
  2:27/q23/p1 Hypocrites objected to God's fire and rainstorm parables as beneath divine majesty. — reasons: Suddi and the companion report cite 2:17 and 2:19 and say the response runs through 'the losers'.
  2:27/q23/p2 Polytheists object
