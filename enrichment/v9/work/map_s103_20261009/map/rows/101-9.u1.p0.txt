## THE CURRENT MAP
101:9/q01 What does ‘his mother is Hawiya’ mean? [فَأُمُّهُۥ هَاوِيَةٌۭ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether umm denotes an abode by maternal analogy, the top of the head, the actual grieving mother in an idiom, or the sinner's origin; the following feminine pronoun and Arabic idiom/poetry are used to decide.)
  101:9/q01/p1 His final abode and only refuge is Hell, called his mother as a child returns to and shelters with its mother. — reasons: Muqatil calls it his origin and abode: neither earth bears him nor sky shades him; he compares umm al-qura (6:92). Al-Farra, Ibn Qutayba, Ibn Zayd, Ibn Abbas, al-Akhfash, Abu Ishaq and others explain a mother's shelter, nurture, feeding, dwelling or place of return. Ibn Qutayba compares figurative father language for God's mercy and Jesus' water and bread as parents, rejects a biological reading, and cites the Arab name mother for earth as origin, return and sustenance; Umayya's poem calls earth mother and refuge. Ibn Atiyya cites Utba's battle saying about earth as mother. Tabari cites 3:151 for the Fire as abode. Al-Akhfash cites a poem about a fallen mother; al-Nahhas also cites mother of the Qur'an and a poem for origin/core. Ibn Kathir reports the two principal interpretations without choosing. Alusi and Bursevi add that the analogy can taunt and that Fire encloses the sinner like a womb; Kuranyolu depicts Hell opening its arms. Asad says umm is what embraces or enfolds, Study Quran derives it from amma, directing one's course, and Elmalili calls the refuge metaphor painfully ironic. Biqai notes earth and dwelling are called mother because they embrace and shelter. Qutb says partial obscurity heightens the later disclosure. Al-Tabatabai calls it a rational metaphor. Al-Nasafi and al-Wahidi stress a mother's place of rest. The Fire is the only remaining shelter, not a literal parent.
  101:9/q01/p2 ‘His mother’ is his head or crown: he is cast headfirst into Hell. — reasons: Ibn Khalid/Abu Khalid al-Walibi glosses umm as the crown or top of the head. Ikrima, Abu Salih, Qatada, Ibn Abbas, al-Kalbi and others describe the headfirst plunge, sometimes to the bottom of Hell. Al-Mawardi cites a poem addressed to Amr; al-Nahhas reports the related fallen-mother poetic line. Ibn Kathir allows head or brain; Bursevi specifies umm al-ra's as brain or its thin covering. Ibn Ashur supplies the ellipsis umm ra'sihi and treats hāwiya as fallen, likening a fall on the crown to death. Elmalili gives either brain's root as Hawiya or a headfirst fall. Al-Qummi calls Hawiya the head itself. Al-Tabatabai objects that this leaves the next verse's feminine pronoun without an apparent antecedent.
  101:9/q01/p3 The phrase invokes the Arabic idiom ‘his mother fell’: he is ruined, with his mother imagined collapsing in grief at his death. — reasons: Qatada and Ibn Abbas cite hawat/hawiyat ummuhu for grave calamity; al-Zamakhshari, al-Razi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, Alusi and others explain the imagined bereavement. The idiom can be a curse, an omen, wonder or even praise of brave dead warriors: Ka'b ibn Sa'd al-Ghanawi and the Hamasa poet supply examples, as do an unnamed poet and a line about morning and night. Ibn Ashur compares thakalathu ummuhu, explains the ellipsis and detached min in Ka'b's elegy, and sees reuse of a fixed expression for an analogous fate; he also illustrates how Arabs depict fortune through a mother's joy with a Bedouin's remark about Abraham (4:125). A report about la umma laka is linked to this verse. Al-Kashf and al-Tibi praise this reading for its relation to the earlier satisfied life (101:7); Elmalili notes its subtle force but favors the refuge reading. Corpus Coranicum reports Fischer's preference and lexical evidence, but objects that the following teaching question normally points to the named Hawiya; Fischer's proposed later addition of verses 10–11 is called unsupported. It allows an echo of the idiom alongside a real abyss.
  101:9/q01/p4 ‘Mother’ marks the Fire as the sinner's origin or native element, because he was created from or dominated by a fiery nature. — reasons: Biqai says the image suggests creation from Hell and a satanic, fiery nature. Bursevi says the fire-created unbeliever returns to his origin and calls this explanation apparent; he also lists refuge, taunt and womb-like enclosure.
101:9/q02 How is ‘fa-ummuhu’ read? [فَأُمُّهُۥ · readings] (turns on: Attested vocalization and dialect/grammatical conditions; the directional and emended readings lack attestation.)
  101:9/q02/p1 The majority read ummuhu with a damma on the hamza. — reasons: Abu Hayyan explicitly contrasts the majority's damma with Talha's kasra; the full recension repeats the contrast.
  101:9/q02/p2 Talha reads fa-immuhu with kasra on the hamza and a doubled, damma-marked mim. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya records the vowels and doubling. Ibn Khalawayh transmits Ibn Durayd's claim that the kasra is a dialect form. Grammarians restrict such kasra to a preceding kasra or ya; al-Samin refers to the fuller discussion at 4:11.
  101:9/q02/p3 Muqatil's directional construal would require fa-ummuhu with fatha, but no such reading is reported. — reasons: Al-Wahidi reports Muqatil as saying people head toward Hawiya, then rejects this as an established interpretation because the necessary fatha is unattested.
  101:9/q02/p4 Bellamy conjectures the consonants fa-ummatun hawiya, ‘a steep downward path.’ — reasons: Corpus Coranicum says no such variant is attested and judges the proposed emendation an easier secondary reading.
101:9/q03 What is Hawiya, and what explains its name? [هَاوِيَةٌۭ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the term denotes a general deep chasm/Hell, a particular lowest gate, or has a Hebrew derivation; lexicography, falling imagery and audience familiarity are cited.)
  101:9/q03/p1 Hawiya is Hell or a deadly deep abyss, named from falling into its depths. — reasons: Al-Layth and lexicographers give both Hell and a bottomless pit; the Kamus derives it from falling. Mawardi, Qurtubi, Jishumi and Tabatabai connect the name with the inhabitants' plunge, Jishumi saying from top to bottom and citing related hawā usage for movement, straying and destruction (28:57, 6:71, 53:53). Baghawi describes a chasm whose bottom cannot be reached. Qurtubi and Ibn Ashur describe mahwa/mahwi as low spaces between mountains, where falls kill; Qurtubi cites a verse. Kashshaf, Bursevi and Alusi cite a report of a seventy-year fall; the fuller Kashshaf ascribes it to the Prophet. Corpus Coranicum says independent dictionary equations with mahwa/mahwat support the topographical meaning, answers Sells's objection about early attestations with lexicography and rhyme, and compares Revelation 9:1,11; it regards Hell as proper name as possibly secondary. The next verse identifies it as a blazing fire.
  101:9/q03/p2 Hawiya specifically names Hell's lowest gate. — reasons: Qurtubi reports the identification; Kashifi, as quoted by Bursevi, calls it the lowest of all Hell's gates. Alusi and Elmalili also report that some restrict the name to the lowest gate.
  101:9/q03/p3 Hawiya comes from Hebrew howa, ‘destruction.’ — reasons: Torrey proposes the Hebrew derivation. Corpus Coranicum doubts that early Meccan listeners knew the rare Hebrew word, found only twice in the Hebrew Bible.
101:9/q04 What does the following ‘what is it?’ refer to, and how does this verse prepare its answer? [هَاوِيَةٌۭ · links] (turns on: Whether the feminine pronoun has the named Hawiya as its concrete antecedent or must recover calamity, Fire, or the preceding sentence from an idiomatic/head reading.)
  101:9/q04/p1 The pronoun refers to Hawiya; its initially obscure threat is explained as blazing Fire in verses 10–11. — reasons: Baydawi, Alusi and Elmalili point the pronoun directly to Hawiya. Alusi says delaying the explanation magnifies strangeness and terror; Qutb calls the image elegant and partly obscure. Biqai says the indefinite wording magnifies the place and denies rest or stable ground. Corpus Coranicum says the delayed clear threat closes the central eschatological sequence and compares teaching questions about obscure places at 83:7–8 and 83:18–19; it rejects Fischer's later-addition theory and Paret's nonspecific antecedent. Al-Tabatabai uses the clear antecedent to object to the head reading. Razi says the Event (101:1–3) is perceptible but the Abyss is not, accounting for different introductory questions.
  101:9/q04/p2 On the idiom reading, ‘it’ refers to the implied calamity; on the head reading, it evokes the understood Fire. — reasons: Kashshaf allows the calamity implied by the first interpretation or Hawiya. Alusi distinguishes the calamity in the imprecation reading, Hawiya in his reading, and the understood Fire in the head reading. Paret proposes reference to the whole preceding assertion, but Corpus Coranicum objects that the teaching-question pronoun usually has a concrete feminine referent.
101:9/q05 What is the final ha in the following question? [هَاوِيَةٌۭ · readings]
  101:9/q05/p1 The final ha is a pausal letter, omitted by some in connected recitation and retained by others in agreement with the written codex. — reasons: Kashshaf gives the alternatives while discussing the following question's pronoun.
