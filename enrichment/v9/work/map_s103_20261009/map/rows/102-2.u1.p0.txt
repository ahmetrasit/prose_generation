## THE CURRENT MAP
102:2/q01 What does ḥattā make the endpoint of the rivalry? [حَتَّىٰ · grammar] (turns on: Whether ḥattā limits the duration of distraction or the extent of what rivals count, and whether a grave visit itself changes them.)
  102:2/q01/p1 The distraction continues through life and ends only at death and burial. — reasons: Al-Samīn makes ḥattā the limit of alhākum; Ibn ʿĀshūr says no further action follows death. Elmalılı says death ends competition; Qatada's tribes die off one by one.
  102:2/q01/p2 The rivalry extends even to counting or praising the dead at the graves; the endpoint is included in the distraction. — reasons: Al-Bursawī, al-Ālūsī, and Elmalılı explicitly include the endpoint. Ibn ʿĀshūr instead connects ḥattā to the utmost object counted, graves themselves.
  102:2/q01/p3 The visit itself may end worldly greed by awakening the visitor. — reasons: Elmalılı allows this as a literal construal, while warning that reflection is not universal.
102:2/q02 Why is 'you visited' in the past tense? [زُرْتُمُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the past form reports an actual past visit, vividly states a certain future, or addresses people from a later time.)
  102:2/q02/p1 The certain future is expressed as already completed. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr compares 16:1; al-Ālūsī and Elmalılı also allow the certainty of death.
  102:2/q02/p2 The address includes people already dead or treats earlier generations as examples for the living. — reasons: Al-Rāzī compares the Qur'anic address about earlier prophet-killers in 2:61; al-Ālūsī treats ancestral deaths as the listeners' own.
  102:2/q02/p3 The addressees are already near death, so the grave is spoken of as reached. — reasons: Al-Rāzī gives this reply to the objection that future death cannot be past.
  102:2/q02/p4 God addresses unbelievers on Judgment Day, after their grave visit. — reasons: Abū Muslim proposes this; al-Ālūsī calls it astonishing, and Elmalılı says the next warning of future knowledge better fits this life or the grave.
  102:2/q02/p5 The completed form presents a past tribal grave visit. — reasons: Paret takes the perfect as evidence of a historical episode; Corpus Coranicum favors the general gnomic statement.
102:2/q03 What does zurtum mean in Arabic usage? [زُرْتُمُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the phrase retains ordinary physical visiting, serves as the attested death idiom, or invokes the root's wider senses.)
  102:2/q03/p1 It means visiting or seeing a place without remaining there. — reasons: Al-Jishumī likens a visit to going to water; Islahi cites a Ḥamāsī poem about revisiting one's land. Bint al-Shāṭi traces z-w-r to inclination and notes Qur'anic uses of ziyārah and tazāwar.
  102:2/q03/p2 In poetry, visiting graves is an idiom for dying. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, al-Ālūsī, Elmalılı and Bint al-Shāṭi cite Jarīr on Abū Mālik; al-Zamakhsharī also cites a year-end line, and Elmalılı cites al-Akhṭal.
  102:2/q03/p3 The root can also express deviation or falsehood. — reasons: Al-Jishumī notes zūr as forged handwriting; Bint al-Shāṭi relates the sensory idea of turning and cites 18:17, 22:30, 25:4, 25:72 and 58:2.
102:2/q04 What does visiting the graves mean here? [زُرْتُمُ ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the verse portrays death, an actual boastful trip, figurative mention of ancestors, or habitual boastful visits.)
  102:2/q04/p1 People remain absorbed in rivalry until they die and are buried. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr and Bint al-Shāṭi favor this. Jarīr and al-Akhṭal attest the death idiom; Ibn Kathīr cites a fever patient's saying that fever would send him to graves. Al-Rāzī answers the visitor objection by future departure for reckoning.
  102:2/q04/p2 They went physically to cemeteries and counted or praised their buried ancestors. — reasons: Reports locate the act in Quraysh or among Ansar. Al-Ālūsī calls the visit literal in the clan account; Ibn ʿĀshūr says grave stands for its occupant and cites al-Nābigha and a line variably attributed to ʿIṣām or Hammām. Al-Qurṭubī cites a poem of wealthy people continuing their boast through stone monuments at their graves.
  102:2/q04/p3 Visiting is a figure for bringing the dead into the boast, whether or not the rivals actually went to the cemetery. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī says it figuratively describes recollection; al-Bursawī calls it a euphemism; al-Ālūsī weighs metaphor, metonymy and representation, while al-Zamakhsharī calls it sarcastic. Abū Ḥayyān objects that this strains ordinary 'visit'.
  102:2/q04/p4 The rebuke targets repeated cemetery visits made to advertise ancestors and one's visits. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya describes visits that crowd out worship and learning. Al-Ālūsī finds this reading remote, though he condemns boastful visits themselves.
102:2/q05 Why call burial a 'visit'? [زُرْتُمُ · rhetoric]
  102:2/q05/p1 The grave is a temporary station before resurrection and a lasting home in Paradise or Hell. — reasons: A Bedouin and ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz infer that a visitor must depart. Al-Rāzī uses departure for reckoning to answer the objection that the dead do not leave; al-Biqāʿī says even a long intermediate stay is short beside eternity. Bint al-Shāṭi includes reckoning and recompense.
102:2/q06 What does al-maqābir denote and how is it formed? [ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · meaning]
  102:2/q06/p1 It is the plural of maqbara or maqbarah, a burial ground containing many graves. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās notes singular vowel variants and Hijazi usage; al-Qurṭubī distinguishes qubūr as plural of qabr and cites poetry and Abū Saʿīd al-Maqburī's name. Ibn ʿĀshūr specifies land with many graves. Al-Qurṭubī distinguishes qabartu, 'I buried', from aqbartu, 'I ordered burial' (80:21).
102:2/q07 Why use al-maqābir rather than qubūr? [ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the plural carries merely a lexical place sense or a distinctive collective image.)
  102:2/q07/p1 It evokes a vast collective cemetery across ages and social classes, fitting the end of accumulation. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭi and her Iʿjāz work prefer this semantic breadth over sound alone; the cadence also fits takāthur. Islahi notes the rhyme and the pity of the compressed journey to death. Al-Qurṭubī and Bint al-Shāṭi say maqābir occurs only here; Bint al-Shāṭi contrasts five uses of qubūr and singular qabr at 9:84.
  102:2/q07/p2 It is treated simply as another word for graves or burial places. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭi argues this misses the collective setting and the swift movement from rivalry to death.
102:2/q08 Who occasioned the warning by boasting at graves? [* · reports] (turns on: Which transmitted setting is intended, and whether the verse is tied to a single contest or speaks generally.)
  102:2/q08/p1 Banū ʿAbd Manāf and Banū Sahm of Quraysh counted living notables, then the dead; Sahm surpassed the other clan. — reasons: Reports differ in detail: some give a margin of three households; al-Bayḍāwī has Sahm invoke their pre-Islamic losses; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ also records the Meccan version, and Study Quran calls it best attested.
  102:2/q08/p2 Jews boasted that their clans outnumbered one another and died astray. — reasons: Al-Qatāda is repeatedly cited for this view; some notes say rivalry distracted them from faith.
  102:2/q08/p3 Two Ansar clans, Banū Ḥāritha and Banū al-Ḥārith, boasted of living then dead members. — reasons: Ibn Burayda names the clans; other reports merely say a clan among the Ansar. The naming and the generic Ansar formulation are variants of this occasion.
  102:2/q08/p4 The warning describes recurrent tribal rivalry rather than one named occasion. — reasons: Qatada depicts one generation after another falling into graves. Al-Wāḥidī applies it to everyone distracted by rivalry; Kur'an Yolu says the general human target remains despite specific reports.
102:2/q09 Was the sūra revealed in Mecca or Medina? [* · sciences] (turns on: Whether the reported Ansar setting and related reports outweigh the common Meccan attribution.)
  102:2/q09/p1 It is Meccan, commonly dated after al-Kawthar. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr identifies it as Meccan; Abū Ḥayyān reports broad exegetical agreement, with al-Bukhārī as an exception. Bint al-Shāṭi gives the common sixteenth place in revelation.
  102:2/q09/p2 It is Medinan. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī prefers this from Ibn Burayda's Ansar occasion, a report from Ubayy, and ʿAlī's grave-punishment report; al-Bukhārī and a minority are also cited for Madinan attribution.
102:2/q10 Does the verse point to punishment in the grave? [زُرْتُمُ ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · theology]
  102:2/q10/p1 Yes: the warning of knowledge upon reaching the graves implies punishment there. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī and al-Naḥḥās infer this; ʿAlī reportedly says Muslims doubted grave punishment until this sūra or its coming warning was revealed. Ibn Kathīr reports al-Tirmidhī grading ʿAlī's report gharīb. Al-Qurṭubī gives a view that they see God's punishment there; Elmalılı says hoarders awaken after losing their possessions.
102:2/q11 What conduct does the verse condemn when rivalry distracts people? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Which goods are being pursued and whether the competition diverts from what truly matters.)
  102:2/q11/p1 Worldly boasting and accumulation divert people from obedience, faith, reflection and preparation for the afterlife. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī names wealth and numbers; al-Rāzī ranks knowledge and virtue above bodily and external goods, citing Abraham's prayer in 26:83 and 57:20 on worldly rivalry. The range includes wealth, kin, allies, armies and pleasure. Ibn ʿĀshūr specifies neglect of Qur'anic evidence and Islam's call; Asad extends the critique to consumerism, power, social disorientation and environmental damage.
  102:2/q11/p2 Competition in knowledge, virtue and good deeds may be praiseworthy, whereas the familiar worldly rivalry is condemned. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says the definite article identifies the blameworthy
