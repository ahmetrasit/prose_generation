18:45/q12 What action does تَذْرُوهُ describe? [تَذْرُوهُ ٱلرِّيَٰحُ · meaning] (turns on: The glosses emphasize dispersal, turning motion, or lifting; al-Qurtubi says their senses are close.)
  18:45/q12/p1 The wind scatters, casts or blows the plant fragments away. — reasons: Al-Farra compares forms from dharā and adhrā and cites al-Mufaddal's poem about casting off; al-Majaz, Ibn Qutayba and al-Tabari give the basic scatter sense, while al-Tabari's anonymous line uses yadhruk for throwing someone from a mount. Abu Ubayda, al-Qutaybi and later transmitters gloss scattering and blowing. Al-Wahidi gives several dialect forms and dispersal; al-Jishumi says tadhriya scatters light objects in every direction. Ibn Atiyya cites 54:31; al-Qurtubi cites a Sibawayh and al-Farra verse about wind blowing a bird off course and says the glosses are close. Abu Hayyan notes dharā and adhrā as paired forms. Bursevi compares winnowing grain; Ibn Ashur says dharw is throwing into the air.
  18:45/q12/p2 The wind carries the fragments to and fro or turns and stirs them. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and Ibn Kaysan give turning and back-and-forth motion in al-Tha'labi; al-Baghawi reports Ibn Abbas's stirring. Al-Qurtubi treats these as close to scattering. Abu Hayyan, al-Alusi and al-Tabataba'i transmit the back-and-forth gloss; al-Biqa'i combines stirring with dispersal. Al-Nahhas transmits al-Farra's related adhraytu for turning a person on a camel.
  18:45/q12/p3 The wind lifts or raises the plant debris. — reasons: Al-Akhfash gives this gloss in al-Tha'labi, Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi; al-Wahidi includes lifting among the motions, and al-Samin also reports it.
18:45/q13 What is the syntactic role of تَذْرُوهُ? [هَشِيمًۭا تَذْرُوهُ · grammar]
  18:45/q13/p1 It is an adjective describing the broken vegetation. — reasons: Al-Samin explicitly parses the verb phrase as a modifier of hashim.
18:45/q14 How is تَذْرُوهُ read? [تَذْرُوهُ · readings] (turns on: Attested recitations use related wāw and yā' roots and different verb forms, with named readers and vowels varying among reports.)
  18:45/q14/p1 The common reading is تذروه from the wāw form. — reasons: Al-Nahhas labels it the common reading; al-Zajjaj says two other forms are possible in Arabic but not recited. Al-Farra and al-Majaz give variant verb forms with comparable scattering sense.
  18:45/q14/p2 A reading has تذريه, with forms derived from the alternate yāʾ or causative verb. — reasons: Al-Farra attributes tadhrihi al-rih to Abdullah; al-Nahhas adds a report from al-Kisa'i. Al-Tha'labi assigns scattering or lifting forms to Talha b. Musarrif; al-Jishumi calls Talha's form a dialect variant. Al-Wahidi and Abu Hayyan cite Ibn Mas'ud. Ibn Atiyya cites Ibn Abbas; al-Kashshaf reports Ibn Abbas's causative form. Ibn al-Jawzi lists Ubayy, Ibn Abbas and Ibn Abi Abla with raised tā', kasra on rā' and silent yā'; Ibn Mas'ud differs with fatḥa on tā'. Al-Samin separates Abdullah's yā' radical from Ibn Abbas's ḍamma tā' causative reading, perhaps derivable from either root. Al-Qurtubi gives Talha and Ibn Mas'ud; al-Alusi calls Ibn Mas'ud's form quadriliteral.
18:45/q15 Is ٱلرِّيَٰحُ read in the plural or singular? [ٱلرِّيَٰحُ · readings] (turns on: Reader attribution and the connotation of singular versus plural wind.)
  18:45/q15/p1 Plural الرياح is the common reading. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj gives the plural. Al-Nasafi attributes it to Hamza and Asim; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin call it the majority form. Al-Kashshaf reports a plural-winds reading of Ibn Abbas with a causative verb.
  18:45/q15/p2 Singular الريح is also read. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya names al-Hasan and others; al-Qurtubi reports Talha's tadhrihi al-rih. Abu Hayyan notes several authorities. Al-Samin names Zayd b. Ali, al-Hasan and al-Nakha'i; al-Alusi additionally lists al-A'mash, Talha, Ibn Abi Layla, Ibn Muhaysin, Khalaf, Ibn Isa and Ibn Jarir. Qiraat-Er associates singular rih in classical usage with destructive wind.
18:45/q16 How is مُقْتَدِرًا formed? [مُّقْتَدِرًا · grammar]
  18:45/q16/p1 It is on the iftaʿala pattern from q-d-r. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba and Ibn al-Jawzi give the derivation.
18:45/q17 What does كَانَ convey about God's power? [وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ مُّقْتَدِرًا · theology] (turns on: Whether past kāna states an eternal or precreation attribute, or can point to future power without a tense-shifting cue.)
  18:45/q17/p1 His power is timeless or has always existed, including before creation. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj prefers Sibawayh and al-Khalil: kāna describes timeless power, and Arabic past tense cannot mean future or present without a shifting particle. Al-Nahhas reports some saying always and prefers Sibawayh's explanation that observed power was already God's. Al-Wahidi reports al-Zajjaj that power was not newly acquired; Ibn Atiyya transmits Sibawayh's rationally recognized prehuman power and al-Hasan's precreation state. Al-Tabrisi follows al-Zajjaj and Sibawayh. Al-Biqa'i calls divine power eternal, not newly acquired. Al-Nahhas reports Abu Ishaq's rejection of an unsignalled future sense.
  18:45/q17/p2 He was able to bring all things into being before they existed. — reasons: Al-Hasan gives the precreation explanation in al-Zajjaj, al-Wahidi and al-Tabrisi; Ibn Atiyya also reports him. It specifies the pre-existing scope of divine ability.
  18:45/q17/p3 The past form may mean He will be or is and will be powerful. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj reports the kā'in wa-yakūn interpretation but argues it conflicts with ordinary tense usage. Al-Nahhas reports the future sense. Al-Tabrisi reports using past power to point toward future events.
18:45/q18 What does God's being مُقْتَدِرًا encompass here? [وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍۢ مُّقْتَدِرًا · theology]
  18:45/q18/p1 Power over all things, especially creating, sustaining, ending and restoring life. — reasons: Muqatil includes resurrection; al-Tabari applies power to ruining gardens and worldly wealth. Al-Zajjaj and al-Wahidi name origination and annihilation; al-Wahidi argues from God's producing and ending vegetation. Al-Kashshaf, al-Qurtubi, al-Nasafi, Ibn Kathir, Bursevi, al-Alusi and al-Biqa'i name creation and destruction; al-Qurtubi and al-Biqa'i include restoration or enlivening. Al-Razi adds nurturing before abolition. Al-Thalabi and al-Baghawi state universal power. Yahya and Elmalili conclude God can do all things. Islahi sees power in both producing and decaying worldly luxuriance.
18:45/q19 What work does the closing power clause do in the parable? [وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍۢ مُّقْتَدِرًا · rhetoric]
  18:45/q19/p1 It concludes from the plant's creation and decay to God's universal power. — reasons: Ibn Ashur calls it parenthetical: God produces things and their opposites and orders causes of decay after causes of persistence; its universal wording gives the conclusion. Abu Hayyan likewise says the scattering illustrates power over creation and extinction.
18:45/q20 How does this parable relate to other Quranic parables and verses? [* · links] (turns on: The cited verses illuminate different parts of the same growth-and-loss image.)
  18:45/q20/p1 It condenses or parallels the water-and-vegetation parable of 10:24. — reasons: Al-Wahidi calls this a shortened version and points to his fuller explanation in Yunus; Ibn al-Jawzi says the comparison is elaborated there. Bursevi calls it a condensation. Ibn Kathir also cites 10:24.
  18:45/q20/p2 Other verses also picture temporary growth that withers or worldly adornment that passes. — reasons: Ibn Kathir cites 39:21 and 57:20 plus an authentic hadith calling the world sweet and green. Al-Alusi cites 87:4–5, where pasture becomes dark dry debris. Islahi uses 57:20 to explain worldly amusement and rivalry. Study Quran cites 39:21 and 57:20; al-Razi cites 22:5 for the rain-fed earth stirring and swelling.
18:45/q21 How does the verse connect with the surrounding garden story and its aftermath? [* · links] (turns on: The sources look either backward to the garden story or forward to the contrast with lasting deeds.)
  18:45/q21/p1 It extends the lesson of the ruined garden and the failure of pride in wealth. — reasons: Al-Tabari links the address to 18:28 and garden boasting to 18:36. Bursevi says the brother's admonition failed because the listener's heart hardened and ended in regret. Study Quran says verses 32–44 show dependence on divine beneficence and the garden's final barren plain. Al-Nahhas transmits Abu Ishaq's allowance of the preceding verse's ʿuqbā on the pattern of bushrā and a poetic tazlaq line from Sibawayh and al-Mufaddal; these are lexical details in the nearby garden context.
  18:45/q21/p2 The next lesson contrasts passing ornaments with enduring righteous deeds. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says righteous work remains in the afterlife while worldly beauty and power fade. Islahi says ornaments do not accompany people to the next world; Kuranyolu contrasts transient wealth and children with enduring righteous deeds.
18:45/q22 What conduct does the parable call for toward worldly goods? [مَّثَلَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the ethical emphasis is avoiding pride and misplaced reliance, limiting consumption, or using worldly life without attachment.)
  18:45/q22/p1 Do not boast in or rely on passing wealth; seek the enduring afterlife and righteous deeds. — reasons: Al-Tabari warns against pride in wealth and urges work
