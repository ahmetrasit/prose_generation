102:2/q12 What is the effect of leaving the object of distraction unstated? [* · rhetoric]
  102:2/q12/p1 The omission widens and intensifies the rebuke to every religious duty displaced by rivalry. — reasons: Al-Rāzī lists remembrance, obligations, recommended worship, knowledge, reflection and contemplation; al-Bayḍāwī says the suppressed object is religious concerns, and al-Ālūsī says the ambiguity magnifies blame.
102:2/q13 How does this verse relate to the preceding and following warnings? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the note explains the lead-in from al-Qāriʿah or the transition to this sūra's following threat.)
  102:2/q13/p1 It recalls al-Qāriʿah: worldly numbers and wealth distract from the deeds weighed at Judgment. — reasons: Al-Rāzī and Ibn al-Zubayr connect preparation for the Calamity (101:1); Islahi says only deeds count in 101:6–11. Bint al-Shāṭi allows a shared warning tone in muṣḥaf order but rejects a revelation chronology link, since the sūras are separated by thirteen in that chronology. Elmalılı urges reflection on scales and destinations.
  102:2/q13/p2 The clause leads to the next 'No indeed' and its afterlife threat. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says kallā rejects their accumulation, sawfa guarantees eventual knowledge, and the omitted object is its evil outcome; Elmalılı also reads the coming rebuke as a warning of what heedlessness will bring.
102:2/q14 What did the Prophet say endures when death ends rivalry? [* · reports] (turns on: Which Prophetic saying is used to expose what remains valuable at death.)
  102:2/q14/p1 One's wealth is only what one consumes, wears out, or gives away in charity. — reasons: This saying follows recitation in al-Ṭabarī; al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, al-Rāzī and al-Bursawī cite versions. Al-Thaʿlabī's Ibn ʿAbbās report defines takāthur as wrongful accumulation and withholding and names five blessings: food, drink, sleep, shade and health.
  102:2/q14/p2 Family and wealth return from the burial, while the person's deeds remain. — reasons: Anas and Ibn Kathīr transmit the Prophet's report of three companions of the dead: family, wealth and deeds.
102:2/q15 What purpose should an actual grave visit serve? [زُرْتُمُ ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · interpretation] (turns on: The visitor's intention and response: admonition and reform, or renewed pride and display.)
  102:2/q15/p1 It should remind visitors of death and the Hereafter, soften the heart and turn them toward good deeds. — reasons: The Prophet first forbade visits then permitted them for admonition in reports from ʿAlī, Abū Saʿīd, Ibn Masʿūd and Abū Hurayra; al-Tirmidhī grades Burayda's reminder report ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ. Al-Qurṭubī says direct sight is stronger than hearing, citing 'news is not like sight'; visiting is more accessible than witnessing death. Elmalılı compares a thoughtful visit to reading a book and says its best result is renewed resolve.
  102:2/q15/p2 Visits made for boasting, display, or idle touring reverse that purpose and fall under the rebuke. — reasons: Al-Rāzī calls their worldly attachment a reversal; ʿAlī says ancestral corpses should prompt humility. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān criticize monuments and shrines; Elmalılı points to wasteful display beside hungry neighbors.
102:2/q16 Are grave visits permitted or recommended? [زُرْتُمُ ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · law] (turns on: How the prohibition, later permission and this verse's rebuke apply to different motives for visiting.)
  102:2/q16/p1 Visits for remembrance are permitted, and some jurists call them recommended; the Prophet's later permission followed an earlier prohibition. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān cite the prophetic change. Elmalılı cites Dürr-i Muhtār for permissibility, including women; several Hanafi manuals recommend visits, and Radd al-Muḥtār recommends weekly visits on specified days. Elmalılı says the nonmutawātir report clarifies the verse's scope but cannot abrogate or restrict it.
  102:2/q16/p2 The verse itself rebukes boastful visits, leaving reflective visits outside its condemnation. — reasons: Elmalılı defends this distinction against what he considers an overbroad reading of al-Ālūsī; he adds that promoting literal visits as the verse's central aim conflicts with the following rebuke.
102:2/q17 May women visit graves? [زُرْتُمُ ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · law] (turns on: Whether the curse precedes general permission and how grief, age and mixed gatherings affect visits.)
  102:2/q17/p1 The later general permission includes women as well as men. — reasons: Some scholars treat the curse report as predating permission; Dürr-i Muhtār permits women to visit. Al-Qurṭubī permits women when separated from men.
  102:2/q17/p2 Women’s visits are disapproved or restricted, especially where grief or mixed gathering risks harm. — reasons: Al-Tirmidhī reports a curse on frequent female visitors and grades it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ; others cite women's lesser patience and greater grief. Al-Qurṭubī allows older women but not young women going out, while allowing any age when separated from men and forbidding tempting mixed gatherings.
102:2/q18 What should a visitor do at a grave? [زُرْتُمُ ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · law]
  102:2/q18/p1 Visit with a present heart; greet, pray, recite and reflect, while respecting the graves. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī prohibits walking or sitting on graves and says to approach known graves from the front. Elmalılı cites the Prophet at Baqīʿ, manuals recommending greeting, standing for prayer, reciting available Qur'an and sometimes approaching from the feet; reports vary about where the Prophet stood. He warns against asking the dead for help and urges asking God for both living and dead.
102:2/q19 Can acts' reward be dedicated to the dead or the Prophet? [زُرْتُمُ ٱلْمَقَابِرَ · law] (turns on: Whether reward transfer requires a specific permission in the Prophet's case.)
  102:2/q19/p1 Rewards of recitation, prayer, fasting and charity can reach the deceased. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀbidīn allows transfer of rewards; Tatarhāniyya recommends intending charity for all believers without diminishing one's reward. Elmalılı allows recitation and dedication; al-Qurṭubī names prayer and Qur'an as benefits to the dead.
  102:2/q19/p2 Dedicating Qur'an recitation's reward to the Prophet requires his specific permission, so blessings and wasīla are preferable. — reasons: Elmalılı reports Ibn Taymiyya's proposed restriction; al-Subkī and others reject it, saying no special permission is needed and citing Ibn ʿUmar.
102:2/q20 How should remembrance of the dead affect one’s conduct? [* · interpretation]
  102:2/q20/p1 The dead's deeds are theirs; ancestral pride is pointless, while their good example can inspire righteous action. — reasons: Elmalılı cites 2:141, a report that people are resurrected with those they love, and 2:165 and 3:31 for love directed to God. He says visits can preserve exemplars and awaken preparation for resurrection and judgment; asking the dead for aid is especially unfitting.
102:2/q21 Is the opening an assertion or an interrogative rebuke? [* · readings] (turns on: Whether the opening carries an explicit interrogative hamza or reads as a statement.)
  102:2/q21/p1 It is a declarative rebuke. — reasons: Al-Bursawī calls the clause declarative and connects ḥattā to the continued distraction.
  102:2/q21/p2 It may be read with an interrogative hamza, a question that asserts the rebuke. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās reads the opening interrogatively; al-Rāzī cites analogous interrogative readings such as aʾ-andhartahum and aʾ-idhā kunnā ʿiẓāman.
102:2/q22 How is this sūra’s exceptional merit explained? [* · sciences]
  102:2/q22/p1 Its thousand-verse merit has been explained as a sixth of the Qur'an because it addresses return to God. — reasons: Ibn al-Milq uses roughly 6,200 verses and al-Ghazālī's six aims; al-Ālūsī says the matter exceeds this calculation.
