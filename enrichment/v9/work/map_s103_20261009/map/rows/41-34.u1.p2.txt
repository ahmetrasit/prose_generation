41:34/q18 How is the verse used in broader arguments about proof, rhetoric, and restraining harm? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: These are distinct uses and surrounding reflections in the cited sources, rather than rival explanations of a single phrase.)
  41:34/q18/p1 The verse expresses the widely recognized inference that beneficence wins affection; eloquence can make such implications immediately accessible. — reasons: Jurjani compares the command to the Prophetic maxim about hearts loving benefactors. In Dalail he distinguishes inference from fixed lexical meaning and says eloquence shortens the route from expressed to implied meaning, illustrating indirect expression through metonymy, metaphor, and representation with three cited poetic lines.
  41:34/q18/p2 A likeness can be directly sensed or require interpretation; removing doubt makes a proof manifest like the sun. — reasons: Jurjani contrasts a cheek and rose alike in redness with an argument and the sun; doubt acts as a mental curtain, and a clear proof leaves denial to defective reason or obstinacy. Interpretive likenesses range from easy to subtle. Qushayri says revealed signs remove excuse from those seeking God.
  41:34/q18/p3 Argument about subtle rhetorical judgments needs a receptive listener and may leave uncertainty after extended reflection. — reasons: Jurjani says critics trust their own taste; persuasion requires willingness to reconsider. He uses Abu Nuwas to show certainty near but unsettled. Bint al-Shati reports this argument, says Jurjani separated rhetoric study from Quranic study, and cites Abu Nuwas's poem of suspicion displacing certainty.
  41:34/q18/p4 Some harmful defiance must be restrained by force when counsel fails. — reasons: Jurjani cites a poetic line about honor and bloodshed as a rational truth confirmed by divine commands, law, and Prophetic practice; tyrants are checked by steel, as dirt must be cleared from a well and illness from a body.
  41:34/q18/p5 The passage urges following evidence rather than leaders and seeking refuge against human and jinn devils. — reasons: Jishumi draws the evidentiary lesson; Razi connects 41:34 and 41:36 with 7:199–200 to show prophets continually sought protection; Muqatil also commands Muhammad to seek refuge from Satan.

  41:34/q19/p2 It refers to the declaration 'There is no god but God'. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya reports the proposal only to reject it as unsupported by the wording.
41:34/q20 What reports illustrate the Prophet's and believers' practice of this command? [* · reports]
  41:34/q20/p1 The Prophet modeled pardon, generosity, and maintaining ties, while his followers answered abuse without revenge. — reasons: Ibn Ashur transmits Aisha on his Quranic character and refusal of personal revenge, and a report through Iyad, Ibn Mardawayh, Jabir, and Ibn Jarir connecting 7:199 to Gabriel's instruction to maintain kinship, give to the deprived, and pardon the wrongdoer. Bursevi and Study Quran transmit similar sayings; Qurtubi gives Abu Bakr's forgiving reply and Ali's advice to Qanbar. The report of the Greatest Imam says praise reversed an abuser's speech.
  41:34/q20/p2 Affectionate, sincere handshaking between Muslims removes sins or rancor. — reasons: Al-Bara ibn Azib reports the Prophet saying sins fall away when two Muslims clasp hands; Ibn al-Arabi cites a saying that handshaking removes rancor, with Malik and Sufyan differing on generalizing the Prophet's greeting of Jafar.
