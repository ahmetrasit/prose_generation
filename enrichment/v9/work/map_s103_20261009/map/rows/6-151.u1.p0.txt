## THE CURRENT MAP
6:151/q01 How does the invitation answer the surrounding dispute? [قُلْ تَعَالَوْا۟ أَتْلُ مَا حَرَّمَ رَبُّكُمْ عَلَيْكُمْ · links] (turns on: Whether the address is tied to the polytheists' invented bans or explicitly extends to everyone.)
  6:151/q01/p1 It replaces the polytheists' invented food and livestock prohibitions with God's certain revealed duties. — reasons: Muqatil excludes their camel taboos; Thalabi, Baghawi, Qurtubi and Ibn Kathir contrast revelation with conjecture; Biqai and Islahi identify God's exclusive authority and the Quraysh's claim to Abraham's religion.
  6:151/q01/p2 The verse announces a universal Islamic law to all hearers. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan explicitly include all humanity; Qurtubi says scholars must also explain the lawful and unlawful, citing 3:187.
  6:151/q01/p3 The invitation calls people upward from ignorance and imitation to knowledge and virtue. — reasons: Biqai transmits Zamakhshari's ethical reading; Qashani locates the vice in whim ruling what is lawful.
6:151/q02 Whom does the command to come address? [قُلْ تَعَالَوْا۟ · referent] (turns on: The immediate audience in context versus the scope of the message.)
  6:151/q02/p1 The Messenger addresses the polytheists in the immediate context. — reasons: Abu Hayyan cites the preceding fabricated bans; Ibn Kathir specifies idol worship, banning God's provision, and child-killing.
  6:151/q02/p2 It summons all people to hear the law. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan say Muhammad's message is universal; Abu Hayyan adds the same ruling applies even on the narrower immediate address.
6:151/q03 What does تَعَالَوْا۟ mean and suggest? [تَعَالَوْا۟ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the original spatial force remains rhetorically active.)
  6:151/q03/p1 It originally meant come upward from a lower place, then became a general invitation to approach. — reasons: Jishumi, Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Bursevi, Alusi and others derive it from elevation; Qurtubi notes singular and plural forms and 33:28; Tabatabai reports the derivation.
  6:151/q03/p2 Its upward imagery also invites listeners out of ignorance. — reasons: Biqai cites Zamakhshari; Alusi says this implication remains even after the word became common.
6:151/q04 What does أَتْلُ mean? [أَتْلُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the terms distinguish first from repeated speech or act from content.)
  6:151/q04/p1 It means recite or read in ordered sequence. — reasons: Jishumi allows reading or retelling; Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and Biqai stress the following of letters or utterances; Tabatabai equates tilawa and qira'a.
  6:151/q04/p2 Tilāwa and qirāʾa are the spoken act; matluw and maqruʾ are what is uttered or the original speech. — reasons: Mawardi prefers this distinction; Jishumi and Tabarsi compare a recitation with a retold narration.
  6:151/q04/p3 Tilāwa and qirāʾa are first utterances, while matluw and maqruʾ are repetitions. — reasons: Ali ibn Isa's proposed distinction is reported by Mawardi.
6:151/q05 How does أَتْلُ relate grammatically to the invitation? [تَعَالَوْا۟ أَتْلُ · grammar]
  6:151/q05/p1 The jussive verb answers the imperative invitation: if they come, he will recite. — reasons: Alusi states the conditional relation; Jishumi explains the deletion of the initial alif under jussive formation; Bursevi calls it the answer to come.
6:151/q06 What is the grammatical function of مَا حَرَّمَ رَبُّكُمْ? [أَتْلُ مَا حَرَّمَ رَبُّكُمْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether recitation governs mā directly, a following question, or a verbal noun.)
  6:151/q06/p1 Mā is chiefly a relative pronoun and object of atlu: recite what the Lord forbade. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya prefers this and calls Zajjaj's other construction strained; Samin prefers it, with an omitted resumptive pronoun; Baydawi also permits a relative reading.
  6:151/q06/p2 Mā is governed by ḥarrama, with recitation conveying a question about what the Lord forbade. — reasons: Zajjaj links this to 6:145; Ibn Atiyya objects that suspending atlu is strained, and Abu Hayyan and Samin say only verbs of thought permit suspension; Alusi defends it because recitation is a kind of saying.
  6:151/q06/p3 Mā is a verbal noun, 'the prohibition,' standing for the prohibited thing. — reasons: Samin explains that a prohibition itself cannot literally be recited; Baydawi allows the verbal noun sense.
  6:151/q06/p4 Mā introduces or is interpreted as an explanatory quotation of the prohibited matter. — reasons: Tabarsi records the explanatory possibility alongside the relative and object analyses.
6:151/q07 What does حَرَّمَ mean? [حَرَّمَ · meaning] (turns on: The narrow lexical ban versus an inclusive sense of firm prescription.)
  6:151/q07/p1 It fundamentally means to prevent or forbid, opposed to releasing or permitting. — reasons: Jishumi relates it to ḥajr and ḥaẓr; Tabatabai notes that forbidding can also carry the sense of commanding avoidance.
  6:151/q07/p2 It may denote a fixed and delineated divine prescription in this list. — reasons: Razi uses this wider sense to explain why positive duties occur under the introductory wording.
6:151/q08 Where does عَلَيْكُمْ attach? [حَرَّمَ رَبُّكُمْ عَلَيْكُمْ · grammar] (turns on: Which of the two verbs governs the prepositional phrase.)
  6:151/q08/p1 It attaches to the nearer ḥarrama, stressing what God has prohibited for the hearers. — reasons: Qurtubi identifies a Basran analysis; Abu Hayyan says most adopt it; Alusi prefers it for the appeal to the audience's Lordship and need to abstain; Samin notes competing governance.
  6:151/q08/p2 It attaches to atlu, a Kufan competing-governance analysis. — reasons: Qurtubi reports the Kufan view; Abu Hayyan attributes preference to Ibn al-Shajari, though his account of proximity differs from Qurtubi's.
6:151/q09 How is أَلَّا تُشْرِكُوا۟ constructed, and how do positive commands enter the list? [أَلَّا تُشْرِكُوا۟ بِهِۦ شَيْـًۭٔا وَبِٱلْوَٰلِدَيْنِ إِحْسَٰنًۭا · grammar] (turns on: The scope of ḥarrama, whether lā negates or prohibits, and whether a suppressed verb or clause supplies the positive duties.)
  6:151/q09/p1 An is explanatory and lā prohibitive, introducing a jussive prohibition; positive commands also imply bans on their opposites. — reasons: Farra favors jussive by the following prohibitions; Zamakhshari invokes implied bans on parental abuse, short measure, unjust speech and broken covenant; Samin defends him; Razi and E lmalili make the entailment explicit. Razi raises the apparent problem that duties appear under what God forbade. Razi objects that on an explanatory parse the coordinated parental kindness can appear forbidden.
  6:151/q09/p2 A pause after 'your Lord forbade' leaves 'upon you not to associate' as an independent exhortation or obligation. — reasons: Ibn al-Anbari and Tabarsi compare 5:105; Itqan defends direct address; Samin and Abu Hayyan call the break weak or distant; Tabatabai rejects imperative-noun ʿalaykum on contextual grounds.
  6:151/q09/p3 An is nominalizing with ordinary negative lā, in nominative or accusative apposition: the recited item is that they not associate. — reasons: Nahhas lists accusative apposition and nominative predicate; Ibn Atiyya admits both; Samin prefers the omitted subject 'the recited thing is that you not associate' over 'the forbidden thing'; Alusi sees semantic difficulty.
  6:151/q09/p4 Anlā is governed by an understood 'I recite the prohibition of shirk' or 'I enjoin you'. — reasons: Zajjaj, Wahidi and Baghawi allow both ellipses; the closing waṣṣākum supports the enjoin reading; Abu Hayyan objects that added ellipsis departs from the apparent wording.
  6:151/q09/p5 An is nominalizing; lā is extra, so the forbidden item is association itself. — reasons: Baghawi cites 7:12; Thalabi, Samin and Itqan report this; Zamakhshari and Abu Hayyan object that the ensuing positive commands and ordinary prohibitive lā resist it.
  6:151/q09/p6 An explains both prohibitions and commands through an omitted 'what He commanded'. — reasons: Abu Hayyan proposes the understood phrase; Ibn Kathir cites the closing testament and poetry; Imru al-Qays supplies an example of joining command and prohibition. Jishumi notes that the passage may detail recited commands rather than only forbidden acts.
  6:151/q09/p7 Anlā is governed by an omitted purpose lām: the recital prevents their association. — reasons: Zajjaj and Wahidi tie inventing bans to treating others as equal to God; Samin reports objections that this cannot account for following commands; Alusi rejects the strained construction.
  6:151/q09/p8 The verb tushrikū may be subjunctive after an or jussive under prohibitive lā. — reasons: Tabarsi and Wahidi note that later clauses can shift mood; Ibn Atiyya prefers the jussive because the prohibited act must remain in the recitation; Tabari adduces rajaz; Samin totals ten syntactic analyses: one genitive, three nominative, six accusative.
  6:151/q09/p9 An is explanatory, but the positive commands coordinate with the opening invitation and recitation. — reasons: Abu Hayyan calls this clear, avoiding inclusion of duties under the prohibited acts; Samin reports the same response.
  6:151/q09/p10 Taḥrīm can mean a fixed, clearly delineated divine prescription, embracing both bans and duties. — reasons: Razi gives this as a separate answer to the objection that the list contains filial kindness.
6:151/q10 What principle guides choosing among the grammatical analyses? [أَلَّا تُشْرِكُوا۟ · grammar]
  6:151/q10/p1 Prefer the likely, strong and eloquent construction over strained or anomalous parses. — reasons: Itqan advises listing plausible alternatives only where no reading clearly prevails; its separate example defends direct-address exhortation here as eloquent.
