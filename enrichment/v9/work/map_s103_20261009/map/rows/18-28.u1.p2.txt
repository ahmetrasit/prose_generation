18:28/q19 How is 'aghfalna qalbahu' read and parsed? [أَغْفَلْنَا قَلْبَهُۥ · readings] (turns on: Whether the heart is accusative object or nominative subject.)
  18:28/q19/p1 The common reading has God as subject and the heart as accusative object. — reasons: Samin identifies na as subject and qalbahu as object; Ibn Atiyya contrasts the alternate nominative reading.
  18:28/q19/p2 A variant opens the lam and raises qalbuhu, making the heart subject. — reasons: Reports name Amr ibn Faid, Musa al-Aswari, Amr ibn Ubayd and Abu Mijlaz. Ibn Jinni says God found or supposed him heedless; Zamakhshari says He found the heart heedless. Alusi takes remembrance as metonym for requital.
18:28/q20 What does 'he followed his desire' mean here? [وَٱتَّبَعَ هَوَىٰهُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether desire means general appetite, the seating demand, or polytheism.)
  18:28/q20/p1 He followed appetite and lower-self wishes against obedience, letting worldly concerns displace remembrance. — reasons: Suddi calls it appetite; Bursevi defines hawa first as what the self loves and then blameworthy desire against law. Razi contrasts poor devotion with chiefs' turn to creatures.
  18:28/q20/p2 The desire was a demand for privileged seating and separation from poor believers. — reasons: The chiefs' demand is the specific behavior; Alusi says their appetite explains why they must not be obeyed.
  18:28/q20/p3 The desire means polytheism. — reasons: Ibn Abbas's Umayya report pairs a heart sealed against monotheism with shirk as desire.
18:28/q21 What does 'his affair was furaṭ' mean? [وَكَانَ أَمْرُهُۥ فُرُطًۭا · meaning] (turns on: The root's senses of falling short, going ahead, excess, loss, remorse and moral opposition.)
  18:28/q21/p1 His affair was wasted, neglected, abandoned or brought to ruin. — reasons: Mujahid and Qatada speak of loss; Farra of neglected obedience; Layth of unattended business. Razi cites poetry and neglect of religion for the world. Tabari prefers waste/destruction through pride. Thalabi adds wasted days; Muqatil compares 6:38, and Yahya cites 39:56 and 6:31.
  18:28/q21/p2 His affair exceeded limits through boasting, extravagance or transgression. — reasons: Farra and Wahidi connect it with Uyayna's boast of leading Mudar and the Arabs; Ibn Abbas via Ata says he sought rank without piety. Abu Ubayd compares an outrunning horse and premature speech; Jishumi and Mawardi distinguish ifrat as excess from farat as falling short.
  18:28/q21/p3 His affair advanced past truth and sound judgment, leaving them behind. — reasons: Kashshaf, Baydawi and Samin compare a horse ahead of the herd; Ibn Zayd and Tabatabai speak of leaving truth behind.
  18:28/q21/p4 His affair was regret. — reasons: Abu Ubayda, Dawud and Ibn Qutayba are cited.
  18:28/q21/p5 His affair was destruction. — reasons: Khabbab through Suddi and other reports use this direct gloss.
  18:28/q21/p6 His affair was opposed to truth. — reasons: Ibn Zayd gives this gloss; Tabarsi applies it to abandoning faith and proof.
  18:28/q21/p7 His affair was prior evil or hasty rushing. — reasons: Abu Zayd derives prior evil from going ahead; Ibn Bahr cites man's haste.
  18:28/q21/p8 His affair was falsehood. — reasons: Thalabi and later transmitters list this gloss.
  18:28/q21/p9 His affair was narrow or constricted. — reasons: Mawardi reports this from Mujahid.
  18:28/q21/p10 His settled condition was injustice or aggression. — reasons: Ibn Ashur derives this from going ahead because evil rushes forward; kana marks an entrenched condition. Bursevi's lexicon includes injustice and aggression.
18:28/q22 Why must the demand to remove the poor be refused? [وَلَا تُطِعْ · interpretation] (turns on: The weight of believers' dignity and the likely outcome of conceding to elites.)
  18:28/q22/p1 The poor believers' devotion and dignity require their company; social rank does not measure worth. — reasons: Bursevi says a person rejecting faith because of poor company is hypocritical, and expulsion violates prophethood; religious leaders attend to inner states. He cites a hadith that God looks at hearts and deeds, a Hafiz couplet, and Abraham's selfless flock gift. Ibn Ashur criticizes elite fixation on appearances.
  18:28/q22/p2 The chiefs' uncertain or outward conversion cannot justify humiliating believers. — reasons: Alusi states the objection then answers it: exclusion could alienate hearts, induce apostasy among recent converts and deter future entrants, outweighing a few unbelievers' continued disbelief.
  18:28/q22/p3 The command blocks concessions to polytheists and tells the Prophet to attend to believers. — reasons: Ibn Ashur calls it a broad rule; Islahi links it to the cave story and sura's opening; Biqai says the cave sleepers foreshadow rescue and elevation over tyrants.
  18:28/q22/p4 Inwardly, do not defer to a veiled person governed by the lower self. — reasons: Alusi warns against humbling oneself for wealth, citing a hadith on losing two-thirds of religion; he and a poet describe rich patrons' painful reproach.
18:28/q23 How does this command connect with other verses and its sura? [* · links] (turns on: How the verse extends 6:52 and fits the surrounding cave passage.)
  18:28/q23/p1 It strengthens the parallel command at 6:52 not to expel people who call on God. — reasons: Razi says 6:52 forbids expulsion while this verse positively commands patient company; Alusi and Ibn Ashur call this more emphatic. Several sources defer their explanation or occasion to al-Anam.
  18:28/q23/p2 It starts a fresh passage after the preceding discussion and resumes the sura's opening after the cave story. — reasons: Razi treats the passage as independent; Islahi says the cave story showed divine help for those serving God's cause.
  18:28/q23/p3 The poor worshippers' remembrance is contrasted with elite heedlessness and the opponents' fate. — reasons: Razi calls remembrance light and distraction darkness; Biqai connects rescue to cave sleepers. The occasion reports mention 18:27 and 18:29; Ibn Kathir recalls 20:131.
18:28/q24 What merits are reported for sitting with worshippers or remembering God? [وَٱصْبِرْ نَفْسَكَ يَدْعُونَ رَبَّهُم · reports] (turns on: The gathering, promised benefit, reported numbers and transmission routes differ.)
  18:28/q24/p1 Morning remembrance or a preacher's gathering surpasses freeing four slaves; evening remembrance surpasses freeing eight descendants of Ishmael. — reasons: Suraqa's report gives dawn to sunrise and four; Anas values eight at twelve thousand each and corrects a four-person version to ninety-six thousand. Abu Umama and a Badr veteran report the four-person comparison for a preacher, transmitted through Kurdus.
  18:28/q24/p2 Morning and evening remembrance surpasses swords or freely given wealth. — reasons: A Prophetic report from Ibn Umar compares it with breaking swords in God's path.
  18:28/q24/p3 Poor emigrants receive complete light and enter Paradise before the wealthy. — reasons: Abu Said's reports vary between fifty thousand and five hundred years; one says the rich first undergo reckoning.
  18:28/q24/p4 A group gathered solely for God's remembrance is forgiven and its bad deeds changed to good. — reasons: Ibn Kathir says Ahmad alone transmits Anas's report; another says angels attend and a sinful associate shares forgiveness.
  18:28/q24/p5 The Prophet preferred sitting among Quran listeners and poor worshippers. — reasons: Reports describe poorly clothed emigrants listening to Quran and the Prophet identifying that gathering as the one commanded to him.
18:28/q25 What inward teaching is drawn from the company of the poor? [وَٱصْبِرْ نَفْسَكَ مَعَ ٱلَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ رَبَّهُم · inward] (turns on: The distinct spiritual benefits attributed to companionship and gaze.)
  18:28/q25/p1 Company with sincere, righteous poor people develops humility, contentment and certainty and leads toward Paradise. — reasons: Amr al-Makki calls it a life of Paradise; Abu Madyan urges humility, a low place and attentive silence. Alusi praises righteous Sufi poverty but condemns corrupt poverty; Ibn Umar reports praise of sitting humbly with the poor.
  18:28/q25/p2 The Prophet's companionship and gaze convey divine disclosure to the poor. — reasons: Qushayri calls his gaze a means toward God until afterlife vision; Alusi calls him a mirror and source of disclosure, while other companions benefit from the poor.
  18:28/q25/p3 Inwardly, the poor persevere in divine presence and must not be looked away from. — reasons: Ibn Ata describes self, heart and spirit there; Wasiti says their eyes never leave God.
