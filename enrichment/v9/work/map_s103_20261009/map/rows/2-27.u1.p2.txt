2:27/q24 What does fisq mean for the people described? [ٱلَّذِينَ · theology] (turns on: The general meaning of disobedience versus the specific status of a sinner.)
  2:27/q24/p1 Fisq is departure from obedience, and the people here are unbelievers. — reasons: Ibn Kathir compares the date leaving its skin and mouse leaving its hole and cites 13:19–25. Elmalili ties the judgment to combined breach, severance, and corruption.
  2:27/q24/p2 Fisq has degrees; only denial that sin is ugly amounts to unbelief. — reasons: Elmalili describes occasional acknowledged sin, habitual sin, and denial; Sunnis retain believer status before the third, Mu'tazilites say an intermediate rank, Kharijites call all three unbelief. Ibn Kathir cites the Sahih report on five harmful creatures including mice.
2:27/q25 What is the primordial covenant in Qashani's inward reading? [عَهْدَ ٱللَّهِ · inward]
  2:27/q25/p1 Innate knowledge of unity planted by the holy intellect, later obscured by bodily desire. — reasons: Qashani reports without grading that God drew Adam's descendants like particles. He reads God's hand as holy intellect and first spirit, Adam as universal rational soul, and offspring as individual potential souls. The primordial Yes is innate assent and proof appears when veils lift.
2:27/q26 What does Qashani's prophetic report say about aims? [وَيَقْطَعُونَ مَآ أَمَرَ ٱللَّهُ بِهِۦٓ أَن يُوصَلَ · reports]
  2:27/q26/p1 God loves elevated aims and hates base ones. — reasons: Qashani cites the Prophet without grading.
2:27/q27 How can divine will and human choice coexist? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether necessity relative to the complete cause negates choice relative to the agent.)
  2:27/q27/p1 God owns all existence and capacity, while humans choose and own their acts within His ownership. — reasons: Tabatabai distinguishes existence from moral attribution and complete from partial causes; a fully caused event is necessary, but possible relative to its human agent. Ali, al-Rida, and al-Sadiq reject both coercion and total delegation. Commands and recompense assume choice; planning and education assume human possibility.
  2:27/q27/p2 Compulsion makes deeds fixed by divine will and voids effective choice. — reasons: Tabatabai states the fatalist inference and rejects its effects on responsibility, justice, reward, and punishment. He rejects materialist determinism's confusion of complete and partial causes.
  2:27/q27/p3 Delegation makes humans independent creators outside divine will. — reasons: Tabatabai reports Mu'tazilites affirming choice but accepting the fatalist premise that divine will cancels it; he objects that God's ownership remains.
2:27/q28 How are sin and divine misguidance attributed? [* · theology]
  2:27/q28/p1 God creates existence; evil as deprivation and blame attach to human acts, while misguidance follows freely chosen evil. — reasons: Tabatabai cites 9:3, 39:62, 32:7, 4:79, 4:78, and 7:28. Al-Rida says God withholds help and seals hearts after persistent rejection; the Prophet and Ali distinguish divine gifts from blameworthy choices. Misfortune is evil relative to a lost blessing.
2:27/q29 What reality underlies deeds and final recompense? [ٱلْخَٰسِرُونَ · theology] (turns on: Whether the descriptions name real states or figures.)
  2:27/q29/p1 Deeds become embodied recompense, already present but veiled; spiritual labels name real states. — reasons: Tabatabai cites 50:22 and many recompense verses; embodied deeds and recompense are compatible accounts. Distinct effects reveal underlying realities, and a hidden happy or wretched life becomes manifest.
  2:27/q29/p2 Prior life and afterlife language is figurative. — reasons: Tabatabai reports most exegetes reading prior life as disposition and afterlife as metaphor; he says many verses resist this.
  2:27/q29/p3 Human life has a real prior stage, a worldly interval, and a later stage. — reasons: Tabatabai says each stage follows the one before it.
2:27/q30 How is the preceding ماذا parsed? [* · grammar] (turns on: Whether mā and dhā form one noun or a subject-predicate expression.)
  2:27/q30/p1 Mādhā is one accusative noun meaning 'what?'. — reasons: Zajjaj applies this to what God intended by the example.
  2:27/q30/p2 Mā is the subject and dhā a relative predicate. — reasons: Zajjaj reports this alternative analysis.
2:27/q31 How is the preceding ٱلْفَٰسِقِينَ governed? [* · grammar]
  2:27/q31/p1 It is accusative after an ellipsis: He misguides none by it except the fāsiqīn. — reasons: Zajjaj supplies the implied verb and exception construction.
2:27/q32 How does the following كَيْف function grammatically? [* · grammar] (turns on: The category assigned to kayfa and whether analogy licenses a jussive.)
  2:27/q32/p1 It is chiefly interrogative; conditional use is rare and its jussive is unattested. — reasons: Abu Hayyan rejects the Kufan and Qutrub analogical jussive.
  2:27/q32/p2 It may be an adverb or a non-adverb noun. — reasons: Abu Hayyan reports Sibawayh for the adverb and Akhfash and Sirafi for the noun; its answer's case follows the verb.
