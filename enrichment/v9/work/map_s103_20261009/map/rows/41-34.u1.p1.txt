41:34/q08 What are the scope and limits of the command to respond well? [ٱدْفَعْ بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ · law] (turns on: The extent to which personal pardon remains best when God's limits or greater harms are at stake, and whether the Prophet's handshaking of Jafar establishes a general practice.)
  41:34/q08/p1 It directs the Prophet and community toward patience and pardon during personal injury and preaching, while preserving God's limits and avoiding greater harm. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says the Prophet completed moral excellence and cites Aisha: he did not avenge himself except for God's inviolable limits; the community is encouraged and 42:40 promises reward. Study Quran says do not surrender to an enemy or accept falsehood, and avoid returning harm if that will not cause greater harm; it cites 13:27, 23:96, 28:54. Islahi says proportionate retaliation remains permitted under 16:125–126, but forgiveness better serves preaching and reform.
  41:34/q08/p2 Patience is appropriate while truth is being conveyed; after active destructive rejection, taking opponents to task can be better or necessary. — reasons: Islahi says the Meccan opponents urged to forgive were later punished by the Companions at God's direction after conclusive communication of truth. Ibn Ashur says greater excellence may require foregoing a particular act of clemency.
  41:34/q08/p3 Handshaking is an applicable social practice. — reasons: Ibn al-Arabi cites the saying that it removes rancor; Sufyan argues the Prophet's greeting of Jafar applies to the community, opposing Malik's restriction.
  41:34/q08/p4 The Prophet's greeting of Jafar was special, so handshaking should not be generalized from it. — reasons: Qurtubi transmits Malik's opposition and Sufyan's contrary reasoning in the same note.
41:34/q09 Was the command to respond well abrogated? [ٱدْفَعْ بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ · sciences] (turns on: Whether later fighting supersedes this imperative or the best response varies with the situation, while pardon remains morally commended.)
  41:34/q09/p1 The sword or fighting verse abrogated the instruction, although forbearance and good company remain recommended. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi reports exegetes citing 9:5; Qurtubi holds abrogation but preserves overlooking, forbearance, and good company as recommended.
  41:34/q09/p2 The instruction remains a conditional moral norm alongside permissible or necessary retaliation. — reasons: Islahi cites 16:125–126 for lawful proportional retaliation and better patience, then distinguishes the stage of conveying truth from active attacks. Ibn Ashur preserves action for God's limits; Study Quran allows avoiding greater harm.
41:34/q10 What result can the better response have on an enemy? [فَإِذَا ٱلَّذِى بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَهُۥ عَدَٰوَةٌۭ كَأَنَّهُۥ وَلِىٌّ حَمِيمٌۭ · interpretation] (turns on: How literally ka-annahu presents friendship: an actual change, an outward resemblance with uncertain sincerity, or a usual partial result that can deepen.)
  41:34/q10/p1 The enemy can become a close, affectionate ally or friend through kindness. — reasons: Muqatil says restraint and pardon can transform opposition; Tabari, Baghawi, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Ibn Kathir, Biqai, Bursevi, Tabatabai, and others affirm the social change. Qashani adds remorse and expulsion of Satan; Biqai pictures practical aid and compares hot water cleansing dirt. Jurjani adduces the Prophetic saying that hearts love their benefactors and cites this verse as a Quranic parallel. Islahi grounds the change in human responsiveness to persistent goodwill; Muhammad won sincere companions, though hardened hearts remained unmoved.
  41:34/q10/p2 The enemy ordinarily only appears like an intimate friend in outward behavior; true intimacy may remain absent. — reasons: Qushayri denies full sincerity; Ibn Atiyya explains the simile ka-annahu; Abu Hayyan and Alusi report him. Ibn Ashur says affection enters and hostility recedes but ordinarily falls short of full friendship. Alusi qualifies that this is only a usual minimum: hostility can sometimes disappear altogether, citing a poem.
  41:34/q10/p3 It quickly yields visible kindness and may win religious opponents into affectionate solidarity. — reasons: Tabrisi describes an enemy in religion resembling an ally in faith and kinship. Ibn Ashur treats the sudden-result clause as the command's practical benefit; Qashani lists quieted hostility, remorse, guidance, and becoming close.
41:34/q11 What do walī and ḥamīm mean here? [وَلِىٌّ حَمِيمٌۭ · meaning]
  41:34/q11/p1 Walī is an ally or supporter, and ḥamīm is a close relative or intimate friend. — reasons: Qatada says close ally or relative; Ikrima separates friend from near relative. Ibn Atiyya adds care and protection; Ibn Ashur says the two qualities jointly oppose enmity. Damghani gives close relative or intimate friend for ḥamīm.
41:34/q12 How does the sudden-result clause work grammatically? [فَإِذَا ٱلَّذِى بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَهُۥ عَدَٰوَةٌۭ كَأَنَّهُۥ وَلِىٌّ حَمِيمٌۭ · grammar] (turns on: Whether ka-annahu walī functions as a circumstantial clause or the predicate of the relative subject, and how idhā is governed.)
  41:34/q12/p1 The relative phrase is subject, sudden idhā is predicate, and ka-annahu walī is circumstantial. — reasons: Samin presents this analysis as making the enemy present in the state of an intimate friend.
  41:34/q12/p2 The relative phrase is subject and ka-annahu walī is its predicate; idhā attaches to resemblance or has no governor. — reasons: Samin reports the two subanalyses of sudden idhā.
41:34/q13 What does the sudden idhā convey? [فَإِذَا · rhetoric]
  41:34/q13/p1 It conveys the rapid appearance of kindness's effect on an enemy. — reasons: Ibn Ashur identifies the prefixed particle as suddenative and ties its force to the command's promised benefit.
41:34/q14 Why describe the enemy as 'the one between you and whom there is enmity'? [ٱلَّذِى بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَهُۥ عَدَٰوَةٌۭ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the expression's force lies in general eloquence or in its open range of hostilities; the readings can coexist.)
  41:34/q14/p1 The fuller relative expression is more eloquent than simply saying 'your enemy'. — reasons: Alusi calls it more expressive despite its length; Tabatabai reports a view that also calls it concise.
  41:34/q14/p2 The expression leaves enmity indefinite in kind, strength, and duration, implying stronger kindness for entrenched hostility. — reasons: Ibn Ashur draws this range from the relative construction.
41:34/q15 What occasion or person is said to underlie the verse? [ٱلَّذِى بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَهُۥ عَدَٰوَةٌۭ · reports] (turns on: Whether the verse's transformation fits Abu Sufyan's biography, especially whether his later closeness came from kindness or conversion; Abu Jahl is a separate reported addressee.)
  41:34/q15/p1 It concerned Abu Sufyan, whose hostility softened through the Prophet's marriage tie with Umm Habiba and later Islam. — reasons: Muqatil or Muqatil ibn Hayyan is transmitted; Jishumi names Ramlah, Umm Habiba; Baghawi and Bursevi add Islam and kinship; Alusi calls him later close to Sunnis and remarks on his grandson Yazid. Qurtubi prefers this report because the verse depicts a former enemy becoming intimate; Elmalılı cites him as an example.
  41:34/q15/p2 It concerned Abu Jahl's harm to Muhammad, for which the Prophet was told to show patience and pardon. — reasons: Muqatil gives Abu Jahl and adds seeking refuge from Satan; Mawardi and Qurtubi transmit this account. Qurtubi considers the Abu Sufyan account stronger.
  41:34/q15/p3 No sound occasion is established by the Abu Sufyan report; the verse concerns kindness toward religious opponents generally. — reasons: Jishumi says the verse does not support Muqatil's account and cites Abu Sufyan's hostile remarks and incitement against Abu Bakr as evidence he was no true ally. Ibn Ashur accepts later friendliness but says conversion, rather than this verse's kindness, explains the change; he distinguishes Umar and Hind as conversion examples.
41:34/q16 What further effects or rewards are attached to patient good conduct? [* · interpretation]
  41:34/q16/p1 It protects against Satan, humbles an adversary, and can make the offender ashamed. — reasons: Ibn Abbas is transmitted by Tabari, Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, and Kuranyolu; Razi and Kuranyolu say persistent patience shames offenders into leaving misconduct. Ali's counsel to Qanbar says silence pleases God and frustrates Satan.
  41:34/q16/p2 It earns esteem and afterlife reward and improves the success of preaching. — reasons: Bursevi contrasts the believer's restraint with opponents' evil; Kuranyolu's Razi says good merits worldly respect and reward; Islahi says the forgiving course benefits reform and Muhammad's character drew decent opponents into sincere companionship.
  41:34/q16/p3 It quiets hostility, brings remorse, expels Satan, and guides toward paradise. — reasons: Qashani says good establishes the responder in the other's heart and reflects divine mercy. He attributes to the Prophet an ungraded saying that the Creator, if visible, would appear as forbearance.
41:34/q17 What inward discipline is read from the verse? [* · inward]
  41:34/q17/p1 Serve God by enduring His servants for His sake, replacing base traits with patience, pardon, and generosity. — reasons: Qushayri says not to avenge oneself and links high ranks to hardship; al-Baqli, via Bursevi, stresses transforming blameworthy traits. Qashani traces good to the heart and evil to the lower self. Study Quran applies repelling to one's own impulses.
  41:34/q17/p2 The closer one is in divine service, the more serious even slight lapses become. — reasons: Ibn Ata, cited by Sul
