83:3/q08 Where and amid what practice was the warning revealed? [* · reports] (turns on: Whether reports placing the first verses among Medinan merchants or the Anṣār establish the occasion and location, or whether the Meccan and transit accounts better fit.)
  83:3/q08/p1 It was revealed in Medina amid widespread dishonest measuring, after which people corrected their practice. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās says Medinan measuring was especially corrupt and the revelation reformed people; Study Quran likewise reports that Madinans weighed honestly afterward. Asad says traditions cited by al-Ṭabarī, al-Baghawī and Ibn Kathīr place at least the opening four verses just after the Prophet arrived. Ibn ʿĀshūr describes an early Medinan Muslim warning and condemnation of polytheists in both Medina and Mecca. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says context suggests a Medinan opening. Iṣlāḥī challenges the report's setting, saying Meccan traders were at least as plausible and the Anṣār were agricultural.
  83:3/q08/p2 A Medinan man named Abū Juhayna with a full and a short measure occasioned the warning. — reasons: Al-Suddī says Abū Juhayna took with the full measure and gave with the short one; the same account calls this the first surah revealed in Medina.
  83:3/q08/p3 Dishonest measuring among the Anṣār occasioned the warning. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī reports this exegetical account but argues that Meccan merchants were no less likely cheats and that the passage's main point is justice, judgment and accountability.
  83:3/q08/p4 The warning concerned a man who kept separate buying and selling scales. — reasons: Study Quran attributes this report to Abū Hurayrah. Muqātil more broadly reports a pre-Islamic market practice using separate measures and scales to take extra and give short.
  83:3/q08/p5 The revelation came between Mecca and Medina to reform people before the Prophet arrived. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān records this as another account alongside the Medinan report.
  83:3/q08/p6 The surah is Meccan, and the trade context is at least as plausible among Meccan merchants. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī argues against limiting the occasion to the Anṣār, who farmed, when Meccans traded; even if Anṣār cheated, he says that does not make the practice especially theirs.
83:3/q09 What reported examples and warnings accompany this verse? [* · reports] (turns on: The sources preserve distinct reports and exempla, not competing versions of one event.)
  83:3/q09/p1 Short measure is linked to drought or crop failure among five communal consequences of wrongdoing. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās transmits a Prophetic warning pairing broken covenants with enemies, abandoning God's judgment with poverty, immorality with plague, short measure with drought, and withheld alms with lack of rain. Al-Qurṭubī says al-Bazzār gives the gist and Mālik also relates it from Ibn ʿUmar. Study Quran's Prophetic version has fornication followed by death and fraud by crop failure and famine; it states no grading.
  83:3/q09/p2 ʿIkrima swore that every measurer or weigher faces the Fire. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī adds that none gives the same amount they themselves demand. Al-Qurṭubī says ʿIkrima maintained this even when asked about his son.
  83:3/q09/p3 Ibn ʿUmar warned merchants that short-measurers stand at resurrection in sweat halfway up their ears. — reasons: Nāfiʿ reports Ibn ʿUmar telling merchants to fear God and give full measure and weight.
  83:3/q09/p4 A dying man with two measures saw them become mountains of fire when struck together. — reasons: Mālik ibn Dīnār tells the story of his neighbor.
  83:3/q09/p5 Cheating the scale blackens the perpetrator's face on Resurrection Day. — reasons: Al-Fuḍayl is the reported speaker.
  83:3/q09/p6 A Bedouin rebuked a ruler taking Muslims' property without measuring it. — reasons: The Bedouin challenged ʿAbd al-Malik ibn Marwān by comparing his conduct with the cheaters' threatened fate.
  83:3/q09/p7 ʿAlī required accurate weight first and any extra gift afterward. — reasons: ʿAbd Khayr says ʿAlī overturned a scale yielding excess saffron. Al-Aṣmaʿī's Bedouin saying places manliness outside measure rims and scale tongues; that saying is also attributed to ʿAlī.
  83:3/q09/p8 A deputy's dawn recitation prompted prayer against a trader who bought full and sold short. — reasons: Abū Hurayrah reports the Medinan deputy's recitation and his own prayer against the trader.
  83:3/q09/p9 Prayer resembles a measure: the one who fills it is repaid fully. — reasons: Ibn Masʿūd uses measuring as an analogy for completing prayer and God's full recompense.
83:3/q10 How does this warning connect to the surrounding surahs and judgment theme? [* · links] (turns on: The notes draw different connections: surah sequence and judgment, innate moral knowledge, or another verse on the balance.)
  83:3/q10/p1 It follows the preceding surah's account of the blessed, the wretched and the Great Day by naming a petty sin for judgment. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says the preceding surah discussed those fates and judgment, then this surah singles out cheating for negligible gain. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says this surah moves from warning cheaters of resurrection to the fates of wicked and righteous.
  83:3/q10/p2 It supplements al-Infiṭār by arguing for judgment from the moral distinction built into human nature. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says both surahs divide the righteous and defiant; al-Infiṭār reasons from God's attributes seen in the body, while this surah uses the human approval of justice and rejection of wrong. He refers to his treatment of 75:14–15.
  83:3/q10/p3 The prohibition against reducing the balance in 55:9 reinforces the charge against short-measurers. — reasons: The commentators reported by al-Wāḥidī compare the sellers' reduction of goods with that prohibition.
83:3/q11 What does the conditional form contribute? [وَإِذَا يُخْسِرُونَ · grammar]
  83:3/q11/p1 The present-tense response states a general or habitual consequence: whenever they give by measure or weight, they short others. — reasons: Al-Burhān says most present-tense conditional responses are general, giving cheating in measure, mocking and arrogance as examples. Al-Samīn identifies يُخْسِرُونَ as the answer to إذا. Al-Bursawī calls this their characteristic conduct.
83:3/q12 What inward reading is drawn from this warning? [* · inward]
  83:3/q12/p1 The true scale of justice weighs character and deeds; arrogance makes people discount others' virtues. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī extends cheating beyond literal measures to moral evaluation, linking arrogant love of superiority and depreciation of others to 3:188. Al-Jishumī states that only piety gives one person superiority over another.
