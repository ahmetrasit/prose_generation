## THE CURRENT MAP
83:1/q01 What does wayl signify here? [وَيْلٌۭ · meaning] (turns on: Whether wayl is the ordinary cry of ruin or punishment, a specific Hell valley, its pus, or the Fire; Ibn Qutayba rejects a literal valley gloss, while al-Rāghib treats it as an application rather than the dictionary sense.)
  83:1/q01/p1 Ruin, grief, reproof, calamity, or painful punishment is assigned to the defrauders. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj and al-Rāzī call it an expression uttered at calamity; al-Naḥḥās calls it reproof; Ibn ʿAṭiyya includes lasting misery. Al-Māwardī lists destruction, the harshest punishment, a war cry of ruin, and a possible origin in 'way to so-and-so.' Ibn Kaysān calls it a distressed cry, and al-Aṣmaʿī includes disgrace and regret. Biqāʿī extends the ruin through this life and the next; Elmalılı says Turkish 'vay hâline' weakens the force. Islāhī says it also curses and censures. Al-Rāghib and Elmalılı distinguish Hell as its figurative or established religious application from its dictionary sense.
  83:1/q01/p2 Wayl is a valley in Hell. — reasons: Muqātil depicts it as seventy years long with nested fiery branches, chambers, trees, worms, snakes, and scorpions. Abū Saʿīd's report says a disbeliever falls forty years; other reports place it between two mountains. Al-Ālūsī calls the ʿUthmān report's chain questionable and suspends judgment on whether the valley is a literal lexical name. Al-Farrāʾ distinguishes the valley report from ordinary wayl; Ibn Qutayba rejects the valley gloss as unsound. Al-Rāghib says it denotes the deserved Hell abode rather than the dictionary meaning.
  83:1/q01/p3 Wayl is the pus of Hell's inhabitants or a valley of that pus. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī describes a valley of Hell's pus; al-Māwardī attributes the pus gloss to Ibn Masʿūd; Elmalılı cites ʿAbd Allāh through Ibn Abī Ḥātim for a pus-filled valley.
  83:1/q01/p4 Wayl is the Fire itself. — reasons: Al-Māwardī reports this from ʿUmar, the client of ʿAfra.
83:1/q02 How is wayl constructed grammatically? [وَيْلٌۭ لِّلْمُطَفِّفِينَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the form is treated as an established nominative subject or an accusative verbal noun or imprecation in a hypothetical construction; annexation and verb derivation affect the preference.)
  83:1/q02/p1 Wayl is a nominative subject and li-l-muṭaffifīn its predicate, presenting the threat as established. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj prefers nominative because doom is fixed; Ibn ʿAṭiyya contrasts accusative wishing or awaiting. Al-Naḥḥās prefers nominative because derived verbs are exceptionally rare, as Muḥammad ibn al-Walīd illustrates with wāl, wāḥ, and wās. Al-Samīn says an indefinite curse or supplication can begin a sentence; al-Būrsawī and al-Ālūsī agree. Makkī prefers nominative for unannexed wayl. Al-Naḥḥās notes that with an omitted predicate one can use waylahu or wayḥahu.
  83:1/q02/p2 Outside the Qur'anic wording, accusative waylan is possible as a verbal noun or imprecation. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj says it would express God assigning doom but the written Qur'an cannot be altered. Al-Naḥḥās says grammarians favor accusative for a form derived from a verb, comparing buʾsan lahu; Makkī favors it when wayl is annexed or definite. Al-Samīn also allows the accusative.
83:1/q03 Who are the mutaffifīn and what does their shortchanging cover? [لِّلْمُطَفِّفِينَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the warning is confined to commercial measure or extends by analogy to any reciprocal due, worship, self-scrutiny, or the inward scale.)
  83:1/q03/p1 They give others less than their due in measure or weight, often while demanding full measure for themselves. — reasons: Abū ʿUbayda, al-Mubarrad, Ibn Qutayba and many commentators give the commercial sense; al-Ṭabarī stresses withheld rights. Al-Rāzī says the small fraud is covert whereas a large one is obvious; al-Būrsawī calls it secret buyer fraud. Elmalılı and Asad appeal to the next two verses for the take-full/give-less contrast. Al-Nasafī makes the threat their predicate. Qatādah urges giving as one wishes to receive.
  83:1/q03/p2 The principle reaches every owed right, word, deed, or social relation. — reasons: Abū Ḥafṣ and al-Qushayrī describe taking one's rights without granting others'; al-Qushayrī adds hiding others' faults, overlooking one's own, and treating a Muslim as oneself. Some scholars and Mālik say every matter can be fulfilled or diminished; Ibn ʿAṭiyya accepts context-dependent excess as another form of taṭfīf. Wahb includes withheld reciprocal recompense; Asad includes moral relations and possessions; Study Quran includes anything God ordains.
  83:1/q03/p3 It includes deficient prayer or worship, including public devotion withheld in private. — reasons: Salmān or Sulaymān compares prayer with a measure repaid in kind; Sufi interpreters and Abū ʿUthmān apply the verse to worship shown to people but neglected in private. Ibn Masʿūd is named in a later version.
  83:1/q03/p4 It includes scrutinizing others' faults while ignoring one's own. — reasons: Tustarī cites 2:44 about urging good while forgetting oneself; Abū Ṣāliḥ Ḥamdūn, Abū Ḥafṣ, al-Qushayrī and Study Quran repeat the reciprocal fault pattern. Tustarī adds that the ignorant expose others' slips and that disclosing a learned secret is accursed; the revelation to David says private sin and public repentance should not expose a servant.
  83:1/q03/p5 On the inward scale of justice, the warning concerns a person's character and deeds. — reasons: Qāshānī says the true scale measures character and deeds, beyond physical trade.
83:1/q04 Why are they called mutaffifīn? [لِّلْمُطَفِّفِينَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the term is explained by the smallness of the missing amount, the brim of a nearly full measure, stealth, or another proposed root.)
  83:1/q04/p1 The name points to the tiny amount removed or withheld. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī, al-Zajjāj, Abū Isḥāq and others derive it from ṭafīf, a slight thing. Al-Rāzī says a large fraud would be noticed; al-Ālūsī says the intensive form marks repeated acts, not large individual amounts. Saʿdī al-Muftī likewise reads habitual action, though allowing a causative form. Elmalılı prefers this derivation. Biqāʿī cites the saying that Adam's children are only a slight measure apart.
  83:1/q04/p2 It comes from ṭaff or ṭafāf, the edge or nearly full rim of a measure. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj and others derive it from the edge; al-Thaʿlabī cites ṭifāf for an almost-full vessel and ka-ṭaff al-ṣāʿ for a vessel left short. Lexicographers say a measurer stops at that level; Biqāʿī suggests removing what rises above the top and cites Ibn ʿUmar's horse nearly reaching the Banū Zurayq mosque to illustrate nearness to a limit. Elmalılı notes usages for a filled measure, a leveled remainder, or its top.
  83:1/q04/p3 It describes a secret theft of a small amount. — reasons: Al-Jishumī reports this as a distinct account and calls the shortfall subtle; al-Būrsawī also stresses concealment.
  83:1/q04/p4 Other proposed roots refer to taking what is close at hand or rationing. — reasons: Biqāʿī lists these alongside reducing a petty amount and says the verse suits a usually tiny theft.
83:1/q05 What event occasioned this warning? [* · reports] (turns on: Which report supplies the immediate setting and whether the reported Anṣār setting fits the surah's provenance and its moral argument.)
  83:1/q05/p1 Medinan traders' dishonest measuring prompted the warning when the Prophet arrived, and they then improved. — reasons: Muqātil says the Prophet warned traders and God confirmed him by revelation. Al-Farrāʾ and a second Ibn ʿAbbās report specify buyers taking extra and sellers giving less; Ibn ʿAbbās traditions describe Medina's people as the worst measurers and later exceptionally accurate. Al-Suyūṭī calls one chain sound. ʿAbd Allāh tells those praising Medinan measures that the warning of standing before God explains their fairness. Islāhī reports the Anṣār version but objects that Meccan traders were at least as plausible and the surah was Meccan.
  83:1/q05/p2 The warning addressed Abū Juhayna, who used separate measures for buying and selling. — reasons: Al-Suddī names him; Abū Hurayra calls him his uncle, and al-Qurṭubī also gives the name ʿAmr. Versions vary whether the larger measure is described as used for taking or buying and the smaller for giving or selling. Study Quran mentions separate scales.
  83:1/q05/p3 The Prophet recited the warning to Medinan market traders engaged in deceptive, risky sales. — reasons: Al-Quraẓī or al-Qurṭubī names mulāmasa, munābadha, and mukhāṭara, likening them to chance transactions; al-Rāzī links the recitation to a report of five public wrongs.
  83:1/q05/p4 The specific Anṣār setting is doubtful; the verse can address Meccan commercial injustice. — reasons: Islāhī argues the surah is Meccan, Meccans traded while Anṣār farmed, and dishonest trade was at least as plausible in Mecca. He centers the verse's argument on innate justice and final accountability.
83:1/q06 Where and when was this surah or its opening revealed? [* · sciences] (turns on: Conflicting Meccan, Medinan, and mixed reports, plus whether the early Medinan trade occasion applies to the entire surah or only its opening.)
  83:1/q06/p1 It was revealed in Medina, especially at the Prophet's arrival; some call it the first revelation there. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan and al-Ḍaḥḥāk place it in Medina; al-Farrāʾ and Ibn ʿAbbās place its warning at arrival. One Ibn ʿAbbās report calls it the first surah revealed there; Abū Ḥayyān records the same claim. The Medinan measurement reports support an early Medinan opening.
