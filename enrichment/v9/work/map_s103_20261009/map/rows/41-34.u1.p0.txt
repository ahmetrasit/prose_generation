## THE CURRENT MAP
41:34/q01 What do the good deed and the evil deed denote? [ٱلْحَسَنَةُ ٱلسَّيِّئَةُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the nouns denote general moral classes, the religious opposition in context, particular ethical traits, or an inward contrast; some reported pairings differ in wording and attribution.)
  41:34/q01/p1 Good and evil conduct or deeds generally, differing in worth, effects, degree, and outcome. — reasons: Tabari relates believers' obedience to deniers who drown out the Quran (41:26). Baydawi names recompense and outcome; Biqai adds intention and effort; Elmalılı adds internal degrees; Ibn Ashur includes invitation and pardon; Islahi stresses preaching and the Hereafter. Alusi doubts that narrow reported pairings are intended.
  41:34/q01/p2 Faith or Islam contrasts with unbelief, shirk, or disobedience. — reasons: Tabari states the final contrast as faith and obedience versus association and disobedience; Ibn Abbas is transmitted for faith versus shirk. Tabrisi reports Islam versus unbelief among several alternatives.
  41:34/q01/p3 The good is saying 'There is no god but God'; the evil is polytheism. — reasons: Ibn Abbas is named by Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, and Alusi for this precise declaration.
  41:34/q01/p4 The good and evil are rival invitations to monotheism and associationism. — reasons: Al-Kalbi is named by Abu Hayyan and Alusi; the contrast concerns calls addressed to others.
  41:34/q01/p5 Forbearance is good and obscenity is evil. — reasons: Al-Dahhak is cited repeatedly; Qurtubi instead transmits knowledge versus obscenity from him, a distinct version.
  41:34/q01/p6 Knowledge is good and obscenity is evil. — reasons: Qurtubi attributes this version to al-Dahhak.
  41:34/q01/p7 Love of the Prophet or his family is good; hostility toward them is evil. — reasons: Ali is the reported authority; Abu Hayyan and Alusi include the Messenger himself alongside his family.
  41:34/q01/p8 Gentleness or tact is good and harshness is evil. — reasons: Ibn Isa says gentle accommodation; Qurtubi lists tact; Abu Hayyan lists leniency. Tabrisi reports this among other contrasting traits.
  41:34/q01/p9 Patience is good and aversion, anger, or flight is evil. — reasons: Mawardi pairs patience with aversion; Ibn al-Jawzi transmits flight versus patience; other reports give patience versus anger. These are related examples but the flight report reverses the cited order.
  41:34/q01/p10 Pardon is good and retaliation or injury is evil. — reasons: Ibn Umayr names forgiveness against retaliation; Qurtubi and Tabrisi include this among illustrative pairs.
  41:34/q01/p11 Good character contrasts with bad character and blameworthy traits. — reasons: Al-Baqli, reported by Bursevi, calls for replacing base traits with praiseworthy ones, especially forbearance.
  41:34/q01/p12 Taqiyya is good and disclosure is evil. — reasons: Tabrisi reports this from Abu Abd Allah.
  41:34/q01/p13 Good arises from the heart and leads to paradise and angels; evil arises from the self and leads to hell and devils. — reasons: Qashani gives the inward origins and destinations of each.
41:34/q02 What is the function of the second lā in the contrast? [وَلَا ٱلسَّيِّئَةُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether lā is syntactically added for emphasis, supplies a reciprocal negation, or distinguishes instances within each generic noun; Samin disputes an objection that conflates the last with the first.)
  41:34/q02/p1 It is an extra particle reinforcing the denial that good and evil are equal. — reasons: Akhfash compares 57:29 and 75:1; Tabari reports Basran grammarians and cites 75:2 while recording an objection about 75:1; Zajjaj, al-Farra, and later commentators adopt or report emphasis. Samin notes that istawā needs two complements and compares 35:21 and 40:58. Alusi compares 35:21; Samarrai notes 35:19–22 and adds differences within each class.
  41:34/q02/p2 It meaningfully negates the reciprocal comparison: neither good equals evil nor evil equals good. — reasons: Akhfash illustrates with Abdullah and Zayd; Tabari says each side is unequal to the other; Ibn Atiyya and Ibn Ashur understand an ellipsis in both directions. Jishumi reports this as an alternative to emphasis.
  41:34/q02/p3 It meaningfully denies equality within the classes of good deeds and evil deeds, whose instances vary in degree. — reasons: Zamakhshari is reported by Samin; Bursevi cites al-Kashshaf and ranges from removing harm to greater good and from minor to major sin; Alusi says this yields answering the worse with the best; Elmalılı and Samarrai also see internal variation. Samin rejects the objection that this analysis still treats lā as extra.
41:34/q03 Why use the abstract nouns for good and evil? [ٱلْحَسَنَةُ ٱلسَّيِّئَةُ · rhetoric]
  41:34/q03/p1 The nouns portray goodness and evil at their full extent and lead into ethical counsel. — reasons: Ibn Ashur contrasts the abstract nouns with nouns naming the doers.
41:34/q04 How does the opening contrast connect to the surrounding passage? [وَلَا تَسْتَوِى ٱلْحَسَنَةُ وَلَا ٱلسَّيِّئَةُ · links] (turns on: Whether 41:34 is attached chiefly to 41:33's praise or reaches back to the opponents' obstruction in 41:26; Ibn Ashur records both connections.)
  41:34/q04/p1 It continues the praise of the caller to God in 41:33 by contrasting the believer's good state and the disbeliever's evil. — reasons: Ibn Ashur compares 40:58; Tabrisi contrasts the opponents' disruption in 41:26 with Muhammad's call in 41:33, saying his practice confirms his message. Ibn Ashur also sees 41:30's divine promise exposing the gulf from the deniers.
  41:34/q04/p2 It resumes the disbelievers' obstruction of Quran recitation and consoles the Prophet, preparing his gentle response. — reasons: Ibn Ashur points to 41:26 and 41:5; Asad identifies scurrilous objections and hostile criticism and compares 13:22; Kuranyolu's Razi cites the closed hearts and noise during recitation.
  41:34/q04/p3 It gives the practical response to hostility that may confront a caller to God. — reasons: Abu Hayyan says to convey good gently and tactfully; Tabatabai says it follows the praise of inviting to God and teaches the best way to make an impact. Elmalılı makes the best call with righteous action the remedy; Islahi relates conduct to preaching's goals.
  41:34/q04/p4 The imperative is either the consequence of the contrast or the conclusion of the preceding argument; it is independent in either case. — reasons: Ibn Ashur states the two possible links and preserves its independent sentence status.
41:34/q05 What should one do when told to repel with what is best? [ٱدْفَعْ بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the command is read as a broad practice or a particular form of response; the greeting reports are treated by Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan as a beginning of the broader practice.)
  41:34/q05/p1 Answer injury, insult, anger, and ignorance with patience, forbearance, pardon, and kindness. — reasons: Ibn Abbas gives patience when angry, forbearance toward ignorance, pardon when wronged; Tabari compares 23:96. Razi says repeated restraint shames offenders; Umar says answer another's disobedience to God with one's own obedience. Abu Firas's poem praises continual pardon. Qurtubi transmits Ali's advice to Qanbar and two poems on silence frustrating a fool, plus Mahmud al-Warraq's advice by relative standing. Bursevi cites the Prophet's practice of ties, pardon, and good treatment; Ibn Ashur notes his Quranic character and Torah description. Study Quran compares 23:96 and 29:46 and cites hadith on ties, giving, pardon, and no reciprocal harm.
  41:34/q05/p2 Greet the opponent with peace upon meeting. — reasons: Mujahid and Ata are repeatedly named. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan say greeting starts the wider virtue but does not exhaust it; Ata also speaks of answering harm with peace.
  41:34/q05/p3 Meet falsehood or error with truth. — reasons: Tabrisi includes this with forbearance and pardon; Tabatabai does likewise; Study Quran attributes it to Ṭs.
  41:34/q05/p4 Return a good deed, praise, or material generosity to the offender, beyond mere forgiveness. — reasons: Nasafi contrasts praise with insult and a good act with a bad act; Biqai, Bursevi, Alusi, Elmalılı, and Kuranyolu's Zamakhshari distinguish benevolence from pardon. Bursevi's Greatest Imam anecdote says praise induced an abuser to praise him. Ibn Atiyya includes generosity in paying and collecting debts.
  41:34/q05/p5 Turn away from the offender's harm or refrain from replying. — reasons: Mujahid recommends turning away; Ali, as quoted by Qurtubi, recommends silence to Qanbar, and Qurtubi adduces poems that silence most frustrates an insulting fool.
  41:34/q05/p6 Shake hands affectionately and sincerely when meeting. — reasons: Ibn al-Arabi applies the verse this way, citing a report that handshaking removes rancor; al-Bara's Prophetic report says two Muslims clasping hands have sins fall away.
  41:34/q05/p7 Repel one's own impulses toward vice by practicing virtue. — reasons: Study Quran explicitly gives this inward application; Biqai includes harm from oneself as well as from others; Qashani contrasts the heart with the lower self and warns that evil for evil grows.
41:34/q06 What object is omitted after 'repel'? [ٱدْفَعْ · grammar]
  41:34/q06/p1 The omitted object is evil, supplied by the preceding contrast. — reasons: Ibn Ashur compares 13:22 and 23:96; Study Quran notes the absence of an expressed object before listing anger, error, ignorance, and wrongdoing as possible instances.
41:34/q07 What does 'that which is best' add to the command? [بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ · rhetoric]
  41:34/q07/p1 It urges the finest available good response, greater than simple pardon, and intensifies the command. — reasons: Baydawi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi, and Alusi say the implied question and answer stress the matter; one who can do the best can readily do a lesser good. Nasafi comp
