## THE CURRENT MAP
6:31/q01 Who are the people declared losers? [ٱلَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا۟ · referent]
  6:31/q01/p1 Deniers of resurrection and the afterlife, especially the previously mentioned worldly life claimants; Quraysh and their followers are included. — reasons: Al-Tabari specifies resurrection, judgment, Paradise and Hell. Ibn ʿĀshūr points to 6:29 and 6:27 and says explicit naming develops their fate; Al-Ālūsī says the relative clause shows persistent denial caused their loss. Muqātil and Study Quran identify Quraysh/worldly life deniers.
6:31/q02 What reports identify a particular denier or occasion for this verse? [ٱلَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا۟ · reports] (turns on: The reports name different people or give different statements.)
  6:31/q02/p1 Abū Jahl acknowledged Muhammad's truth but refused to follow a man from Banū ʿAbd Manāf. — reasons: Abū Yazīd al-Madanī reports a handshake and this statement before the verse was revealed.
  6:31/q02/p2 Abū Jahl said he did not call Muhammad a liar but rejected the message. — reasons: Nājiya ibn Kaʿb reports the verse followed this admission.
  6:31/q02/p3 Al-Ḥārith ibn ʿĀmir privately believed Muhammad truthful but feared Arab retaliation and loss of safety if he followed him. — reasons: Maqātil identifies him.
6:31/q03 What loss have the deniers incurred, and why? [خَسِرَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the verse is explained as the failed faith transaction, squandered human capacities, or the word's basic lexical sense.)
  6:31/q03/p1 They forfeit afterlife reward and gain punishment by exchanging faith for unbelief. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī likens faith and Paradise to the capital of a ruined transaction; Wāḥidī's authorities say the worst loss is one's soul, like losing a merchant's capital; Ibn ʿĀshūr specifies afterlife rather than worldly deprivation. Al-Rāzī counts lost reward and incurred punishment as two losses. Biqāʿī says every attainable reward is lost; Bayḍāwī adds lost blessing and enduring punishment; Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls it a failed bargain.
  6:31/q03/p2 They squander faculties meant for lasting knowledge and virtue on passing pleasure, and lack provisions for the afterlife. — reasons: Al-Rāzī names bodily and intellectual faculties as capital, transient pleasure as imagined profit, and alienation after death from both spiritual preparation and worldly attachments; Biqāʿī adds outer senses, reason and prophetic miracles. Study Quran relays Rāzī's investment analogy.
  6:31/q03/p3 Loss fundamentally means decrease, though some derive it from destruction. — reasons: Al-Jishumī reports both derivations.
6:31/q04 What does 'meeting God' mean here? [بِلِقَآءِ ٱللَّهِ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether meeting is understood as resurrection and recompense, an implied object such as reward, literal vision, or a representational encounter.)
  6:31/q04/p1 Resurrection, return to God, and His judgment and recompense. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās includes reward and punishment and cites 2:46; Ibn ʿAṭiyya includes God's power and the consequences of deeds; Rāzī says benefit and harm then lie with God alone. Kashshāf likens standing before the Lord to a guilty servant detained and rebuked. Qaffāl cites the warning concerning a false oath for property. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī observes the passage's progression from resurrection and gathering to meeting.
  6:31/q04/p2 Meeting God's promised reward and punishment, or the recompense of one's deeds, is figuratively called meeting God. — reasons: Wāḥidī cites ordinary speech about a dead person meeting his deeds; Ṭabrisī attributes the promise gloss to Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Ḥasan; al-Ālūsī notes an implicit complement and possessive magnification in Abū Ḥayyān.
  6:31/q04/p3 The phrase retains actual meeting or vision of God. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī rejects extending contextual figurative uses to this verse and argues denial of God and His vision implies denial of existence; Nasafī notes denying resurrection also denies vision. Kashshāf reports full recognition of Him as another alternative.
  6:31/q04/p4 It pictures the afterlife's unimpeded manifestation of God's approval and wrath, like servants meeting their lord after absence. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr contrasts God's delayed worldly warning and choice with immediate afterlife consequences (18:49), and cites the hadith on loving to meet God and 40:15.
  6:31/q04/p5 Meeting primarily means encounter, with extended uses for sight, contemporaneity, calamity and battle. — reasons: Al-Jishumī gives the lexical range.
6:31/q05 What does 'until' terminate or connect? [حَتَّىٰٓ · grammar] (turns on: The syntactic reach of ḥattā and whether loss can end.)
  6:31/q05/p1 Their denial continues until the Hour, while their loss has no endpoint. — reasons: Wāḥidī, Zamakhsharī, Rāzī, Bayḍāwī and others say the governing verb is 'denied'; Ṭabrisī adds that regret then comes too late. Ibn ʿĀshūr rejects ending loss because its deprivation occurs at resurrection and not all deniers lose worldly goods.
  6:31/q05/p2 The ordinary loss lasts until Judgment, when a worse calamity eclipses it. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī analogizes Satan's curse 'until Judgment' at 38:78. Ibn ʿĀshūr reports and rejects commentators who end the loss there.
  6:31/q05/p3 Before 'when', ḥattā is initial and indicates cause rather than an endpoint. — reasons: Ibn al-Ḥājib says the sense is effectively 'when the Hour comes'.
6:31/q06 What event is 'the Hour' here? [ٱلسَّاعَةُ · referent] (turns on: Whether the lament and burden occur at actual resurrection, its onset, or death.)
  6:31/q06/p1 The resurrection, gathering and universal judgment. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī specifies raising the dead from graves; Abū Ḥayyān argues simultaneous bearing of burdens better fits actual resurrection and says those in barzakh experience its interval as a day. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it the conventional name for resurrection and gathering.
  6:31/q06/p2 The moment resurrection begins, marked by sudden onset. — reasons: Wāḥidī prefers this because it accords with baghtatan; Elmalılı reports the onset time is known only to God.
  6:31/q06/p3 Death or one's personal Hour, the beginning of afterlife states. — reasons: Zamakhsharī and Rāzī cite the saying that whoever dies has had his resurrection/Hour; Biqāʿī and Elmalılı call death the beginning of transition. Al-Ālūsī reports death as the referent. Abū Ḥayyān argues actual resurrection fits better.
  6:31/q06/p4 The Hour is so close after death that it strikes like an immediate surprise. — reasons: Kashshāf offers this alternative.
6:31/q07 Why is it called 'the Hour'? [ٱلسَّاعَةُ · rhetoric] (turns on: The proposed relation of the name to speed, sudden onset, brevity, or fame.)
  6:31/q07/p1 Reckoning and recompense occur swiftly. — reasons: Wāḥidī, Qurṭubī, Samīn and Elmalılı explain the name through rapid accounting; al-Ālūsī says sāʿa first denotes a short interval.
  6:31/q07/p2 It arrives at an unexpected, unknown instant. — reasons: Wāḥidī prefers sudden onset because baghtatan follows; Elmalılı says only God knows its hour.
  6:31/q07/p3 Its duration is brief compared with eternity. — reasons: Nasafī compares the intervening interval with the eternal sequel; al-Ālūsī also reports this.
  6:31/q07/p4 The definite article identifies the known, uniquely famous Hour. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya says it is established in minds and implied by meeting God; Samīn compares 'the star' and al-Thurayyā. Al-Ṭabarī calls it the known Hour.
6:31/q08 What does 'suddenly' convey? [بَغْتَةًۭ · meaning]
  6:31/q08/p1 An unforeseen onset without knowing its time or receiving warning. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj cites Arab usage and a poem of unforeseen death; Ibn ʿAbbās says God alone knows the Hour; Samīn stresses mere speed of an expected event is insufficient and cites poetry. Biqāʿī says its precise time is unknowable even generally.
6:31/q09 How is 'baghtatan' parsed? [بَغْتَةًۭ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the accusative describes a state or supplies/emphasizes a verbal action.)
  6:31/q09/p1 An accusative circumstantial verbal noun describing the Hour or those it overtakes. — reasons: Naḥḥās and Ibn ʿAṭiyya cite Sībawayh's nonproductive parallel qataltuhu ṣabran; Naḥḥās adds a poetic laʾyan bi-laʾy. Abū Ḥayyān allows either subject or object as its possessor.
  6:31/q09/p2 An absolute or cognate accusative, possibly supplied by an implied verb. — reasons: Kashshāf and Nasafī allow a verbal noun of surprise; Abū Ḥayyān and Samīn distinguish an omitted verb from the root or an omitted 'they came'; al-Ālūsī accepts these alternatives.
6:31/q10 What reports describe the Hour's arrival? [جَآءَتْهُمُ ٱلسَّاعَةُ بَغْتَةًۭ · reports] (turns on: The reports describe different preceding circumstances.)
  6:31/q10/p1 It can arrive while someone eats or two people trade, leaving their acts unfinished. — reasons: Ibn Abī Ḥātim transmits the Prophetic report through Abū Hurayra.
  6:31/q10/p2 A caller will announce the Hour three times before it arrives. — reasons: Ibn Abī Ḥātim reports ʿIkrima.
6:31/q11 How does 'jāʾa' here relate to Qur'anic use of 'atā'? [جَآءَتْهُمُ · links]
  6:31/q11/p1 Its force depends on the full verse and context; the supplied comparison is not completed. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī contrasts the Hour here and 6:40–41; his other discussion describes Ghāfir 40:82–85, where peoples mocked messengers and believed only after seeing punishment, too late to help. He cautions against isolating either verb.
6:31/q12 What does 'ḥasra' mean? [حَسْرَتَنَا · meaning]
  6:31/q12/p1 Intense regret or grief for something lost. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās glosses it as regret. Baghawī compares a traveler whose mount fails; al-Rāghib derives it from exposure or exhausted strength. Ṭabrisī says remorse exhausts the regretful.
6:31/q13 What does calling out to 'our regret' do? [يَٰحَسْرَتَنَا · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the figurative address is described as summoning regret or alerting the hearers.)
  6:31/q13/p1 It figuratively summons regret
