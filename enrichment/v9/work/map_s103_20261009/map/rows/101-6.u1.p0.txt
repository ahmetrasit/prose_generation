101:6/q01 How does man function grammatically? [مَن · grammar]
  101:6/q01/p1 It is the subject, with the following clause as predicate. — reasons: Al-Nahhas states the subject-predicate analysis.
101:6/q02 Does the singular man describe one person or a group? [مَن · grammar]
  101:6/q02/p1 The singular relative denotes a plural group. — reasons: Islahi makes the collective reference explicit.
101:6/q03 What makes these balances heavy? [ثَقُلَتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether weight describes predominating good deeds, adherence to truth, intrinsic worth and approval, divine determination, or inward perfections.)
  101:6/q03/p1 The person's good deeds outweigh evil deeds or otherwise prevail. — reasons: Muqatil, al-Tabari, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, al-Zamakhshari, al-Baydawi, Ibn Kathir, Elmalili, Islahi, and Study Quran explain the weight as prevailing good. Al-Tabrisi stresses abundance; al-Biqa'i says the many kinds of good deeds outweigh falsehood. Ibn Atiyya adds faith and says a believer's balance is never disastrously light. Ibn Abbas describes beautiful obedient deeds outweighing ugly unbelieving deeds.
  101:6/q03/p2 Adherence to truth gives them weight; falsehood is light. — reasons: Abu Bakr's counsel to Umar says a balance filled only with truth or good deeds rightly grows heavy, whereas one filled with falsehood or sins is light. Muqatil agrees; al-Nasafi and al-Bursevi adopt the point. Islahi says only truth, meaning good deeds, receives weight and invokes 7:8-9.
  101:6/q03/p3 Heaviness is the weight and worth of deeds before God, signifying His approval. — reasons: Al-Farra's comparison of weights supports valued deeds; Qutb speaks of values counted by God. Ibn Ashur calls it metonymy for approval: Arabic weight denotes merit, honor and sound judgment, while 18:105 uses lack of weight for lack of merit. Kur'an Yolu links abundant good to approval.
  101:6/q03/p4 God makes the scales heavy or light as He wills under pre-eternal decree. — reasons: Al-Wasiti cites a report that the scales are in God's hand and He raises or lowers people.
  101:6/q03/p5 In the inward reading, real sciences, virtues and perfections carry the weight. — reasons: Al-Qashani identifies the weight with real sciences, psychic virtues and the perfections of heart and spirit; enduring righteous deeds ascend, whereas perishable pleasures have no weight.
101:6/q04 How does the verse's wording convey its favorable sense? [ثَقُلَتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · rhetoric]
  101:6/q04/p1 Heaviness itself implies worthwhile righteous deeds, so the deeds need not be named. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says Arabic weight idiom already implies merit and approval; the Quranic opposite in 18:105 says some are given no weight. The omitted good deeds are understood.
101:6/q05 What does mawazin mean here? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · meaning] (turns on: Whether mawazin names an actual scale, the valued things weighed, or a figure for judgment and proof.)
  101:6/q05/p1 It can be the plural of mawzun: weighed deeds of value and standing before God. — reasons: Al-Farra compares ordinary Arabic talk about matching a coin's weight; al-Nahhas, al-Razi, Elmalili and al-Alusi transmit this explanation. Al-Zamakhshari, al-Mawardi, al-Nasafi and al-Bursevi give both morphological possibilities. Kur'an Yolu prefers the deeds rendering; Elmalili says it needs no figurative account of the plural.
  101:6/q05/p2 It is the plural of mizan, referring to balances or scales. — reasons: Al-Mawardi and al-Zamakhshari set this beside the alternative plural of mawzun. Al-Alusi explains original mawzan and the change of quiescent waw to ya after kasra; Elmalili also gives the derivation. Study Quran distinguishes balance as revealed just order in 6:152, 7:85, 11:84-85, 42:17, 55:7-9, 57:25 from eschatological scales in 21:47.
  101:6/q05/p3 The balances are a figure for reckoning or equitable judgment. — reasons: Mujahid denies a real scale; al-Mawardi illustrates reckoning through the saying that one's tongue is one's measure and a poem about disputants' speech. Ibn Atiyya says a balance is people's fairest measure. The Mu'tazila, Mujahid, al-Dahhak and al-A'mash reason that deeds are accidents that cannot be physically weighed; Elmalili reports many later scholars favoring just judgment. Al-Tabari and al-Farra cite Arabic weight for equivalence, and a poet's balance for answering speech.
  101:6/q05/p4 The balances are proofs and arguments. — reasons: Abd al-Aziz ibn Yahya cites the poet who calls each disputant's answering argument his balance.
101:6/q06 Why is the word mawazin plural? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the plural counts deeds, magnifies a single instrument, names its parts, or counts separate balances or aspects.)
  101:6/q06/p1 The plural reflects many kinds of deeds or things to be weighed. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says each person has many distinct things weighed; al-Biqa'i stresses the many kinds of good deeds. Al-Alusi and Elmalili allow multiplicity by considerations; al-Qurtubi cites poetic plural usage for separate events.
  101:6/q06/p2 The plural magnifies a single balance. — reasons: Al-Alusi compares Ad's rejection of the messengers in 26:123; al-Bursevi and Elmalili also give magnification or honor.
  101:6/q06/p3 The plural names the balance's parts. — reasons: Al-Alusi compares saying a person's temples have turned gray; Elmalili records the explanation too.
  101:6/q06/p4 The plural can indicate multiple balances for distinct aspects. — reasons: Al-Alusi cites poetry about flashes of lightning and rays of suns for multiplicity by aspect; Elmalili also allows conceptual multiplicity.
  101:6/q06/p5 The plural can distribute the weighing among people. — reasons: Al-Bursevi allows a scale per person; Study Quran says the plural suggests each person's experience of being weighed.
  101:6/q06/p6 The plural can refer to one actual balance in ordinary poetic usage. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi cites a poem using plural balances even on the view of one balance held by Gabriel.
101:6/q07 How many balances are used? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · referent] (turns on: Whether the plural counts actual instruments or has another explanation.)
  101:6/q07/p1 One balance weighs all communities and all deeds. — reasons: Al-Alusi calls this the better-known and sounder view; Elmalili calls it stronger; Study Quran says most hold it. Al-Qurtubi reports a single balance held by Gabriel.
  101:6/q07/p2 There may be a balance for each person or distinct balances for different aspects. — reasons: Al-Bursevi allows a scale assigned per person; al-Alusi allows multiple balances by considerations.
101:6/q08 What is weighed in the balances? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · referent] (turns on: How deeds that have passed can be measured: as transformed acts, records, created counterparts, or visible signs.)
  101:6/q08/p1 Deeds themselves are weighed, taking beautiful luminous or ugly dark bodily forms. — reasons: Ibn Abbas describes beautiful obedient deeds and ugly unbelieving deeds. Al-Biqa'i says deeds are embodied; al-Alusi reports good forms in the right pan and bad forms in the left and rejects the claim that transformation is impossible. Elmalili says deeds can have bodily effects and afterlife forms; al-Razi reports the option among theologians.
  101:6/q08/p2 Written records of deeds are weighed. — reasons: Al-Qushayri reports this; al-Qurtubi says the two-pan balance weighs records. Al-Razi's theologians offer records because past acts no longer exist. Al-Bursevi says this displays justice and removes excuses; al-Biqa'i, al-Alusi and Elmalili include it among accounts.
  101:6/q08/p3 Light and darkness stand for good and evil in the weighing. — reasons: Al-Razi's theologians propose light and darkness as signs, and al-Bursevi and Elmalili include them as manifestations of deeds or their effects.
  101:6/q08/p4 God creates separate substances or bodies corresponding to deeds, then weighs them. — reasons: Al-Qushayri describes a created substance for each part of an act. Al-Alusi says this avoids transforming acts and reports a claimed transmitted support.
101:6/q09 What physical form does the balance have? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · reports]
  101:6/q09/p1 It has a tongue and two pans, though its full nature is unknown. — reasons: Ibn Abbas gives tongue and pans; al-Hasan affirms two pans without describing their nature. Al-Qurtubi says records are placed in such a scale. Kanz al-Asrar and al-Alusi describe its breadth as the heavens and earth; Elmalili says God alone knows its nature.
101:6/q10 Where is the balance and who attends it? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · reports]
  101:6/q10/p1 It is between Paradise and Hell, attached to the Throne, with Gabriel and Michael attending it. — reasons: Nawadir al-Usul says Gabriel holds its column and Michael oversees it. Elmalili repeats the location; al-Qurtubi reports Gabriel holding the single scale.
101:6/q11 How do weight and the result appear during weighing? [ثَقُلَتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · reports] (turns on: Whether the movement follows worldly gravity, reverses it, or the result is shown by luminous and dark signs.)
  101:6/q11/p1 The heavy side sinks first, then rises to Illiyyin; the light side rises first, then falls to Sijjin. — reasons: Al-Alusi adopts al-Qurtubi's description by analogy with worldly gravity.
  101:6/q11/p2 Good deeds rise when they outweigh, while the unbeliever's side is heavy because the other side is empty. — reasons: A later scholar reverses ordinary gravity and cites 35:10; al-Alusi finds that verse inconclusive.
