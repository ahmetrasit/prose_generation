## THE CURRENT MAP
70:19/q01 Who is the human described here? [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the generic noun and the exception for worshippers indicate all humanity, or the warning primarily identifies unbelievers; two reports name particular men.)
  70:19/q01/p1 Humanity as a species is meant, with worshippers excepted from the blameworthy response. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari, al-Nasafi, al-Itqan, and Study Quran infer generality from the exception for those who pray (70:22-23); al-Qurtubi adduces the comparable generic al-nafs and exception in 6:151 and 103:1-3. Ibn Ashur compares generic human sayings at 96:6-7 and 21:37. Al-Alusi and al-Jishumi report this as one possible scope; Study Quran reports both scopes and prefers generality.
  70:19/q01/p2 Disbelievers are especially or specifically intended. — reasons: Al-Tabari calls the subject the disbeliever, and transmits Ibn Abbas's identification with associators and al-Dahhak's description of the disbeliever. Al-Mawardi transmits al-Dahhak and al-Kalbi; al-Qurtubi transmits al-Dahhak. Ibn Atiyya says the generic noun particularly targets unbelievers because the warning applies to them most forcefully. Al-Alusi, al-Jishumi, and Study Quran record this possible scope.
  70:19/q01/p3 It concerns Abu Jahl as a particular example. — reasons: Ibn Abbas's report in al-Durr says the verse was revealed about Abu Jahl. Al-Alusi transmits another Ibn Abbas report through al-Tusti but says a specific instance does not rule out the general meaning.
  70:19/q01/p4 It identifies Umayya ibn Khalaf al-Jumahi. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi transmits this identification from Muqatil.
70:19/q02 What does halūʿ mean? [هَلُوعًا · meaning] (turns on: Whether the term itself names the paired reactions, one reaction or desire, a deeper lack of restraint, or eager pursuit of perceived good; Ibn Ashur appeals to poetry to distinguish halʿ from distress.)
  70:19/q02/p1 It is a twofold disposition: intense distress or impatience at harm and greed or withholding when good comes. — reasons: Al-Hasan, al-Farra, Abu Ubayda, Tha'lab, Ibn Abbas, and many authors use 70:20-21 as God's explanation; reports vary between panic, despair, irritation, insufficient patience or gratitude, greed, and refusal to share. Al-Nahhas specifies poverty and withholding obligatory rights when wealthy; al-Kashshaf and al-Nasafi allow wealth or health versus poverty or illness; al-Qurtubi specifies God's due and people's share. Al-Farra derives the pattern from jazaʿ. Al-Kashshaf, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, and al-Alusi cite a swift, light she-camel to explain rapid reactions; al-Qurtubi cites a poetic line for that usage. Al-Durr cites Bashr ibn Abi Hazim's poetry. Al-Wahidi records Amr ibn Ma'dikarib's line and al-Mubarrad's appeal to seeking refuge from panic in battle. The prophetic reports describe anxious greed and disabling cowardice among the worst traits. Ibn Ashur reports Tha'lab's explanation but objects that the two reactions do not entail one another as one lexical definition.
  70:19/q02/p2 It means distress, fretfulness, agitation, grief, or impatience. — reasons: Al-Majaz appeals to the next verses for failure to endure. Al-Tabari transmits Ikrima, Ibn Zayd, and Qatada; al-Tha'labi also reports sorrow or grief from Qatada and Ibn Zayd. Al-Wahidi transmits al-Farra. Ibn Atiyya relates agitation to fears and desires; Ibn al-Jawzi includes Ibn Qutayba's intense distress; al-Durr cites Bashr ibn Abi Hazim for Arabic usage. Ibn Ashur says many linguists use distress but argues it is an effect rather than the trait itself, testing the distinction with Amr ibn Ma'dikarib's verse. Abu Hayyan glosses the related jazaʿ as fear and cites a poetic line.
  70:19/q02/p3 It means miserliness or stinginess. — reasons: Sa'id ibn Jubayr, al-Hasan, and al-Dahhak are among those reported with this gloss. Al-Qurtubi explains withholding God's due from received wealth. Ibn Ashur classifies this as an effect of the broader weakness of restraint.
  70:19/q02/p4 It means greed, covetousness, or voracious desire, sometimes specifically for what is unlawful. — reasons: Al-Hasan, Husayn, Ikrima, Mujahid, and Ibn Abbas are variously transmitted with the gloss; al-Dahhak describes someone never satiated, or never satisfied with amassed wealth. The al-Suddi report from Ibn Abbas and Ibn al-Jawzi's report specify unlawful objects. Ibn Ashur treats greed and hunger as effects of a broader trait.
  70:19/q02/p5 It is love of pleasure and flight from pain. — reasons: Ibn Kaysan and al-Asamm add the moral countercommand: give what one loves and patiently endure what one dislikes.
  70:19/q02/p6 It is restlessness as the soul follows desires and changes with possession or loss. — reasons: Sahl describes fluctuation with appetite. Ibn Ata describes contentment when possessing and resentment at loss, even of a small worldly gain; Bursevi repeats this. Abu al-Hasan al-Warraq adds cursing in affliction and supplication under trial.
  70:19/q02/p7 It means haste, swift agitation, or quickness. — reasons: Ata supplies the haste gloss, though al-Tha'labi's excerpt cuts off another gloss. Abu Muslim compares a fast, light she-camel. Ibn Ashur also lists haste among effects rather than the central trait.
  70:19/q02/p8 It means narrowness of heart. — reasons: This gloss is attributed to Muqatil by al-Tha'labi, al-Jishumi, and al-Baghawi.
  70:19/q02/p9 It is weak self-restraint before actual or anticipated pleasure or pain. — reasons: Ibn Ashur prefers this broader trait. Amr ibn Ma'dikarib's line separately denies halʿ and jazaʿ, so he rejects treating them as synonyms; he says al-Marquzi's mild-versus-severe-grief explanation fails. Greed, irritation, miserliness, hunger, and cowardice are effects. The condition becomes apparent on perceiving benefit or harm.
  70:19/q02/p10 It is intense eagerness for perceived personal good. — reasons: Al-Tabatabai prefers eagerness; perceived harm frustrates it and causes panic, while perceived benefits prompt selfish retention. Children's pursuit of apparent good, resistance to sharing, later rational revision, and persistent appetite illustrate how it works.
  70:19/q02/p11 It means weakness. — reasons: Al-Mawardi attributes this gloss to Abu al-Ghiyath.
  70:19/q02/p12 It expresses the ego's dark, impure attachment, producing cowardice and greed. — reasons: Al-Qashani interprets the distress and withholding of the following verses as manifestations of attachment to the ego's nature.
70:19/q03 How does the syntax describe the human as halūʿ? [خُلِقَ هَلُوعًا · grammar]
  70:19/q03/p1 Khuliqa is the predicate of inna, and halūʿan is a circumstantial accusative, possibly prospective. — reasons: Al-Nahhas parses the two words this way; Abu Muslim in al-Jishumi also calls halūʿan a circumstantial expression; al-Samin specifies a prospective circumstance.
70:19/q04 How do the following descriptions of distress and withholding relate to halūʿ? [هَلُوعًا · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the next two clauses are a lexical definition of one compound trait or name distinct effects of a deeper trait.)
  70:19/q04/p1 They are explanatory clauses or descriptions that unpack the preceding word. — reasons: Al-Burhan says explanatory clauses are common in the Quran. Al-Itqan cites rhetoricians that such clauses remove ambiguity, and Abu al-Aliya applies this here. Ibn Jinni, quoted in al-Burhan and al-Itqan, says one should not pause before the explanatory clause because it completes its antecedent like a relative clause or adjective. Al-Qurtubi says jazuʿan and manuʿan are adjectives brought before the condition, or predicates of an implicit kana; many exegetes take God's following words as the clearest explanation of halūʿ.
  70:19/q04/p2 The two distinct reactions are effects of a broader underlying trait, so they do not jointly define a single lexical sense. — reasons: Ibn Ashur rejects Tha'lab's loose equation: if two clauses defined one compound word, their different meanings would have to imply one another. He treats distress and other glosses as consequences of weak restraint.
70:19/q05 Why is the statement introduced with emphatic inna? [إِنَّ · rhetoric]
  70:19/q05/p1 It emphasizes a startling human trait, warns against it, and explains the preceding statement. — reasons: Al-Alusi says the emphatic sentence gives the reason for what precedes; Ibn Ashur says inna draws attention to the surprising trait and warns against it.
70:19/q06 In what sense was the human created with this disposition? [خُلِقَ هَلُوعًا · theology] (turns on: Whether creation names a real useful human impulse, a latent weakness, or figuratively describes deeply habituated blameworthy reactions; the infant and believers' exception are invoked for the figurative reading.)
  70:19/q06/p1 A real, innate but governable impulse is created for human benefit; blame falls on its misuse. — reasons: Al-Tabatabai says eagerness and self-love enable pursuit of happiness, while excess, deficiency, poor judgment, and appetite turn the gift toward misery, withdrawal, hoarding, rejection of truth, and violated rights. Reason can redirect eagerness toward fear of sin and haste to forgiveness; patience is prescribed in worldly hardship and duty. The believers' exception means the impulse is rightly governed, not absent. Ibn Ashur says the latent inclination surfaces on sensing benefit or harm, need not exclude moral training or contrary states, and hoarding can be checked by contemplating consequences. Al-Jishumi says desire is needed for moral responsibility but requires instruction in patience and restraint; al-Nasafi says revelation commands opposition to temperament. Al-Tabatabai rejects al-Zamakhshari's metaphorical escape.
