6:80/q20 Why does Abraham say رَبِّى rather than use a pronoun? [رَبِّى · rhetoric]
  6:80/q20/p1 Calling God 'my Lord' conveys care and Abraham's submission; repeating the name strengthens the point. — reasons: Alusi says any willed harm could have a nurturing purpose, or the wording confesses God's rule and ownership. He says repeating the divine designation instead of a pronoun is reinforcing and pleasurable.
6:80/q21 What does God's encompassing knowledge establish here? [وَسِعَ رَبِّى كُلَّ شَىْءٍ عِلْمًا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the clause grounds divine permission of events, praises knowledge or rebukes idols, predicts harm, or denies creaturely influence.)
  6:80/q21/p1 God knows every thing and event, so any harm or benefit occurs within His knowledge and wise will. — reasons: Tabari grounds it in God's creation and contrasts ignorant idols. Kaʿb refers to the Mother Book, creation and deeds. Razi says misfortune may serve wisdom and benefit. Nasafi, Bursevi, Ibn Ashur and Study Quran relate the knowledge to any harm God permits; Tabatabai says God knows each event and prevents or allows it. Ibn Kathir says no hidden matter escapes Him.
  6:80/q21/p2 The statement praises God's knowledge, contrasts idols' ignorance, or explains His wise acts. — reasons: Tabatabai reports these as other commentators' readings, then objects that power would suit the taunt and wisdom should be named to support the claim of only good acts.
  6:80/q21/p3 God may already know an idol or natural object will injure Abraham, so His knowledge explains the exception. — reasons: Tabatabai reports examples of a falling idol and the sun's heat, but objects that power or wisdom would better explain harm than knowledge alone.
  6:80/q21/p4 Knowledge and will leave no creature able to affect God's actions through intercession. — reasons: A reported view cites 2:255; Tabatabai rejects it because knowledge alone needs power and will, while cited intercession verses acknowledge causal mediation by divine permission.
6:80/q22 How is عِلْمًا parsed? [عِلْمًا · grammar] (turns on: Whether ʿilman specifies the subject's knowledge or functions as a figurative verbal object.)
  6:80/q22/p1 It is an accusative specification converted from the subject: 'my Lord's knowledge encompasses everything'. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and Samin give this; Samin prefers it and compares 19:4. Nahhas says the noun specifies or explains the preceding statement. Samin adds that 'everything' is the object of wasiʿa on either analysis.
  6:80/q22/p2 It is an absolute or cognate-like object because encompassing entails knowing. — reasons: Abu al-Baqa offers the figurative connection; Samin calls the metaphor remote. Alusi permits a cognate-like verbal noun.
6:80/q23 What does the final call to remember ask the people to recognize? [أَفَلَا تَتَذَكَّرُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the emphasis is abandonment of idols or explicit comparison of the two kinds of reasoning.)
  6:80/q23/p1 They should reflect that lifeless idols are powerless and leave their worship for the Creator. — reasons: Tabari, Wahidi, Baghawi, Ibn Kathir, Biqai, Bursevi and Alusi say the question rebukes heedlessness. Biqai and Alusi say reason already contains the distinction and needs prompting; Ibn Ashur adds God's attributes evident in creation.
  6:80/q23/p2 They should compare sound with corrupt reasoning and the capable God with incapable partners. — reasons: Zamakhshari and Baydawi formulate the contrast; Abu Hayyan reports Zamakhshari and Razi's point that denial of rivals brings no punishment. Kuranyolu urges a rational comparison between demonstrated monotheism and idolatry. Razi says affirming unity does not itself deserve punishment.
6:80/q24 What is the syntax of أَفَلَا تَتَذَكَّرُونَ? [أَفَلَا تَتَذَكَّرُونَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the last question is an independent sentence or coordinated with the earlier denial.)
  6:80/q24/p1 It is an independent admonitory and reproachful sentence, with no syntactic position. — reasons: Samin calls it a fresh sentence of rebuke.
  6:80/q24/p2 It is coordinated with Abraham's denial of their argument. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says it reproaches their failure to recall the idols' unfitness and God's attributes.
6:80/q25 What wider lessons about argument and reliance on God are drawn from this exchange? [* · theology] (turns on: The notes draw both an epistemic lesson about proof and a theological lesson about fear and divine causation.)
  6:80/q25/p1 True proof and divine guidance defeat inherited error; debate is praiseworthy for truth and blameworthy for falsehood. — reasons: Razi uses 6:83 and says Quranic and hadith censure concerns false disputation. Abraham's clear proof makes the people's argument ineffectual in Razi, Abu Hayyan, Ibn Kathir and Kuranyolu. Tabatabai treats guidance itself as proof of lordship.
  6:80/q25/p2 All hope and fear belong ultimately to God, and His wise will governs even trials. — reasons: Biqai says Abraham hopes in and fears God alone. Razi says impending hardship should not be mistaken for idol vengeance. Qashani denies independent created causation. Islahi compares Shuʿayb in 7:89: sincere aims still need God's help through trials. Tabatabai says the fear argument answers the opponents' strongest appeal; prophetic preaching uses warning as well as glad tidings.
6:80/q26 How does this exchange connect with other verses and the sūra? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the contextual emphasis is narrative sequence, prophetic continuity, or the structure of Abraham's proof.)
  6:80/q26/p1 It continues Abraham's declaration of 6:79 and recalls Hud's rejection of idol threats. — reasons: Ibn Ashur links the waw to 6:79 and compares 11:54; Ibn Kathir narrates Hud's challenge in 11:53–56; Razi, Abu Hayyan, Alusi and Tabatabai also invoke Hud's people's threat.
  6:80/q26/p2 Abraham's example supports Muhammad's identical call to monotheism and guidance to preach without forcing belief. — reasons: Islahi says Abraham and later prophets bring the same message; Abraham is singled out because Israelites and Ishmaelites claim him, though Judaism, Christianity and Ishmaelite polytheism are later distortions. Muhammad revives and completes Abraham's religion for his descendants.
  6:80/q26/p3 The no-fear assertion completes the guidance proof and supplies its own argument against partners. — reasons: Tabatabai says the exception is a concession, divine knowledge explains it, and the final appeal invokes innate reason; his opponents take guidance merely as ending further argument.
6:80/q27 What adjacent linguistic illustrations are recorded with the verse's discussion? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: These source excerpts contain adjacent examples rather than competing explanations of a word in 6:80.)
  6:80/q27/p1 Poetic and everyday animal imagery can imply human rank, poverty, or distress. — reasons: Jurjani says uncracked hooves imply a king rather than a low servant; Ibn Durayd's cracked-hoof insult supports that association. He explains tawlab for a poor child and other animal descriptions of wretched people. A gazelle-young allusion makes a birth question apt; the anecdote connects anger, hunger and the family's tears. Al-Asmaʿi insists on jadʿan with dāl as 'poorly fed' against al-Mufaḍḍal's reading.
  6:80/q27/p2 Attributing قوم to a rooster's group treats it as rational; an established lion metaphor licenses nonhuman adjective agreement. — reasons: Jurjani points to preceding hum and the rational pronoun in the rooster case, and to al-ḍāriya rather than a human plural for men pictured as lions.
  6:80/q27/p3 Other lexical examples gloss الغمرة as overwhelming distress and فرادى as one by one. — reasons: Abu Hayyan derives the former from covering water and cites poetry; he records Tamim's tanwin of farād and differing derivations.
  6:80/q27/p4 Other recitation examples distinguish vowel length, yāʾ opening, and consonant conditions. — reasons: Nashr says the three madd lengths depend on a reader's established choice; large assimilation for Abu Amr permits three lengths though most shorten, and the chapter distinguishes the condition and cause of madd. It also reports Medinan, Ibn Kathir and Abu Amr opening yāʾ in innī akhāf and innī arāk. Ibn Mujahid notes variant vowels for raʾā before a quiescent consonant.
  6:80/q27/p5 A separate verbal noun example explains why some forms avoid suggesting affected effort. — reasons: Burhan reports Abu al-Baqa and al-Raghib on ʿuluwwan at 17:43 in place of taʿāliyan; he says the tafaʿʿul form can suggest human affectation.
