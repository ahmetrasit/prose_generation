55:9/q09 How are the commands in this verse linked grammatically to the preceding balance clause? [* · grammar] (turns on: Whether the prior clause is an explanatory prohibition, a rationale coordinated with the command, or a purpose clause.)
  55:9/q09/p1 The command explains or follows the preceding ban on transgression. — reasons: Zajjaj says fair weight and no short measure confirm taking the earlier words as a prohibition. Razi says establishing weight justly explains not exceeding the balance at 55:8. Alusi coordinates the imperative with the negated clause by reading it as a verbal noun; another parsing makes the earlier lā prohibitive for parallelism. Ibn Ashur coordinates with the earlier prohibition if it is explanatory. Tabatabai reads an as explanatory, lā taṭghaw as prohibition, aqimu as a coordinated command and lā tukhsiru as a second prohibition reinforcing the first. Study Quran distinguishes the warning against tyranny or oppression from the command against withholding another's due, citing 11:84 and 83:1-3.
  55:9/q09/p2 The command coordinates with 'He set the balance' and states its rationale. — reasons: Ibn Ashur gives this coordination when the prior clause supplies a rationale; Tabatabai says fair weighing follows naturally from the institution of the balance.
  55:9/q09/p3 The earlier an is a subjunctive purpose clause, 'lest you transgress'. — reasons: Tabatabai rejects this parsing because it makes coordination of imperatives with a statement strained; Study Quran reports a subjunctive warning against tyranny and oppression.
55:9/q10 How does fair weighing relate to the balance established in the preceding verses? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the earlier balance is chiefly a physical scale or a general standard whose practical instance is fair weighing.)
  55:9/q10/p1 The verse applies the ordinary scale instituted by God to fair transactions. — reasons: Tabatabai most likely takes the preceding balance as an ordinary scale and this command as its explanation, barring excess and short measure. Kashshaf says God placed measuring instruments on earth for judgments and transactions. Muqatil's surrounding exposition places the balance that establishes justice alongside the raised sky and prostrating plants; he says plants prostrate by shifting shadows, the sky stands 500 years above earth, and the following earth supports creatures and palms, wheat and barley; 41:47 is cited for fruits emerging from coverings.
  55:9/q10/p2 Concrete weighing is one application of a universal standard of truth or justice. — reasons: Tabatabai presents this consequence if the earlier balance has the broader sense. Ibn Ashur says mizan and surrounding verbs, prepositions and qist support both senses together. Islahi says the signs show God's care for justice, and honest trade applies the cosmic principle in daily life; Kuran Yolu says qist and scales show universal balance enacted in lived situations. Study Quran says the balance is repeated three times to connect divine creation and human transactions.
55:9/q11 What reported incidents or sayings illustrate this verse? [* · reports] (turns on: The notes cite different reports, ranging from practical scale correction to warnings about dishonest weights and a saying on cosmic justice.)
  55:9/q11/p1 Ibn Abbas told a man giving extra to set the scale's pointer upright. — reasons: Ibn Abi Hatim reports Ibn Abbas citing God's command to establish weight with justice.
  55:9/q11/p2 Ali tipped a saffron seller's overfull scale and told him to weigh evenly before giving extra. — reasons: Wahidi reports the event to support his preferred order: make the beam level first, then give voluntarily.
  55:9/q11/p3 Ibn Abbas warned the Mawali that even small shortfalls ruined earlier peoples. — reasons: Tabari reports his appeal to fear God in measuring and weighing and his reminder that God may add without loss to honest measure.
  55:9/q11/p4 A dying neighbor who used different giving and receiving measures suffered a fiery ordeal. — reasons: Malik ibn Dinar said the man saw two mountains of fire; his family disclosed the double measures, and destroying them did not relieve him (Bursevi).
  55:9/q11/p5 The Prophet is reported to say the heavens and earth are founded on justice. — reasons: Study Quran gives this report without grading it.
55:9/q12 What moral and social lesson does the verse teach? [* · interpretation] (turns on: How broadly the command is applied: reciprocal trade, social order, universal justice, or duties toward God.)
  55:9/q12/p1 Give others the fairness one wants, because just measures sustain society. — reasons: Qatada makes reciprocity explicit (Thalabi, Qurtubi); Qushayri applies it to receiving food and drink. Islahi says cheating attacks the foundation of the created order and disorder spreads through a society or nation. Kuran Yolu says concrete fairness needs care, good-faith judgment and full effort. Muqatil says the balance establishes justice and forbids fraud; Kashshaf says God's instruments serve fair giving and taking.
  55:9/q12/p2 The repeated positive and negative commands stress the danger of disobeying justice. — reasons: Islahi says the Qur'an uses both formulations when disobedience has dangerous consequences. Kuran Yolu sees the use of qist with scale language as a demand to put universal justice into practice. Study Quran treats justice as fundamental to creation and transactions.
  55:9/q12/p3 For duties to God, justice means following revelation without adding to religion. — reasons: Kuran Yolu applies the balance principle to worship and religious obligation.
55:9/q13 How does this command clarify the preceding ban on transgressing the balance? [* · links]
  55:9/q13/p1 Transgression includes cheating the person for whom one weighs, by wronging, reducing, or exceeding the proper limit. — reasons: Ata from Ibn Abbas explains the ban as not cheating the recipient of the weight. Abu Ubayda and Zajjaj gloss transgression as wronging, reducing, or exceeding the limit (Wahidi).
