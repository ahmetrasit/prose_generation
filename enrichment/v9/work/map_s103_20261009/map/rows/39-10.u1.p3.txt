39:10/q24 What doctrinal implications are drawn from the verse and its context? [* · theology]
  39:10/q24/p1 The verse gathers the qualities of faith, taqwā, excellence, and patience. — reasons: Al-Baqlī names four traits; direct knowledge of God and attributes without inference from created events is his inward account of faith. Kuranyolu says the verse encapsulates Muslim duties.
  39:10/q24/p2 It testifies to divine power, knowledge, just judgment, and human responsibility for action. — reasons: Al-Jishumī infers perception without instruments, no divine willing of evil or punishment without guilt, and encouragement of knowledge, migration and obedience. He connects 39:7 and 39:9 to denials that God approves disbelief, transfers another's guilt, or creates acts within humans against their will; he says good and evil agents receive fitting recompense.
39:10/q25 What inward recompense and progress are read across the verse? [* · inward]
  39:10/q25/p1 Faith, piety, excellence, and patience unfold as direct knowledge, unveiled nearness, vision, and abiding in God. — reasons: Al-Baqlī describes direct knowledge, shedding attachments, unveiled divine beauty, steadfast concealed states, and rewards of inspiration here and immersion in eternity and abiding in attributes hereafter. Al-Qāshānī likewise joins effacement, contentment and infinite gardens of attributes.
  39:10/q25/p2 The seeker never exhausts the divine domain or its manifestations. — reasons: The Najmī interpretations quoted by Bursawī and Rūmī counsel continuing beyond each unveiling; Ālūsī describes unending beauty and manifestations, relating them to 55:29.
39:10/q26 Was verse 39:10 assigned a different place of revelation? [* · sciences]
  39:10/q26/p1 Some commentators place verses 10 and 23 in the Madinan period. — reasons: Study Quran reports this view without naming its proponents.
39:10/q27 What accompanying source excerpts discuss literary comparison rather than directly explaining 39:10? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: The cited passages themselves concern different poetic and rhetorical material.)
  39:10/q27/p1 Al-Bāqillānī criticizes a poem's weak joins, familiar wordplay, diction, forced rhyme, and uneven imagery. — reasons: He finds an opening disconnected and derivative, the distance line pleasing but commonplace, the stellar reins objectionable, two panegyric lines alien, tribal names stylistically clumsy despite a sound threat, and genealogical rhyme forced; he contrasts a stronger Banu Tamīm line.
  39:10/q27/p2 Al-Jurjānī surveys rational and conventional similes, including figurative spaciousness. — reasons: In Asrār he compares night and pilgrim stars, moon emerging from clouds as relief, clear light interrupted by darkness as joy and grief, fire and coal as justice and injustice, a generous person's wide character as a road, and the breadth of hope against a barren plain; he introduces another darkness comparison. The width examples illustrate why a moral metaphor can feel sensory.
  39:10/q27/p3 Al-Jurjānī argues rhetorical composition is the ordered relation of meanings. — reasons: In Dalāʾil he reasons that verbal sequence alone cannot explain unequal judgments of quality; thought must work on the craft's object, and word order follows meanings already ordered in the mind.
39:10/q28 What other adjacent lexical or exegetical extracts appear in the notes? [* · links]
  39:10/q28/p1 A destination may be described as a place judged by God alone. — reasons: Al-Jishumī reports Abū ʿAlī's comparison with a matter referred to a prince; this excerpt is from a different part of his commentary.
  39:10/q28/p2 Mabwaʾ denotes a settled, secure and suitable dwelling or station. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites al-Harawī's definition and applies the root to the Prophet's placing believers in defensible battle stations, mentioning 59:9, 39:74 and 3:121.
  39:10/q28/p3 A separate report says only sincere religion is accepted. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī transmits the Prophet's answer that ostentatious charity earns no reward and connects it to 39:3.
  39:10/q28/p4 Gratitude should recur more often than patience. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī reasons that blessings persist moment by moment, while patience arises in particular acts or hardships.
