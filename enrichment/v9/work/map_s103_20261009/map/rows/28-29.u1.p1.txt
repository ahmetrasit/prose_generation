28:29/q14 What tree is associated with the fire and call? [نَارًۭا · reports] (turns on: Reports name different tree species; Ibn Kathīr grades the samurah report's chain fair.)
  28:29/q14/p1 It was an acacia or samurah tree. — reasons: Ibn Masʿūd calls it a fresh green samurah; Ibn Kathīr rates that chain fair. Ibn ʿAṭiyya also transmits an acacia identification from Ibn Masʿūd.
  28:29/q14/p2 It was an oleander. — reasons: Wahb is named for this identification by Ibn ʿAṭiyya.
  28:29/q14/p3 It was hawthorn or azarole. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya attributes hawthorn to Qatāda and notes another report of azarole.
  28:29/q14/p4 It was an ʿullayq or ʿawsaj bush. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr transmits ʿullayq from Wahb and ʿawsaj from some People of the Book.
28:29/q15 What does the indefinite nāran suggest? [نَارًۭا · rhetoric]
  28:29/q15/p1 It signals a strange, wondrous or unlikely fire. — reasons: Elmalılı reads wonder in the indefinite tanwīn; al-Biqāʿī says Moses' emphatic claim of seeing fire responds to the improbability of a flame in a remote cold place.
28:29/q16 Who says 'wait' and what does amkuthū mean? [قَالَ ٱمْكُثُوٓا۟ · interpretation]
  28:29/q16/p1 Moses told his family to remain in place while he went toward the fire. — reasons: Būrsawī explicitly identifies Moses as speaker and glosses amkuthū as staying and waiting.
28:29/q17 Why is the command to the family plural and masculine? [لِأَهْلِهِ ٱمْكُثُوٓا۟ · grammar]
  28:29/q17/p1 The plural indicates several companions; masculine agreement follows precedence or includes sons. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the plural establishes more than one companion. Al-Biqāʿī invokes grammatical masculine precedence, possible sons, and the need to shelter women.
28:29/q18 What news did Moses hope to obtain? [بِخَبَرٍ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether khabar concerns the lost route or whether the fire itself offers welcome or danger.)
  28:29/q18/p1 Directions or information about the road and destination. — reasons: Al-Suddī, Ibn ʿAbbās and many commentators say the party was lost and Moses expected people by the fire. Al-Māwardī and al-Ṭabrisī specify whether the route was straight or diverted; Būrsawī specifies Egypt. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī uses the parallel 20:10 as stronger evidence. Asad treats 'about which route to take' as ellipsis; al-Zajjāj says he wanted to know why the fire was lit.
  28:29/q18/p2 He sought to learn whether the fire signaled comfort or danger. — reasons: Al-Māwardī and al-Ṭabrisī give this as an alternative to route information.
28:29/q19 Why call the hoped-for information khabar? [بِخَبَرٍ · meaning]
  28:29/q19/p1 Khabar is ordinary news; nabaʾ carries greater importance and benefit. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī's linguists distinguish the two; he derives nabaʾ from emergence and observes singular khabar twice in Moses' story, against nabaʾ for weighty accounts of earlier peoples and messengers at 27:22, 38:67, 78:2, 64:5, 14:9, 10:71, and 11:120.
28:29/q20 What does jadhwah denote? [جَذْوَةٍۢ · meaning] (turns on: The lexical range runs from a thick log without flame through burning wood to flame or ember itself; poetry and reports support different restrictions.)
  28:29/q20/p1 A substantial piece of wood or log, with or without fire. — reasons: Abū ʿUbayda compares jadhwah with jadhmah, a thick log; al-Zajjāj and al-Mubarrad agree. Ibn Muqbil's jadhā and Kuthayyir's verse attest large firewood; al-Samīn notes the poem about nonflaring logs but also a poem describing a blaze. Ibn ʿAṭiyya suggests roots used as desert fuel, citing al-Sulamī's smoking-roots line. Al-Rāghib and al-Mufradāt describe wood left after burning; the plural appears as jadhā or judhiyy. Al-Kashshāf allows fire at its end or none, while this context calls for a usable burning piece.
  28:29/q20/p2 A piece, stump, root or branch of wood containing fire, sometimes without a flame. — reasons: Qatāda specifies a tree base with fire at the tip, Mujāhid its base, al-Kalbī a partly burned stick, and Ibn Zayd fire within wood. Reports vary between burning end, partly burned stick, glowing coal and ember. Al-Samīn says this context requires a burning piece; al-Ālūsī says min al-nār clarifies it. Qatāda links it to winter warmth.
  28:29/q20/p3 A flame, meteor, or glowing firebrand itself. — reasons: Al-Kalbī speaks of a flame; Ibn ʿAbbās uses meteor, ember, or flaming brand in different reports. Al-Kashshāf cites an anonymous poem of a blazing brand. Al-Samarrāʾī calls it an ember or piece of fire; Elmalılı renders it Turkish 'eksi'.
28:29/q21 How is the jīm of jadhwah read? [جَذْوَةٍۢ · readings] (turns on: Three Arabic dialect vowelings of the first radical; kasra is most widespread and explicitly preferred by two lexicographic commentators.)
  28:29/q21/p1 With kasra, jidhwah. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr, Nāfiʿ, Abū ʿAmr, Ibn ʿĀmir and al-Kisāʾī are named among its readers; al-Ṭabarī prefers the best-known Ḥijāzī, Baṣran and some Kūfan form without rejecting fatḥa. Ibn Khālawayh calls kasra most eloquent. The forms are dialectal, and Qiraat-ER notes a slight stump nuance.
  28:29/q21/p2 With fatḥa, jadhwah. — reasons: ʿĀṣim is the main named reader; al-Qurṭubī also names al-Sulamī and Zirr ibn Ḥubaysh, and Abū Ḥayyān excepts al-Jaʿfī from ʿĀṣim. Al-Ṭabarī accepts the less common form.
  28:29/q21/p3 With ḍamma, judhwah. — reasons: Ḥamza is the main named reader, with al-Aʿmash, Ṭalḥa and Abū Ḥaywa also reported; Ibn al-Jawzī adds Khalaf and al-Walīd from Ibn ʿĀmir, while al-Qurṭubī names Yaḥyā. Qiraat-ER notes dictionaries attest all three vowels.
28:29/q22 How does min al-nār attach and function? [مِّنَ ٱلنَّارِ · grammar]
  28:29/q22/p1 It qualifies jadhwah and marks the source of the burning piece. — reasons: Al-Samīn attaches the phrase to jadhwah rather than ātīkum, saying this fire is not simply the first-mentioned fire and Arabic may repeat a noun with a pronoun or definite article. Al-Ālūsī, citing Qatāda's burning tree base, takes min as source from the fire Moses had noticed.
28:29/q23 What does taṣṭalūn mean? [تَصْطَلُونَ · meaning]
  28:29/q23/p1 To warm yourselves or shelter from cold by fire. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya traces it to ṣalā, the heat of fire, with tāʾ changed to ṭāʾ. Būrsawī notes variant forms of the noun ṣalāʾ. Reports tie the purpose to the winter cold; al-Biqāʿī says they might draw near the fire.
28:29/q24 What would Moses do if he found no news? [بِخَبَرٍ أَوْ جَذْوَةٍۢ · interpretation]
  28:29/q24/p1 He would bring back a burning branch or ember for warmth. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās sets the firebrand as the fallback if no information emerged. Al-Ṭabarī compares 20:10; other commentators stress the two immediate needs, guidance and warmth.
28:29/q25 Why does Moses say laʿallī and laʿallakum? [لَعَلِّىٓ لَعَلَّكُمْ · rhetoric]
  28:29/q25/p1 They express hope and humility amid uncertainty. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī relates tentative speech to the sūra's theme of humility; al-Sāmarrāʾī contrasts hopeful laʿallī in al-Qaṣaṣ with future sīn and resolve in al-Naml, saying circumstances may let speakers move between commitment and hope without contradiction.
28:29/q26 How does this wording compare with the fire scene in al-Naml? [لَعَلِّىٓ ءَاتِيكُم جَذْوَةٍۢ · links]
  28:29/q26/p1 Al-Qaṣaṣ uses hope, one 'come,' and a possible ember to fit a fearful scene; al-Naml uses certainty, repeated coming, and a bright flame for strength. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī links al-Qaṣaṣ's fear back before Moses' birth and al-Naml's future sīn and repetition to empowerment; atā occurs twelve times in al-Naml and six in al-Qaṣaṣ. A shihāb is a bright flame, qabas a flame taken from a larger fire and also a root for acquiring knowledge; it brings more light and warmth than a jadhwah, which may lack flame. His lecture notes say al-Qaṣaṣ only promises a possible ember, not expressly taking it from the fire, and that each wording accurately suits its context.
28:29/q27 What other textual links are made to this scene? [* · links]
  28:29/q27/p1 The fire episode recurs or is explained at 20:9–10, 27:7–8 and elsewhere. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn al-Jawzī, Abū Ḥayyān and Asad point to earlier discussions; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī uses 20:10 for guidance. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the Qur'an adds the mountain fire to Exodus's journey account. Study Quran also names 26:10; its introduction continues from the fire to God's speech, miracles, and Aaron's help at 28:30–35.
  28:29/q27/p2 Moses' promise of news echoes the sūra's closing promise that God will show signs. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī sees a deliberate verbal echo between saʾātīkum and sayurīkum at 28:93.
28:29/q28 What reading variants occur in the nearby pronouns? [لِأَهْلِهِ إِنِّىٓ لَعَلِّىٓ · readings] (turns on: Distinct phonetic vowel variants of the suffix and possessive yāʾ.)
  28:29/q28/p1 The suffix of li-ahlihi has ḍamma instead of kasra in one reading. — reasons: Ibn Mujāhid names Ḥamza and Ibn Saʿdān from Isḥāq al-Musayyibī for both 20:10 and 28:29; the rest have kasra. Qiraat-ER connects the shift to the following imperative's hamzat al-waṣl and says it does not change meaning.
  28:29/q28/p2 The first-person possessive yāʾ is opened with fatḥa in innīya or laʿalliya. — reasons: Qiraat-ER calls both prosodic variants without semantic change.
28:29/q29 What legal point is drawn from traveling with his family? [وَسَارَ بِأَهْلِهِۦٓ · law]
  28:29/q29/p1 A husband may take his wife where he wishes under guardianship, subject to a binding agreement. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī says marriage covenants especially deserve fulfillment.
