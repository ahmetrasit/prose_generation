22:11/q19 What theological lesson is drawn from conditional worship and trials? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether the moral focus is steadfastness, inward intention, God's justice, or associating other powers with Him.)
  22:11/q19/p1 Steadfast worship means gratitude and patience, while conditional service exposes self-interest and loss. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī says gratitude and patience would cure instability. Sahl al-Tustarī portrays the believer as charging ahead through monotheism, prophetic emulation and supplication; a reported saying from Zayd ibn Aslam says obedience admits one to Paradise and disobedience is refusal. Iṣlāḥī says God alone controls benefit and harm, and trials met with submission increase faith and reward.
  22:11/q19/p2 Appetite or confidence in one's own deeds can masquerade as worship. — reasons: Tustarī says immediate pleasure makes a person content and deprivation drives him toward disbelief. Al-Sulamī reports serving the self for reward, demanding recompense, and overlooking divine favor in the ability to worship; fear of worldly disgrace produces disgrace beyond.
  22:11/q19/p3 God does not wrong servants by punishing them for their sins. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī defines injustice as transgressing what one owns and says God does not punish without sin.
  22:11/q19/p4 This warning applies whenever Muslims revere the Qur'an ceremonially but fail to live by its guidance. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī applies it to public and private obedience and funerary use of the Qur'an; Kur'an Yolu calls it a recurring type in every age and place.
  22:11/q19/p5 Claims to serve God while associating other powers with Him are baseless. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says the hypocrite seeks God and Satan at once and that deities pursued for benefit cause more harm than good.
22:11/q20 What general rules about interpreting the Qur'an do the cited notes give? [* · sciences] (turns on: The distinction is between unsupported opinion and learned interpretation anchored in revelation.)
  22:11/q20/p1 Interpretation dependent on prophetic text or established evidence cannot be asserted by uninformed opinion. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī cites prophetic warnings reported from Ibn ʿAbbās with varying wording and no grading, Abū Bakr's refusal to speak without knowledge, and 7:33. Even a lucky correct guess remains culpable conjecture.
  22:11/q20/p2 The Prophet was charged with explaining revelation, and learned people may interpret it with care. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī cites the mission to explain revelation and Ibn Masʿūd's ten-verse learning practice. He rejects an inference that the Prophet explained only a few verses: the ʿĀʾisha report has an unknown transmitter, Jaʿfar ibn Muḥammad al-Zubayrī. Early reluctance reflects fear of error, like juristic caution, although completed religion grounds rulings in text or indication.
22:11/q21 What literary and grammatical examples are supplied in the source excerpts? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: These are distinct literary or grammatical examples present in the supplied notes, rather than competing explanations of one verse word.)
  22:11/q21/p1 Sībawayh gives idiom and ellipsis examples. — reasons: Layta shiʿrī is lightened through frequent use, as proverbs may preserve heavy forms. After good news, haniʾan mariʾan is accusative with an omitted verb, such as 'may it be established for him' or 'may it gladden him'.
  22:11/q21/p2 Al-Bāqillānī criticizes decorated verse whose imagery and place-names outstrip its meaning. — reasons: He finds al-Buḥturī technically polished yet weakened by lightning supposedly lighting underground Batn Wajrah, padding with landmarks, and a pilgrimage stanza with sweet language but little substance. He rejects a reading of a fresh lightning flash from the beloved's direction because it damages the poem's order.
  22:11/q21/p3 Al-Jurjānī analyzes the visual comparison of joined lām and alif and the merit of a poetic image. — reasons: He describes letters touching in the middle, reads al-Mutanabbī's intent as close compression rather than literal embrace, and says a judge's distinction about possible borrowing does not affect the image's hyperbolic thinness.
  22:11/q21/p4 Al-Jurjānī restricts technical metaphor to resemblance-based transfer for hyperbole. — reasons: Al-Āmidī casually calls majlis for its occupants metaphor, but al-Jurjānī calls this contiguity; al-Āmidī's formal list supports the narrower sense. The original sense remains like a lender's claim when a man is called lion, sea or moon.
  22:11/q21/p5 A rare light reading of الدواب is rejected as weak. — reasons: Ibn Jinnī says al-Zuhrī alone is reported to lighten the doubled bāʾ, with weak analogy and transmission; lengthened alif compensates for adjacent identical consonants.
  22:11/q21/p6 A source identifies several phrases as examples of rare Qur'anic vocabulary. — reasons: Al-Zarkashī lists them in the study of gharīb al-Qurʾān without further verse-specific detail in these notes.
  22:11/q21/p7 A cited observation contrasts a withholding rich person with his anxious, bequest-making behavior when ill. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī supplies the comparison.
22:11/q22 Why and how was the Kashshāf commentary undertaken? [* · sciences]
  22:11/q22/p1 Demand from learned readers, earlier dictation, travel to Mecca and a patron's eagerness led its author to undertake the work in old age. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī describes requests from learned, eloquent people of justice; their proposed title; his hesitation over his era's weak rhetorical learning; his earlier openings and Baqara dictation in question-and-answer form; renewed demand on travel; and Abū al-Ḥasan ʿAlī ibn Ḥamza ibn Wahhās's eagerness, even to travel to Khwarazm.

  22:11/q23/p1 One view calls it Madinan except verses 52–55. — reasons: Study Quran attributes this to Ibn Kathīr.
  22:11/q23/p2 Another calls it Makkan except verses 19–22. — reasons: Study Quran attributes this to al-Qurṭubī.
  22:11/q23/p3 Most authorities treat the sūra as containing both Makkan and Madinan material. — reasons: A tradition says it came by night and day, at home and on journeys, in war and peace, with abrogating and abrogated as well as clear and allegorical verses. 22:11/q24 What virtues accompany the exhortation to sincere conduct? [* · inward]
  22:11/q24/p1 Humility, forbearance, gentleness, fidelity and truthfulness cultivate sound relations and character. — reasons: Bursawī says they bring affection, supporters, won hearts, brotherhood and completed virtue, and prays to leave base traits for good ones.
