2:133/q20 Why say 'your God' and 'God of your fathers'? [إِلَٰهَكَ وَإِلَٰهَ ءَابَآئِكَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Shared tradition, honor, or creator argument.)
  2:133/q20/p1 It names one shared deity and reassures Jacob of continuity with his and his fathers' path. — reasons: Razi stresses reassurance; Abu Hayyan the common deity; Ibn ʿAshur says 'your God' invokes taught attributes amid surrounding idolatry.
  2:133/q20/p2 It honors Jacob's rank and the sons' lower station. — reasons: Qushayri connects the phrase with reverence and submission.
  2:133/q20/p3 It may point to rational proof through the creator of their ancestors. — reasons: Razi connects this possibility with 2:21.
2:133/q21 Why repeat ilah before 'your fathers'? [وَإِلَٰهَ ءَابَآئِكَ · grammar]
  2:133/q21/p1 It avoids coordinating directly onto a genitive pronoun and can heighten eloquence. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and Samīn cite the grammatical convention; Ibn ʿAshur says repetition is eloquent but not obligatory.
2:133/q22 What does ilah mean here? [إِلَٰهَكَ · meaning]
  2:133/q22/p1 Ilah is the generic noun 'god'. — reasons: Study Quran distinguishes it from the proper name Allah.
2:133/q23 Who counts as a father here? [ءَابَآئِكَ · meaning] (turns on: Literal breadth, figurative extension, or predominance.)
  2:133/q23/p1 The term includes parent and ancestor, and can include an uncle by kinship usage or likeness. — reasons: Majaz and many commentators cite the Prophet calling al-ʿAbbas father, father's peer or remnant of forefathers, including the ʿUrwa episode. Ibn ʿAbbas invokes grandfather; Tabatabaʾi cites 12:38 and 6:74. Thaʿlabi cites Joseph's aunt as mother in 12:100.
  2:133/q23/p2 The uncle or grandfather sense is figurative rather than literal identity with a parent. — reasons: Jishumi says one may deny 'father' of an uncle; Razi says Ibn ʿAbbas's assertion concerns legal standing, not lexicon.
  2:133/q23/p3 Ishmael's inclusion can arise from predominance of actual fathers in a mixed list. — reasons: Ibn Kathir and Alusi give this; Samīn objects that singular uncle usage needs another explanation.
2:133/q24 What forms of ab are reported? [ءَابَآئِكَ · meaning]
  2:133/q24/p1 Arabic has dialectal and plural forms of ab and verbs for nourishing or adopting a father. — reasons: Wahidi cites Ahmad ibn Yahya, Sibawayh and poetry for abuka, abak, abuk, duals and plurals; al-Layth gives yaʾbu and taʾabba; Kashshaf cites poetic al-abina.
2:133/q25 Which readings of 'your fathers' are reported? [ءَابَآئِكَ · readings] (turns on: Plural, singular, plural analysis of abika, or omission.)
  2:133/q25/p1 The common reading has plural abaʾika. — reasons: Zajjaj and Tabari cite consensus; Jishumi opposes Yaḥya's singular as against it, and Razi says anomalous Ubayy does not displace transmission.
  2:133/q25/p2 A variant has singular abika, with Abraham as father and Ishmael and Isaac coordinated separately. — reasons: Attributed to Ibn ʿAbbas, Hasan, Yahya ibn Yaʿmar, Jahdari and Abu Raja. Its motive is avoiding calling the uncle father; Nahhas criticizes the rationale and awkward separation of Isaac.
  2:133/q25/p3 Written abika may be a sound plural of ab with nun omitted in annexation. — reasons: Ibn Jinni prefers it over forced singular-for-plural; Sibawayh's abin and abun and cited poems support the analysis.
  2:133/q25/p4 Ubayy omits the ancestor word and reads 'the God of Abraham'. — reasons: Thaʿlabi, Kashshaf, Razi, Abu Hayyan and Samīn report it; Razi calls it anomalous.
2:133/q26 How do the ancestor names relate to 'fathers'? [ءَابَآئِكَ إِبْرَٰهِۦمَ وَإِسْمَٰعِيلَ وَإِسْحَٰقَ · grammar] (turns on: The underlying reading of the ancestor word.)
  2:133/q26/p1 They are genitive appositions or explanations; an omitted 'I mean' may also govern an accusative. — reasons: Akhfash, Zajjaj, Nahhas, Tabarsi, Qaffal, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan and Samīn analyze the names. Nasafi likewise identifies the names as appositional clarification, though his list says Abraham, Isaac and Jacob.
  2:133/q26/p2 With singular abika, Abraham alone apposes father, while the others are separately coordinated. — reasons: Zajjaj gives the analysis, intended to avoid calling Ishmael father.
2:133/q27 Why are these ancestors named and ordered? [إِبْرَٰهِۦمَ وَإِسْمَٰعِيلَ وَإِسْحَٰقَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Seniority or rhetorical honor.)
  2:133/q27/p1 Ishmael precedes Isaac because he was older, Abraham's firstborn. — reasons: Ibn Zayd, Abu Zayd, Tabarsi, Qaffal, Qurtubi, Alusi and Asad say so; Ibn ʿAshur gives a fourteen-year gap.
  2:133/q27/p2 The list honors Jacob as questioner, Abraham as origin, Ishmael as Muhammad's ancestor, and outstanding forefathers. — reasons: Abu Hayyan gives the sequence and says they were the best of their eras; Ibn ʿAshur compares genealogical poetry.
2:133/q28 How do the foreign ancestor names decline and pluralize? [إِبْرَٰهِۦمَ وَإِسْمَٰعِيلَ وَإِسْحَٰقَ · grammar] (turns on: Foreign-name declension and rival plural forms.)
  2:133/q28/p1 The foreign proper names are diptote; an actually Arabic-derived Ishaq or Yaqub would decline. — reasons: Akhfash, Nahhas, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan and Samīn explain; Kisaʾi permits Arabic derivations while Abu Hayyan distinguishes an Arabic verbal noun.
  2:133/q28/p2 Broken plurals vary; regular sound masculine plurals preserve the stems. — reasons: Sibawayh and Khalil give barahim and samaʿil; Kufans offer forms with replacement ha. Ibn Yazid/Abu al-ʿAbbas contests lost hamza and permits abarih and asamiʿ; Ahmad ibn Yahya cites diminutive analogy. Nahhas favors sound plurals.
2:133/q29 How is Ibrahim pronounced in canonical readings? [إِبْرَٰهِۦمَ · readings] (turns on: The vowel before the final mim.)
  2:133/q29/p1 Ibrahima has long i before the final mim. — reasons: Qiraat-ER calls this the standard canonical form.
  2:133/q29/p2 Ibrahama has long a before the final mim. — reasons: Qiraat-ER calls it a canonical phonological adaptation.
2:133/q30 What does the sons' worship pledge teach? [نَعْبُدُ إِلَٰهَكَ وَإِلَٰهَ ءَابَآئِكَ · interpretation]
  2:133/q30/p1 They affirm the one God without partners and continue the patriarchs' submission. — reasons: Tabari stresses exclusive worship and lordship; Dahhak says their reply affirms unity; Qushayri calls it inherited monotheism. Razi says fuller divine attributes are omitted for brevity; Ibn Kathir cites 3:83 and 21:25.
2:133/q31 What does naming Ishmael show about kinship? [إِسْمَٰعِيلَ · interpretation]
  2:133/q31/p1 Jacob's sons belong to Abraham's wider family, including Ishmael's line. — reasons: Elmalılı uses this to interpret the promised prophet from Israel's brothers as an Ishmaelite.
2:133/q32 Which son was the intended sacrifice? [إِسْمَٰعِيلَ وَإِسْحَٰقَ · reports] (turns on: The identity of the sacrificial son.)
  2:133/q32/p1 Isaac is the sacrifice in a well-known view. — reasons: Ibn ʿAtiyya mentions this beside 'son of the two sacrificed.'
  2:133/q32/p2 Ishmael was the intended sacrifice. — reasons: Ibn ʿAshur rejects the Jewish Isaac claim: the Torah's son is unique, while Isaac was never Abraham's only son.
2:133/q33 What does ilahan mean syntactically? [إِلَٰهًۭا وَٰحِدًۭا · grammar] (turns on: Apposition, circumstance, or specification.)
  2:133/q33/p1 It apposes 'your God' or 'God of your fathers' to state oneness expressly. — reasons: Nahhas allows definite/indefinite apposition; Kashshaf compares 96:15–16. Baydawi notes possible plural misunderstanding; Samīn describes Basran allowance of unmodified indefinite apposition.
  2:133/q33/p2 It is a circumstantial accusative describing one deity in worship. — reasons: Akhfash, Tabari and Ibn ʿAtiyya favor this. Ibn ʿAshur says wahidan carries the description while the repeated noun clarifies.
  2:133/q33/p3 It is an accusative of specification, 'we mean one God'. — reasons: Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Bursevi and Samīn offer it; Abu Hayyan and Samīn's Sheikh object that specification cannot be indefinite or ambiguous.
2:133/q34 Why explicitly add 'one God'? [إِلَٰهًۭا وَٰحِدًۭا · rhetoric]
  2:133/q34/p1 It prevents inference of multiple ancestral gods and reinforces strict monotheism. — reasons: Baydawi, Abu Hayyan, Samīn, Bursevi, Alusi and Tabatabaʾi stress the ambiguity. Ibn ʿAshur cites Quranic and poetic repetition where a new predicate adds meaning; Ibn Jinni contrasts pointless bare repetition.
2:133/q35 What does muslimun mean? [مُسْلِمُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Submission as disposition or entry into Islam.)
  2:133/q35/p1 They submit humbly and obediently to God alone, affirming His oneness in belief and practice. — reasons: Tabari defines obedience and worship; Ibn ʿAbbas says affirmation of unity. Tabarsi adds servitude and humility; Alusi includes obeying commands and Biqaʿi no choice against God's command.
  2:133/q35/p2 They entered and remain in Islam, God's religion and that of every prophet. — reasons: Tabarsi cites 3:19; Ibn Kathir cites 3:83, 21:25 and the prophets' shared religion; Tabatabaʾi says Abraham's descendants inherited it.
2:133/q36 How is 'we submit to Him' attached? [وَنَحْنُ لَهُۥ مُسْلِمُونَ · grammar] (turns on: Circumstance, coordination, or an independent clause.)
  2:133/q36/p1 It is circumstantial, attached to worship's subject, object or both. — reasons: Tabari prefers it; Kashshaf, Tabarsi, Baydawi and Razi give different attachments. Bursevi ties it to worshippers.
  2:133/q36/p2 It coordinates with 'we worship' as a second enduring answer. — reasons: Abu Hayyan prefers direct coordination over ellipsis: the verb denotes renewed worship and the nominal clause stable surrender. Ibn ʿAshur agrees.
  2:133/q36/p3 It is a separate nominal assertion or emphatic parenthesis. — reasons: Ibn ʿAtiyya prefers a complete statement, 'we were and will be so.' Kashshaf proposes parenthesis; Abu Hayyan and Samīn's Sheikh reject that technical label because no linked part of the same speaker
