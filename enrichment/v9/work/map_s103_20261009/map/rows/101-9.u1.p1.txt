101:9/q07 What inward reality does the abyss represent? [هَاوِيَةٌۭ · inward] (turns on: The inward accounts specify different contents of the light-scale person's abyss: corporeal nature or veiling and ignorance.)
  101:9/q07/p1 The abyss is the hell of bodily or corporeal nature for a person ruled by evil deeds and psychic vices. — reasons: Qashani places the light-scale person's home in bodily nature's hell. Bursevi says the truly weighed things are hidden primordial capacities and intellectual receptivities in right- and left-hand pans.
  101:9/q07/p2 The abyss is veiling and a hot fire of ignorance, blindness, ego, passion, Satan and the world. — reasons: Bursevi reports the Ta'wilat Najmiyya's inward reading as the origin of those with light scales.
101:9/q08 What do reports about souls say about being taken to Hawiya? [فَأُمُّهُۥ هَاوِيَةٌۭ · reports]
  101:9/q08/p1 Believing souls welcome a new believer and ask about others; a person who died earlier but has not joined them was taken to mother Hawiya. — reasons: Al-Hasan transmits a Prophetic version calling Hawiya a wretched mother and nurse; al-Ash'ath transmits the exchange. Durr gives several Prophetic versions, including asking about living people, allowing the newcomer rest from worldly distress, and welcoming with merciful souls like news-bearers; Ibn Kathir notes Ibn Mardawayh's longer marfu version from Anas. Abu Ayyub's version includes inspecting deeds, rejoicing at good and praying over bad; Ibn al-Mubarak says Salam al-Tawil raised it from Thawr to the Prophet. Sa'id ibn Jubayr says dead relatives rejoice or grieve at news and ask whether a deceased has arrived. Al-Tabatabai notes parallel reports from Anas, al-Hasan and al-Ash'ath.
101:9/q09 What happens in Wahb's village story associated with Hawiya? [هَاوِيَةٌۭ · reports]
  101:9/q09/p1 A punished village's souls are buried in Hawiya, identified as Sijjin, a vast fiery mass. — reasons: Wahb says Jesus questions a village where people, jinn, animals and birds died; their sins were serving false powers and loving worldly life. One respondent says the others are muzzled with fiery bridles, while he was caught in their punishment despite differing from them and fears his own fate. Jesus tells his companions that barley bread, plain water and sleeping on dung among dogs are better if accompanied by well-being in both worlds.
101:9/q10 Why use this word and this compact wording? [فَأُمُّهُۥ هَاوِيَةٌۭ · rhetoric] (turns on: The notes offer rhyme and antithetical ellipsis as distinct explanations of the wording.)
  101:9/q10/p1 The rare word Hawiya helps preserve the sura's rhyme. — reasons: Itqan says the Qur'an often chooses rarer synonyms, listing dayza, al-Hutamah, Saqar, Laza and Hawiya, in part for each sura's rhyme. Corpus Coranicum also invokes rhyme against doubts about the topographical sense.
  101:9/q10/p2 The compressed clause uses ellipsis and a contrasting pattern with the earlier life of contentment. — reasons: Biqai says a hostile life is omitted in the second clause and a mother in the first (101:7), producing antithetical ellipsis. Al-Kashf favors the idiom's emphatic contrast with the pleasurable life, while al-Tibi calls it clearest; Alusi notes room for discussion.
101:9/q11 How does this ending connect to the sura and its neighbors? [* · links]
  101:9/q11/p1 The sura moves from the Hour to two opposed outcomes and ends with the wretched; the next sura opens with the cause of their misery. — reasons: Biqai relates 101:1, 101:8–9 to the opening of sura 102. Alusi says the passage first describes what all share, then each group's particular state. Study Quran says the final six verses contrast heavy and light scales. Tabatabai describes the Meccan sura as a resurrection warning with glad tidings, dominated by warning. Corpus Coranicum says the Hell revelation closes its central eschatological sequence and compares riddling tension in Q 100, 90:12–16, 97:2–4 and 104:5–9.
101:9/q12 When was the sura containing this verse revealed? [* · sciences]
  101:9/q12/p1 It is an early Meccan sura, probably after al-Tin. — reasons: Asad's introduction places it after sura 95; Tabatabai also calls it Meccan.
101:9/q13 How do the condemned fall into Hell? [فَأُمُّهُۥ هَاوِيَةٌۭ · interpretation]
  101:9/q13/p1 They fall into the Fire on their heads. — reasons: Abu Salih and Qatada explicitly describe the headfirst fall in Tabari's two recensions.
101:9/q14 How is the preceding contented life described? [* · links]
  101:9/q14/p1 The life is called content because its possessor is content with it, or because it causes contentment. — reasons: Ibn Ashur calls radiya a transferred description of life, furnishing the preceding contrast to the miserable fate of this verse.
