2:14/q15/p1 Human chiefs or leaders in disbelief or hypocrisy. — reasons: Al-Suddi, Qatada, Ibn Masʿud and Ibn ʿAbbas identify leaders; al-Jishumi prefers this as the majority, natural in context, and excluding soothsayers. Al-Razi says senior leaders can corrupt the land unlike juniors; Baydawi, Bursevi and Ibn Ashur distinguish senior and junior hypocrites. Elmalili says exegetes agree these are human satans and describes secret pacts; Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi say distance from faith can encompass named groups.
  2:14/q15/p2 Jewish leaders who urged denial, particularly Kaʿb b. al-Ashraf and others. — reasons: Ibn Abbas traditions identify Jews urging opposition to the Messenger; al-Thaʿlabi and al-Jishumi give five names across Medina, Aslam, Juhayna, Asad and Syria, with name variants; Ibn Abi Hatim names Kaʿb and cites Abu al-ʿAliya, al-Suddi and al-Rabiʿ. Muqatil calls them Kaʿb and followers. Some treat them as soothsayers.
  2:14/q15/p3 Polytheists or companions among polytheists and hypocrites. — reasons: Qatada and al-Rabiʿ name polytheists; Mujahid includes fellow hypocrites, while other reports include disbelieving associates or brothers.
  2:14/q15/p4 Soothsayers, sometimes named and linked to their jinn companions. — reasons: Al-Dahhak and others identify them as soothsayers; Bursevi lists five with differing names, and explains their supposed knowledge of secrets, cures, and divination by attendant satans. Al-Alusi says this explains the name, but al-Jishumi says context excludes them.
  2:14/q15/p5 Jinn satans themselves. — reasons: Reports from al-Kalbi and Ibn Abbas are cited; Ibn Atiyya finds it unlikely in context, al-Alusi implausible, and al-Jishumi prefers the human leaders.
  2:14/q15/p6 Inner demons or a person's evil impulses. — reasons: Asad and Raghib allow Qur'anic shaytan for satanic impulses opposed to truth and morality; Study Quran lists inner demons as an interpretation here.
2:14/q16 How is شياطين formed and inflected? [شَيَٰطِينِهِمْ · grammar] (turns on: Broken plural inflection versus anomalous sound-plural treatment.)
  2:14/q16/p1 It is an inflected broken plural, with original nūn retained in annexation. — reasons: Al-Nahhas gives genitive case after إلى and a genitive suffix. Al-Samin offers faʿālīl or faʿālīn patterns; al-Alusi confirms broken plural.
  2:14/q16/p2 A rare anomalous plural treats it like a sound masculine plural: الشياطون. — reasons: Al-Samin compares the colloquial بساتون and cites 26:210; al-Alusi notes the anomalous reading.
2:14/q17 What does إنا معكم assert? [إِنَّا مَعَكُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether 'with' denotes religion, aid, intelligence sharing, or active harm.)
  2:14/q17/p1 They share their leaders' religion and remain in their faction. — reasons: Ibn Hisham, Ibn Abbas, al-Thaʿlabi, Damghani, al-Tabrisi, al-Nasafi and others read continuing religious allegiance. Damghani cites analogous use in 11:58 and 67:28; al-Alusi specifies Judaism and calls it a spiritual alliance.
  2:14/q17/p2 They offer support or assistance against Muhammad and believers. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabi, al-Jishumi, Ibn al-Jawzi, and Abu Hayyan offer support alongside or instead of shared religion.
  2:14/q17/p3 They share intelligence about believers. — reasons: Abu Hayyan gives this as one of four interpretations.
  2:14/q17/p4 They join in harming Muslims and suppressing God's message. — reasons: Abu Hayyan lists this as another interpretation.
2:14/q18 How is إنا معكم grammatically constructed? [إِنَّا مَعَكُمْ · grammar] (turns on: The grammatical status of مع, especially when its ʿayn is still.)
  2:14/q18/p1 إنا contracts إننا by deleting one nūn; معكم is a locative or adverbial predicate of companionship. — reasons: Al-Nahhas says contraction avoids successive nūns and treats معكم as accusative through an implied state; al-Wahidi calls مع adverbial; al-Samin identifies the middle nūn as omitted, cites 3:93 and analyzes an omitted place predicate rather than time. Al-Tabrisi cites a poetic accusative parallel.
  2:14/q18/p2 A silent ʿayn can make مع function as a preposition. — reasons: Al-Nahhas reports this analysis; al-Samin disputes it and says even the poetic silent form remains an adverb. Yunus and al-Akhfash treat detached معا as a maqsur noun; a poet's شعباكما معا allows predicate or circumstantial parsing.
2:14/q19 How is معكم read? [مَعَكُمْ · readings] (turns on: Vocalization of the ʿayn.)
  2:14/q19/p1 The ʿayn is vowelled. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi call this the majority reading.
  2:14/q19/p2 The ʿayn is still, an anomalous tribal form. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi assign it to Ghanm and Rabiʿa; al-Samin limits the still form to poetry.
2:14/q20 How is the plural mīm read near معكم إنما? [مَعَكُمْ إِنَّمَا · readings] (turns on: Whether ṣila is made before the following hamza; other plural-mīm environments supply the comparative rule.)
  2:14/q20/p1 The plural mīm has ṣila before a following hamzat al-qaṭʿ in Warsh's route. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari lists معكم إنما; al-Hudhali gives Ibn Jammaz a generally no-ṣila route except this environment.
  2:14/q20/p2 The other readers keep the mīm still in this environment and all readers still it at pause. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari also gives dialectal mīm and preceding hāʾ vowel rules elsewhere: Abu Amr kasras both; Nafiʿ, Ibn Kathir, Ibn Amir, Asim and Abu Jaʿfar damma mīm; Hamza, Kisai and Khalaf damma both; Yaʿqub harmonizes. Ruways has two pause options in يَغْنِهِمُ الله; readers agree on damma after a dammated preceding letter.
2:14/q21 Why is their claim to believers verbal and their claim to leaders emphatic and nominal? [قَالُوٓا۟ ءَامَنَّا قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا مَعَكُمْ · rhetoric] (turns on: How audience knowledge, intended reassurance, and social benefit motivate the differing clause forms.)
  2:14/q21/p1 They feign a new act of belief before believers but affirm stable allegiance to leaders, whose doubts need reassurance. — reasons: Al-Razi, Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, al-Harali, al-Alusi and Ibn Ashur explain the contrast. Hypocrites lack sincere belief and cannot convincingly claim perfect faith before perceptive Companions; Ibn Ashur says overemphasis itself would arouse suspicion. Al-Alusi prefers this account; the leaders might suspect genuine conversion after their careful Muslim-facing show. Al-Harali stresses their fixed allegiance.
  2:14/q21/p2 They assert strongly to leaders because they sincerely wish to reassure them, while they care little about believers. — reasons: Al-Alusi reports this but finds it unpersuasive.
  2:14/q21/p3 They avoid claiming perfect faith before perceptive believers, who would reject it, but their leaders accept emphatic reassurance. — reasons: Al-Razi, Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan and al-Zamakhshari develop this; al-Alusi reports and finds the stand-alone explanation unpersuasive.
  2:14/q21/p4 Emphasis claims eligibility for Muslims' legal treatment and protection from conflict. — reasons: Al-Alusi notes they had already sworn by God and the Last Day to deceive believers, citing 63:1 as context.
2:14/q22 What does إنما do in the last statement? [إِنَّمَا · grammar] (turns on: Whether restriction belongs to the particle itself or to contextual interpretation.)
  2:14/q22/p1 The added ما stops إن from governing, leaving نحن subject and مستهزئون predicate. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi gives this analysis with a nearby مصلحون example; Abu Hayyan agrees on suspended government and says restriction arises from context, not the particle alone. He rejects deriving restriction from affirmative إن plus negative ما and denies attested governing إنما, citing 13:7, 18:110, and 79:45.
  2:14/q22/p2 إنما restricts the hypocrites' claim: they are only mocking, not sincere believers. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says this emphatically reverses their public performance and confirms loyalty.
2:14/q23 How does the final clause connect to إنا معكم? [إِنَّا مَعَكُمْ إِنَّمَا نَحْنُ مُسْتَهْزِءُونَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the last clause is a fresh answer, semantic reinforcement, or syntactic apposition.)
  2:14/q23/p1 It answers the leaders' implied objection: why appear friendly to believers? — reasons: Al-Razi, Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, al-Alusi, Ibn Ashur and Elmalili describe this resumption. Al-Alusi says learned interpreters prefer it though innamā weakens the implied-question scenario; Ibn Ashur prefers it because it confirms allegiance while resolving puzzlement.
  2:14/q23/p2 It emphasizes or explains their enduring unbelief and allegiance. — reasons: Al-Razi and Baydawi propose emphasis, replacement, or a stronger substitute because belittling Islam magnifies unbelief; al-Nasafi says mockery treats Islam as invalid and confirms Judaism; Ibn Ashur says restriction reinforces allegiance.
  2:14/q23/p3 It is syntactically an accusative apposition or explanatory replacement for the first statement. — reasons: Al-Samin offers the apposition against an independent sentence; Ibn Ashur lists encompassing apposition and al-Alusi lists badal or necessary implication, but Ibn Ashur prefers the explanatory restart.
2:14/q24 What does مستهزئون mean? [مُسْتَهْزِءُونَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the expression is lexical ridicule, contextual denial, or an alternative etymological sense.)
  2:14/q24/p1 Mockers who ridicule, deride, belittle, or treat others contemptuously. — reasons: Ibn Hisham says 'mocking and playing'; al-Wahidi defines huzʾ as outward display unlike the inward state; al-Jishumi calls derision and jest synonymous and seriousness their opposite. Al-Tabrisi gives related verbal patterns; Ibn Atiyya cites Aws b. Hajar and Sibawayh-type forms. Al-Razi and Baydawi connect the root with lightness and rapid movement; al-Qurtubi cites a rajaz example; al-Ghazali lists mimicry, speech, gesture, and sign. Al-Alusi rejects al-Razi's definition as outward agreement with inward harm as linguistically overbroad.
