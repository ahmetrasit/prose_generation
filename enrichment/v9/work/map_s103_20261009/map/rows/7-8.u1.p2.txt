7:8/q20 What is the inward or moral scale? [ٱلْوَزْنُ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · inward] (turns on: Which human faculties or ethical criteria a spiritual application treats as the pans, tongue, or weighted acts.)
  7:8/q20/p1 One weighs self, thoughts, and breaths by justice and truth, becoming humble or content with divine witnessing. — reasons: Sulami reports a spiritual instruction to weigh the self by justice and thoughts and breaths by truth.
  7:8/q20/p2 Soul and spirit weigh obedience by scripture; heart and intellect weigh reward by promise and warning; gnosis weighs divine approval. — reasons: Sulami distinguishes the scales and calls flight and seeking the two pans of gnosis and secret.
  7:8/q20/p3 The inner scale has justice for its tongue, sense and intellect for pans; lasting virtue and sincere intention give it weight. — reasons: Qashani applies it at the lesser resurrection, yielding the heart's paradise of divine attributes; temporary sensations, desire, and vice lack weight.
  7:8/q20/p4 Reason, knowledge, and sound will function as balances for truth, lawful conduct, and felicity. — reasons: Tha'labi relays a moral interpretation distinguishing true and false, licit and illicit, and happiness and misery.
  7:8/q20/p5 Different balances suit bodily, spiritual, secret, and hidden ethical qualities. — reasons: Bursawi relays the Najmi interpretation and the Prophetic saying that good character is heaviest because humans emulate God's qualities.
7:8/q21 How is physical heaviness itself explained? [ثَقُلَتْ · theology] (turns on: Whether heaviness is an additional attribute or reducible to constituent parts.)
  7:8/q21/p1 Heaviness is a distinct downward tendency of a heavy object. — reasons: Abu Hashim, reported by Jishumi, treats it as a positive attribute. Tabrisi defines weight as downward force and lightness as upward force.
  7:8/q21/p2 Heaviness reduces to an object's composing parts; lightness is only absence of heaviness. — reasons: Abu Ali, reported by Jishumi, disagrees with Abu Hashim about heaviness, though both deny positive lightness.
7:8/q22 What grammatical examples elsewhere clarify وزن as a noun of measure? [ٱلْوَزْنُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether measure expressions act as verbal nouns, concrete adjectives, or independent specifications.)
  7:8/q22/p1 Accusative measure specifies a quantity independently, while وزن سبعة can be a verbal noun in the accusative or a concrete adjective. — reasons: Sibawayh uses هذا درهم وزنا and عشرون درهما for specifications not identical with their head nouns. He distinguishes named descriptions from nonidentical measures; al-Khalil analyzes وزن سبعة by verbal versus concrete meaning.
  7:8/q22/p2 Related Arab expressions permit variant syntactic analyses and unusual exemplars. — reasons: Abu al-Khattab's هذا عربي حسبه is illustrative rather than normal speech; Yunus reports عشرون أضعافها beside common عشرون أضعافا. Sibawayh treats definite ضرب الأمير as a new predicate, not an indefinite adjective, and derives سواء in هذا درهم سواء from an otherwise unattested verbal noun.
7:8/q23 What other arguments about sound and meter are associated with the word وزن in the supplied notes? [ٱلْوَزْنُ · rhetoric] (turns on: Which aspect of verbal form, if any, is treated as the basis of inimitability.)
  7:8/q23/p1 Consonant harmony alone cannot account for Quranic inimitability; arrangement of meanings is decisive. — reasons: Jurjani allows harmony as an eloquence feature or ease of pronunciation, but says sound without meaningful order could otherwise qualify, and difficulty preserving sound with sense concedes priority to sense.
  7:8/q23/p2 Recognition of the Quran's miraculous character differs by audience. — reasons: Al-Ashari distinguishes immediate perception of appearance from inferred recognition of miracle; Burhan says non-Arabs infer it, while eloquent Arabs directly perceive inability to match it.
  7:8/q23/p3 Quranic miracle accounts based on meter, eloquence, unseen reports, or lack of contradiction have different objections. — reasons: Alusi rejects unusual meter by citing metrical verses and Musaylima, eloquence by uncertain comparisons, unseen reports by lack of a threshold, and no-contradiction by 36:69 beside metrical verses. He reports the no-contradiction view and says inimitability itself is certain.
7:8/q24 What separate reading and lexical comparisons mention the name al-Wazn? [ٱلْوَزْنُ · readings] (turns on: These are distinct comparative uses in the supplied excerpts, not competing readings of 7:8.)
  7:8/q24/p1 A hamza can be prolonged or pronounced between full realization and substitution in an unrelated reading. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari reports several authorities for hamza plus madd in أأعجمي, favors bayna bayn as al-Dani's wording and analogy, and notes Dhu al-Rumma's meter supports long or intermediate readings, not substitution.
  7:8/q24/p2 Al-Wazn is a star name in an Arabic example of uncertain identification. — reasons: Bint al-Shati cites al-Wazn and Hadar sometimes confused with Suhayl to explain محلف; she distinguishes Qur'anic حلف, used for false oaths, from أقسم and argues dictionaries' synonymy misses usage.
