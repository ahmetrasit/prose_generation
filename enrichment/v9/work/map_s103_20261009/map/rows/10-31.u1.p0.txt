## THE CURRENT MAP
10:31/q01 What is rizq, and why is God called the Provider? [يَرْزُقُكُم · meaning]
  10:31/q01/p1 Rizq is a continuing usable gift or benefit; God alone is the Provider because He creates it. — reasons: Jishumi says the recipient may use it and others cannot withhold it; the divine title is exclusive as Lord is, despite ordinary possessive usage. Qushayri grounds God's provision in His sole creation.
10:31/q02 What provision comes from heaven and earth? [يَرْزُقُكُم مِّنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether sky and earth name rain and vegetation, wider causes, spiritual gifts, or their inhabitants.)
  10:31/q02/p1 Rain comes from heaven and plants or crops from earth. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and the commentators in Wahidi, and Bg and Ts in Study Quran, give this pairing. Razi says animal diets ultimately depend on plants, since endless animal consumption is impossible; Ibn Kathir cites 80:27–31 and 67:21 for the rain-fed produce. Ibn Ashur includes grain, fruit and pasture. Abu Hayyan calls these essential to life and a gift from upper and lower worlds. Muqatil, Baghawi, Ibn Atiyya and others join the pairing to the larger proof.
  10:31/q02/p2 The provision covers the full range of heavenly and earthly causes and benefits. — reasons: Jishumi reports al-Asamm's clouds, rain, celestial bodies and vegetation. Biqai adds other sky benefits and other means of life from earth. Alusi names rain and sun above and materials below, the earth receiving what heaven supplies. Kuranyolu adds warmth, resources and habitats, and says sustenance matters because other resources help only while life continues. Kashshaf calls it God's abundant favor and widened mercy; Baydawi permits both sources to be taken broadly.
  10:31/q02/p3 Provision also includes bodily and spiritual gifts. — reasons: Qushayri distinguishes bodily obedience or failure from spiritual connection, heedlessness, punishment or respite; for spirits he names realities of intimacy as provision. Asad connects physical and spiritual sustenance with heaven, earth, hearing and sight; Study Quran reports this from Q.
  10:31/q02/p4 Provision may be understood as coming from inhabitants of heaven and earth, with a noun omitted. — reasons: Baydawi proposes the inhabitants as a reading with ellipsis; Samin and Alusi explain the required omitted ahl or possessive construction.
10:31/q03 What does min in 'from heaven and earth' express? [مِّنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether min directly marks origin or depends on an omitted expression for inhabitants.)
  10:31/q03/p1 It marks the source of provision. — reasons: Abu Hayyan says min indicates the origin of the essential provision and the generosity of both worlds. Samin and Alusi allow this straightforward construction; Alusi sees no need to change it.
  10:31/q03/p2 It may be partitive, with an implied possessive referring to the inhabitants. — reasons: Samin requires omitted ahl; Alusi describes an implicit idafa. Alusi objects that the verse does not explicitly place God among the inhabitants of heaven and earth.
  10:31/q03/p3 It may explain a kind, after an omitted word for inhabitants or provisions. — reasons: Samin says the reading of kind needs omitted ahl; Alusi describes an explanatory min after an ellipsis.
10:31/q04 How does am introduce the question about hearing and sight? [أَمَّن · grammar] (turns on: The grammatical status of man and whether am is disconnected, with the shared rhetorical effect of moving to a new proof.)
  10:31/q04/p1 It transfers the argument to another question without cancelling the first. — reasons: Abu Hayyan requires an interrogative hamza for am to amount to bal plus interrogation. Samin calls it a Qur'anic transfer rather than cancellation; Alusi and Ibn Ashur likewise treat it as transition.
  10:31/q04/p2 It is disconnected am meaning bal alone, followed by the explicit interrogative man. — reasons: Samin says neither a preceding interrogative hamza nor equivalence licenses connected am; compare 27:84. He still describes the rhetorical move as a transfer.
  10:31/q04/p3 Am can precede man because man is not inherently interrogative and stands for the interrogative alif. — reasons: Akhfash says am supplies an indispensable sense. He extends the explanation to am after hal: interrogation originally belongs to alif, and a cited poem has hal ... am hal.
10:31/q05 What does God's ownership of hearing and sight mean? [يَمْلِكُ ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether ownership specifies creation, protection, ongoing control, or a spiritual capacity; Abu Hayyan disputes how much the wording alone specifies.)
  10:31/q05/p1 He creates, forms, and gives these faculties. — reasons: Wahidi, Qurtubi and others take ownership as creation or gift. Kashshaf, Baydawi and Nasafi stress their remarkable formation. Alusi prefers creation because the sequence joins creative power to provision, citing 35:3; Ibn Kathir compares 67:23 and 6:46, and Study Quran 46:26, 23:78, 32:9 and 67:23. Ibn Ashur calls them subtle and useful. Abu Hayyan reports Zamakhshari's creation gloss but cautions that its detail is not entailed by the words.
  10:31/q05/p2 He protects these delicate organs from harm. — reasons: Kashshaf, Baydawi, Nasafi and Alusi mention their vulnerability to frequent injury. Abu Hayyan reports this from Zamakhshari but says that such a nuance does not follow from the wording.
  10:31/q05/p3 He governs their operation and can strengthen, preserve, or remove them. — reasons: Tabari and Ibn Kathir say God can take them away; Jishumi reports Abu Muslim's granting and removal. Abu Hayyan says hearing enables perception and sight surveys creation, and that both stand for the senses generally as the noblest. Elmalili calls them means of using provision. Tabatabai and Kuranyolu say sensory equipment lets people benefit from provision; without it they would perish. Kuranyolu says the senses work under divine laws. Abu Hayyan resists reading more specific creative or protective details from the wording.
  10:31/q05/p4 He enables reception of admonition and lessons, and spiritual sight or blindness. — reasons: Jishumi reports Abu Muslim's moral interpretation. Qushayri says monotheism perfects some people's sight while others are blinded to realization. Elmalili connects the senses with recognizing truth, certainty and external reality, citing 3:18, 6:103 and 17:85; he says true perception is a divine act and invokes the sacred hadith of God becoming a devoted servant's hearing.
10:31/q06 Why is hearing singular but sights plural? [ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the contrast is chiefly in the diversity of perceptions or in the nouns' forms and reference.)
  10:31/q06/p1 Hearing is shared or uniform, while seeing is more diverse. — reasons: Biqai says most people share hearing equally; Elmalili says seeing is more various or numerous. Suyuti says sound is one kind whereas sight takes diverse colors and forms.
  10:31/q06/p2 Hearing is a collective verbal noun, while plural sights makes universal reference explicit. — reasons: Suyuti calls hearing verbal-noun-like but sight an organ name. Ibn Ashur says singular hearing denotes the species, whereas singular sight could be mistaken for one individual's sight; he contrasts 17:36 and cites 6:46.
10:31/q07 How does God bring the living from the dead and the dead from the living? [يُخْرِجُ ٱلْحَىَّ مِنَ ٱلْمَيِّتِ وَيُخْرِجُ ٱلْمَيِّتَ مِنَ ٱلْحَىِّ · interpretation] (turns on: The scope of life and death: biological generation, simple animation, faith, knowledge, or the broader human and functional sense; Razi and Alusi favor biological examples, Tabatabai the broader contextual one.)
  10:31/q07/p1 Living beings and plants arise from nonliving matter or reproductive material, which in turn comes from living things. — reasons: Muqatil names human life from semen. Wahidi lists plant from earth, human from sperm, bird from egg, stalk from grain and palm from seed, plus reverses. Baghawi, Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Biqai and Ibn Ashur add drops, eggs, seeds, embryos, chicks and crops. Razi says most favor this as nearer the literal sense; Alusi prefers the animal/drop reading. Ibn Ashur says definite nouns denote species. Elmalili adds organic life from food and inorganic matter; he argues changes between opposed natures reveal power beyond regular nature. Ibn Kathir holds the general wording includes all earlier interpretations. Study Quran points to 30:19 and 6:95.
  10:31/q07/p2 God gives life and death or reverses living and lifeless states generally. — reasons: Jishumi and Baydawi state the direct life/death reading. Ibn Kathir says the verse's generality embraces the interpretations, while Kuranyolu says living beings emerge from lifeless things and become lifeless.
  10:31/q07/p3 A believer arises from an unbeliever and an unbeliever from a believer. — reasons: Qushayri gives this interpretation; Wahidi, Qurtubi, Razi, Nasafi and Alusi include it. Razi says most prefer the literal reproductive example instead; Alusi likewise prefers that first explanation.
  10:31/q07/p4 The contrast can encompass scholars from their opposites and other spiritual reversals. — reasons: Nasafi explicitly adds scholars to animals, chicks, crops and believers emerging from their opposites; Tabatabai connects human life with reason and religion in this context.
  10:31/q07/p5 In context it is the emergence of reasonable, religious human life from its opposite. — reasons: Tabatabai argues the sequence progresses from human sustenance through human senses to human life. He favors the broad functional sense of productive life arising from an unproductive source and the reverse, over a merely biological restriction.
