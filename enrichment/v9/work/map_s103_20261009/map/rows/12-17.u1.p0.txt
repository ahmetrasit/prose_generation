## THE CURRENT MAP
12:17/q01 What happened when the brothers brought this story to Jacob? [قَالُوا۟ يَٰٓأَبَانَآ · reports] (turns on: The reports give different amounts of dialogue and different accounts of Jacob's physical reaction.)
  12:17/q01/p1 They returned at night, feigning grief, and told Jacob they had raced, left Joseph with their things, and lost him to a wolf. — reasons: Muqatil places their return at the evening prayer and calls the outing hunting; Ibn Kathir says they had already put Joseph in the well. Qurtubi says darkness made their excuse easier and cites a saying about favors at night and excuses for daytime wrongs. Bursevi says Jacob asked after them, their sheep, and Joseph, and they said the matter was worse.
  12:17/q01/p2 Jacob asked what had happened and where Joseph was, heard the wolf story, cried out, and asked for Joseph's shirt. — reasons: Thalabi's al-Suddi says Jacob first feared harm to the brothers or sheep; Study Quran's Bg says they told him the sheep were safe before his question. Qurtubi gives the dialogue and his request for the shirt.
  12:17/q01/p3 Jacob fainted at the news and did not revive until dawn. — reasons: Qurtubi's al-Suddi and Ibn Hibban report Judah found no sign of life, lamented losing brother and father, and Reuben supported Jacob on waking; Wahb reports Judah checking breath and pulse and rebuking the brothers. Study Quran's Q adds that they poured water on Jacob.
12:17/q02 What did the brothers mean by نَسْتَبِقُ? [نَسْتَبِقُ · interpretation] (turns on: The text names no equipment or venue; the attributed shooting reading, reports, word form, and practical purpose supply the proposed details.)
  12:17/q02/p1 They raced one another on foot to see who was fastest. — reasons: Al-Suddi, Muqatil, al-Jubba'i and others are cited; Wahidi and Razi answer the objection that adult running is childish by its use in war and protecting flocks, as do Qushayri and Alusi. Tabatabai locates it in open country; Study Quran's R says wolves had taken sheep. Islahi cites the race to the door at 12:25.
  12:17/q02/p2 They competed in archery or shooting. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba notes sabq can mean a prize; Mawradi derives the form from sabāq. Thalabi, Wahidi, Tabarsi, Qurtubi, and Alusi cite a reading ascribed to ʿAbd Allah, with forms recorded as nantaḍil, nantaṣil, or nintadil, meaning shooting. Wahidi and Razi cite the Prophetic prize rule and comparison of arrow distance. Alusi favors archery on the reading's evidence; Qurtubi reports Azhari's distinction between arrow contests and horse races.
  12:17/q02/p3 They went hunting or competed to catch game. — reasons: Muqatil calls the outing hunting; Mawradi offers catching game as one possible contest, as do Ibn Jawzi, Abu Hayyan, and Alusi.
  12:17/q02/p4 They competed at practical work, including watering, herding, or gathering wood. — reasons: Mawradi proposes their work; Abu Hayyan and Alusi mention divided farm or flock tasks.
  12:17/q02/p5 They could have raced on horseback. — reasons: Abu Nasr al-Qushayri lists horseback competition alongside archery and running.
  12:17/q02/p6 The form means striving for precedence in a mutual race; iftiʿāl and tafāʿul can overlap. — reasons: Kashshaf, Baydawi, Nasafi, Samin, Alusi, and Zamakhshari as quoted by Tabatabai compare the derived forms; Raghib defines sabq as preceding in movement. Tabatabai says each agent's self-directed effort entails comparison, citing good deeds and the door race against al-Manar's proposed distinction. Jishumi calls istibaq racing to get ahead.
  12:17/q02/p7 Istibāq specifically means trying to get ahead, in distinction from reciprocal racing. — reasons: Al-Manar applies the distinction here and contrasts racing to good deeds and to the door; Tabatabai rejects it because those examples also involve striving to get ahead.
12:17/q03 What does the verse imply about the legitimacy and purpose of racing? [نَسْتَبِقُ · law]
  12:17/q03/p1 Racing and archery are legitimate practices, especially as training for war or defense. — reasons: Jishumi reports permissibility of foot racing and wrestling while noting debate over payment and binding contracts. Tabarsi says archery is permitted by consensus and horse and camel races by his school. Ibn al-ʿArabi cites the Prophet's foot and horse races and his two races with Aisha; Qurtubi cites Salama ibn al-Akwaʿ's race reported by Muslim, the Prophet's horse races in Malik from al-Hafya and Thaniyyat al-Wadaʿ, and travelers' archery in Muslim. Bukhari's al-ʿAdba report adds the Prophet's lesson that God lowers worldly things that rise.
12:17/q04 Which contests may carry a prize or wager? [نَسْتَبِقُ · law] (turns on: The disagreement concerns the scope of the prize hadith, military benefit, and whether a paid contest becomes gambling.)
  12:17/q04/p1 Prizes are confined to archery, camel racing, and horse racing. — reasons: Qurtubi cites consensus, Abu Hurayra's hadith in al-Nasa'i, and reliance by Hijazi and Iraqi jurists; Shafiʿi calls wagering elsewhere gambling. Wahidi and Razi cite the same threefold Prophetic rule. The purported addition 'or wing' was, Qurtubi says, fabricated by Abu al-Bakhtari for al-Rashid and scholars rejected his reports.
  12:17/q04/p2 Only horses and archery may carry prizes, with horse racing preferable. — reasons: Malik grounds the restriction in military usefulness; Qurtubi observes that the hadith's wording also includes camels.
  12:17/q04/p3 Only horse racing may carry a wager. — reasons: Qurtubi attributes this to jurists who treat horse racing as the customary Arab race.
  12:17/q04/p4 Any contest may carry a wager. — reasons: Qurtubi attributes this to ʿAtaʾ but argues it would permit gambling, which is agreed to be forbidden.
  12:17/q04/p5 Foot racing and wrestling with prizes are forbidden. — reasons: Tabarsi attributes this to his school and al-Shafiʿi; Jishumi also notes disagreement over compensation.
  12:17/q04/p6 Foot racing and wrestling with prizes are allowed. — reasons: Tabarsi attributes permission to Abu Hanifa and some colleagues of his own school; Jishumi records the related dispute over compensation.
12:17/q05 What stake arrangements are permitted in a race? [نَسْتَبِقُ · law] (turns on: Whether the third entrant has a real chance to win and thereby prevents a two-person stake from being gambling.)
  12:17/q05/p1 A voluntary known prize from a donor or a one-sided stake is allowed. — reasons: Qurtubi says neither arrangement is disputed; the winner may take the one-sided stake and the loser cannot reclaim it.
  12:17/q05/p2 Two-sided stakes require a genuine third contestant who could win without risking money. — reasons: Qurtubi describes how either racer or the third entrant receives both stakes if victorious; Abu Ali ibn Khayran says the entrant's ability must be uncertain, whence muhallil. A Prophetic report and Saʿid ibn al-Musayyib support the qualifying horse, as do Shafiʿi and most scholars. Malik first dispensed with it but later required it, a view Qurtubi calls better. Without one, Qurtubi reports consensus that the wager is forbidden gambling.
  12:17/q05/p3 A qualifying third entrant is unnecessary for two-sided stakes. — reasons: Qurtubi reports Malik's earlier view; Malik later changed it and Qurtubi favors his later requirement.
12:17/q06 What makes a race or archery contest properly specified? [نَسْتَبِقُ · law]
  12:17/q06/p1 The course, competitors, and victory criterion must be specified appropriately. — reasons: Qurtubi derives from Malik's report a known distance, comparable horses, and separate distances for trained and untrained horses; he reserves the military Sunnah for horses prepared against enemies, not civil conflict. He also requires a known finish for camels, and a target, round count, and hit criterion for archery. He reports Umar's preference that the owner ride, while Qurtubi requires an adult rider; Shafiʿi and Muhammad ibn al-Hasan measure a lead by muzzle or hindquarters, also for archers. In a report of the Prophet racing Abu Bakr and Umar, Abu Bakr's second place means his horse's head reached the Prophet's horse's rear.
12:17/q07 What is the brothers' مَتَٰعِنَا? [مَتَٰعِنَا · referent]
  12:17/q07/p1 Their clothes and other belongings or baggage. — reasons: Ibn Abbas says clothes and belongings; Baghawi, Ibn Jawzi, Abu Hayyan, Qurtubi, and Study Quran's Bg emphasize garments. Abu Hayyan says they took off their clothes while competing and sees this as evidence Joseph was young rather than adolescent. Bursevi includes provisions and notes matāʿ means food containers at 12:65.
12:17/q08 Why did they say they left Joseph by the belongings? [وَتَرَكْنَا يُوسُفَ عِندَ مَتَٰعِنَا · interpretation] (turns on: The narrative leaves their stated reason for leaving him implicit; sources infer it from his placement among their things.)
  12:17/q08/p1 They present Joseph as guarding the baggage. — reasons: Muqatil and Tabarsi make him the watcher; Qurtubi also says he guarded clothes and other belongings. Mawradi says this may have been their reason for leaving him.
  12:17/q08/p2 They present the place as safe, showing care for Joseph and denying neglect. — reasons: Mawradi proposes concern for him. Alusi says clothes and provisions kept him within sight, briefly alone, unlikely to face danger; he also quotes Shaykh al-Islam on the safe setting.
