## THE CURRENT MAP
95:5/q01 What does being returned to the lowest of the low mean? [رَدَدْنَٰهُ أَسْفَلَ سَٰفِلِينَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the descent names an observable bodily reversal, a final place or form of punishment, or corruption of the human's original moral disposition; the following exception is a major test.)
  95:5/q01/p1 Return to decrepit old age, with bodily weakness and sometimes lost reason. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿIkrima, Ibrāhīm, Qatāda and others describe strength, beauty, senses, movement, voice and intellect ebbing; 16:70, 22:5 and 36:68 parallel the reversal. Al-Ṭabarī prefers this visible change because even deniers see aging, whereas they deny Hell; it proves resurrection (95:7). Al-Ḍaḥḥāk and al-Kalbī liken the elder to a child. Al-Farrāʾ and Ibn ʿAṭiyya say generic humanity need not imply every individual ages so far. Ibn Qutayba, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī and al-Baghawī compare the weak, ill and children, with senile elders lower still. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Nasafī detail bent backs, white hair, wrinkled skin, weakened senses and gait; Abū Ḥayyān calls that elaboration excessive. Bursawī cites a Persian poem; Corpus Coranicum cites Qurʾanic usage, Arabic poetry and radda against Birkeland's Hell argument. Al-Ḥākim authenticated the Ibn ʿAbbās report on reciters according to al-Durr. Ibn ʿĀshūr objects that bodily aging ill fits the emphatic oath and the sūra's purpose; Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī object that believers also age, making the apparent exception difficult.
  95:5/q01/p2 God casts the unbeliever into Hell or its lowest depths. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan, Mujāhid, Abū al-ʿĀliya, Ibn Zayd and others transmit this reading. ʿAlī describes Hell's gates as descending and being filled from below. Al-Wāḥidī, Ibn Kathīr and others cite the connected exception for believers and compare 103:2–3; al-Qushayrī says the general opening then narrows to disbelievers. Al-Nuḥḥās reports al-Ṭabarī's opposing argument: the audience witnesses aging but denies the Fire. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 4:145 for the lowest depth. Birkeland argues ordinary aging cannot explain exceptional humiliation, but Corpus Coranicum rejects his conclusion. Ibn ʿAbbās' reported poem of ʿAlī uses the phrase for the depth near Hell.
  95:5/q01/p3 The person becomes one of Hell's people, hideous in form; some reports specify the form of a pig. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Nasafī, al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī connect the ugliness to ingratitude for fine creation. Abū al-ʿĀliya specifically reports a pig form, while other descriptions say the ugliest shape without naming an animal. Al-Ālūsī favors the Judgment Day reading as most contextual. Bursawī says inner traits become outward forms at resurrection; Ibn Burjān adds an animal or vermin form in the intermediate realm and disfigurement afterward.
  95:5/q01/p4 People fall into disbelief, misguidance, and moral or spiritual baseness. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj and al-Qurṭubī compare humanity's loss and believers' exception in 103:2–3. Al-Biqāʿī says desire defeats reason, prompting idolatry, injustice and falsehood; rational people thus sink beneath unaccountable beasts and deserve punishment. Quṭb similarly contrasts animals' faithfulness to their nature with humans' capacity to rise even beyond angels, citing the Ascension. Ibn ʿĀshūr locates the baseness especially in belief: idolatry, deifying time and denying the Creator despite 51:21, and lists human sacrifice, infanticide and burning widows among its moral results. Al-Qāshānī depicts the veiled soul imprisoned in nature. Al-Iṣlāḥī invokes choice, innate guidance and consequent divine humiliation. Samarrai also includes the unbeliever's distressed earthly life, citing 20:124 and 22:31. Asad ascribes decline to human acts and omissions; al-Biqāʿī and Ibn ʿĀshūr stress the initial sound disposition.
  95:5/q01/p5 For the disbeliever, decline runs from youth to age and finally to Hell. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās gives this successive course and adduces a line attributed to ʿAlī using the low expression for a depth by Hell.
  95:5/q01/p6 The lowest are idolaters, while the human in the preceding clause is Muḥammad. — reasons: Al-Durr explicitly marks this Ibn ʿAbbās report weak; it also calls the four caliphs the following exception and assigns geographical senses to the oaths.
  95:5/q01/p7 Human arrogance is brought low through bodily filth that recalls created dependence. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī's reported explanation compares pretensions to divinity with Pharaoh's claim in 79:24; humiliation restores awareness of God's decree.
  95:5/q01/p8 The basest person exploits another person's religion for gain. — reasons: In Mālik's report, Rabīʿa approved this answer; Ibn ʿĀshūr applies it to polytheist leaders who secured power by misleading their followers into idol worship.
  95:5/q01/p9 The phrase can embrace age, Hell, moral decline, and distress together. — reasons: Samarrai says the wording admits all transmitted readings; the verse endings intentionally carry multiple senses. Elmalılı traces a broad reversal of purity, health, knowledge and dignity into weakness, ignorance, pain, death and Hell.
95:5/q02 What does ثُمَّ indicate here? [ثُمَّ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the particle locates a later event in time, emphasizes the rank difference, or does both.)
  95:5/q02/p1 A delay in time after humanity's excellent creation. — reasons: Elmalılı calls this the ordinary sense and says every human must share some measure of the best form before decline. Samarrai includes an interval before the fall.
  95:5/q02/p2 A difference in rank: reversal from finest form to lowest is astonishing. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says changing an existing state is more remarkable than originating one. Al-Ālūsī and Samarrai also allow rank succession.
  95:5/q02/p3 It marks both temporal delay and a vast difference in rank. — reasons: Samarrai explicitly joins the interval and the contrast in status.
95:5/q03 How does رَدَدْنَٰهُ construe the descent? [رَدَدْنَٰهُ أَسْفَلَ سَٰفِلِينَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether radda denotes a change of state with two objects, a one-object verb plus circumstantial description, or motion to a place.)
  95:5/q03/p1 Radda means make or render, taking the pronoun and asfal as two objects. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī cites a poetic line for the construction. Ibn ʿĀshūr treats it like a verb of becoming and says the word literally means returning something to an owner or place but here figuratively changes its state.
  95:5/q03/p2 Radda takes one object and asfal describes the resulting condition circumstantially. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī proposes state change; al-Samīn allows a circumstantial modifier of the object. Bursawī calls this analysis clearer.
  95:5/q03/p3 Radda means returning or placing someone in a location, with asfal naming an omitted place or adverbial destination. — reasons: Al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī supply a place; Ibn ʿĀshūr allows an elided ilā or a place adverb, and calls divine agency literal here. Al-Iṣlāḥī calls asfal a place adverb and sāfilīn circumstantial with the object, reflecting a person's inclination to disgrace.
  95:5/q03/p4 Asfal is 'the lowest' and sāfilīn describes those lowly people or ranks. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr treats asfal as a comparative or superlative annexed to debased people. Al-Ālūsī finds a masculine rational plural awkward for places; rhyme and personifying places do not convince him, so he suggests inhabitants of descending Hell levels. The Study Quran reports a parsing in which 'lowly' describes the condemned person's abasement.
95:5/q04 To whom does the pronoun in رَدَدْنَٰهُ refer? [رَدَدْنَٰهُ · referent] (turns on: Whether al-insān in 95:4 is a generic human or a specific person, and whether a general opening narrows to unbelievers.)
  95:5/q04/p1 The human being mentioned in the previous verse, used generically for humankind. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr explicitly identifies the antecedent and says its two earlier senses carry through. Samarrai says the wording does not specify unbelievers or another group; al-Farrāʾ and others treat the singular human as collective. Some of humanity undergoes senility, not necessarily every person.
  95:5/q04/p2 The unbeliever specifically. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī says the opening is general but the ending concerns disbelievers; Ibn Kathīr ties the Fire to failure to obey God and follow messengers. The following exception for believers supports this restriction. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports a view restricting al-insān to unbelievers and thus making the exception disconnected.
  95:5/q04/p3 Muḥammad specifically. — reasons: This occurs in al-Durr's explicitly weak report, which then identifies the lowest as idolaters and the exception as four caliphs.
95:5/q05 Why is سَٰفِلِينَ plural after a singular human? [سَٰفِلِينَ · grammar]
  95:5/q05/p1 The singular al-insān names the human kind collectively, so plural agreement follows its meaning. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ says singular asfal sāfil would also be grammatical and gives shifts of number at 39:33 and 42:48. Al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Wāḥidī and al-Iṣlāḥī give the same explanation; al-Rāzī's full text also cites 2:106. Al-Ṭabarī notes that this collective sense permits believers to be excepted like 103:2.
95:5/q06 How is أَسْفَلَ سَٰفِلِينَ read? [أَسْفَلَ سَٰفِلِينَ · readings] (turns on: Presence or absence of the definite article in the transmitted wording.)
  95:5/q06/p1 The usual reading has sāfilīn without the definite article. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and his fuller text attribute the indefinite reading to the majority.
