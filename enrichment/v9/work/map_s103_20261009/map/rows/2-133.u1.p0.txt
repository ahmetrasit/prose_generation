## THE CURRENT MAP
2:133/q01 How does the opening am work? [أَمْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether am is disconnected, connected through ellipsis, assertive, or a simple question.)
  2:133/q01/p1 Disconnected am means 'rather' plus a question that reproaches the audience and denies their presence. — reasons: Majaz cites al-Akhtal after completed speech. Zajjaj, Wahidi, Jishumi, Tabarsi, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, Samīn, Bursevi and others explain the shift. Razi contrasts disconnected 32:1–3 with connected 79:27.
  2:133/q01/p2 Connected am supplies an omitted first alternative about the claim that the prophets were Jews or about absence at the deathbed. — reasons: Wahidi cites 2:108; Zamakhshari prefers this for Jewish addressees. Razi, Biqaʿi and Alusi offer alternatives. Abu Hayyan and Samīn reject deletion of the first coordinate as unattested, distinguishing deletion of the second and rare omission after other conjunctions.
  2:133/q01/p3 It means 'rather' alone, asserting that the audience or its ancestors witnessed the testament. — reasons: Abu Hayyan reports this reading; Asad says the construction has no interrogative force. Majaz also offers 'rather, you saw.'
  2:133/q01/p4 It functions as interrogative hamza or hal. — reasons: Abu ʿUbayda cites al-Akhtal's Wāsiṭ line. A Yemeni dialect is alleged; Abu Hayyan and Samīn say grammarians do not attest it and call Tabari's mid-speech account unusual.
2:133/q02 Whom does the opening challenge address? [كُنتُمْ · referent] (turns on: Which audience the second-person pronoun denotes.)
  2:133/q02/p1 The People of the Book, especially Jews, with Christians included in some explanations. — reasons: Rabiʿ, Abu al-ʿAliya and Hasan identify them; many authors relate their claim that Jacob bequeathed Judaism.
  2:133/q02/p2 Believers are addressed and learn the unseen event through revelation. — reasons: Kashshaf, Baydawi, Nasafi and Alusi report this; Alusi calls that knowledge a prophetic sign.
  2:133/q02/p3 The people of Mecca are addressed. — reasons: Abu al-ʿAliya's report names Meccans.
2:133/q03 What does the witness question prove? [كُنتُمْ شُهَدَآءَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether lack of presence or inherited testimony is the rebuke's premise.)
  2:133/q03/p1 They did not witness Jacob's testament and cannot claim he prescribed their later religions. — reasons: Jishumi and Tabarsi say the question form forces admission. Ibn ʿAtiyya and Abu Hayyan note missing eyewitness, transmitted, or rational proof; Biqaʿi says their scripture refutes them. Al-Hasan likewise says Jews, Christians and others were absent, though the sons affirmed the covenant.
  2:133/q03/p2 Their ancestors witnessed or transmitted Jacob's Islamic testament, so descendants knowingly misattribute a religion to him. — reasons: Ibn ʿAtiyya, Zamakhshari, Razi, Abu Hayyan, Alusi and Kuranyolu connect this to the alternative reading of am.
2:133/q04 What occasion of revelation is reported? [كُنتُمْ شُهَدَآءَ · reports] (turns on: Whether the reported Jewish claim is an established occasion.)
  2:133/q04/p1 Jews told Muhammad that Jacob instructed his sons to follow Judaism at death. — reasons: Muqatil, Thaʿlabi, Wahidi, Baghawi, Ibn al-Jawzi and others report this occasion.
  2:133/q04/p2 The Jewish-claim occasion was not located and is treated with reservation. — reasons: Al-Suyuti could not find it, as Alusi notes; one believers-address reading discounts it.
2:133/q05 What does shuhada mean? [شُهَدَآءَ · meaning] (turns on: The lexical scope of shuhada.)
  2:133/q05/p1 People present as witnesses, opposite of absent. — reasons: Wahidi, Jishumi and Tabarsi explain the root and related courtyard or gathering usages; Alusi allows 'witnesses' or 'present.'
  2:133/q05/p2 It may also mean martyrs. — reasons: Samīn mentions the lexical alternative.
2:133/q06 How is shuhada parsed? [شُهَدَآءَ · grammar]
  2:133/q06/p1 It is the predicate of kana and is diptote. — reasons: Nahhas explains the final plural feminine pattern; Qurtubi also identifies the predicate.
2:133/q07 What does death's arrival mean? [حَضَرَ يَعْقُوبَ ٱلْمَوْتُ · meaning]
  2:133/q07/p1 The causes or signs of death approached Jacob while speech remained possible. — reasons: Ibn ʿAtiyya and Qurtubi argue that speech at the instant of death is impossible. Abu Hayyan cites 14:17 and a poet's 'I am death'; Samīn calls the expression metonymic.
2:133/q08 What form and usage does hadara have? [حَضَرَ · meaning]
  2:133/q08/p1 It denotes being present or witnessing, as opposed to absence. — reasons: Wahidi gives hadrat for a courtyard; Abu Hayyan notes an anomalous imperfect pattern.
2:133/q09 How is hadara read? [حَضَرَ · readings] (turns on: Vowel of the dad.)
  2:133/q09/p1 The familiar form is hadara, with fathah on dad. — reasons: The reported kasra is a dialect alternative.
  2:133/q09/p2 A dialect reads hadira, with kasra on dad. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and Samīn call the damma imperfect unusual, perhaps blending verbal patterns.
2:133/q10 How are Jacob and death inflected? [يَعْقُوبَ ٱلْمَوْتُ · grammar] (turns on: Which noun is object and which is subject.)
  2:133/q10/p1 Jacob is a fronted accusative object; death is the nominative subject. — reasons: Sibawayh says Jacob receives emphasis as the item requiring explanation; Ibn ʿAtiyya, Abu Hayyan and Samīn agree.
  2:133/q10/p2 Some reverse the cases. — reasons: Samīn reports Jacob nominative and death accusative.
2:133/q11 Is Yaqub an Arabic derived name? [يَعْقُوبَ · grammar]
  2:133/q11/p1 The prophet's name is foreign and indeclinable. — reasons: Abu Hayyan distinguishes a declinable Arabic partridge word and rejects derivations from following a twin or numerous descendants, which would require declension.
2:133/q12 What does the image of death coming add? [حَضَرَ يَعْقُوبَ ٱلْمَوْتُ · rhetoric]
  2:133/q12/p1 It portrays death as an absent thing that must arrive. — reasons: Abu Hayyan calls it a striking euphemism and cites a prayer calling death the best absent thing awaited.
2:133/q13 How does the second idh relate to the first? [إِذْ حَضَرَ يَعْقُوبَ ٱلْمَوْتُ إِذْ قَالَ لِبَنِيهِ · grammar] (turns on: Apposition or direct adverbial governance.)
  2:133/q13/p1 The second idh apposes and explains the first. — reasons: Akhfash, Tabari, Zajjaj, Wahidi, Jishumi, Ibn ʿAtiyya, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Abu Hayyan, Samīn and Alusi give this; Alusi calls it inclusion, and grammarians differ over repeating the governor.
  2:133/q13/p2 The second idh is directly governed by hadara. — reasons: Jishumi, Tabarsi, Qaffal, Nasafi and Samīn allow this; Qaffal says both clauses mark one time.
  2:133/q13/p3 The second idh is an adverb governed by shuhada. — reasons: Jishumi and Nasafi report it; Tabarsi places the first idh under shuhada, while Qaffal distinguishes the witnesses' and Jacob's times.
2:133/q14 What happened before Jacob questioned his sons? [إِذْ قَالَ لِبَنِيهِ · reports] (turns on: Which reported circumstances preceded the exchange.)
  2:133/q14/p1 In Egypt he saw idol and fire or celestial worship and gathered his sons out of fear. — reasons: Al-Kalbi is cited by Thaʿlabi and Baghawi; Qaffal, Abu Hayyan and Alusi give variants.
  2:133/q14/p2 Offered the prophets' choice of life or death, Jacob chose death but requested time to advise sons and descendants. — reasons: ʿAtaʾ's report is cited by Thaʿlabi, Wahidi and Baghawi; Ibn ʿAtiyya, Qurtubi and Abu Hayyan give variants, including twelve sons.
  2:133/q14/p3 Jacob's sons themselves worshipped idols and fire at death. — reasons: Razi transmits Ibn ʿAbbas's version through the Qadi, who rejects it because their prompt answer and the Quran imply settled righteousness.
2:133/q15 Why did Jacob ask about worship? [مَا تَعْبُدُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Formal worship or one's wider orientation to God.)
  2:133/q15/p1 He tested and renewed his sons' pledge to continue monotheism. — reasons: Ibn ʿAtiyya, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi, Alusi, Elmalılı and Kuranyolu stress the continuing charge; Razi and Abu Hayyan see the prophets' concern for children's religion.
  2:133/q15/p2 He urged all acts to seek God alone and feared worldly distraction more than idols. — reasons: Abu Hayyan attributes this to Qaffal; Bursevi cites Raghib, 14:35 and the Mathnawi's account of heedlessness.
2:133/q16 Why is ma used rather than man? [مَا · rhetoric] (turns on: Breadth of objects or inquiry into qualities.)
  2:133/q16/p1 Ma covers unknown, rational and nonrational objects, including idols, and tests the sons without suggesting an answer. — reasons: Kashshaf contrasts man; Ibn ʿAtiyya names idols, fire, sun and stones. Tabarsi, Qurtubi and Biqaʿi stress exclusion of inanimate gods; Taftazani says man applies after rational identity becomes known.
  2:133/q16/p2 Ma asks the nature or qualities of what is worshipped. — reasons: Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan and Samīn compare asking whether Zayd is jurist or physician.
2:133/q17 What is ma's grammatical role? [مَا تَعْبُدُونَ · grammar] (turns on: Accusative object or nominative subject.)
  2:133/q17/p1 It is the fronted accusative direct object of taʿbudun. — reasons: Nahhas, Abu Hayyan and Samīn explain the interrogative's initial position.
  2:133/q17/p2 It is nominative with an omitted resumptive pronoun. — reasons: Alusi says object parsing loses the progression from asking what a thing is to identifying who it is.
2:133/q18 What does 'after me' mean and add? [مِنۢ بَعْدِى · interpretation]
  2:133/q18/p1 It means after Jacob's death, when his sons must keep the charge without him. — reasons: Ibn ʿAtiyya, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan and Samīn give the temporal sense. Biqaʿi notes successors may stray and sons' lives do not cover all later time.
