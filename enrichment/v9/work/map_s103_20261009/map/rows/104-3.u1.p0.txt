## THE CURRENT MAP
104:3/q01 What does yaḥsabu mean here? [يَحْسَبُ · meaning]
  104:3/q01/p1 He supposes or falsely estimates that wealth has secured immortality. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭi distinguishes literal counting, figurative estimation, planning and divine reckoning; she says the Qurʾānic three-letter verb marks mistaken supposition, comparing the Queen of Sheba's mistake (27:44), 18:9 and 76:19. She surveys rebuking interrogatives at 29:2, 45:21, 47:29, 23:115, 29:4, 2:214, 3:142 and 9:16, and says all thirty-one open-vowel imperfect occurrences warn against misestimation. Al-Sāmarrāʾī distinguishes ḥasaba-yaḥsubu, 'to count,' from ḥasiba, 'to suppose'; Bint al-Shāṭi also notes ḥasab can mean inherited distinction.
104:3/q02 How is yaḥsabu read? [يَحْسَبُ · readings] (turns on: The sīn vowel and the attested reciters or dialects; the notes say the sense is unchanged.)
  104:3/q02/p1 With fatḥa on the sīn, yaḥsabu. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports this from al-Aʿraj, Abū Jaʿfar and Shayba; Ibn ʿĀshūr names Ibn ʿĀmir, ʿĀṣim, Ḥamza and Abū Jaʿfar. Sibawayh calls ḥasiba-yaḥsabu sound and more analogical, though among exceptional imperfect vowels; he contrasts the three patterns shared by transitive and intransitive verbs with intransitive faʿula-yafʿulu, adduces al-Farazdaq's yanʿamu, faḍila-yafḍulu and māta-tamūtu, and relays al-Khalīl's judgment of kāda-takādu as irregular. His discussion of hamzated roots compares abā-yaʾbā with qaraʾa-yaqraʾu and ḥasiba-yaḥsabu, marks jabā-yajbā and qalā-yaqlā weakly transmitted, and cites better-attested ʿamara-yaʿmuru and yaḥzuru; he also discusses amara-yaʾmuru, akala-yaʾkulu, waʿadahu-yaʿiduhu and weak ʿaḍaḍtu-taʿaḍḍu by analogy. Al-Sāmarrāʾī calls this form more common in ordinary language. Both vocalizations mean 'supposes,' according to Qiraat-ER.
  104:3/q02/p2 With kasra on the sīn, yaḥsibu. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās describes it as the Prophet's dialect on the rare faʿila-yafʿalu pattern. Al-Durr cites Jābir's report of the Prophet's pronunciation, authenticated by Ibn Ḥibbān and al-Ḥākim and cited by Ibn Mardawayh and al-Khaṭīb. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it the majority reading and one of two dialectal forms; Qiraat-ER calls both canonical, with the same sense. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says every mutawātir reading may be recited in prayer and cites al-Jazarī's collection of ten in al-Nashr.
104:3/q03 How does the yaḥsabu sentence attach to what precedes? [يَحْسَبُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the clause resumes, supplies the hoarder's circumstance, or implies a satirical question.)
  104:3/q03/p1 It is a new, independent sentence. — reasons: Al-Samīn, al-Biqāʿī, al-Ālūsī and Ibn ʿĀshūr allow resumption; Ibn ʿĀshūr says an independent assertion can convey denial and taunt the hoarder.
  104:3/q03/p2 It is a circumstantial clause describing the hoarder. — reasons: Al-Samīn says it can describe the subject of jamaʿa; Ibn ʿĀshūr says it satirically portrays the slanderer's greed.
  104:3/q03/p3 An omitted interrogative makes it a taunting question. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the implicit question expresses mockery or amazement. Neuwirth once proposed inserting an explicit interrogative because four other early Meccan uses have one (75:3, 75:36, 90:5, 90:7) and 83:1–6 is a woe followed by a polemical question; Corpus Coranicum says she later seems to withdraw that emendation.
104:3/q04 What does the anna clause supply to yaḥsabu? [يَحْسَبُ أَنَّ مَالَهُۥٓ أَخْلَدَهُۥ · grammar]
  104:3/q04/p1 The anna clause and its governed words fill both objects of yaḥsabu. — reasons: This is al-Naḥḥās's syntactic analysis.
104:3/q05 Why repeat mālahu explicitly? [مَالَهُۥٓ · rhetoric]
  104:3/q05/p1 Naming wealth where a pronoun could stand reinforces the point. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī and his full version give this as emphatic wording.
104:3/q06 What does akhladahu mean as a verb? [أَخْلَدَهُۥ · meaning]
  104:3/q06/p1 It means to cause someone to remain or live forever, or for a very long time. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī and al-Ālūsī equate akhlada with khallada; al-Ālūsī allows unlimited or very long duration. Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies the hamza as causative. Bint al-Shāṭi defines khuld as permanent endurance and says Qurʾānic worldly uses express illusion or deny human permanence: 21:34, 21:8, 26:129; she counts 87 root occurrences. Labīd's line says survivors are neither left nor immortal; Neuwirth cites Ṭarafa's Muʿallaqa 54 for the pre-Islamic immortality motif.
104:3/q07 What time does the past form akhladahu express? [أَخْلَدَهُۥ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether completed wording projects a presumed future certainty or describes wealth's support up to now.)
  104:3/q07/p1 It presents the hoped-for future as already achieved. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ, al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī compare saying a sinner has perished or entered Hell before the consequence arrives. Al-Ṭabrisī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī say yaḥsabu makes the past verb prospective. Al-Rāzī, al-Bursevī and Ibn ʿĀshūr say the completed form depicts the hoarder's certainty as if survival were guaranteed; al-Ālūsī calls it intensification. Al-Ṭabrisī also says the cause can be described as an accomplished result before death occurs.
  104:3/q07/p2 It retains a past sense: wealth has sustained or prolonged his life so far. — reasons: Al-Samīn expressly allows an actual past meaning. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān say wealth has supported and protected his life, so he expects that support to continue; al-Qurṭubī reports the idea that it gave him life already, while also explaining a past-for-future use.
104:3/q08 Who is the agent and object of akhladahu? [مَالَهُۥٓ أَخْلَدَهُۥ · grammar] (turns on: The grammatical subject and object of the causative verb; al-Ālūsī dismisses the inversion.)
  104:3/q08/p1 Wealth is the imagined agent and the man is its object. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr explains the causative hamza this way; the normal interpretation throughout the commentaries takes wealth as what supposedly preserves its owner.
  104:3/q08/p2 The man is the agent and wealth the object: he thinks he can keep his wealth forever. — reasons: ʿIṣām al-Dīn proposes this inversion; al-Ālūsī rejects it. He adds that mishap or death will take the money, and reports the saying that a miser's wealth should expect a mishap or an heir.
104:3/q09 What does the claim that wealth made him immortal mean? [مَالَهُۥٓ أَخْلَدَهُۥ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the man's claim is literal, an illusion shown by his conduct, an extension or preservation of life, an expectation beyond death, or an ironic allusion to deeds.)
  104:3/q09/p1 He imagines wealth will prevent death and keep him alive in this world. — reasons: Muqātil says this lasts until wealth is spent, though neither wealth nor children can immortalize him. Al-Ṭabarī ties the illusion to amassed, counted and withheld money. Al-Zajjāj describes acting like a rich person who never expects death. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Biqāʿī, Quṭb and others link it to trusting money against danger; Quṭb adds imagined escape from God's decree and judgment. Ibn ʿĀshūr says worldly survival is the hoarder's highest wish because he denies an eternal life beyond it. Al-Ālūsī allows a literal delusion caused by arrogance, distraction from judgment, or belief that material causes and wealth control health and safety. Al-Ṭabrisī says he behaves this way despite knowing everyone dies.
  104:3/q09/p2 His wealth gives him long hopes or distracts him, so he lives as if death will never come. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan's reading reported by al-Rāzī and al-Zamakhsharī connects this to heedlessness and distant wishes; al-Ḥasan remarks that certain death seems doubtful to people. Al-Bayḍāwī distinguishes attachment, distraction and long hopes. Al-Bursevī and Ibn ʿĀshūr say the claim is figurative, not anyone's real conviction; al-Ālūsī calls it a representational metaphor. Al-Biqāʿī says the sinner turns from God toward fleeting pleasures. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it an emphatic simile satirizing greed. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī links earthly attachment and perceived self-sufficiency to boundless wants, tyranny and superiority (96:6–7).
  104:3/q09/p3 He thinks his wealth will lengthen his lifespan. — reasons: ʿIkrima gives the explicit extension of lifespan; the same report recurs through Ibn Abī Ḥātim, al-Māwardī, al-Qurṭubī and al-Durr.
  104:3/q09/p4 Wealth has sustained and protected his life, so he supposes it will continue to do so. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls wealth the substance and support of life; Abū Ḥayyān says it has maintained and protected him throughout his lifespan. The past form can thus name the support already experienced.
  104:3/q09/p5 The wording alludes to deeds or afterlife striving as the real way to lasting life. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Nasafī name righteous deeds leading to abiding bliss; al-Rāzī adds good remembrance in this world, with enduring bliss in the next. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Biqāʿī and al-Ālūsī formulate the afterlife pursuit. Al-Ālūsī calls this reading very far-fetched and notes that another scholar did not treat it as independent.
  104:3/q09/p6 His lasting buildings, planting and landworks show an expectation of prolonged life or remembrance. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī cites fortified homes, trees and land development; al-Rāzī specifies brick and plaster and adds the wish to be remembered after death. Al-Bursevī adds digging rivers; al-Ālūsī ties all these acts to long hopes. Islahi compares the monuments and palaces of 26:128–29.
  104:3/q09/p7 He thinks wealth will benefit him after death. — reasons: Al-Māwardī gives this as a further possibility, separate from lifespan extension and escape from death.
  104:3/q09/p8 He loves wealth so much that he thinks losing any of it would kill him. — reasons: Al-Rāzī presents this as a third interpretation: the miser guards money as if keeping it were preserving his own life, and calls it plausible for a miser.
  104:3/q09/p9 He expects money to bring him to an immortal station. — reaso
