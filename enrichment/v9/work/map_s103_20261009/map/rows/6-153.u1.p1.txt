6:153/q08 How is mustaqīman parsed, and why is it accusative? [مُسْتَقِيمًۭا · grammar] (turns on: Whether the accusative describes the present path as a state or specifies its straightness.)
  6:153/q08/p1 It is a circumstantial accusative describing the demonstrative/path. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās, al-Samīn and Ibn ʿĀshūr give the ḥāl analysis; al-Samīn allows the attention particle or demonstrative as governor. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 11:72; Iṣlāḥī makes the demonstrative expression govern it. Al-Wāḥidī refers back to 6:126.
  6:153/q08/p2 It may be a specification or emphasis that the path is also straight. — reasons: Jishumī records tamyīz as an alternative to ḥāl, saying the wording draws attention to straightness as well as pathhood.
6:153/q09 What does following the path require? [فَٱتَّبِعُوهُ · interpretation] (turns on: The sources develop following as practical obedience and adherence to transmitted communal guidance.)
  6:153/q09/p1 Act on the full revealed way, its commands, prohibitions, permissions and restraints. — reasons: Al-Māwardī defines following as acting; Jishumī adds affirmation and lawful/unlawful observance; Abū Ḥayyān includes all obligations. Al-Qurṭubī cites the Prophet's instruction to take commands and stop at prohibitions; al-Rāzī says follow in general and detail.
  6:153/q09/p2 Hold to the Prophet's Sunnah, early precedent and community amid disagreement. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī adduces al-ʿIrbāḍ's farewell counsel, ʿUmar b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz's letter on moderation and predestination, Sahl's warning about future censure, and Abū al-ʿĀliya's appeal to the original practice.
6:153/q10 What are the forbidden 'paths'? [ٱلسُّبُلَ · referent] (turns on: Whether 'paths' denotes rival communities, doctrinal innovations, concrete forbidden customs, or all deviation from Islam.)
  6:153/q10/p1 Other religions and ways of disbelief, including Judaism, Christianity, Zoroastrianism and idolatry. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās and many commentators give these examples; al-Māwardī includes superseded books and former religions. Al-Bayḍāwī says desires multiply with temperaments and customs, while evidence yields one way.
  6:153/q10/p2 Innovations, passions, doubtful doctrines, anomalous legal opinions and speculative disputation. — reasons: Mujāhid names innovations and doubts; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Qurṭubī add deviant legal doctrines and excessive debate or theology. Sahl, al-Qāshānī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī describe desires and customary doctrines.
  6:153/q10/p3 Misguidances and any routes other than Islam. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās's broad gloss is transmitted repeatedly; Ibn Zayd says these routes separate people from Islam. Ibn Qutayba illustrates right and left side tracks.
  6:153/q10/p4 Self-imposed bans on livestock and crops. — reasons: Muqātil identifies this concrete form of misguidance; Abū Ḥayyān's note names Muqātil as transmitter while wording attributes the identification to Mujāhid.
  6:153/q10/p5 Ways driven by the soul and its ulterior aims. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī calls them custom- and desire-made doctrines; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites criminals, corrupters, ignorance, conjecture and desire in 6:55, 7:142, 10:89 and 53:23.
6:153/q11 Why is God's path singular but the forbidden paths plural? [صِرَٰطِى ٱلسُّبُلَ · rhetoric] (turns on: The contrast may express theological unity or the concrete image of a main road and side tracks.)
  6:153/q11/p1 Truth and guidance are one; false routes multiply and branch, causing division. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr compares singular light and plural darknesses in 2:257; al-Burhān and al-Itqān compare singular guardian of believers with plural guardians of unbelievers. Elmalılı allows time- and place-specific true rulings while insisting each case has one truth; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī links truth's unity to coherent propositions. Al-Bayḍāwī contrasts proof with variable desires.
  6:153/q11/p2 The plural evokes crooked little tracks branching from the main road. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr calls them baniyyāt al-ṭarīq leading to homes or pasture; he says sabīl can itself equal ṣirāṭ, citing 12:108. Ibn Qutayba gives the Arab counsel to avoid side tracks.
6:153/q12 What line-drawing report illustrates the verse, and how is it transmitted? [صِرَٰطِى ٱلسُّبُلَ · reports] (turns on: The narration's companion, line count, attribution and chain grading vary.)
  6:153/q12/p1 The Prophet drew one central line and side lines, each with a devil calling to it, then recited the verse. — reasons: Ibn Masʿūd and Jābir versions recur. Ibn Kathīr reports al-Ḥākim's authentication of both Ibn Masʿūd routes and suggests ʿĀṣim heard each; he prefers Ibn Masʿūd despite possible attribution as a Companion statement. Al-Qurṭubī calls al-Dārimī's route sound; Jābir's al-Shaʿbī route is called undependable by al-Ḥākim. Some reports call the sides Satan's paths; one Jābir form has two on each side, which Ibn ʿĀshūr says may count both sides together.
  6:153/q12/p2 The drawing has six slanting lines on each side, yielding twelve and then seventy-two routes. — reasons: Al-Nasafī alone specifies six each side, then subdivides each into six to obtain seventy-two divergent routes.
  6:153/q12/p3 Ibn Masʿūd instead describes a path starting near Muhammad and ending in Paradise, with side paths ending in Hell. — reasons: The report is conveyed by ʿAbd al-Razzāq, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr and al-Durr; callers entice people toward the side roads.
  6:153/q12/p4 A second prophetic parable makes Islam the path, limits its walls, prohibitions its doors, Scripture its first caller and conscience its inner caller. — reasons: Al-Tirmidhī grades the report from al-Nawwās b. Samʿān ḥasan gharīb.
6:153/q13 How is fa-tafarraqa formed and read? [فَتَفَرَّقَ · grammar] (turns on: The underlying two tāʾs can be reduced by deletion or assimilation.)
  6:153/q13/p1 It is subjunctive after an implied an as the consequence of the prohibition. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās and al-Samīn parse the verb as the response to 'do not follow'; this makes division the predicted result.
  6:153/q13/p2 It comes from fatatafarraqa, with one tāʾ deleted in the common form. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī, al-Bursawī and al-Ālūsī explain deletion of one of two adjacent tāʾs; al-Samīn notes the majority's light form.
  6:153/q13/p3 The tāʾs can assimilate into a doubled form. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī reports assimilation, al-Samīn names al-Bazzī and allows deletion or assimilation, and Qirāʾāt-Er says the articulation does not alter meaning.
6:153/q14 What does the bāʾ in 'fa-tafarraqa bikum' do? [بِكُمْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the preposition directly marks caused scattering or accompaniment that implies causation.)
  6:153/q14/p1 It is causative: the paths divide and scatter you. — reasons: Al-Samīn allows an object-like semantic role; al-Bursawī and al-Ālūsī say the bāʾ makes the paths the agent, stronger than saying travelers divide themselves.
  6:153/q14/p2 It marks accompaniment: you diverge together with the paths. — reasons: Al-Samīn reports a circumstantial reading; Ibn ʿĀshūr compares dhahabtu bi-Zayd with adhhabtuhu, so figurative accompaniment yields a causative force.
6:153/q15 What is the result of following divergent paths? [فَتَفَرَّقَ عَن سَبِيلِهِۦ · interpretation] (turns on: The phrase can be glossed as departure from religion, disruption of unity, and its ultimate destination.)
  6:153/q15/p1 They cause deviation from God's religion and shatter religious unity. — reasons: Mujāhid glosses deviation and opposition; al-Māwardī adds loss of unity in defending religion; al-Ṭabrisī calls it God's chosen religion. Al-Qurṭubī cites 6:159; Ibn Kathīr cites 42:13 and earlier peoples' disputes; Elmalılı says factionalism weakens those who abandon tawḥīd.
  6:153/q15/p2 Side roads lead ultimately to Hell, while the one path leads to Paradise. — reasons: Ibn Masʿūd's road image and Qatāda's report give the contrasted destinations; al-Bursawī connects deviation here to slipping from the afterlife bridge and cites a report on many who slip.
  6:153/q15/p3 The word literally means becoming scattered. — reasons: Asad gives this literal rendering.
6:153/q16 To whom does the pronoun in 'His path' refer? [سَبِيلِهِۦ · referent] (turns on: The antecedent is God, but the literary account depends on who says 'My path'.)
  6:153/q16/p1 To God; the phrase denotes His religion or Islam. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the context determines the referent, and if 'My path' is divine, the third person is iltifāt. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī locates the person shift here even when 'My path' belongs to Muhammad.
  6:153/q16/p2 It may follow the Prophet's quotation of God's command or a new divine address. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī uses these alternatives to account for the switch after a prophet-owned 'My path'.
6:153/q17 What does 'that is what He charged you with' refer to? [ذَٰلِكُمْ وَصَّىٰكُم بِهِۦ · referent] (turns on: Whether the demonstrative gathers the immediate final injunction or the wider revelation and preceding ordinances.)
  6:153/q17/p1 The comprehensive charge to follow the one path and avoid divergent paths. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says the closing repeats both commands; al-Bursawī counts this as the tenth duty; al-Biqāʿī and Ibn ʿĀshūr stress the threefold repeated testament, each closing a group of duties.
  6:153/q17/p2 The Qurʾān, Islam, previous commands, avoidance of sin, or obedience that averts punishment. — reasons: Jishumī transmits this range without selecting one.
