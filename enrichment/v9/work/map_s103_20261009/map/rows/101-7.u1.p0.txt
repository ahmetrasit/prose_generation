## THE CURRENT MAP
101:7/q01 What does ʿīsha mean and what does its form convey? [عِيشَةٍۢ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the question concerns the noun's lexical form, its grammatical kind, or the experience its wording suggests.)
  101:7/q01/p1 It is a verbal noun meaning living or life. — reasons: Rāzī and Ibn ʿĀshūr compare khīfa, a verbal noun meaning fear; Ibn ʿĀshūr derives it from al-ʿaysh.
  101:7/q01/p2 It is a noun of kind, here denoting a particular life. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī notes its kasra and compares the form of a noun for sitting.
  101:7/q01/p3 It evokes continuous, steady living and pleasure. — reasons: Biqāʿī says the inhabitant continually moves and lives; the feminine form suggests one constant state of clarity and pleasure, unlike changing worldly conditions.
101:7/q02 What life is promised to the person with heavy scales? [عِيشَةٍۢ رَّاضِيَةٍۢ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the promised state is identified as Paradise, described as enduring bliss, or read inwardly as a higher spiritual life.)
  101:7/q02/p1 Life in Paradise that pleases and never disappoints its inhabitant. — reasons: Muqātil limits this outcome to sincere monotheists and says they never grow dissatisfied. Qatāda, Mujāhid and many commentators identify the life with Paradise; Mujāhid's report and other cross references mention 69:21. Qushayrī and Baghawī tie it to heavy good deeds; Biqāʿī locates its abode in the lofty Garden, opposite 101:9.
  101:7/q02/p2 Lasting bliss. — reasons: Al-Ḍaḥḥāk derives this from ʿaysh, life, rather than maʿāsh, livelihood; Biqāʿī also describes consistent, lasting pleasure.
  101:7/q02/p3 A satisfied, true life in gardens of divine attributes above the gardens of deeds. — reasons: Qāshānī's inward reading associates heavy scales with real sciences and perfections of the psyche, heart and spirit.
101:7/q03 What does rāḍiya mean, and how can life bear this description? [رَّاضِيَةٍۢ · grammar] (turns on: The participle can be parsed as possessive, figuratively passive or transferred, literally active of the life, or as a concealed metaphor; feminine marking is an objection to the possessive parse.)
  101:7/q03/p1 It is a relational or possessive adjective: a life endowed with satisfaction. — reasons: Naḥḥās, al-Zajjāj as cited by Baghawī and Rāzī, and al-Khalīl and Sībawayh as cited by Ibn ʿAṭiyya treat it as a possessive construction. Abū Isḥāq and Abū Bakr cite forms meaning possessing milk, merit or dates; Jishumī supplies two poetic examples for possessing hardship and dates. Ālūsī calls this the familiar analysis. Al-Sīrāfī and other scholars object that a relational form construed with dhū is normally a noun without feminine agreement, whereas this word has hāʾ.
  101:7/q03/p2 It means pleasing or pleased-with: the possessor of the life is satisfied with it. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj says this has the same exegetical outcome as the possessive analysis. Ālūsī and Elmalılı allow a figurative passive sense or transfer of the possessor's satisfaction to the life; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls this rational metaphor, since the life causes or is the time of satisfaction. Ālūsī also treats an active participle as implying that its possessor chooses it and stays with it.
  101:7/q03/p3 The life itself is pleased with its inhabitants and remains with them. — reasons: Al-Sīrāfī offers this as an active sense of the adjective, accounting for its feminine ending; Elmalılı repeats that the life stays attached to its people.
  101:7/q03/p4 The life itself actively satisfies its inhabitants by yielding gently to their wishes. — reasons: Qurṭubī says its softness and submission give the life an active role; the couches, fruit branches and rivers in Paradise illustrate its generous compliance.
  101:7/q03/p5 It is a concealed metaphor with an imaginative metaphorical feature. — reasons: Ālūsī and Elmalılı report this analysis from works of rhetoric without supplying a more specific image.
101:7/q04 How is the feminine hāʾ of rāḍiya explained? [رَّاضِيَةٍۢ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the suffix is ordinary agreement, marks intensity, preserves a weak form, or is incompatible with the proposed relational analysis.)
  101:7/q04/p1 It agrees with life because rāḍiya is an active participle or figurative attribution, not a relational noun. — reasons: Ālūsī answers the objection to a feminine relational form by making the active participle imply choosing and remaining with the life, or allowing figurative attribution. Al-Sīrāfī's account has the life pleased with its inhabitants.
  101:7/q04/p2 The hāʾ is an intensive suffix. — reasons: Al-Sīrāfī compares ʿallāma and rāwiya; Ālūsī accepts this and says the suffix is not confined to the faʿʿāl pattern, citing riwāya or rāwiya. Elmalılı repeats the point.
  101:7/q04/p3 The hāʾ preserves the weak adjective's form by preventing loss of yāʾ. — reasons: Al-Sīrāfī adduces nāqa mashliya and kalba mujriya and other weak forms. Ālūsī accepts the explanation; miṣakka is either exceptional or follows the resemblance of doubled roots to weak ones. Elmalılı reports the same possibilities and Ālūsī's praise of Al-Sīrāfī's subtle analysis.
  101:7/q04/p4 A possessive relational form should not take feminine marking, so the hāʾ challenges that analysis. — reasons: A distinguished scholar says such forms mean possessing a quality, function as nouns with dhū rather than adjectives modifying an overt noun, and are therefore not feminized; Al-Sīrāfī points to this verse as the difficulty.
101:7/q05 Why call the life rāḍiya rather than marḍiyya? [رَّاضِيَةٍۢ · rhetoric]
  101:7/q05/p1 The active form intensifies the promise of a life that surrounds its worthy owner with lasting contentment. — reasons: Elmalılı contrasts rāḍiya with marḍiyya and invokes 89:27–30; Ibn ʿĀshūr says calling the life satisfied is a rational metaphor because it causes contentment or is its time.
101:7/q06 How do Paradise's blessings illustrate the life yielding to its inhabitants? [عِيشَةٍۢ رَّاضِيَةٍۢ · reports]
  101:7/q06/p1 Its furnishings, fruit, and water respond freely to a friend of God. — reasons: Qurṭubī says raised couches about a hundred years high lower for a saint to sit, then rise; high fruit branches bend within reach whether the saint sits or stands, with 69:23 cited; a river moves wherever the saint goes, above or below, with 76:6 cited. A report has the saint point with a staff, making a channel-less river flow wherever wanted among palaces and halls. These are his evidence that the life itself grants satisfaction.
101:7/q07 What effect does leaving the pleasing life undescribed in detail have? [عِيشَةٍۢ رَّاضِيَةٍۢ · rhetoric]
  101:7/q07/p1 It lets the reader feel the shade of contentment and the most restful bliss. — reasons: Quṭb says the verse deliberately leaves the pleasant life's details unstated.
101:7/q08 What makes the scales heavy and brings the pleasing life? [* · interpretation] (turns on: What is credited with weight: good deeds, the monotheistic confession, God's decree, or inward spiritual attainment.)
  101:7/q08/p1 Abundant good deeds outweigh evil and bring salvation or lasting Paradise bliss. — reasons: Qushayrī says the scales are heavy with good deeds. Iṣlāḥī says only deeds help on that Day and a just, special balance weighs them; 21:47 is cited. Kur'an Yolu says abundant good earns God's approval and rich Gardens. Qāshānī says enduring righteous deeds weigh more than perishable pleasures; he rejects a preceding annihilation reading as unfit for the heavy/light distinction. The light-scale counterpart has little good according to al-Qummī.
  101:7/q08/p2 Sincere monotheism, expressed in the confession that there is no god but God, makes the scales heavy. — reasons: Muqātil says only sincere monotheists reach this outcome and remain satisfied in Paradise.
  101:7/q08/p3 God makes scales heavy or light according to His will and pre-eternal decree. — reasons: Al-Wāsiṭī answers a question about whether deeds cause the weight by citing a report that the scales are in God's hand and He raises or lowers people.
  101:7/q08/p4 Real sciences and perfections of the psyche, heart, and spirit constitute weight. — reasons: Qāshānī says ascent in God's scale is weight and descent is lightness; enduring righteous deeds matter, whereas passing pleasures have no weight. The ensuing life is in gardens of attributes above gardens of deeds.
101:7/q09 What is weighed, and how is the weighing understood? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the weighing concerns deeds directly, their records, or corresponding created substances; the mode of an unseen event is also disputed or withheld.)
  101:7/q09/p1 Deeds themselves are what the scales weigh. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī describes a just, special balance for human deeds and cites 21:47. Kur'an Yolu prefers mawāzīn as the things weighed, namely deeds; it also reports the plural of mīzān, scales, as a metonym for those deeds. Its author cautions that the actual method is unseen and cannot be speculated about.
  101:7/q09/p2 The written records of deeds are weighed. — reasons: Qushayrī reports this as one account of how deeds are weighed.
  101:7/q09/p3 Created substances corresponding to each component of one's acts are weighed. — reasons: Qushayrī reports that their weight provides the measure of deeds.
  101:7/q09/p4 The actual way deeds are weighed cannot be specified beyond revelation. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu says events of the afterlife are unseen and one may not speculate about their measurement.
101:7/q10 How does this promise connect with the opposite fate in the adjacent verses? [* · links] (turns on: How the verse's positive clause is matched against the light-scale clause: direct contrast, ellipsis, or support for a particular reading of its wording.)
  101:7/q10/p1 The passage contrasts the reward of heavy scales with the fate of light scales. — reasons: Ālūsī says it divides people into two groups after describing what is common to all.
