## THE CURRENT MAP
2:283/q01 What does safar mean and evoke? [سَفَرٍۢ · meaning]
  2:283/q01/p1 Travel, whose root conveys uncovering or disclosure. — reasons: Wahidi cites travel exposing character, the book making clear, dawn, uncovering a face, reconciliation, sweeping, and Ibn al-Aʿrabi's al-Akhtal line; al-Azhari says the traveler emerges into open country and cites daylight and Sirius; Razi repeats these cognates.
2:283/q02 How does 'on a journey' work in the sentence? [عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍۢ · grammar]
  2:283/q02/p1 It means being in the state of travel; ʿalā evokes firm establishment in it. — reasons: Abu Hayyan glosses the phrase 'while traveling'; Alusi calls it a dependent metaphor and allows coordination or a circumstantial reading.
2:283/q03 Why mention travel here? [عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍۢ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the conditional describes a typical obstacle or limits lawful pledging.)
  2:283/q03/p1 Travel is the common setting where writing or witnesses are unavailable; comparable obstacles also call for security. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi, Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, Elmalılı and Ibn Ashur cite scarcity of scribes, tools, witnesses, night, busyness, or doubtful debtor standing. Burhan notes a conditional need not be legally restrictive. Razi compares the fear clause in 4:101.
  2:283/q03/p2 The travel condition is restrictive for pledges. — reasons: Mujahid, Dahhak and Dawud follow the apparent conditional wording; Dahhak also requires absence of a scribe. Tabari rejects its restriction on pledges by the armor report, while others reject it as customary context.
2:283/q04 What is read and meant by 'a scribe'? [كَاتِبًۭا · readings] (turns on: Whether the absent resource is a writer, writing materials, or written documents, and how the consonantal text is read.)
  2:283/q04/p1 Kitāban, writing or writing materials, including pen, ink and paper. — reasons: Ibn Abbas, Ubayy, Mujahid and Abu al-Aliya say a writer may be present without materials; Tabari records variant specific lacks of ink, paper, or writer. Nahhas calls it anomalous; Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi find its sense apt despite codex spelling.
  2:283/q04/p2 Kātiban, a single writer. — reasons: Tabari prefers the codex and regional reading; Nahhas relates it to 2:282's 'let a writer write'; Thalabi cites the majority and codex. Other sources note the common reading.
  2:283/q04/p3 Kuttāban or kitāban as plural writers. — reasons: Dahhak reads a plural of 'writer'; Nahhas says kitāb can be a collective of scribes; Ibn Abbas reasons each case has a scribe.
  2:283/q04/p4 Kutuban, written books or records. — reasons: Abu al-Aliya's plural of kitāb fits multiple cases.
2:283/q05 How is 'you do not find a scribe' attached syntactically? [وَلَمْ تَجِدُوا۟ كَاتِبًۭا · grammar]
  2:283/q05/p1 It may coordinate with the condition, attach to kāna's predicate, or be a circumstantial clause. — reasons: Samin distinguishes the latter two as nominative-context constructions; Abu Hayyan also lists attachments.
2:283/q06 What is a rahn or pledged security? [فَرِهَٰنٌۭ · meaning]
  2:283/q06/p1 An item retained or delivered as security for a debt; the root also conveys permanence, detention or firmness. — reasons: Wahidi and Razi explain transfer from verbal noun to object name; Qurtubi says it is recoverable by sale proceeds. Ibn Ashur adduces 74:38 and Zuhayr; Farisi cites Abu Zayd and 66:12; poetry in Aʿsha and others shows pledged property, persons and enduring food.
2:283/q07 Do rahana and arhana differ in use? [فَرِهَٰنٌۭ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the two verbs are interchangeable in collateral usage and whether arhana carries a separate bidding sense.)
  2:283/q07/p1 Both can mean to pledge collateral. — reasons: Zajjaj, Ibn al-Aʿrabi and al-Akhfash allow both; poetry attributed to Ibn Hammam is adduced, but al-Asmaʿi disputes its wording. Wahidi, Qurtubi, Samin and Tabrisi record the disagreement.
  2:283/q07/p2 Rahana is standard for pledging, while arhana may mean outbidding, giving another's pledge, or keeping something going. — reasons: Abu Ali and Abu Zayd distinguish loans and sales from bidding high; Farra gives another-person sense; Ibn Atiyya and Samin cite the high-price camel, and rahantu lisani excludes arhana.
2:283/q08 How is 'pledges' read and formed? [فَرِهَٰنٌۭ · readings] (turns on: The transmitted vowels and alif, the regularity of their plural patterns, codex spelling, and usage in horse racing.)
  2:283/q08/p1 Ruhun, with two dammas and no alif, is a transmitted plural of rahn. — reasons: Ibn Kathir and Abu Amr read it, with Ibn Abbas, Ubayy's transmission, and others; some analyze it like rare suquf. Zajjaj prefers it for codex fit and valid transmission. Abu Amr distinguishes ordinary pledges from rihān for horse wagers; poetry supports rahn.
  2:283/q08/p2 Rihān, with kasra and alif, is the regular plural of rahn. — reasons: Most readers use it; Akhfash, Tabari, Nahhas, Razi and Samin favor its productive pattern such as hibal from habl. Sibawayh's caution weighs against invented plurals of plurals; Qiraat-ER also calls it canonical.
  2:283/q08/p3 Ruhn, with a still hāʾ, is a lightened form of ruhun. — reasons: Reports assign it to Ikrima, Asim, Meccans or particular Ibn Kathir and Abu Amr transmissions; Nahhas explains dropping ḍamma for weight and prefers an adjectival analogy; Akhfash criticizes the rare pattern. Thalabi treats vowelled and unvowelled forms as dialect variants.
2:283/q09 How does 'pledges held' function grammatically? [فَرِهَٰنٌۭ مَّقْبُوضَةٌۭ · grammar] (turns on: The omitted clause element and whether rahn functions as object noun or verbal noun.)
  2:283/q09/p1 A nominative subject with omitted predicate, or predicate of an omitted subject such as 'the security'; an implied command to take one is also possible. — reasons: Jishumi allows nominative and imperative or accusative ellipses; Razi, Baydawi, Qurtubi's Mahdawi and Samin lay out the alternatives; Alusi treats it as security, a thing to take, or a prescription.
  2:283/q09/p2 The pledge noun is a lexicalized verbal noun used as an object, not a cognate accusative. — reasons: Farisi, Wahidi, Razi and Samin distinguish object usage from a verbal noun used adverbially; Ibn Atiyya says its noun status permits the plural with kasra.
2:283/q10 When may a pledge be taken? [عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍۢ فَرِهَٰنٌۭ · law] (turns on: Whether the travel and scribe conditions restrict the law or describe a typical need for security.)
  2:283/q10/p1 Pledges are valid during travel or residence, with or without an available scribe. — reasons: The Prophet pledged armor to a Jewish seller in Medina; reports differ over twenty or thirty measures of barley and the seller's name. Majority jurists treat travel as an example; Razi compares 4:101. Ibn Atiyya says the verse's general meaning itself extends to home.
  2:283/q10/p2 Only during travel, and for some authorities only if a writer is unavailable. — reasons: Mujahid, Dahhak and Dawud take the travel and lack-of-scribe conditions literally; Dahhak directs writing instead whenever possible.
  2:283/q10/p3 If writing is available, pledging is permissible but ordinarily not recommended without another reason. — reasons: Elmalılı distinguishes legal permission from prudent resort to collateral.
2:283/q11 What does qabḍ mean in 'held in possession'? [مَّقْبُوضَةٌۭ · meaning]
  2:283/q11/p1 Grasping and holding something in the palm. — reasons: Jishumi contrasts emphatic ḍād with qabṣ using un-emphatic ṣād, which means holding with the fingertips.
2:283/q12 What does possession do to a pledge's legal effect? [مَّقْبُوضَةٌۭ · law] (turns on: At what stage the contract binds, and whose physical custody satisfies maqbudah.)
  2:283/q12/p1 Taking possession is required for validity or binding completion; a mere verbal pledge has no operative security. — reasons: Shafi'i and Abu Hanifa treat possession as a condition of validity; many cite 'held.' Sa'id ibn Jubayr and some reports specify receipt by creditor. Ibn Atiyya says creditor or agent certainly counts, and Baghawi says the pledgor cannot reclaim collateral while debt remains.
  2:283/q12/p2 Malik treats offer and acceptance as a binding contract before delivery; possession completes its benefit and can be compelled. — reasons: Malikis invoke 5:1 and 17:34 and covenant fulfillment; reports distinguish validity, bindingness and usefulness. KuranYolu says valid but binding upon delivery, while other notes say already binding, a nuance in reporting.
2:283/q13 Whose custody counts as possession of a pledge? [مَّقْبُوضَةٌۭ · law] (turns on: Whether a proxy or neutral custodian counts as a valid holder.)
  2:283/q13/p1 Only creditor possession counts, not holding by a neutral third party. — reasons: Al-Hakam, Qatada, Ata, Ibn Abi Layla and a report from Ahmad require the creditor's own receipt.
  2:283/q13/p2 Possession by the creditor's agent suffices. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya reports consensus, and Alusi infers it from the passive maqbudah; Abu Hayyan agrees.
  2:283/q13/p3 A neutral custodian's possession counts. — reasons: Malik, his school and the majority treat the custodian as creditor's representative; Qurtubi says loss in the custodian's care creates no liability for creditor or trustee.
2:283/q14 What if a pledge returns to the debtor? [مَّقْبُوضَةٌۭ · law] (turns on: Whether renewed debtor custody undoes the security, and whether its legal form matters.)
  2:283/q14/p1 Return to the debtor voids the pledge. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya relates this to its defining held state; Qurtubi follows loss of possession. Abu Hayyan reports Malik says absolute return voids it.
  2:283/q14/p2 A loan or deposit back to the debtor preserves it. — reasons: Abu Hanifa makes this exception.
  2:283/q14/p3 Prior possession continues to count after return. — reasons: Qurtubi reports Shafi'i this way, but Abu Hayyan reports him as voiding it whenever returned; the reports conflict.
