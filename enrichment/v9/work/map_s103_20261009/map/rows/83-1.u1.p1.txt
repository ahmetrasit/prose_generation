l-Muʾminūn. Al-Burhān says reliable transmitters establish the ordering and counts 85 Meccan surahs. Al-Suyūṭī, as cited by Asad, is among authorities calling it last Meccan; Islāhī treats the surah as Meccan.
  83:1/q06/p3 Its parts span Mecca and Medina, or it was revealed between the cities. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās and Qatāda reportedly say it is Medinan except the final eight Meccan verses. One account places the first four after arrival and verses 5–28 in Medina, with the final eight among the last in Mecca. Asad weighs the main body as late Meccan and the opening as early Medinan, a threshold like surah 29; another account places revelation between the cities before arrival.
83:1/q07 What consequences and moral warnings does the verse support? [* · interpretation] (turns on: How the warning is applied: direct afterlife threat, communal calamity, the gravity of slight fraud, duties of fair exchange, and illustrative reports.)
  83:1/q07/p1 Fraud in measure brings divine punishment, including Hell and standing before the Lord. — reasons: ʿIkrima repeatedly swears even his own son, if he measures or weighs, faces the Fire; Ibn ʿUmar warns cheaters stand in sweat, with versions reaching half the ears or the mouth. Al-Fuḍayl says the cheating balance blackens the face. Abū Ṣāliḥ Ḥamdūn tells a holder of scales to recall the scale that will weigh him. ʿAbd Allāh points to 83:6 for accountability. Ibn ʿUmar includes knowingly renting a false measure or employing a cheater; al-Rāzī says even intending fraud is a grave sin.
  83:1/q07/p2 Cheating measures brings failed crops, famine, or drought among five paired communal wrongs and calamities. — reasons: Prophetic reports pair broken covenants with enemy domination, unjust judgment with poverty, sexual immorality with death or plague, and withholding zakat with withheld rain. Al-Qurṭubī says al-Bazzār transmits the gist and Mālik gives an Ibn ʿUmar route; Study Quran states no grading. One shorter report pairs only covenants and crops.
  83:1/q07/p3 The warning covers even minute or intended wrongdoing and implies that greater theft is graver. — reasons: Al-Rāzī reports that proponents of the threat address Muslims: unbelief would already explain the threat and 83:4–5 reminds believers of resurrection. He says intention itself is major sin and favors a threat for any amount without conditioning it on the theft threshold, repentance, or outweighing good deeds. A Bedouin challenges ʿAbd al-Malik to consider seizure of much more Muslim property if slight fraud brings this threat.
  83:1/q07/p4 Fair measurement matters because transactions and reciprocal justice depend on it. — reasons: Al-Rāzī cites 55:7–9 and 57:25 on the balance and justice. Qatādah tells people to give fairly as they wish to receive; Islāhī sees the trader's protest against being cheated as evidence of innate recognition of justice. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ invokes Shuʿayb's ban on corruption in 11:85. Abū Ḥafṣ and Asad extend the reciprocal duty to all social rights.
  83:1/q07/p5 Justice should precede voluntary generosity when measuring. — reasons: ʿAlī stopped a saffron seller giving extra and ordered exact due weight first, then any freely chosen surplus; al-Kashshāf says this trains fairness and distinguishes obligation from extra giving. Mālik advises pouring freely until the buyer's measure is full and releasing one's hand without holding back.
  83:1/q07/p6 A measurer should beware the trade's temptation and both measures and scales. — reasons: An Arab or Ubayy warns against seeking favors from those dependent on the rims of measures and tongues of scales; al-Aṣmaʿī also ascribes the saying to a Bedouin and perhaps ʿAlī. Ibn ʿAbbās warns Persians that earlier peoples perished through measure and scale, naming them because they use both whereas Mecca weighs and Medina measures.
  83:1/q07/p7 Dishonest gain can return as a terrifying afterlife image. — reasons: Mālik ibn Dīnār says a dying neighbor who used two measures saw them become two fiery mountains; striking them together made them appear larger.
  83:1/q07/p8 The warning can be heard in later public worship and market conduct. — reasons: Abū Hurayra reports Medina's deputy reciting the surah at dawn and then praying against a trader who bought full but sold short; another report says he heard the verse when the Prophet left Sabāʿ ibn ʿArṭafa in charge during Khaybar.
  83:1/q07/p9 The revelation to David warns against exposing another's private sin. — reasons: Tustarī reports God saying people sin privately and repent publicly, although God does not want their wrongdoing exposed.
83:1/q08 How does this opening connect with the preceding surah and the theme of judgment? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the link is drawn from the preceding surah's judgment and records, from innate moral knowledge, or from God's attributes and nurture.)
  83:1/q08/p1 It develops al-Infiṭār's ending by detailing judgment, the righteous and the wicked. — reasons: Al-Jishumī, Biqāʿī and Islāhī say the prior surah ends with resurrection and divergent fates and this surah elaborates them. Al-Rāzī cites 82:19, where all command belongs to God; Abū Ḥayyān says the new opening singles out a contemptible small-gain sin. Biqāʿī says recording every deed in 82:10 and exact weighing in 21:47 make petty fraud a grave offense. The Study Quran says the surah moves from shortchanging to contrasting fates and ends with sinners mocking the Prophet and believers.
  83:1/q08/p2 It argues from human moral awareness of justice to resurrection and God's fair judgment. — reasons: Islāhī contrasts al-Infiṭār's argument from bodily creation with this surah's appeal to moral nature: humans love justice, resent being cheated, and expect different treatment for good and evil. Injustice arises when other motives overpower that nature; 75:14–15 is cited for the Qur'anic argument. Verses 1–6 say cheating betrays failure to expect standing before the Lord, and deniers resist accountability.
  83:1/q08/p3 God's nurturing, wisdom, and mercy frame the surah's demand for fair reckoning. — reasons: Biqāʿī says the divine nurturer must question and distinguish those He raises; the name al-Taṭfīf signals the theme. He reads the basmala as perfect wisdom and power, universal mercy through creation and guidance, and special mercy for His party. Qushayrī stresses God's beginningless being, majesty, beauty, expected kindness, and rightful decree.
83:1/q09 What virtue is reported for reciting this surah? [* · reports]
  83:1/q09/p1 Its reciter will receive sealed nectar on the Day of Resurrection. — reasons: Al-Jishumī transmits this from Ubayy ibn Kaʿb reporting the Prophet.
83:1/q10 How many verses does the surah have? [* · sciences]
  83:1/q10/p1 It has thirty-six verses. — reasons: Al-Durr al-Manthūr gives this with its classification of the surah as Meccan.
83:1/q11 What variant reading is reported in the accompanying passage? [* · readings]
  83:1/q11/p1 Kūfan readers except ʿĀṣim and Yaḥyā read ran with kasra on rāʾ; the rest read it with fatḥa. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī reports this reading of the later word ran.
83:1/q12 What does the near-full-measure comparison teach about people? [* · interpretation]
  83:1/q12/p1 Adam's descendants are close to one another; superiority belongs only to piety. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī reports a hadith likening Adam's descendants to a slightly underfilled measure and saying distinction is by piety. Al-Jishumī says no one surpasses another except through piety.
