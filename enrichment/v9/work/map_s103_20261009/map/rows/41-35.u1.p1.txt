41:35/q08 Why repeat 'none receives it' in the second clause? [وَمَا يُلَقَّىٰهَآ وَمَا يُلَقَّىٰهَآ · rhetoric]
  41:35/q08/p1 The repetition emphatically praises and elevates the rare moral act. — reasons: Abu Hayyan says the repetition emphasizes the trait's excellence; al-Alusi says it reinforces praise. Ibn Ashur says repeating the full clause rather than coordinating leaves none of the statement subordinate to a conjunction. Biqa'i says the repeated passive emphasizes that God enables the patient to acquire it.
41:35/q09 Why shift from plural patient people to singular possessor of a great share? [ٱلَّذِينَ صَبَرُوا۟ ذُو حَظٍّ عَظِيمٍۢ · rhetoric]
  41:35/q09/p1 The singular marks the recipient's rarity and high rank. — reasons: Biqa'i explicitly reads the shift from the plural patient people to the singular possessor this way; Ibn Ashur adds that the possessor construction presents goodness as a settled disposition and warns that the counsel is not easy for everyone.
41:35/q10 Why say 'those who were patient' rather than simply 'the patient'? [ٱلَّذِينَ صَبَرُوا۟ · rhetoric]
  41:35/q10/p1 The past form marks patience as an established prior trait, while the present verbs depict later cultivation. — reasons: Ibn Ashur contrasts the two present-tense verbs with the past 'were patient' and says this explains the choice over a simple adjective.
41:35/q11 What rhetorical force does the wording around the better response have? [وَمَا يُلَقَّىٰهَآ · rhetoric] (turns on: How to account for the force of the wording as reported by al-Nasafi.)
  41:35/q11/p1 It may answer an imagined question about how one should act. — reasons: Al-Nasafi offers this analysis of the wording.
  41:35/q11/p2 Emphatic la and the stronger comparative intensify the command. — reasons: Al-Nasafi alternatively takes la as emphatic and says a comparative replaces an expected simple adjective to heighten the response.
41:35/q12 What follows logically from the two restrictions? [* · rhetoric]
  41:35/q12/p1 Every patient person has a great share. — reasons: Salih Efendi treats the clauses as universal premises; al-Alusi reconstructs a first-figure syllogism: every patient person receives the act, and everyone who receives it has great fortune. The positive universal conclusion, called the noblest categorical form, helps a patient person recognize his good lot and remain patient.
41:35/q13 What incidents or persons are connected with this counsel? [* · reports] (turns on: Different transmitted identifications and illustrative events.)
  41:35/q13/p1 Abu Bakr bore an insult until he replied; an angel then left and Satan arrived, so the Prophet left. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya reports the episode and the Prophet's explanation; al-Tabari transmits Qatada's version, specifying that the Prophet would not sit with Satan.
  41:35/q13/p2 The Prophet was told to forgive and bear Abu Jahl's harm. — reasons: Muqatil says the Prophet disliked Abu Jahl, and patience in anger might turn an enemy into a close ally; he adds that Muhammad must seek refuge from Satan.
  41:35/q13/p3 A report relates it to Abu Sufyan ibn Harb, who harmed the Prophet and later became a close friend. — reasons: Al-Nasafi reports this as an occasion of revelation.
41:35/q14 How is this difficult counsel applied in conduct and preaching? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the note draws out a universal ethical aim or its application to the Prophet's preaching.)
  41:35/q14/p1 It is a high, universal moral aim: answer wrong with the better response despite natural resistance. — reasons: Biqa'i says it exceeds mere forgiveness, forbearance and patience, serves religious and worldly interests, contradicts human disposition and must be sought from God. Kuran Yolu calls it a universal moral principle with patience as its first condition. Ibn Ashur urges training in it through resolve and patience. Islahi calls it lasting wisdom to cultivate.
  41:35/q14/p2 The Prophet should meet ignorant opposition with decency and forgiveness so the message can proceed. — reasons: Islahi says the Prophet bears a noble call; patient pardon of naive opponents who disrupt it benefits preaching. Muqatil applies the counsel to Abu Jahl's harm and the possibility that an enemy becomes a close ally.
41:35/q15 What inward examples describe the share and demands of nearness to God? [* · inward] (turns on: The notes give different inward illustrations: love's allotment and the discipline of divine service.)
  41:35/q15/p1 Spiritual love grants different shares; its perfected drink is inexhaustible. — reasons: Bursevi says some have a share of love but not fullness, while others never reach satiation, the state of perfection. Ibn al-Farid's cited verse says wasting life leaves no share of its wine-like blessing. Yahya ibn Muadh describes intoxication by love's cup; Abu Yazid replies that the strong continue drinking without exhausting it or becoming satiated even after seven seas.
  41:35/q15/p2 Nearness to God requires finer conduct and brings stricter accountability. — reasons: Ibn Ata in Bursevi contrasts proper conduct on entering and leaving divine service with bad conduct there, says poor manners nearby are graver than at a distance, and says God may pardon the ignorant for major sins while holding the elect to account for a glance or turning aside.
