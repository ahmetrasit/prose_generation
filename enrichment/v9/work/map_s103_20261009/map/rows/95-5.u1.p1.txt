95:5/q07 What does the root of أَسْفَلَ express? [أَسْفَلَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the low sense describes place, status or age; Arabic usage permits several extensions.)
  95:5/q07/p1 Literal lower position can extend to debasement, defeat, powerlessness, or decrepit age. — reasons: Ibn Jawzī describes downward direction for heavy bodies and contrasts upward direction for light bodies; he records lowest Hell and the lower army (4:145; 8:42), defeated plotters (37:98), and extreme age. Ibn ʿĀshūr says physical lowliness commonly signifies contempt and worst belief; Corpus Coranicum cites 37:98 and 41:29 for figurative subordination but adopts senility here.
  95:5/q07/p2 The lowly are the weak, sick, helpless, children, and elderly. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba derives sāfil like ʿāl from safala and ʿalā; he and later authors use this class to explain the depth of senility. Ibn ʿAbbās in al-Ṭabrisī includes fear and diminished reason.
95:5/q08 Why does God explicitly say that He returns the human low? [رَدَدْنَٰهُ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the object is moral change through causes or direct placement in the abode of punishment.)
  95:5/q08/p1 It states God's control over both the beginning and the end of human life. — reasons: Samarrai says the passive could suggest that a different agent undid God's creation; the active divine subject closes that possibility.
  95:5/q08/p2 For moral decline, God's agency is figurative through chains of causes. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says God creates higher causes and their interactions, which produce direct causes; he compares attributing a city's construction to a prince and shadow movement to God (25:45–46). Asad points to his note on 2:7.
  95:5/q08/p3 For placement in Hell, God's agency is literal. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr contrasts the locative interpretation with his causal account of moral decline: radd places a person where deserved.
95:5/q09 How does the next verse's exception relate to this descent? [أَسْفَلَ سَٰفِلِينَ · links] (turns on: Whether believers are spared the destination, spared senility, or keep their reward despite suffering senility; the scope of 'human' also determines connection.)
  95:5/q09/p1 On the Hell or moral-fall reading, believers are a connected exception from the human kind. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ and al-Ṭabarī compare the exception from loss in 103:2–3. Al-Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān and Ibn Kathīr use connectedness to favor the Fire; Ibn ʿĀshūr says believers return from shirk to original fitra.
  95:5/q09/p2 On the aging reading, the exception is disconnected: believers can age but retain reward and relief from duty. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says aging affects believers and unbelievers, while earlier righteous work continues to count; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān add that the pen or duty is lifted when reason fails. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī objects that a connected exception would contradict universal aging.
  95:5/q09/p3 Qurʾān reciters are excepted from senility itself. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās and ʿIkrima say reciters or memorizers do not lose knowledge after knowing it; al-Durr links the claim to 22:5 or 16:70 and reports al-Ḥākim's authentication.
  95:5/q09/p4 If 'human' means only the unbeliever, the believers' exception is disconnected. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports this view for Hell or reversal of bodily form.
95:5/q10 What happens to a believer's deeds and accountability in senility? [رَدَدْنَٰهُ أَسْفَلَ سَٰفِلِينَ · interpretation]
  95:5/q10/p1 Earlier righteous deeds continue to earn reward, and lost capacity is excused. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās says senile people in the Prophet's time were excused yet kept prior reward. Ibrāhīm, ʿIkrima and Qatāda extend the credit for best work done in youth or health; reports say no liability after loss of reason. Al-Thaʿlabī's report from Anas also contrasts children's unrecorded sins and credited good deeds, and says angels continue good credit and omit sins in extreme age. The prophetic report in al-Durr likewise credits good and withholds bad. Al-Dāmaghānī says deeds cease being recorded after decrepitude, while al-Baghawī distinguishes lost present capacity from continuing credit for earlier practice.
95:5/q11 What does the visible reversal from youth to age prove? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the argument must use what its addressees already witness or can invoke revelation about the unseen.)
  95:5/q11/p1 It shows God's power to resurrect and prepares the challenge in 95:7. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī argues the audience acknowledges bodily change but denies Hell; the witnessed reversal establishes divine power to restore the dead. Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls senility an instructive sign within the human species.
  95:5/q11/p2 Visible proof is unnecessary because revelation and prophetic signs make unseen truths evident. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās reports this counterargument against limiting the reasoning to observed aging.
95:5/q12 How does this verse fit the sūra and its surrounding passages? [* · links] (turns on: How the human descent functions in the oath's argument and whether the oath sites themselves exemplify it.)
  95:5/q12/p1 The sūra contrasts humanity's excellent beginning with a fall caused by refusing guidance, while the faithful retain reward. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī links the best human form, the Prophet and the preceding sūras to divergent human ends; without divine guidance a person mistakes the self for perfection. The secure city and preceding solitude mirror excellence and degradation. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the clause central and coordinated with 95:4 under the oath; the subtle inward truth warrants emphatic swearing. Al-Ālūsī relates the preceding sūra's perfected Prophet to human outcomes. Al-Iṣlāḥī says the oaths validate choice and divine retribution; Samarrai says the oath's answer fits sites of revelation and the later judgment argument. The Study Quran parallels 103:2–3.
  95:5/q12/p2 The oath sites recall earlier demonstrations of divine favor and punishment. — reasons: Farāhī reads the Mount of Figs as Jūdī: Adam's broken covenant cost a favor, repentance brought guidance, and at Noah's time wrongdoers drowned while the righteous were saved (7:27; 11:44,48). He reads Jesus' rejected-stone saying as transfer of trust from Israel and Sinai as deliverance from Pharaoh and a law for the oppressed; Abraham's leadership was promised only to faithful descendants (2:124). Al-Biqāʿī relates the oath to a Torah sequence from Sinai, Seir and Paran and a history of ideal creation, law and fall. The Study Quran reports fig leaves and the olive's light as possible foreshadowing.
95:5/q13 What does the verse teach about human choice and final accountability? [* · theology]
  95:5/q13/p1 Humans can squander sound nature through desire and choice; divine judgment must answer the resulting deeds. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says desire overcomes reason and misused faculties make people culpable; because many die uncompensated, wisdom requires resurrection and enduring punishment. Al-Aqlaishī contrasts reason's ascent with desire's descent. Al-Iṣlāḥī calls this a divine law of free will and reward or humiliation; Asad says human acts and omissions corrupt positive disposition. Ibn al-Zubayr adds that each is enabled for an appointed end and urges sincerity and thanks, citing 32:13. Al-Qushayrī and al-Ṭabrisī connect disbelief to wretchedness after an originally rational, accountable creation.
95:5/q14 How does the verse's wording carry more than one sense? [* · rhetoric]
  95:5/q14/p1 Its endings pair meanings, especially old age and Hell for asfal sāfilīn. — reasons: Samarrai says al-amīn holds security and trust, ghayr mamnūn lasting and unreproached reward, dīn religion and recompense, and aḥkam wisdom and judgment. Al-Biqāʿī describes iḥtibāk: the first clause leaves evil deeds and punishment implicit, and the exception implies preserving original fitra.
95:5/q15 What is reported about the sūra's place of revelation? [* · sciences] (turns on: Reports differ between the majority Meccan attribution and the minority Medinan attribution.)
  95:5/q15/p1 Most call it Meccan. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī report the majority; al-Ālūsī argues that unanimous identification of the secure city with Mecca supports it. The Study Quran calls it likely early-middle Meccan after sūra 85; Asad also places it after 85.
  95:5/q15/p2 Ibn ʿAbbās and Qatāda call it Medinan. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān cites both; al-Ālūsī calls the Ibn ʿAbbās report one variant, and the Study Quran notes the minority view.
