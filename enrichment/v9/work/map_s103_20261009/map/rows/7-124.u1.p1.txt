7:124/q13 Why did Pharaoh threaten the magicians after their conversion? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the immediate explanation is Pharaoh's defeat or his political story about an alleged prior conspiracy.)
  7:124/q13/p1 Moses' victory and Pharaoh's loss of divine support provoked the threat. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says Pharaoh threatened crucifixion after seeing God abandon him and Moses overcome him. Elmalılı situates the plot claim and punishments after the public defeat and conversion.
  7:124/q13/p2 He used an alleged conspiracy with Moses to discredit their conversion and protect his political power. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf says Pharaoh alleged a pre-exodus plot to expel Copts and settle Israelites, as propaganda against the conversion. Al-Ṭabrisī says he sought to fool the crowd into seeing politics instead of truth; he also reports an alternative that the magicians really arranged such a plan in Egypt before arriving. Ibn Kathīr reports his accusation that Moses and the magicians planned to seize the city and expel its leaders. Elmalılı reports Pharaoh's plot claim. Qur'an Yolu transmits al-Rāzī's view that public defeat made Pharaoh fear popular allegiance to Moses and the magicians, with loss of unpaid labor as his unspoken concern.
7:124/q14 What does the report about Moses and the chief magician say? [* · reports]
  7:124/q14/p1 Moses asked whether the chief magician would believe if defeated; the magician promised belief if his supposedly unbeatable magic lost, and Pharaoh heard. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf transmits this account.
7:124/q15 How should Pharaoh's preceding challenge about believing without permission be understood? [* · readings] (turns on: The reading as declaration or question, and whether the spoken question functions as a threat.)
  7:124/q15/p1 Its declarative reading rebukes the magicians, and the permission claim asserts control over their beliefs. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf gives the rebuking declarative reading. Qur'an Yolu says their belief was public, so Pharaoh's words function as a threat; the subsequent punishments confirm this. It says his demand for permission shows attempted control of actions, beliefs and consciences.
  7:124/q15/p2 Its interrogative reading expresses denial and incredulity. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf records this reading alongside the declarative one.
7:124/q16 What does the magicians' endurance of the threat show? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether endurance is explained as public proof of sincere faith or as the strength given by direct witnessing.)
  7:124/q16/p1 Their unshaken resolve publicly demonstrates sincere faith and the truth of Moses' miracle. — reasons: Elmalılı says a sham collusion would have collapsed under Pharaoh's threat, whereas sincere believers became firmer. Their recognized skill at magic made suspicion understandable, so public testing dispelled it. He says divine wisdom required decisive public proof that their conversion was sincere and Moses' sign was not magic.
  7:124/q16/p2 Direct witnessing enables them to endure afflictions otherwise unbearable. — reasons: Al-Sulamī transmits Samnūn's inward explanation for why the magicians ignored Pharaoh's threat.
7:124/q17 What does the magicians' reply about returning to their Lord mean in answer to the threat? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether return to God stresses inevitable death, martyrdom and reward, or the final judgment of both parties.)
  7:124/q17/p1 Death is inevitable regardless of Pharaoh, so the threat cannot ultimately harm them. — reasons: Elmalılı gives this as one possible meaning. Ibn ʿĀshūr also says their return to God answers Pharaoh's inability to harm them.
  7:124/q17/p2 Execution would make them martyrs who meet God and receive His mercy and reward. — reasons: Elmalılı says they could welcome death for truth. Ibn ʿĀshūr adds hope for forgiveness and punishment of Pharaoh, or welcome martyrdom and meeting God.
  7:124/q17/p3 Pharaoh and magicians will all die and stand before God for judgment between oppressor and oppressed. — reasons: Elmalılı records this third possible meaning.
7:124/q18 Why is the magicians' 'no harm' response stated in al-Shuʿarāʾ rather than here? [* · links]
  7:124/q18/p1 The more severe, detailed al-Shuʿarāʾ scene states their fearlessness once, thereby covering the milder, shorter al-Aʿrāf account. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī says al-Shuʿarāʾ gives no respite before crucifixion, making its threat hotter; 'no harm' fits that fuller scene. Fearlessness there entails fearlessness under al-Aʿrāf's lesser threat, whereas placing the statement only here could suggest otherwise. Repeating it would impair concision. He contrasts al-Aʿrāf's account of Israel's history, signs, sins and ingratitude with al-Shuʿarāʾ's movement to Pharaoh's end and Israel's rescue, and says each wording suits its scene's artistic design.
7:124/q19 How is tanqimū in the magicians' reply read and understood? [* · readings]
  7:124/q19/p1 The common reading has kasra on the qāf, while al-Ḥasan reads fatḥa; both mean taking vengeance or punishing. — reasons: Al-Jishumī reports both vocalizations and their shared sense from al-Ḥasan.
7:124/q20 Where does this confrontation sit in the sūra's account? [* · links]
  7:124/q20/p1 Verses 104–126 give one of the Quran's most detailed accounts of Moses confronting Pharaoh and the sorcerers. — reasons: The Study Quran locates this scene within that extended account.
