## THE CURRENT MAP
7:9/q01 What does the balance or weighing at Judgment signify? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · theology] (turns on: Whether the balance is a physical instrument, a figure for justice, or a measure of truth in acts; whether deeds have bodily weight and what the weighing demonstrates.)
  7:9/q01/p1 A real balance is set up and publicly weighs on Judgment Day. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya cites the visible balance in a Prophetic report and warns against abandoning the apparent sense; Ibn al-Jawzi cites hadith. The majority describe a beam, two pans and tongue. Ibn Kathir cites 21:47 and other judgment verses; al-Tabataba'i reports large-pan narratives but treats their form as explanatory imagery.
  7:9/q01/p2 The balance is a figure for exact justice, judgment, or the abundance of good deeds. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya quotes Abu Talib's poetry, then rejects this reading. Razi cites figurative Arabic weights, 18:105 and a poem on equal arguments; he argues deeds are accidents and literal weighing adds no knowledge. Imam al-Sadiq likewise says attributes lack bodily weight and God needs no weighing. Study Quran links commercial and cosmic balance verses.
  7:9/q01/p3 Deeds are tested against the truth proper to each act; that truth is their measure. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i says prayer, almsgiving, speech and other acts have distinct standards; the book makes their truth manifest (39:69; 45:29). He rejects arbitrary balances and argues justice needs a criterion for mixed deeds; prophets and successors embody right action and belief.
  7:9/q01/p4 The scale can be read inwardly as justice, with sense and intellect as its pans. — reasons: Qashani distinguishes enduring rational virtues, sincere intentions and good works from transient sensations, desires and vices; the former bear weight at a lesser resurrection.
  7:9/q01/p5 Reason, knowledge and sound will act as moral balances. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi reports reason distinguishing truth and falsehood, knowledge distinguishing lawful and unlawful, and sound will distinguishing felicity and misery.
7:9/q02 Why is mawāzīn plural? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · grammar] (turns on: Whether grammatical plurality points to Arabic usage, weighed things, components, separate instruments, or separate measures of truth.)
  7:9/q02/p1 The plural may denote a single balance by Arabic usage. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi and al-Baghawi compare other Arabic plurals; al-A'sha's line has plural breasts and forearms for one each, and al-Baghawi cites 'messengers.' Al-Zajjaj offers this too, but Razi objects that the plain plural permits several scales.
  7:9/q02/p2 The plural reflects the many deeds or records weighed on one balance. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj and the majority reported by Abu Hayyan use the plurality of weighed things; al-Tabataba'i objects that this does not establish the criterion for mixed deeds.
  7:9/q02/p3 There are multiple balances or standards, perhaps for persons or kinds of acts. — reasons: Razi prefers several balances from the plain plural and 21:47, possibly for acts of heart, body and speech. Al-Tabataba'i assigns distinct truths to prayer, almsgiving, fasting and pilgrimage.
  7:9/q02/p4 Each servant has a separate balance. — reasons: Al-Baghawi reports the view and Abu Hayyan attributes it to Hasan; Ibn Atiyya rejects Hasan's account, distinguishing one balance from each person's individual weighing.
  7:9/q02/p5 One great balance has a separate balance suspended for each servant. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi reports this alternative; al-Baghawi states it separately from a simple individual balance.
  7:9/q02/p6 The plural names the balance's combined parts. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi and al-Baghawi list two pans, beam and tongue or indicator, all needed for weighing.
7:9/q03 What is weighed? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · referent] (turns on: Which object or quality receives weight, and whether multiple reported objects can all be weighed.)
  7:9/q03/p1 Written records of deeds are weighed. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi and Ibn Atiyya reason that deeds are accidents and weight belongs to bodies. The ninety-nine scrolls and testimony card report illustrates this; Ibn Kathir says al-Tirmidhi graded it sound. Abu Hayyan says God creates weight in the records. Bursevi reports this as the majority view.
  7:9/q03/p2 Deeds themselves are weighed, made bodily or appearing in forms. — reasons: Ibn Abbas describes believers' deeds as beautiful and unbelievers' as ugly; Ibn Kathir cites traditions of the Qur'an, suras and righteous deeds appearing in forms. Al-Tabataba'i distinguishes moral qualities from physical movements, yet allows embodied appearance.
  7:9/q03/p3 The persons themselves may be weighed. — reasons: A heavy glutton weighs less than a gnat's wing; Ibn Kathir also cites Ibn Masud's thin legs outweighing Uhud and 18:105. Bursevi repeats the huge man's report.
  7:9/q03/p4 Only the ending of a person's deeds is weighed. — reasons: Wahb ibn Munabbih says God grants good or evil endings according to what He wills for the servant.
  7:9/q03/p5 The truth contained in each deed is weighed. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i says one standard measures each act; true worship has weight and false acts none. The book discloses this truth (39:69; 45:29), and prophets and successors can embody its standard.
  7:9/q03/p6 Deeds, their records or forms, and their doers may all be weighed at different times. — reasons: Ibn Kathir reconciles the deed-form, scroll-card and person-weight reports rather than forcing one object.
  7:9/q03/p7 Lasting rational virtues, intentions and deeds carry weight, not passing sensations. — reasons: Qashani's inward account places intellect and sense in the pans and distinguishes lasting from transient qualities.
  7:9/q03/p8 Only deeds undertaken for God's pleasure and reward in the hereafter have weight. — reasons: Islahi cites 18:103–105: acts without those aims count as evil and lack weight.
7:9/q04 What makes the scales heavy or light? [خَفَّتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether weight expresses net good versus evil, the presence of good or truth, inward quality, divine acceptance, or physical heaviness.)
  7:9/q04/p1 Good deeds make them heavy, while few or absent good deeds leave them light. — reasons: Mujahid explains light scales as scant good works; Ibn Ashur calls lightness a matching metaphor for their absence. Hasan says the good-deed pan should grow heavy while sin's pan grows light. Ali ibn Abi Talib's report says good deeds add weight and sins lighten it.
  7:9/q04/p2 Good and bad deeds are compared; whichever exceeds the other determines the result. — reasons: Razi divides people by predominance and denies equal totals; Jabir and Ibn Abbas report one deed or grain can tip the result. Study Quran attributes the light balance to evil outweighing good.
  7:9/q04/p3 Truth, sincerity and right intention give a deed weight; falsehood has none. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i uses prayer, almsgiving and speech as examples of distinct truths. Abu Bakr and al-Harith contrast following truth with falsehood; Islahi makes seeking God's pleasure and the hereafter decisive. Qashani weighs sincere lasting virtues.
  7:9/q04/p4 A person's inner reality outweighing their outward state gives a heavy balance; the reverse gives a light one. — reasons: Ali's report gives this criterion; al-Tabataba'i transmits it but says it is not an explanation of these verses.
  7:9/q04/p5 God's pleasure can make even a single date of charity fill the scale. — reasons: David asks how the vast balance can be filled; God gives this answer in the reports of Ibn al-Jawzi and Bursevi.
  7:9/q04/p6 Heaviness is downward tendency inherent in a heavy object, or its composing parts; lightness is absence of heaviness. — reasons: Jishumi reports Abu Hashim's distinct-attribute account and Abu Ali's parts account, noting their agreement about lightness.
7:9/q05 Who are those whose scales are light? [وَمَنْ خَفَّتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · referent] (turns on: Whether the wording restricts the light-scaled to deniers or includes sinful believers, and what counts as weighty good.)
  7:9/q05/p1 They are disbelievers who deny God's signs. — reasons: Muqatil's identification is repeated by al-Wahidi; Razi reports it as the majority view because 'wronging Our signs' means denial. Al-Nasafi says without faith no good works count. Al-Baghawi and others connect their lightness with denial.
  7:9/q05/p2 They include anyone whose evil deeds exceed the good, including sinful believers. — reasons: Razi gives the two unequal totals; Abu Hayyan says the apparent division includes obedient and sinful believers and unbelievers. Ibn Abbas and Jabir give a one-deed tipping rule; Study Quran reports the predominance of evil.
  7:9/q05/p3 They have no good deeds with weight or deeds lacking the required truth. — reasons: Ibn Ashur calls lightness the absence of good deeds; al-Tabataba'i says false works have no truth-weight. Islahi says acts not aimed at God's pleasure or the afterlife carry no weight.
7:9/q06 Are unbelievers' deeds weighed? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · theology] (turns on: Whether unbelievers' works can carry weighable merit or whether 18:105 and 25:23 rule out their weighing.)
  7:9/q06/p1 Yes; the apparent division includes unbelievers, and some of their deeds can be weighed. — reasons: Abu Hayyan includes believers and unbelievers; al-Qurtubi, as reported by Alusi, says deeds not dependent on Islam can lessen punishment, citing Abu Talib. Ibn Abbas's report gives unbelievers' deeds an ugly light form.
