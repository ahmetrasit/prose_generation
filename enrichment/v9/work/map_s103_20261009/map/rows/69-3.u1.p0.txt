## THE CURRENT MAP
69:3/q01 How are the first mā and adrāka construed? [وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the analysis foregrounds the nominal interrogative clause or the underlying prepositional construction of darā.)
  69:3/q01/p1 The first interrogative mā is the subject and adrāka its predicate; the addressee is its first object. — reasons: Bayḍāwī, Ālūsī, Bursawī, and Elmalılı give the subject–predicate analysis. Elmalılı identifies -ka as first object. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the expression a fixed idiom with causative adrā, explains the shifting object roles, and says the following interrogative suspends its governance; he notes grammarians' inadequate or omitted treatment.
  69:3/q01/p2 The causative verb derives from darā, normally construed with bi; its objects can therefore have different constructions. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says the preposition bi is sometimes omitted and the causative hamza changes the verb's argument structure; he applies this to the following clause.
69:3/q02 What does 'what made you know' ask? [وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the question seeks knowledge of the event's nature or what will occur in it.)
  69:3/q02/p1 It asks what could inform the hearer of the Reality and its nature. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī addresses Muhammad; al-Zajjāj asks what thing could inform the hearer; Bursawī derives adrāka from knowledge and glosses it 'what informed you, Muhammad.' Al-Nasafī asks what could inform one of its nature.
  69:3/q02/p2 It asks what will happen at the Resurrection. — reasons: Al-Māwardī transmits this reading from Yaḥyā ibn Sallām.
69:3/q03 Who is addressed by the singular 'you'? [أَدْرَىٰكَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the grammatical singular is specifically Muhammad or a general audience addressed through him.)
  69:3/q03/p1 The Prophet Muhammad is directly addressed. — reasons: Muqātil, al-Ṭabarī, al-Baghawī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī, and Bursawī speak of the Prophet as the addressee; Ibn al-Jawzī and al-Baghawī emphasize that he had not witnessed the terrors.
  69:3/q03/p2 The singular address is generic, or reaches hearers through the Prophet. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the addressee is indefinite and the masculine singular is generic; Bursawī allows that hearers are addressed through the Prophet.
69:3/q04 Does the formula 'what made you know' imply an explanation will follow? [وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether this instance is read through the reported Quran-wide distinction between adrāka and yudrīka, or as an unanswered question about inconceivable reality.)
  69:3/q04/p1 Yes: Qur'anic 'what made you know' is followed by teaching, unlike 'what makes or could make you know.' — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī transmits Sufyān's contrast and cites 33:63; al-Māwardī and al-Qurṭubī transmit Yaḥyā ibn Sallām, and al-Qurṭubī also Sufyān ibn ʿUyaynah. Ibn ʿĀshūr attributes the report to Ibn ʿAbbās, Sufyān, and Yaḥyā, qualifies it 'if sound,' and cites 33:63 and 42:17; he says al-Rāghib's observation clarifies it, citing 101:10–11, 97:2–3, and 82:18–19. Ṭabāṭabāʾī suggests Ibn ʿAbbās meant that God informed the Prophet, while the contrasting form withholds knowledge.
  69:3/q04/p2 This rhetorical question receives no answer because the ultimate reality exceeds anticipation or imagination. — reasons: Asad explicitly links the absence of an answer to the event's inconceivability.
69:3/q05 What effect does the question produce? [وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا ٱلْحَآقَّةُ · rhetoric] (turns on: The notes emphasize different features of the interrogative: awe, a jolt to attention, reproach, or an unfamiliar name.)
  69:3/q05/p1 It magnifies the Resurrection, evokes its terror, and conveys that its full reality exceeds imagination. — reasons: Muqātil points to its severity; al-Ṭabarī reports Qatādah; al-Qushayrī stresses gravity; al-Wāḥidī's language scholars say the Prophet knows it by description but is addressed as though he did not; Ibn ʿAṭiyyah invokes unknown horrors and details; al-Qurṭubī combines grandeur with incomplete knowledge. Al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Biqāʿī, Bursawī, Ālūsī, Elmalılı, Ibn ʿĀshūr, Asad, Iṣlāḥī, and Study Quran stress the limits of conception. Ibn ʿĀshūr explains an association between asking about extraordinary things and their greatness, and offers a denial-of-knowledge reading with the same awe. Bursawī says every imagined state falls short; Study Quran compares 101:2–3; Iṣlāḥī asks what will befall stubborn deniers.
  69:3/q05/p2 Its abrupt, ambiguous wording jolts an indifferent listener into attention. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says the truncated expression focuses attention on the opening subject through uncertainty, and compares the startling address in al-Qāriʿah, where he promises further explanation.
  69:3/q05/p3 The question also reproves the listener. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyyah joins reproach to the evocation of terrors beyond knowledge.
  69:3/q05/p4 It introduces a name unfamiliar to the Prophet and his people. — reasons: Al-Māwardī reports al-Aṣamm's observation that al-Ḥāqqah was unfamiliar in their speech.
69:3/q06 How much can anyone know of the Reality before witnessing it? [وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا ٱلْحَآقَّةُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the emphasis is the distinction between reported knowledge and sight, or the general inability of creatures to encompass the event.)
  69:3/q06/p1 Its occurrence can be known by divine description or report, but its full nature and terrors cannot be comprehended before they are seen. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī's language scholars distinguish knowledge by description from direct knowledge; al-Baghawī, Ibn al-Jawzī, and al-Qurṭubī cite the Prophet's lack of witness. Al-Biqāʿī says the past-tense formula leaves imagination open. Bursawī says the Prophet knew it would occur but not its manner; Elmalılı says divine report and faith disclose it beforehand without encompassing it, whereas occurrence will disclose it fully.
  69:3/q06/p2 No creature can grasp or describe its true magnitude or nature. — reasons: Al-Nasafī stresses the Hour's greatness and severity; Bayḍāwī calls its true nature beyond anyone's grasp; Abū Ḥayyān says its ordeals exceed description; Ālūsī says every estimate falls short; Ibn ʿĀshūr says its extraordinary nature defeats imagination; Asad calls it beyond anticipation.
69:3/q07 How is the second mā al-Ḥāqqah parsed? [مَا ٱلْحَآقَّةُ · grammar] (turns on: Which term functions as the nominal predicate, and how the interrogative blocks governance by the preceding verb.)
  69:3/q07/p1 It is a nominal question with interrogative mā as subject and al-Ḥāqqah as predicate, referring back to the first mention. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās describes subject and predicate and a predicate about the first al-Ḥāqqah; al-Wāḥidī identifies the second noun as predicate and referring to the first. Al-Thaʿlabī allows the first al-Ḥāqqah to be subject with its predicate following. Bursawī reports nested nominal clauses linked by the repeated noun.
  69:3/q07/p2 The interrogative mā is the predicate, making the Reality a wondrous, terrifying subject of inquiry. — reasons: Bursawī expressly prefers this analysis over the reported nested-clause analysis.
  69:3/q07/p3 The second mā is nominative because an interrogative is not governed by the preceding adrāka. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj and al-Wāḥidī state the rule; al-Wāḥidī glosses the resulting sense as 'What could make you know what it is?' Ibn ʿĀshūr says the third mā suspends adrā from governing its objects.
69:3/q08 What syntactic role does the second question have after adrāka? [أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا ٱلْحَآقَّةُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the interrogative clause fills a direct object slot or depends on an elided preposition.)
  69:3/q08/p1 The question 'what is al-Ḥāqqah?' supplies adrāka's second object. — reasons: Al-Nasafī calls it a clause in the second-object slot; Bursawī and Elmalılı say the same. Ibn ʿĀshūr discusses the verb's object structure and suspension by the interrogative.
  69:3/q08/p2 The clause is accusative after an omitted preposition, suspended as an indirect question rather than governed directly as the second object. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān derives the construction from darā with bi and its causative form. Ālūsī explicitly rejects direct second-object governance in favor of the omitted-preposition analysis.
69:3/q09 What does al-Ḥāqqah refer to here? [ٱلْحَآقَّةُ · referent] (turns on: Whether al-Ḥāqqah is explained as the Day of Resurrection, its disclosure of truth, or an inward manifestation.)
  69:3/q09/p1 It is the Resurrection, the Hour, or the Day of Judgment. — reasons: Al-Jishumī reports al-Aṣamm's identification; al-Durr transmits Qatādah, who adds that it makes each worker realize his deeds; Bursawī calls it a name for the Resurrection; Study Quran says the surah is named for the Day of Judgment in its opening; al-Thaʿlabī reports Ibn ʿAbbās and Qatādah identifying the coming calamity with the Resurrection.
  69:3/q09/p2 It is the ultimate laying-bare of truth, an overwhelming and inevitable reality. — reasons: Asad renders it the laying-bare of truth and stresses awe and the limits of human conception; Study Quran calls ḥāqqah an emphatic form of ḥaqq, truth or reality, which none can avoid.
  69:3/q09/p3 Inwardly, it is the absolute singular manifestation in unity that annihilates distinctions under the One, the Subduer. — reasons: Bursawī transmits al-Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyyah: this manifestation is true because established in itself; it invokes 40:16.
69:3/q10 Why is the event named al-Ḥāqqah? [ٱلْحَآقَّةُ · meaning] (turns on: The named event is linked to certainty, fulfilled divine truth, disclosed deeds and recompense, the overpowering of unbelievers, or inward self-establishment.)
  69:3/q10/p1 Its arrival is established, due, inevitable, and truly beyond denial. — reasons: Bursawī derives the name from ḥaqq, 'to be due and established,' citing 20:15. Al-Jishumī reports Ibn al-Anbārī's 'inevitable event'
