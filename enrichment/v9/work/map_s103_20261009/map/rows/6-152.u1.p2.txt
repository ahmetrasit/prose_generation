6:152/q23 What speech does the command to be just cover? [وَإِذَا قُلْتُمْ فَٱعْدِلُوا۟ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the unqualified word speech is universal or primarily refers to testimony and adjudication because of the kin clause.)
  6:152/q23/p1 All consequential speech must be truthful and fair, including testimony, judgment, advice, preaching, reports, and mediation. — reasons: Jishumi lists wills, legal opinions, rulings, and schools; Qadi adds concise comprehensible preaching, restrained enjoining of right, accurate narratives and message transmission. Abu Hayyan rejects restriction without evidence. Ibn Ashur details evaluations, reconciliation, promises, oaths, praise, insults, and commercial disclosure; Study Quran and Kuran Yolu generalize integrity in speech. Tabrisī says habitual just speech also leads to just action.
  6:152/q23/p2 Its primary application is testimony or judgment, especially in disputes involving kin. — reasons: Razi reports commentators' restriction to testimony or commands and prohibitions; Asad prefers an evidentiary setting because kin are mentioned. Qurtubi and Baydawi emphasize judgments, and Ibn Ashur and Islahi take this as a central application without denying wider speech.
6:152/q24 What is the force of when you speak? [وَإِذَا قُلْتُمْ · rhetoric]
  6:152/q24/p1 It means when intending to speak and allows silence where just speech is impossible. — reasons: Burhan explains Arabic can name intended action by the act, comparing ablution before prayer in 5:6. Ibn Ashur says the condition allows and sometimes requires silence; he cites Umar rebuking a man for revealing his sister's former transgression to a suitor.
6:152/q25 Why command just speech rather than only forbid falsehood? [فَٱعْدِلُوا۟ · rhetoric]
  6:152/q25/p1 The positive command calls for disclosing truth while allowing permissible indirect speech where no right is at stake. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says a flat falsehood ban could condemn equivocal speech under fear or accommodation; he cites a hadith that indirect expressions leave room to avoid lying. Burhan argues justice in speech implies justice in action more strongly, comparing the ban on saying uff to parents at 17:23.
6:152/q26 Who is the relative in even if a relative? [وَلَوْ كَانَ ذَا قُرْبَىٰ · referent] (turns on: Which omitted person is supplied as the subject of kāna.)
  6:152/q26/p1 The person spoken about, testified for or against, or judged may be the speaker's relative. — reasons: Wahidi reports Zajjaj's explanation that the omitted party may be beneficiary or accused, comparing 5:106. Ibn Ashur identifies the circumstantial waw and concessive law, comparing 4:135; affection must neither invent a right nor conceal liability. Samin also allows the person spoken to to be kin.
  6:152/q26/p2 The person addressed in speech may be a relative. — reasons: Samin explicitly includes the addressee as well as the person spoken about; the grammatical subject is omitted.
6:152/q27 How is even if he is a relative parsed? [وَلَوْ كَانَ ذَا قُرْبَىٰ · grammar]
  6:152/q27/p1 Dha qurba is the predicate of kana, whose subject is omitted; the waw is circumstantial and law concessive. — reasons: Jishumi gives the predicate analysis; Ibn Ashur explains the concessive circumstantial construction and its emphasis on the exceptional kin case; Thalabi and Wahidi explain the omitted party.
6:152/q28 What inward discipline is read in just speech? [وَإِذَا قُلْتُمْ فَٱعْدِلُوا۟ · inward] (turns on: Whether the spiritual emphasis falls on God-oriented speech itself or prior correction of the speaker.)
  6:152/q28/p1 True speech is of, through, and for God, free of self-interest, and should be sparse and grounded in knowledge. — reasons: Tustari distinguishes God-directed speech from self-directed speech, warns that evil suspicion, irrelevant talk, and misuse of limbs destroy certainty, truthfulness, and scruple. Abu Sulayman teaches remembrance before speech; Muhammad ibn Hamid counsels words with no liability. Bursevi says only realized people can speak through God without claims and cites a Persian hoopoe verse.
  6:152/q28/p2 One should correct one's own faults before criticizing others. — reasons: Al-Rabi ibn Khaytham says dissatisfaction with himself keeps him from fault-finding; Jawzajani requires self-discipline before enjoining right and lists hunger, night vigil, remembrance, righteous company, travel, solitude, restrained speech, and patience.
6:152/q29 What is God's covenant that must be fulfilled? [وَبِعَهْدِ ٱللَّهِ أَوْفُوا۟ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the genitive identifies a covenant imposed by God, made to God, or protected by God; the broad reading includes these together.)
  6:152/q29/p1 It embraces God's commands and prohibitions together with lawful vows, oaths, and human agreements He orders people to keep. — reasons: Jishumi says all senses are intended; Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi, Elmalili, Ibn Ashur, Islahi, and Kuran Yolu include obligations and valid pledges. Nasafi also lists promise and threat. Ibn Ashur includes polytheists' alliances and peace pacts, Arab poetry praising fidelity, Meccan Hilf al-Fudul and Mutayyibin pacts, and violation of Mecca's safety promise to Abraham. Razi stresses secret oaths can be broken unseen; Biqai includes what one owes or is owed.
  6:152/q29/p2 It chiefly means the religious duties and ordinances God imposed. — reasons: Maturidi, Ibn Jarir, and Study Quran identify commands and prohibitions; Ibn Kathir adds God's Book and the Messenger's Sunnah. Tabatabai prefers this in context, citing 17:34 and 5:106, but allows a pledge to God. Baydawi names justice and religious law.
  6:152/q29/p3 It is a vow, oath, or pledge made to God or sworn by God's name. — reasons: Said ibn Jubayr cites confirmed vows and 16:91; Nahhas mentions covenants with God or oaths to people; Mawardi excludes sinful oaths. Razi notes private oath fidelity is subtle.
  6:152/q29/p4 It is the primordial covenant. — reasons: Nasafi lists it; Tabrizi places it on the Day of the Covenant. Qashani and Bursevi draw inward implications from it.
6:152/q30 How can the genitive God's covenant be understood? [بِعَهْدِ ٱللَّهِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether God makes the covenant, receives the pledge, or authorizes a covenant between people.)
  6:152/q30/p1 God is the covenanting subject, imposing obligations on people. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and Samin explain this possessive relation; Samin cites 33:23 and 48:10.
  6:152/q30/p2 God is the object: people pledge to Him. — reasons: Abu Hayyan, Samin, and Ibn Ashur allow this analysis; Samin cites 33:23 and 48:10.
  6:152/q30/p3 The attribution explains that God commands keeping human covenants. — reasons: Samin calls this an explanatory possessive; Abu Hayyan and Ibn Ashur also permit human-to-human covenants under God's command.
6:152/q31 Why is God's covenant placed first in its clause? [وَبِعَهْدِ ٱللَّهِ أَوْفُوا۟ · rhetoric]
  6:152/q31/p1 Fronting emphasizes special priority for fidelity to God's covenant. — reasons: Alusi says the fronted phrase emphasizes it; Ibn Ashur appeals to an audience proud of pacts and contrasts 2:217: they should keep their chosen covenant with God.
6:152/q32 What inward covenant does the phrase suggest? [بِعَهْدِ ٱللَّهِ · inward] (turns on: Different inward applications emphasize exclusive devotion or self-discipline before social correction.)
  6:152/q32/p1 It means unity, obedience, exclusive worship and love of God under a primordial pledge renewed later. — reasons: Qashani joins unity and obligation; Bursevi says see none beside the Lord and cites Persian verses on love for the Friend from primordial morning to time's end.
  6:152/q32/p2 The chief covenant is enjoining right and forbidding wrong after disciplining oneself. — reasons: Jawzajani says personal correction precedes correcting others and specifies ascetic exercises when the self resists good.
6:152/q33 What does that which He enjoined point back to? [ذَٰلِكُمْ وَصَّىٰكُم بِهِۦ · referent] (turns on: Whether the demonstrative simply collects the immediately listed duties or identifies their scriptural addressees.)
  6:152/q33/p1 The duties just enumerated: orphan care, fair measures within ability, just speech, and covenant keeping. — reasons: Farisi and Wahidi list these duties; Tabrisī, Bursevi, Alusi, and Biqai say the closing gathers and stresses them. Tabatabai says the covenant is reinforced; Wahidi and Farisi cite 48:10's reward for covenant keeping.
  6:152/q33/p2 It may refer to Torah commands given to Jews or Qur'anic commands given to Muslims. — reasons: Mawardi reports both possible audiences of the demonstrative.
6:152/q34 What is the purpose of the closing call to remember? [لَعَلَّكُمْ تَذَكَّرُونَ · interpretation]
  6:152/q34/p1 People should recall the instructions, reflect, take heed, and act rather than neglect them. — reasons: Jishumi lists remembrance, admonition, avoiding neglect, reflection, and recalling punishment. Wahidi, Tabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzi, Ibn Kathir, Elmalili, Bursevi, and Alusi stress action; Ibn Kathir says it stops former conduct. Ibn Atiyya treats la'allakum as hope from the human perspective.
6:152/q35 Why does this group end with remembrance rather than understanding? [لَعَلَّكُمْ تَذَكَّرُونَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the ending reflects complexity, familiar but forgotten virtues, or the different form of prohibitions and commands.)
  6:152/q35/p1 The first five duties are obvious, while these four need reflection to find a just balance. — reasons: Razi gives this explanation, followed by Abu Hayyan, Samin, and Alusi; Tabatabai says remembering social harm to weak persons, commerce, and truth demands reflection.
