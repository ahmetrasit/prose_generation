## THE CURRENT MAP
6:82/q01 What does faith mean here? [ءَامَنُوا۟ إِيمَٰنَهُم · interpretation] (turns on: Whether faith names assent alone or also the acts and freedom from wrongdoing that perfect it.)
  6:82/q01/p1 It is belief in God alone and in what must be believed. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says the omitted object is God, supplied by the context. Al-Alusi says it includes what is obligatory to believe. Tabatabai says initial assent to lordship is qualified by rejection of partners; Ibn Ashur notes Abraham's people acknowledged God while adding gods, like Arab polytheists.
  6:82/q01/p2 In religious usage faith includes doing obligations and avoiding major sins. — reasons: Jishumi distinguishes the linguistic sense, affirmation, from this religious usage.
  6:82/q01/p3 Faith can mean assent, even where a major sin or association is also present. — reasons: Razi's school argues that adding freedom from wrongdoing would be redundant if it already formed part of faith. Al-Alusi cites 12:106 for faith in God coexisting with association; he also says faith may be obscured or alternate with unbelief.
  6:82/q01/p4 The secure combine faith with good works of every kind. — reasons: Study Quran attributes this to Razi.
6:82/q02 Which believers receive this promise? [ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ · referent] (turns on: Whether the wording's general form governs or the occasion and an unrestricted reading of wrongdoing limit its subjects.)
  6:82/q02/p1 Believers generally, across communities, who meet the verse's condition. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi records a general view. Abu Hayyan calls general scope apparent. Tabatabai argues that faith and wrongdoing affect human nature in every era, rejecting restriction to Abraham's community. Wahidi derives ultimate safety for whoever dies without shirk; the traveler reports illustrate application beyond Abraham.
  6:82/q02/p2 Abraham alone. — reasons: Ali is reported to exclude this Muslim community. Some accounts specify Abraham alone rather than his companions. Tabari says the unrestricted wrong reading was limited by some to Abraham.
  6:82/q02/p3 Abraham and his companions. — reasons: A report from Ali names Abraham and his companions and expressly excludes this Muslim community; another version narrows it to Abraham alone.
  6:82/q02/p4 Those who migrated to Medina. — reasons: Attributed to Ikrima; Tabari lists the Emigrants as another restriction used to explain an all-wrongdoing reading.
6:82/q03 What does 'mix' mean here? [يَلْبِسُوٓا۟ إِيمَٰنَهُم · meaning]
  6:82/q03/p1 It means combining or covering faith with something alien to it. — reasons: Jishumi glosses lubs as mixing. Ibn Ashur describes a metaphor of mixing like substances, citing 2:42, especially claims about lordship. Tabatabai calls it covering innate faith and obstructing its beneficial effect rather than erasing it.
6:82/q04 How is yulbisū read? [يَلْبِسُوٓا۟ · readings] (turns on: The verb's vocalization and form, and whether Mujahid's wording counts as a reading or an explanatory gloss.)
  6:82/q04/p1 The majority read yalbisū with an open yāʾ, 'they mix'. — reasons: Al-Samin gives the majority vocalization and its simple-verb sense.
  6:82/q04/p2 Ikrima reads yulbisū with a rounded yāʾ, 'they make mix'. — reasons: Al-Samin derives it from form IV; Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan also report the vocalization.
  6:82/q04/p3 Mujahid's bi-shirk replaces bi-ẓulm as an explanatory wording. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya reports the wording as a reading; Abu Hayyan says it is a gloss, not a canonical reading, since it differs from the written text.
6:82/q05 What does ẓulm mean in this verse? [بِظُلْمٍ · meaning] (turns on: Whether context and the Prophetic explanation specify shirk, or the general word and 'mixing' extend the condition to sins or other harms.)
  6:82/q05/p1 It is shirk, associating partners with God, including idol worship. — reasons: The recurrent proof is the Prophet's explanation to worried Companions using Luqman 31:13; Tirmidhi grades that report hasan sahih. Abu Bakr, Ibn Abbas, Ubayy, Salman, Hudhayfa, Alqama, Ibrahim, Qatada, Mujahid and others are reported with this gloss. Tabari prefers the sound prophetic report. Ibn Ashur says shirk violates God's exclusive right to worship; Biqai says the indefinite term includes every degree of shirk. Al-Alusi says 'great injustice' points to its fullest instance, though the word can denote other wrongs. Idolaters claimed intercession and nearness to God (39:3).
  6:82/q05/p2 It covers every forbidden act or neglected command, or injury to oneself or others. — reasons: Tabari says the fully general reading would assure only the sinless and its proponents restrict the verse's audience; others leave lesser sinners to God's will. Mawardi records general and restricted versions. Baydawi mentions 'sin'. A commentator cited by Tabatabai includes religious and worldly harm, but Tabatabai rejects treating accidental bodily damage as the verse's concern and says that scope would leave no real instance. Ibn al-Jawzi's root definitions include putting something outside its place or acting concerning what one does not own; Tabarsi cites poetry on cutting a camel wrongly and diverting rain. Ibn al-Jawzi also records ordinary injustice (2:35 and other verses), the proverb ma zalam, undug land, claims of oppression, a severe oppressor, not leaving a path, and tooth-water or shine. Tabatabai traces expansion from social injustice to lawbreaking, sin and accidental harm.
  6:82/q05/p3 It includes grave sins that corrupt the effect or reward of faith, but excludes minor wrongs expiated by obedience or repentance. — reasons: Al-Jubba'i and al-Balkhi say grave sins cancel reward. Zamakhshari says 'mixing' fits sin, not disbelief, and cites 9:36; Jishumi invokes a garment metaphor of grave sin. Tabatabai grades relevant wrong by understanding: major sins include disobeying parents, consuming orphans' goods, murder, adultery and wine; 4:31 supports forgiveness of lesser sins. He excludes mistakes of forgetfulness or ignorance and extends higher ranks to disliked, permissible or inwardly obstructive acts. Ibn Ashur objects that Abraham's debate concerned monotheism before a law and God's response would likewise address that point.
  6:82/q05/p4 It can denote doubt, misguidance, Kharijite belief, or allegiance to the Imams' enemies. — reasons: Tabatabai reports Abu Basir's variant from al-Sadiq as doubt, another as shirk; al-Sadiq also names Kharijites and followers, and misguidance and what exceeds it. Tabatabai takes these varied reports as degrees of a general term.
  6:82/q05/p5 It is hidden association through any remnant of self or turning to other than God. — reasons: Qashani makes essential monotheism unmingled with self-manifestation the condition of true security; Ibn Tahir says the faithful do not turn elsewhere during trials.
  6:82/q05/p6 In broader Qur'anic usage the root can mean denial, diminution, or theft. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi cites denial of signs at 7:9, 7:136, 17:59; diminution at 4:49, 18:33, 21:47; theft at 5:38-39 and 12:75. These are lexical comparisons, not claims that 6:82 means theft or diminution.
6:82/q06 How is the verse's clause structure parsed? [ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَلَمْ يَلْبِسُوٓا۟ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ لَهُمُ ٱلْأَمْنُ وَهُم مُّهْتَدُونَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the opening relative phrase is an answer or independent subject, how the nested nouns predicate one another, and whether the two wāw clauses are relative, circumstantial or independent.)
  6:82/q06/p1 The relative phrase is a subject; 'those' starts a nested subject clause, with 'for them is security' as its predicate. — reasons: Nahhas makes alladhina and ulaika successive subjects, al-amn the subject of lahum, and the full nested clause the first predicate; al-Alusi prefers this broad parse. Al-Samin gives the explicit three-subject nesting; Ibn Atiyya makes al-amn nominative and lahum prepositional predicate. Abu Hayyan notes an omitted subject or predicate can be supplied.
  6:82/q06/p2 The relative phrase is predicate of an omitted 'they'; 'those' begins a new sentence. — reasons: Al-Samin ties this to a spoken answer by Abraham, his people, or God instructing the Prophet, and permits an omitted 'say/they said'. Al-Alusi calls the omitted-subject analysis remote.
  6:82/q06/p3 'Those' is apposition to the relative phrase and 'for them' its predicate, with security a governed subject or delayed subject. — reasons: Al-Samin records both variants of the placement/function of lahum and al-amn; al-Alusi calls the appositional alternative remote.
  6:82/q06/p4 'Those' is a second subject, 'for them' its predicate, and 'security' an active subject. — reasons: Al-Samin lists this as a fourth independent-report parse.
  6:82/q06/p5 'Those' is a separator; 'for them is security' predicates the relative phrase. — reasons: Attributed to Nahhas and al-Hawfi by al-Samin, who calls it odd because demonstratives do not serve as separators; Abu Hayyan calls their analysis remote.
  6:82/q06/p6 'They did not mix' continues the relative clause. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and al-Samin allow coordination under alladhina.
  6:82/q06/p7 'They did not mix' is a circumstantial clause. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and al-Samin cite 19:20 as a parallel negative circumstance. Al-Samin rejects Ibn Asfur's rarity claim and Ibn Khuruf's mandatory-waw claim.
  6:82/q06/p8 'And they are guided' is a second nominal sentence, either independent or circumstantial. — reasons: Nahhas gives it a subject-predicate structure. Al-Samin allows independence or circumstance; Ibn Ashur prefers the full sentence as coordinated with the earlier nominal sentence, citing 64:1 and 57:2.
