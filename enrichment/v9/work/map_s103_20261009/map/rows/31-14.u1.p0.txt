## THE CURRENT MAP
31:14/q01 Whose speech is the command about parents, and how does it fit Luqmān’s counsel? [وَوَصَّيْنَا ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ بِوَٰلِدَيْهِ · links] (turns on: Whether the first-person divine command interrupts the quoted counsel or is itself part of that counsel or an address to Luqmān.)
  31:14/q01/p1 It is a divine interjection in Luqmān’s speech, reinforcing his warning against shirk before his counsel resumes. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās calls it an interruption; Bursevī links it to 31:13. Al-Ālūsī explicitly rejects attribution to Luqmān or a command addressed to him. Elmalılı and Samarrāʾī call it God's own inserted speech; Samarrāʾī says God takes over because parents' standing is great. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says gratitude to parents is itself obedience to God.
  31:14/q01/p2 It remains part of Luqmān’s advice to his son, reported by God. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān report this possibility; al-Ālūsī attributes it to al-Baḥr and dismisses it.
  31:14/q01/p3 God speaks these words to Luqmān as an additional instruction to him. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān report the view; al-Ālūsī reports and dismisses it.
31:14/q02 To whom does the singular command concerning parents apply? [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ بِوَٰلِدَيْهِ · referent] (turns on: Whether the singular refers to all people or the person named in the reported occasion.)
  31:14/q02/p1 It addresses human beings generally despite the singular form. — reasons: Ibn Kāmil states that its singular wording has universal force; al-Ālūsī says God enjoined people to care for their parents.
  31:14/q02/p2 It specifically concerns Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ and his parents. — reasons: Muqātil names his father Mālik and mother Ḥamna bint Sufyān; al-Naqqāsh gives Mālik and Ḥamna daughter of Abū Sufyān ibn Umayya. These names vary in wording across the reports. The occasion reports are given separately below.
31:14/q03 What is enjoined concerning the parents? [وَوَصَّيْنَا ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ بِوَٰلِدَيْهِ · interpretation]
  31:14/q03/p1 God enjoins kindness, care, and dutifulness toward both parents. — reasons: Al-Suddī glosses the command as kindness; al-Ṭabarī, al-Ālūsī, and others express it as care and filial honor. Samarrāʾī adds that a helpless newborn would perish without parental care.
31:14/q04 Why say walidayn rather than abawayn here? [بِوَٰلِدَيْهِ وَلِوَٰلِدَيْكَ · rhetoric]
  31:14/q04/p1 Walidayn evokes birth, especially the mother’s role, and suits the ensuing pregnancy and weaning account and her greater claim. — reasons: Samarrāʾī says both duals mean mother and father by masculine predominance, like al-qamarān for sun and moon. He contrasts Qur'anic duties and prayers using walidayn (17:23, 4:36, 6:151, 14:41, 71:28, 29:8, 46:15) with abawayn in inheritance, and notes the father’s burden is not described here.
31:14/q05 What happened in the reported occasion of revelation? [وَوَصَّيْنَا ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ بِوَٰلِدَيْهِ · reports]
  31:14/q05/p1 The passage was revealed concerning Saʿd when his mother threatened to starve unless he left Islam; he refused, and she eventually ate. — reasons: Muṣʿab says she refused food and drink, fainted, and her sons gave her water; Saʿd says relatives pried open her mouth for liquid. A variant says she went three days without food and Saʿd would remain Muslim even if she had a hundred lives. Reports connect 29:8 and 31:15; Abū Hubayra specifically assigns the following no-obedience command to him. Al-Qurṭubī prefers the Saʿd attribution, echoed as his preference by Abū Ḥayyān.
31:14/q06 How far does obedience to parents extend? [بِوَٰلِدَيْهِ · law] (turns on: How a parent's request weighs against religious duties and recommended worship.)
  31:14/q06/p1 Care and obedience do not extend to shirk, major sin, or neglect of an individual duty; obey in permissible matters and consider yielding in recommended matters. — reasons: Muqātil says Saʿd should refuse shirk yet treat his parents kindly and follow the Prophet. Al-Qurṭubī distinguishes forbidden, obligatory, permissible, and recommended acts. The counsel leads into 31:15.
  31:14/q06/p2 A child should not obey a mother who prevents attendance at night prayer out of concern. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan supplies this as a disputed example within the broader obedience question.
31:14/q07 Why is the mother given special care, and how much more than the father? [حَمَلَتْهُ أُمُّهُۥ وَفِصَٰلُهُۥ · interpretation]
  31:14/q07/p1 The mother has a greater claim because she alone bears pregnancy, birth, nursing, sleepless care, and upbringing; reports give her three shares of care before the father’s one. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Qurṭubī infer three maternal measures and one paternal measure from the wording and the Prophet's repeated answer 'your mother' three times, then 'your father.' Al-Ālūsī calls the Bahz ibn Ḥakīm report sound. Ibn Kathīr cites 17:24 and recalls her nights awake; al-Biqāʿī, Bursevī, and Kuran Yolu stress her unique sacrifices. Al-Ālūsī cites a poem about pregnancy, feeding and self-sacrifice and an Arab carrying his mother to pilgrimage; the latter report shows repayment remains small.
31:14/q08 What does wahn upon wahn describe? [وَهْنًا عَلَىٰ وَهْنٍۢ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the weakness belongs to the mother, the child, both, parental seed, or body and resolve; and whether the layers are continuous growth or distinct burdens.)
  31:14/q08/p1 The mother undergoes repeated or increasing weakness and hardship as pregnancy advances and through birth. — reasons: Mujāhid, Qatāda, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḍaḥḥāk, al-Ḥasan and others vary the gloss between weakness, hardship, strain, burden and effort. Ibn ʿAbbās also mentions development upon development. Al-Ṭabarī cites Zuhayr's use of wahn for frailty; al-Māwardī and al-Qurṭubī cite Qanab ibn Umm Ṣāḥib's verse. Al-Zajjāj and later readers specify pregnancy, labor, delivery or postpartum recovery. Al-Jishumī cites 3:139. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Nasafī emphasize day-by-day increase.
  31:14/q08/p2 The mother’s inherent or bodily weakness is compounded by pregnancy. — reasons: Abū Muslim and al-Qurṭubī report a naturally weak constitution becoming weaker in pregnancy; al-Biqāʿī likewise says inherent weakness increases. Bursevī includes weakness of constitution and character.
  31:14/q08/p3 The child’s weakness is layered on the mother’s weakness. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī and al-Māwardī report Mujāhid's referent. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Ālūsī include it among alternatives; al-Ālūsī says this avoids an objection concerning the child's decreasing weakness.
  31:14/q08/p4 It is the weakness of the father’s seed followed by the mother’s seed. — reasons: Ibn Baḥr is reported by al-Māwardī; al-Ḍaḥḥāk and al-Ḥasan are reported by al-Ṭabrisī.
  31:14/q08/p5 It describes the child’s successive weak stages of development. — reasons: Abū Kāmil lists seed, clot, fetus, bones, infant, nursing child and weaned child; al-Samīn says this can make the word the object of 'carried'; Abū Ḥayyān mentions this alternative.
  31:14/q08/p6 It combines bodily weakness with weakness of resolve. — reasons: Al-Māwardī proposes this as a further possible sense.
31:14/q09 How is wahnan ʿalā wahn constructed grammatically? [حَمَلَتْهُ أُمُّهُۥ وَهْنًا عَلَىٰ وَهْنٍۢ · grammar] (turns on: Which word governs the accusative and whether the circumstance belongs to mother or child.)
  31:14/q09/p1 Wahnan is a circumstantial accusative describing the mother as she carries the child. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī says she carried him while weakened. Al-Ṭabrisī takes it as a verbal noun standing for an omitted tahnu; ʿalā wahn can qualify it or attach to its verb. Al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī allow intensified noun or implied possessor constructions. Al-Ṭabrisī also construes the preceding carrying clause as circumstantial with implied qad, comparing 2:28. Al-Bayḍāwī and Elmalılı agree on maternal circumstance.
  31:14/q09/p2 Wahnan is a cognate or absolute object of an implied 'she weakened' because of pregnancy. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī calls it an accusative verbal noun and says the pregnancy clause implies that she weakened; al-Ālūsī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī allow an omitted verb or emphatic cognate object.
  31:14/q09/p3 An omitted preposition makes it 'in weakness,' and ʿalā wahn describes that weakness. — reasons: Abū al-Baqāʾ offers this analysis in al-Samīn; al-Qurṭubī reports al-Naḥḥās's second-object analysis after an omitted preposition.
  31:14/q09/p4 Wahnan is the object of 'carried,' describing the child through embryonic stages, or a circumstance of the child. — reasons: Al-Samīn contrasts it with the mother's circumstance; al-Ālūsī relates the child circumstance to Mujāhid's interpretation. Abū Ḥayyān calls it a developmental alternative.
31:14/q10 How is wahn vocalized? [وَهْنًا عَلَىٰ وَهْنٍۢ · readings] (turns on: Dialectal or morphological vocalization of the hāʾ; the notes say the sense is unchanged.)
  31:14/q10/p1 Both occurrences have a quiescent hāʾ, the majority form. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān identify this as the majority reading; the alternate has the same meaning.
  31:14/q10/p2 Both occurrences have fatḥa on the hāʾ. — reasons: Reported from ʿĪsā al-Thaqafī and a transmission from Abū ʿAmr, also al-Ḍaḥḥāk and ʿĀṣim al-Jaḥdarī. Ibn Jinnī allows a verbal noun like waḍar and waḥar. Abū ʿAlī suggests guttural influence; Abū Ḥayyān mentions dialectal analogy and regular verbal noun; al-Ālūsī mentions the Kufan guttural pattern and notes al-Qāmūs does not restrict either noun form. Al-Zamakhsharī cites two conjugations; al-Qurṭubī notes multiple Arabic forms. Meaning is unchanged.
