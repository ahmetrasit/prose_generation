## THE CURRENT MAP
3:200/q01 What does 'be patient' require? [ٱصْبِرُوا۟ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether general obedience or a specific setting defines the command.)
  3:200/q01/p1 Steadfastness in religion and obedience, including duties, abstaining from sin and trials. — reasons: Tabari favors unrestricted scope because God specified no exception. Hasan includes ease and hardship until death; Said names obligations, Qatada God's rights, Razi doctrinal argument, worship, avoiding sins, illness, poverty, drought and fear. Alusi cites patience in affliction, obedience and abstinence.
  3:200/q01/p2 Patience in jihad beside the Prophet. — reasons: Zayd reads all three military; Ibn al-Anbari prefers this for the first. Al-Farra' says endure with the Prophet.
  3:200/q01/p3 Restrain oneself at affliction's first shock and meet trials without despair. — reasons: The Prophet locates true patience at the first shock; Harith bears affliction, Jurayri prepares for it, Junayd restrains agitation, Dhu al-Nun gives five signs. Ibn Ata links certainty; other sayings name worldly and eternal fruit and a path through contentment and modesty.
  3:200/q01/p4 Endure the five prayers and maintain mosque worship. — reasons: Hasan glosses patience as prayers; Abu Hurayra applies the whole verse to timely prayer, resisting desires and remaining in mosques.
3:200/q02 How is the first imperative parsed? [ٱصْبِرُوا۟ · grammar]
  3:200/q02/p1 It is a plural imperative with its nūn dropped. — reasons: Nahhas gives this grammatical analysis.
3:200/q03 What does 'vie in patience' require? [وَصَابِرُوا۟ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether reciprocal patience faces an enemy, a social trial, delayed relief, or serves emphasis.)
  3:200/q03/p1 Outlast enemies in war and other opponents. — reasons: Tabari invokes the reciprocal Arabic form. Farra', Zamakhshari and Nasafi require at least as much endurance as enemies; Ibn Ashur says the first to yield loses. Antara, Zufar and an unnamed poet illustrate battle. Biqai includes hypocrites; Razi adds enjoining right and answering doubts.
  3:200/q03/p2 Patiently await God's promise of relief and victory. — reasons: Muhammad ibn Ka'b and Ata emphasize expectation; a saying calls waiting for relief worship. Abu Umar prefers this, Qurtubi says the enemy reading is majority.
  3:200/q03/p3 Bear the conduct of relatives and neighbors and act generously. — reasons: Razi includes nonretaliation (7:199; 25:72), preferring others (59:9), and forgiveness (2:237), distinguishing shared from private hardship.
  3:200/q03/p4 Persevere in maintaining daily prayers. — reasons: Hasan applies the verb to the prayers.
  3:200/q03/p5 Persevere against the lower self and its desires. — reasons: Baydawi and Bursevi call the ego a chief adversary; Study Quran reports the saying that return from battle is return from lesser to greater struggle against passions.
  3:200/q03/p6 The second verb repeats the first for emphasis. — reasons: Abu Hayyan transmits this as another interpretation.
3:200/q04 Why are the three endurance commands ordered separately? [ٱصْبِرُوا۟ وَصَابِرُوا۟ وَرَابِطُوا۟ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the escalation is military, collective, or inward.)
  3:200/q04/p1 They progress from general patience to harder mutual struggle and persistent vigilance. — reasons: Zamakhshari, Baydawi, Bursevi and Alusi single out a harder special form; Alusi compares Gabriel after angels and the middle prayer after prayers. Ibn Ashur emphasizes the endurance contest; Alusi rejects an elaborate alternative as strained.
  3:200/q04/p2 They expand from individual patience to shared endurance and coordinated community strength. — reasons: Razi distinguishes personal and social duties; Tabatabai says mutual reliance multiplies strength and ribat binds religious activity in all circumstances. Alusi reports but criticizes Razi's elaborate faculty scheme.
  3:200/q04/p3 They describe inward ascent from restraint to binding the heart to God. — reasons: Junayd intensifies patience then binds the secret to God outwardly and inwardly; Bursevi ranks patience below musabara below ribat.
3:200/q05 What does 'rābiṭū' require? [وَرَابِطُوا۟ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether binding denotes frontier defense, prayer, general constancy, communal unity, inward vigilance or an Imam; prayer may be literal or figurative.)
  3:200/q05/p1 Guard vulnerable frontiers against enemies, mounted or on foot. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, Tabari and others trace ribat to tethered horses, then extend it to guards without mounts; 8:60 supports readiness. Al-Akhtal and Labid attest horses and sentries. Ibn Ashur distinguishes lookout from merely readying horses, citing old mountain passes, later coastal stations, Uhud and the Confederates.
  3:200/q05/p2 Wait between prayers and maintain mosque worship. — reasons: Abu Salama's argument is that no military frontier existed in the Prophet's day. Ali, Jabir and Abu Hurayra report the Prophet's ablution, mosque-walking and waiting formula. Qurtubi cites Khalil and Shaybani for literal breadth; Ibn Atiyya, Ibn Ashur and Alusi read analogy. Ibn Kathir questions one attribution and calls the Abu Ayyub verse link unusual.
  3:200/q05/p3 Stay firm in duty, binding the heart and body to worship. — reasons: Abu Ubayda and Ibn al-Anbari cite Akhtal's use of ribat for constancy; Wahidi cites Labid. Razi links jihad and prayer by heart-binding; Qurtubi includes disciplined intention and body; Elmalili includes congregational attachment.
  3:200/q05/p4 Hold the inner secret in witnessing and guard the self. — reasons: Junayd binds the secret to God; Ja'far binds spirits to direct witnessing; Qashani assigns watchfulness to the spirit. Razi disciplines inner powers; other allusions invoke the reproachful self and God's approval.
  3:200/q05/p5 Bind the community's strengths across its religious duties. — reasons: Tabatabai extends the collective command to coordinated action in hardship and ease.
  3:200/q05/p6 Remain attached to the exemplary guides or Imams. — reasons: Reports from Ja'far vary: exemplary guides, watchfulness with the Imam, or attachment to the Imams; preceding patience commands also vary.
  3:200/q05/p7 Hold to God's promise of victory without tiring. — reasons: Abu Hayyan attributes this to Ubayy and Muhammad ibn Ka'b.
3:200/q06 Who technically counts as a frontier guard? [وَرَابِطُوا۟ · law] (turns on: Whether the question concerns the guard's status or safe residence.)
  3:200/q06/p1 One who travels to a frontier and stays a period; a settled resident is not technically a murābiṭ. — reasons: Ibn al-Mawwaz and Ibn Atiyya distinguish local families who still protect the post. Elmalili includes a modern armed sentry in barracks or outposts.
  3:200/q06/p2 A fighter may take family to a secure post, but should leave them from an unsafe frontier. — reasons: Ibn Khuzaymandad fears their capture at an insecure post.
3:200/q07 What reward do reports attach to frontier service? [وَرَابِطُوا۟ · reports] (turns on: The reports differ in duration, numerical reward and conditions; some large figures have chain criticism.)
  3:200/q07/p1 A day and night surpass a month of fasting and prayer. — reasons: Salman adds continuing provision and deeds after death and safety from grave testing. Some versions except necessary interruption; Ibn Kathir defends the Salman route via Shurahbil.
  3:200/q07/p2 A day of guard is better than the world and all it contains. — reasons: Sahl's report is transmitted by Bukhari; versions mention a whip's space in Paradise and morning or evening travel.
  3:200/q07/p3 A guard dying on duty receives continuing deeds and provision and protection from grave trials. — reasons: Fadala, Uqba, Abu Hurayra and Salman transmit variants; Ibn Kathir cites Tirmidhi's hasan sahih and corroboration for Ibn Lahi'a. Qurtubi makes death on duty the condition, contrasting charity, useful knowledge and a child's prayer; guarding faith enables all worship.
  3:200/q07/p4 A night equals or exceeds a thousand nights of worship. — reasons: Uthman's versions differ between equality and superiority.
  3:200/q07/p5 A day creates seven vast trenches against Hell. — reasons: Jabir describes each as seven heavens and earths wide; Tabarani calls its chain acceptable.
  3:200/q07/p6 A day outside Ramadan equals a century; in Ramadan a millennium. — reasons: Ubayy's report promises continued reward even after safe return; Qurtubi draws that inference. Ibn Kathir calls the route gharib and munkar and its transmitter accused.
  3:200/q07/p7 One night surpasses a thousand years of worship at home. — reasons: Anas counts 360 days per year and a thousand years per day; Ibn Kathir calls it gharib and its transmitter weak and accused.
  3:200/q07/p8 Guarding wins special praise and reward. — reasons: At Hunayn Anas ibn Abi Mirthad guarded a pass against Hawazin; a sentry-prayer report has a broken link. A contented dusty horseman, Ibn al-Mubarak's Tarsus poem, and even a campaign horse tether illustrate merit.
  3:200/q07/p9 Prayer at a frontier is worth two million prayers. — reasons: Abu al-Shaykh transmits Anas's report.
  3:200/q07/p10 Frontier guarding exceeds combat in merit. — reasons: Ibn Umar reasons that guarding Muslim lives outweighs shedding enemy blood.
  3:200/q07/p11 A day is better than a thousand days elsewhere. — reasons: Tirmidhi calls the Uthman route hasan gharib; Ahmad adds Uthman's invitation to serve.
  3:200/q07/p12 Three coastal guard days equal a year of ribat. — reasons: Umm al-Darda' transmits this.
  3:200/q07/p13 Eyes that guard in God's cause escape Hell. — reasons: Abu Rayhana's cold-night report and Ibn Abbas pair guarding with weeping from God-fear; Mu'adh's unpaid sentry report excepts only passage under 19:71.
