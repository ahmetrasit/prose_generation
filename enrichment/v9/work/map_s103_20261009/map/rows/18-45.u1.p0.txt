## THE CURRENT MAP
18:45/q01 Whom does the command to give this parable address? [وَٱضْرِبْ لَهُم · referent] (turns on: Whether the address is tied to the wealthy opponents in the preceding passage, Meccans more broadly, deniers of the world's end, or all people.)
  18:45/q01/p1 Arrogant wealthy people who preferred their possessions to poor believers. — reasons: Muqatil identifies Meccan unbelievers; al-Tabari, al-Tha'labi, al-Qurtubi and al-Tabrisi connect them with the demand to expel devoted poor believers (18:28). Al-Razi specifies wealthy polytheists boasting of wealth and supporters. Islahi describes people blinded by worldly love; Kuranyolu adds pride in sons, denial of monotheism and the claim that only worldly life exists (68:14–15; 45:24). Al-Asamm connects the command to the preceding passage.
  18:45/q01/p2 The Prophet's people or the people of Mecca generally. — reasons: Ibn Abbas says the Prophet's people; al-Asamm relates an identification with the people of Mecca.
  18:45/q01/p3 People denying that worldly life will end. — reasons: Al-Asamm relates this identification among the alternative addressees.
  18:45/q01/p4 Everyone living in this world. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i says the image applies to worldly life for everyone.
18:45/q02 What condition of worldly life does the parable portray? [مَّثَلَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا كَمَآءٍ فَأَصْبَحَ هَشِيمًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the image chiefly represents rapid loss of beauty, the coexistence of pleasure and harm, or the world's remarkable condition.)
  18:45/q02/p1 Its beauty and flourishing rapidly give way to decay and disappearance. — reasons: Muqatil connects the fading world with the Hereafter's arrival. Yahya, al-Zajjaj, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, al-Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, al-Tabrisi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Razi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Ibn Kathir, al-Biqa'i, Bursevi, al-Alusi, Elmalili and al-Tabataba'i trace fresh rain-fed growth into dry windblown fragments. Al-Zajjaj, al-Kashshaf, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi and al-Alusi say the vanished growth is as though it never existed. Ibn Ashur maps youth, prosperity, novelty and adornment onto rain, growth and dispersed loss; the shared turn from good to bad makes an abstract state visible. Al-Razi compares 22:5, where earth stirs and swells. Kashifi compares youth to a tree dried by death and hopes blown away. Islahi cites 57:20 on amusement, adornment and rivalry in wealth and children.
  18:45/q02/p2 Its blessings are inseparable from adversity, and joy from grief. — reasons: Al-Mawardi gives this as an alternative purpose. Al-Qushayri says worldly pleasures conceal sorrow, sweetness bitterness, gifts trials, and promises fail. Ibn al-Jawzi lists changing fortunes and grief paired with joy as an alternative reading.
  18:45/q02/p3 The world has a remarkable condition worthy of a parable. — reasons: Al-Baydawi allows the world's extraordinary quality as the object of comparison; al-Alusi reports the command as explaining that remarkable condition.
18:45/q03 Why is worldly life compared specifically to water? [ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا كَمَآءٍ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether water's own properties supply the likeness or the whole plant cycle does.)
  18:45/q03/p1 Both change location and condition and cannot be retained. — reasons: The sages reported by al-Tha'labi and al-Qurtubi say neither remains fixed; al-Burhan adds that water slips from a closed hand.
  18:45/q03/p2 Both eventually disappear. — reasons: The sages in al-Tha'labi say water vanishes as worldly life ends; al-Qurtubi transmits the same comparison.
  18:45/q03/p3 Contact with either brings unavoidable effects and trials. — reasons: The sages say one cannot enter water without becoming wet, just as worldly life exposes one to harms and trials.
  18:45/q03/p4 A sufficient amount benefits, while excess harms or destroys. — reasons: The sages in al-Tha'labi and al-Qurtubi contrast useful provision with damaging surplus; al-Burhan repeats this analogy.
  18:45/q03/p5 The comparison is to the entire rain, growth, decline and dispersal scene. — reasons: Al-Burhan calls this the fuller comparison. Bursevi and al-Alusi explicitly reject restricting the likeness to water alone; Ibn Ashur analyzes the shared passage from prosperity to loss as a composite simile.
18:45/q04 How is كَمَآءٍ attached grammatically? [وَٱضْرِبْ كَمَآءٍ · grammar] (turns on: The valency of اضرِب and whether the kāf starts an independent explanation, supplies a second object, or modifies an omitted verbal noun.)
  18:45/q04/p1 It begins an explanatory clause with an omitted subject: worldly life is like water. — reasons: Al-Wahidi and Ibn Atiyya supply the omitted subject. Abu Hayyan and al-Samin transmit Ibn Atiyya's parse. Al-Alusi prefers this fresh explanation and objects that a second object of ḍaraba is awkward. Bursevi explains the comparison in these terms.
  18:45/q04/p2 It is the second object of اضرِب: make the likeness resemble water. — reasons: Abu Hayyan prefers this parse. Al-Samin prefers it because ḍaraba can mean ṣayyara and credits al-Baqa with the proposal. Al-Baydawi also permits water as a second object.
  18:45/q04/p3 It modifies an omitted verbal noun: strike a likeness in a manner like water. — reasons: Al-Hawfi assumes aḍrib takes one object and supplies ḍarban; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin report him. Al-Alusi rejects this analysis.
18:45/q05 What is the water sent from the sky? [كَمَآءٍ أَنزَلْنَٰهُ مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ · referent] (turns on: The sources distinguish the water's identity from the range of meanings of السماء.)
  18:45/q05/p1 Rain. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi and al-Wahidi identify the water as rain. Al-Biqa'i says it descends by God's power at the needed time. Al-Tabataba'i says rain is named because other water sources begin with it. Al-Samin parses أَنزَلْنَٰهُ as an adjective of water.
  18:45/q05/p2 Sky can mean a cloud or the direction above. — reasons: Bursevi gives both possible senses of heaven in the phrase.
18:45/q06 What happens in فَٱخْتَلَطَ بِهِۦ نَبَاتُ ٱلْأَرْضِ, and what does ب do? [فَٱخْتَلَطَ بِهِۦ نَبَاتُ ٱلْأَرْضِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the plants mix with one another because of rain or the water itself mixes into the plants, with a corresponding causal or participant sense of ب.)
  18:45/q06/p1 Plants become abundant and intertwine with each other because of the water; ب marks cause. — reasons: Al-Mawardi, al-Wahidi, al-Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Bursevi, al-Alusi and Ibn Ashur give the dense-growth account. Ibn Atiyya and Ibn Ashur explicitly call the bā' causal; Ibn Ashur denies that it makes ikhtalaṭ transitive. Al-Samin allows cause and cites al-Zamakhshari. Al-Razi says rain strengthens and entangles kinds of plants. Al-Biqa'i includes water mingling with soil and producing varied dense growth.
  18:45/q06/p2 Water mingles with and saturates the plants until they flourish; ب can mark the other participant in mixing. — reasons: Al-Mawardi gives this alternative. Abu Ishaq in al-Wahidi says water penetrates the plants. Al-Asamm, the Kashshaf's alternative, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, al-Samin and al-Alusi describe saturation; al-Kashshaf, al-Razi and al-Samin say either of two mixed things can be grammatically described as mingling with the other. Al-Samin also allows transitivity for ب.
18:45/q07 Why does the wording make vegetation mix with water? [فَٱخْتَلَطَ بِهِۦ نَبَاتُ ٱلْأَرْضِ · rhetoric]
  18:45/q07/p1 The reversal emphasizes abundant water and luxuriant growth. — reasons: Al-Baydawi and al-Alusi observe that ordinary phrasing would make water mix with plants; the verse makes water seem the dominant mass. Al-Tabataba'i says the wording highlights rain's role in forming the vegetation.
18:45/q08 Why mention earth after sky? [ٱلسَّمَآءِ ٱلْأَرْضِ · rhetoric]
  18:45/q08/p1 The paired words create antithesis. — reasons: Ibn Ashur explicitly identifies the sky and earth contrast.
18:45/q09 Does أَصْبَحَ mean became or literally became by morning? [فَأَصْبَحَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether morning is an actual temporal detail or أَصْبَحَ functions as a general verb of becoming.)
  18:45/q09/p1 It means became, with no necessary morning time. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya cites a line by Rabi' b. Dab' for a resultant state; al-Samin cites poetry too. Al-Alusi says the verse describes eventual drying. Ibn Ashur calls this common usage; al-Tabataba'i denies a morning restriction.
  18:45/q09/p2 It may mean that the plant became dry by morning. — reasons: Al-Samin notes that calamities commonly strike in the morning. Al-Alusi reports the literal-morning account as motivated by heavenly calamities arriving at night, but says only eventual drying is described.
18:45/q10 What does the فَ before أَصْبَحَ convey? [فَأَصْبَحَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the conjunction marks swift change directly or telescopes an unspoken period of growth.)
  18:45/q10/p1 It conveys how quickly flourishing growth vanishes. — reasons: Al-Alusi reports this reading of the immediate sequence.
  18:45/q10/p2 It resumes the account after an omitted period of flourishing. — reasons: Al-Alusi reports an explanatory fā' with a time interval understood. Al-Mawardi notes that the water's absence before drying is implicit for concision.
18:45/q11 What does هَشِيمًا mean and how is it formed? [هَشِيمًۭا · meaning]
