## THE CURRENT MAP
51:52/q01 What does “likewise” compare? [كَذَٰلِكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the demonstrative recalls the preceding rejection or anticipates the following statement.)
  51:52/q01/p1 The present rejection of Muhammad repeats earlier peoples’ rejection of their messengers and their sorcery or madness charges. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj and Abū Isḥāq call it the same recurring state; al-Bayḍāwī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī say the following clause explains the comparison. Al-Ālūsī links it to discordant talk at 51:8.
  51:52/q01/p2 The matter is simply so: the following statement about earlier peoples supplies what “likewise” anticipates. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says the omitted-subject construction points forward; al-Naḥḥās gives the same nominal sense.
51:52/q02 How is “likewise” construed grammatically? [كَذَٰلِكَ · grammar] (turns on: The case of the kāf and whether a verb after negative ما can govern it.)
  51:52/q02/p1 It is nominative, the predicate of an omitted subject: “the matter is so.” — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās states this option; al-Qurṭubī links it to a warning to associators; al-Samīn calls it stronger; al-Ālūsī says it points forward.
  51:52/q02/p2 It is accusative, qualifying an omitted verbal noun or expressing a comparison with earlier messengers. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās relates it to Quraysh’s predecessors; al-Qurṭubī applies that reading to disobedient monotheists. Makkī makes it an adjective of an omitted verbal noun; al-Samīn says أتى after negative ما cannot govern it.
  51:52/q02/p3 Attachment to the following verb under negative ما, or to the later speech verb, is syntactically untenable. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Samīn say what follows negative ما cannot govern an earlier phrase; al-Ālūsī also rejects attachment to the speech verb.
51:52/q03 Who are “those before them” and “them”? [ٱلَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِم · referent] (turns on: Whether earlier communities as a whole or their unbelievers alone are meant.)
  51:52/q03/p1 They are earlier communities before Quraysh or the Meccan unbelievers. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī explicitly calls them Quraysh’s predecessors. Al-Wāḥidī’s Wajīz says earlier peoples of Mecca.
  51:52/q03/p2 The phrase may be narrowed to unbelievers among those predecessors. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī records this proposal but finds it unconvincing.
51:52/q04 Does “any messenger” literally include every prophet and every person’s response? [مِّن رَّسُولٍ · interpretation] (turns on: How broadly the indefinite messenger and plural speakers range, especially Adam, law-preserving prophets, and believers.)
  51:52/q04/p1 The statement describes every earlier messenger as facing the accusation from his people. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī explains sorcerer as crafty and madness as obscured understanding. Biqāʿī qualifies that at least some in each generation said it.
  51:52/q04/p2 It concerns messengers, while law-preserving prophets who followed an earlier messenger are outside the claim. — reasons: Al-Rāzī invokes prophets who affirmed prior law and Adam as objections, then distinguishes those following an earlier messenger; al-Ālūsī and Kur’an Yolu report his distinction.
  51:52/q04/p3 The communal charge expresses a widespread or majority response, not every individual’s words. — reasons: Al-Rāzī notes some believed; omission of believers serves consolation. Al-Ālūsī reports this solution and says its extension to objections about particular messengers is weak; Kur’an Yolu also reports it.
  51:52/q04/p4 It concerns messengers sent directly to an existing people with a message. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports that this excludes mere reaffirmers of earlier law; Adam lacked a community beyond Eve.
  51:52/q04/p5 It concerns descendants of Adam, excluding Adam himself. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports the proposal and objections to it.
  51:52/q04/p6 The law-preserving prophet distinction is insufficient. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says reports and verses also call those prophets messengers; it also leaves Adam, sent without being accused.
51:52/q05 How does the arrival clause relate to “they said”? [مَآ أَتَى مِّن رَّسُولٍ إِلَّا قَالُوا۟ · grammar] (turns on: The clause relation and implied speech construction are distinct compatible analyses.)
  51:52/q05/p1 An omitted pronoun before “sorcerer” supplies the predication: “he or this is a sorcerer.” — reasons: Al-Zajjāj supplies huwa before sāḥir; al-Ālūsī reports a predicate construal alongside direct quotation of the labels.
  51:52/q05/p2 “Any messenger” is the subject of “came,” and “except they said” describes the circumstance of his arrival. — reasons: Al-Samīn explicitly makes the latter clause circumstantial for those before them.
51:52/q06 What does “sorcerer” mean in the accusation? [سَاحِرٌ · meaning]
  51:52/q06/p1 It means a sorcerer, also rendered spellbinder. — reasons: Asad says spellbinder translates literal sorcerer. Al-Ṭabrisī adds crafty; Bursevī says opponents dismissed miracles as magic.
51:52/q07 How does “or” relate the two charges? [أَوْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the alternatives belong to different speakers, express doubt, or are quoted as alternative labels.)
  51:52/q07/p1 It distributes accusations among speakers: some said sorcerer, some madman, some both. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān cites Noah’s people using madness alone (26:27); Biqāʿī and al-Ālūsī also give this reading.
  51:52/q07/p2 It can mark doubt between incompatible allegations. — reasons: Biqāʿī observes sorcery presupposes cleverness while madness implies its opposite.
  51:52/q07/p3 It can present two quoted alternatives rather than a single predicate. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports this construal alongside 'he is a sorcerer.'
51:52/q08 What does “madman” mean in the accusation? [مَجْنُونٌ · meaning]
  51:52/q08/p1 It can mean mentally ill, or someone believed to be possessed or under a jinn’s control. — reasons: Kur’an Yolu gives both senses with 2:61, 7:157, 7:184, and 15:6; al-Ṭabrisī describes obscured understanding; Asad translates madman.
51:52/q09 Why did people make these accusations? [سَاحِرٌ أَوْ مَجْنُونٌ · interpretation] (turns on: Which cause or analogy the source emphasizes; these accounts can coexist.)
  51:52/q09/p1 They rejected messengers who challenged their desires and customs, dismissing signs as sorcery and warnings about resurrection or punishment as madness. — reasons: Biqāʿī names desired customs; Bursevī contrasts miracles and resurrection; Iṣlāḥī calls the labels stock excuses for signs and warnings.
  51:52/q09/p2 Their response arose from ignorance or varied defects of understanding and character. — reasons: Al-Nasafī names ignorance. Al-Bāqillānī says Arabic fluency alone cannot explain rejection: opponents varied in ignorance, stubbornness, envy, ingratitude, and weak reasoning, whereas poets Labīd, Kaʿb, and Ḥassān accepted Islam.
  51:52/q09/p3 Their accusations echoed Pharaoh’s attempt to discredit Moses. — reasons: Study Quran cites 51:39 and records further parallels to Muhammad’s accusers at 10:2, 52:29, 15:6–7, 23:70, 34:8, and 68:51.
51:52/q10 How does the following question about mutual counsel link to this accusation? [* · links]
  51:52/q10/p1 Its pronoun refers to the accusation implied by “they said,” and its question marvels at the recurrence across generations. — reasons: Al-Samīn says earlier and later generations appear to have passed the same charge to one another.
51:52/q11 What purpose does this recurring pattern serve here? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the verse is read as consolation, instruction, warning, or a lesson; these purposes can coexist.)
  51:52/q11/p1 It consoles Muhammad: earlier messengers faced the same denunciation, so he should not grieve. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says believers are omitted for consolation. Iṣlāḥī says the Prophet is neither uniquely opposed nor negligent; al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, Elmālili, and Study Quran stress the parallel.
  51:52/q11/p2 It calls the Prophet to persevere, turn from wrongdoers, and remind receptive hearers. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says God will support him and his companions and that he should continue resolutely despite fabricated stories.
  51:52/q11/p3 It warns Quraysh that rejection may bring the fate of Pharaoh’s followers. — reasons: Study Quran cites 51:39.
  51:52/q11/p4 Earlier communities’ stories provide lessons for reflective readers. — reasons: Al-Jishumī gives this reason for recounting the narratives.
51:52/q12 How does the verse connect with its surrounding passage and other verses? [* · links] (turns on: Which neighbouring theme or scriptural parallel is foregrounded.)
  51:52/q12/p1 The same accusations against Muhammad were made by Meccan unbelievers. — reasons: Muqātil is reported by al-Wāḥidī; Study Quran lists sorcery, possession, and other charges.
  51:52/q12/p2 It resumes the opening account of deniers’ discordant speech and their denial of resurrection, before warning of the promised day. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī relates it to 51:8; Ṭabāṭabāʾī describes the closing section’s return to opening themes.
  51:52/q12/p3 It parallels Pharaoh’s denunciation of Moses and other accounts of prophetic rejection. — reasons: Study Quran points to 51:39 and to 15:11, 34:34, 36:30, 43:7, and 43:23; it also cites accusations against Muhammad at 10:2, 52:29, 15:6–7, 23:70, 34:8, and 68:51.
