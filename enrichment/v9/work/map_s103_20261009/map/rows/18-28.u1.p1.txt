18:28/q09 How does 'seeking His face' function grammatically? [يُرِيدُونَ وَجْهَهُۥ · grammar]
  18:28/q09/p1 It is a circumstantial expression describing their uninterrupted sincere invocation and intention. — reasons: Qushayri says devotion persists morning and evening.
18:28/q10 What does 'do not let your eyes pass beyond them' mean? [وَلَا تَعْدُ عَيْنَاكَ عَنْهُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether ada with an simply denotes passing or includes contemptuous recoil.)
  18:28/q10/p1 Keep attention on poor believers; do not turn the gaze past them toward wealthy people. — reasons: Ibn Abbas says not to pass believers for others. Tabari derives it from surpassing; Razi cites poetry; Tabatabai cites Raghib's root sense of overstepping. Abu Hayyan favors ordinary lexical turning away over semantic incorporation.
  18:28/q10/p2 The wording combines passing over the poor with recoiling from or looking down on them. — reasons: Zamakhshari explains ada with an through semantic inclusion of withdrawing or rising above, comparing 4:2; Nasafi says this intensifies the warning. Abu Hayyan objects that Basran grammar licenses inclusion only when necessary.
  18:28/q10/p3 Do not repel them or regard them with a contemptuous look. — reasons: Ata's Ibn Abbas report frames the gaze as rejection; Qurtubi describes recoil from one regarded as contemptible.
  18:28/q10/p4 Inwardly, do not turn attention from these seekers to anything other than God. — reasons: Wasiti and Dhu al-Nun reason that these devotees' eyes never turn from God; Qushayri says the Prophet's gaze leads them toward God until afterlife vision.
18:28/q11 How is 'do not let your eyes pass' read? [وَلَا تَعْدُ عَيْنَاكَ · readings] (turns on: Verb form, case and number of the eyes, and whether causative morphology changes valency.)
  18:28/q11/p1 The common reading makes the two eyes the subject. — reasons: Farra and Akhfash explain the dual subject; Qurtubi rejects the objection that the verb must govern the eyes directly.
  18:28/q11/p2 Hasan reads a causative form with 'your eyes' accusative. — reasons: Ibn Jinni glosses turning the eyes from worldly adornment. Zamakhshari and Lawamih say hamza makes the verb transitive; Abu Hayyan objects that the base is already transitive and should then take two objects.
  18:28/q11/p3 Hasan, Isa and al-Amash are reported with a doubled verb. — reasons: Zamakhshari and Abu al-Fadl invoke doubling to explain transitivity, citing al-Nabigha; Abu Hayyan disputes it. A still ayn is also reported from Hasan.
  18:28/q11/p4 A reported variant has the eye singular. — reasons: Kashshaf records it with a poetic witness for turning away.
18:28/q12 What is the syntax of addressing the eyes? [وَلَا تَعْدُ عَيْنَاكَ عَنْهُمْ · grammar] (turns on: How the eyes syntactically relate to the Prophet and the verb.)
  18:28/q12/p1 The two eyes are subject, while the prohibition addresses their owner through them. — reasons: Qurtubi compares 9:55; Ibn Ashur calls this elegant compression. Samin cites poetry treating the eyes as one unit and desire as metaphorically attributed to them.
  18:28/q12/p2 The eyes can be parsed as a part-for-whole substitute for an addressee subject. — reasons: Samin prefers this parse; he says a following circumstance from the replaced pronoun is rare and cites poetry.
  18:28/q12/p3 The verb may have an omitted object, 'looking at them'. — reasons: Samin supplies al-nazar.
18:28/q13 How is 'desiring worldly adornment' construed? [تُرِيدُ زِينَةَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا · grammar] (turns on: Where the circumstance attaches and how singular agreement is explained.)
  18:28/q13/p1 It is a circumstantial present clause describing the possible state of seeking worldly adornment. — reasons: The meaning experts, Zamakhshari and Qurtubi take it as hal; Qurtubi cites Imru al-Qays. Alusi favors the ordinary parse and says a genitive relation can license attachment. On anomalous readings the hidden verb subject supplies the agent.
  18:28/q13/p2 The circumstantial parse has agreement or attachment difficulties. — reasons: Abu Hayyan asks why a dual eye subject would not take turidan; if the Prophet is subject, attaching a circumstance to the genitive possessor is difficult. Samin allows singular agreement for paired eyes and cites poetry; Alusi rejects the objection.
18:28/q14 What is the worldly adornment toward which the gaze is warned? [زِينَةَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا · referent] (turns on: Whether it is general elite company or a specific reported moment.)
  18:28/q14/p1 It is elite company, status and finery, as contrasted with poor believers' shabby dress. — reasons: Ibn Zayd calls it the worldly elite. Ibn Kathir recalls 20:131; others mention wealthy and noble people's clothing and appearance.
  18:28/q14/p2 One reported explanation places the gaze during the poor worshippers' seeking forgiveness and treats it as desire for status. — reasons: Thalabi reports this rebuke.
18:28/q15 Does the warning attribute worldly desire to the Prophet? [تُرِيدُ زِينَةَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether it describes a real motive, missionary concern, or a hypothetical danger.)
  18:28/q15/p1 It warns about a possible act; the Prophet wanted leaders to believe, not worldly gain. — reasons: Wahidi compares 80:5-6; Tabarsi says the Prophet sometimes accommodated leaders for the sake of their followers. Qurtubi compares a warning against shirk despite prophetic protection at 39:65; Bursevi takes turid as future-oriented. Alusi prefers attaching desire to the eye.
  18:28/q15/p2 The verse reproaches an actual or possible neglect of poor believers in courting elite support. — reasons: Thalabi reports a rebuke for wanting status; Islahi says concern to spread religion risked neglect, while his next note directs the stern rebuke at arrogant elites.
18:28/q16 Who is the heedless person whose wishes must not be obeyed? [مَنْ أَغْفَلْنَا قَلْبَهُۥ · referent] (turns on: Which historical petitioner is named and how far the indefinite wording extends.)
  18:28/q16/p1 The description originally concerns Quraysh's unbelieving chiefs and extends to anyone with that character. — reasons: Tabari and Abu Hayyan see leaders seeking expulsion; Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan favor Quraysh from the Meccan setting. Ibn Ashur stresses indefinite 'whoever' and broad application.
  18:28/q16/p2 It is Uyayna ibn Hisn, sometimes with his father, al-Aqra, or people like him. — reasons: Khabbab names Uyayna and al-Aqra; Ibn Abbas in Wahidi names Uyayna and his father. The point is his demand to remove the poor.
  18:28/q16/p3 It is Umayya ibn Khalaf, who wanted the poor expelled and Meccan nobles favored. — reasons: Ibn Abbas glosses heedlessness as sealing against monotheism and desire as polytheism; Rabi says Umayya was distracted when addressed.
  18:28/q16/p4 The warning applies generally to any heedless person driven by the lower self. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says the indefinite relative broadens the occasion; Alusi extends it to all self-driven matters.
18:28/q17 What is the heedlessness of the heart and what is 'Our remembrance'? [أَغْفَلْنَا قَلْبَهُۥ عَن ذِكْرِنَا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the term is specified by revelation and faith, neglected duty, or inward attention.)
  18:28/q17/p1 It is inattentiveness to God, the Quran, faith or monotheism. — reasons: Muqatil says Quran; Ibn al-Jawzi names monotheism, Quran and Islam; Mufaddal specifies Quran. Ibn Ashur sees a constitution inattentive to divine unity; Ibn Abbas says the heart was sealed against monotheism.
  18:28/q17/p2 Heedlessness is wasted time, idleness or neglect of duties, while obedience gives the heart life. — reasons: Sahl and Qushayri define it through time; Tustari describes a thousand deaths ending in separation and lives ending in meeting the Real, and says sin kills while obedience revives.
  18:28/q17/p3 It is forgetting commanded acts and blessings, following lower desires, or slackness. — reasons: Abu Uthman stresses duties and blessings; Ibn al-Jalla stresses slackness; Mawardi reports anything causing heedlessness.
  18:28/q17/p4 It is long hope and preoccupation with gifts or creatures instead of their Giver. — reasons: Jawzajani and Mawardi cite long hope; Qushayri contrasts gifts and Giver. Razi calls divine remembrance light and creaturely preoccupation darkness, because God is necessary existence and contingent things derive from nonexistence.
  18:28/q17/p5 Heedlessness may be merciful bodily rest or punitive veiling from God. — reasons: Qasim distinguishes life's needed rest from forgetting God's watch; another inward report calls it the heart's punishment and veil.
18:28/q18 What does 'We made his heart heedless' mean? [أَغْفَلْنَا قَلْبَهُۥ · theology] (turns on: Whether Afala is causative or descriptive and how divine action relates to chosen conduct.)
  18:28/q18/p1 God caused or created heedlessness in the heart. — reasons: Sibawayh calls initial hamza augmentative; Razi argues one cannot intend a particular or undirected heedlessness, so God creates it, and causative Afala is commoner. He answers the waw objection because heedlessness need not immediately cause desire. Sunni authors cite divine creation of acts; Ibn Ashur distinguishes innate disposition from chosen desire.
  18:28/q18/p2 God found the person or heart heedless rather than causing that state. — reasons: Ibn Jinni says a causal consequence would use fa rather than waw and cites Afala usage with 2:74, 45:29 and 50:4. Razi transmits Amr ibn Madikarib's poem but prefers causation; Alusi objects that 'finding' suggests new knowledge.
