## THE CURRENT MAP
55:9/q01 What does it mean to establish the weight? [وَأَقِيمُوا۟ ٱلْوَزْنَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether establishing weight specifies leveling a physical scale, sustaining that practice, or extending the measure of justice to conduct and the inward life.)
  55:9/q01/p1 Set the scale straight and weigh fairly in giving and receiving. — reasons: Muqatil specifies the scale's tongue; Abu al-Darda, Ata, Mujahid, al-Kalbi and Muqatil specify a level tongue or beam (Thalabi, Wahidi, Baghawi, Qurtubi, Alusi). Ibn Uyayna distinguishes setting it by hand from justice in the heart (Thalabi, Baghawi, Tabrisi, Qurtubi, Alusi). Wahidi prefers leveling first, then voluntarily giving extra; the report about Ali illustrates that order. Ibn Abbas likewise instructed a man giving extra to set the pointer upright (Ibn Abi Hatim). Qushayri says to apply the standard one wants when receiving food and drink. Ibn Ashur calls iqama a metaphor for doing something in its fullest intended way, comparing prayer at 2:3; Razi also glosses it as straightening a bent object. Tabrisi includes both giving and receiving; Tabatabai says shortchanging can harm buyer or seller.
  55:9/q01/p2 Maintain the practice of just weighing continually. — reasons: Razi compares aqimu with upholding prayer at 2:43 and says the verb may take a preposition or become transitive by hamza. Qurtubi compares it with keeping prayer on time or attending a market: weighing fairly must not be abandoned.
  55:9/q01/p3 Make all conduct, words and judgments conform to justice. — reasons: Bursevi says weight is knowing a thing's measure and extends to intended acts and words. Alusi cites al-Raghib for that breadth. Biqai includes measure, weight, judgment and quantity; Elmalili adds judging oneself and others by one standard and weighing words and deeds. Asad calls for weighing deeds fairly.
  55:9/q01/p4 Establish spiritual unity through truthful outward and inward resolve. — reasons: Ibn Ata, reported by Sulami, calls for truthful outward conduct, a clear interior, true secret and firm resolve: be wholly God's and He will be truly yours.
55:9/q02 What does 'the weight' encompass? [ٱلْوَزْنَ · referent] (turns on: Whether weight is limited to physical quantities or also names a standard for wider conduct.)
  55:9/q02/p1 Actual weighing and other quantitative measures. — reasons: Kashshaf includes every instrument for weighing or measuring quantities, used in judgments and transactions. Ibn Ashur says concrete weight balances by heaviness but the verse also includes quantities and volumes. Bursevi describes weight as knowing a thing's measure.
  55:9/q02/p2 A standard extending to all human actions, speech and judgment. — reasons: Bursevi and al-Raghib as cited by Alusi extend it to acts and words; Biqai lists judgment as well as measure and quantity. Elmalili applies it to speech and deeds. Ibn Ashur says the surrounding wording supports both concrete and abstract senses and calls their convergence Qur'anic inimitability.
55:9/q03 What does 'qist' mean and how is its form explained? [بِٱلْقِسْطِ · meaning] (turns on: The gloss of justice is shared; the notes differ over its linguistic origin and the significance of related Arabic forms.)
  55:9/q03/p1 Qist means justice or fairness. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, Qurtubi, Ibn Ashur and Tabatabai state the gloss. Mujahid, reported by Mawardi, glosses equitable weighing as just weighing; Kuran Yolu says qist connects universal balance with lived fairness.
  55:9/q03/p2 The term for justice is associated with Roman usage or qistas. — reasons: Mujahid, reported by Qurtubi, calls qist a Roman borrowing. Ibn Ashur describes it as a shortened Arabic form of Roman-derived qistas, also found in fuller form at 21:47; these reports vary in how they state the borrowing.
  55:9/q03/p3 The Arabic forms distinguish just aqsaṭa from unjust qaṣaṭa. — reasons: Razi says qist is a noun rather than a verbal noun; the hamza in aqsaṭa yields doing justice, while qaṣaṭa means deviating from fairness. He derives al-aqsaṭ in 2:282 semantically from muqsiṭ rather than unjust qāsiṭ, despite the normal root-based comparative.
55:9/q04 How does the bāʾ in 'with qist' work? [بِٱلْقِسْطِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the preposition makes justice an accompanying condition or the criterion of measurement.)
  55:9/q04/p1 It marks accompaniment: justice constantly accompanies conduct. — reasons: Ibn Ashur gives this parsing and compares 6:152 and 5:8.
  55:9/q04/p2 It marks causation: justice is the criterion by which measurement is made. — reasons: Ibn Ashur gives this alternative and compares 6:152 and 5:8. Tabrisi separately notes that a preposition in the related expression at 9:5 can mean 'in' or 'at' every ambush; he does not explicitly apply it to this bāʾ.
55:9/q05 What does 'do not diminish the balance' prohibit? [وَلَا تُخْسِرُوا۟ ٱلْمِيزَانَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the balance is a trading scale, a standard of justice, or the balance of deeds in the afterlife; some sources explicitly combine these senses.)
  55:9/q05/p1 Do not give deficient measure or weight, cheat, or wrong the other party. — reasons: Muqatil, Majaz, Ibn Qutayba, Tabari, Ibn Zayd, Thalabi, Mawardi, Wahidi, Baghawi, Ibn Jawzi, Qurtubi and others explain reduction as short measure; Majaz adds wronging people, and Thalabi includes cheating in buying. Wahidi invokes 83:3 and 11:84; Qurtubi also cites 11:84, Ibn Jawzi and Abu Hayyan 83:3. Wahidi's reported meaning scholars restrict the warning to intentional shortfall, excusing unavoidable imprecision. Bursevi distinguishes this shortchanging from commercial loss in lexicons; Sa'di al-Mufti says weighed goods, not the instrument, are reduced. Ibn Ashur relates the warning to pagan merchants and 83:1-3. Nasafi and Kashshaf also oppose unjust excess; Abu Hayyan and Sa'di distinguish earlier excess from later reduction. The common form can mean spoil the measure (Ibn Atiyya); Tabrisi says both verb forms can be used.
  55:9/q05/p2 Do not impair justice in actions, judgment, or the virtuous mean. — reasons: Mawardi says the wrong varies if mizan means justice, judgment or measure. Biqai includes every instrument of justice and all conduct. Qashani calls reduction falling short of the virtuous mean and quotes a philosopher calling justice God's scale. Ibn Ashur allows neglect of justice where mizan has that sense. Elmalili extends fair standards to words, deeds and judgments; Asad says deeds must not fall short.
  55:9/q05/p3 Do not leave one's balance of deeds deficient on Judgment Day. — reasons: Mawardi offers the deeds scale; Qurtubi calls it an alternative. Kashifi's Persian verses warn that small, large and hidden fraud will be exposed at Judgment (Bursevi). Al-Raghib joins worldly fairness with avoiding afterlife loss and distinguishes Qur'anic loss from trade loss (Bursevi); Alusi cites 7:9 and says both are intended and interrelated. Kuran Yolu and Elmalili also apply it to one's afterlife scales.
55:9/q06 What exactly is said to become deficient in the final clause? [ٱلْمِيزَانَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether mizan directly names the scale or standard, or stands for the goods being weighed.)
  55:9/q06/p1 The measured goods or weight become deficient, with 'balance' naming them by ellipsis or figurative usage. — reasons: Razi says the measured object is reduced. Sa'di al-Mufti explicitly denies damage to the scale (Bursevi). Alusi reports either an omitted noun for what is weighed or figurative use of balance for the weighed object; these are alternative constructions of the same referent.
  55:9/q06/p2 The balance or standard itself is diminished or disabled. — reasons: Bursevi says not to diminish the balance or what it measures because evenness is its purpose. Mawardi varies the wrong according as mizan is justice, judgment or measure. Biqai includes instruments of justice. Alusi says its proper function is to make measurements equal.
55:9/q07 How is 'tukhsirū' read and analyzed? [تُخْسِرُوا۟ · readings] (turns on: The authority for alternate vocalizations, whether khasira and akhsara share this transitive meaning, and whether an open form requires an omitted fī.)
  55:9/q07/p1 The common transmitted reading has ḍamma on tāʾ and kasra on sīn, from akhsara. — reasons: Zajjaj calls this the transmitted reading and says akhsartu and khasirtu al-mizan both occur in Arabic. Ibn Atiyya says most readers use akhsara, to cause shortfall or spoil. Qurtubi and Samin give the two vowels; Samin adduces 83:3. Wahidi's language scholars recognize both forms; Tabrisi contrasts causative 'I made it lose' with the intransitive form.
  55:9/q07/p2 An open tāʾ, usually with kasra on sīn, reads the verb from khasira with the same sense. — reasons: Bilal ibn Abi Burda is named by Nahhas, Wahidi and Ibn Atiyya; Abu Hayyan and Samin also name Zayd ibn Ali, and Qurtubi names Aban from Uthman. Nahhas and Qurtubi call it a dialect form. Ibn Atiyya says khasira and akhsara both reduce or spoil; Samin compares jabara and ajbara. Zajjaj concedes both forms in Arabic but rejects reciting an alternative without a sound report from a recognized authority, saying the unnamed early reader does not suffice.
  55:9/q07/p3 Other open-tāʾ readings vary the sīn vowel, including fatḥa and ḍamma. — reasons: Ibn Jinni reports Bilal's open tāʾ and sīn via Ibn Atiyya. Kashshaf lists fatḥa on tāʾ and ḍamma, kasra or fatḥa on sīn; Abu Hayyan, Alusi and Qurtubi also note variants. Samin says khasira has both vowel variants, and Zamakhshari explains some forms through deletion of fī.
