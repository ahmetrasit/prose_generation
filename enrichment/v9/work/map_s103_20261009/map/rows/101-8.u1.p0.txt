## THE CURRENT MAP
101:8/q01 What are the mawāzīn, and what is weighed? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether mawāzīn names an actual instrument, weighty deeds, or a figure for judgment, and whether deeds themselves can be weighed.)
  101:8/q01/p1 A real balance weighs deeds or their written records at the Resurrection. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya attributes a balance with a beam to most scholars, jurists, and hadith scholars, saying its familiar form makes divine judgment intelligible and trusted. Ibn Abbas in Razi describes a tongue and two pans: beautiful obedient deeds outweigh and lead to Paradise, whereas ugly unbelievers' deeds are light and lead to Fire; al-Hasan affirms two pans without further description. Qurtubi specifies written records of good and bad deeds on a pan and tongue and refers to earlier discussions. Study Quran distinguishes Qur'anic balance as revelation and just order (6:152, 7:85, 11:84–85, 42:17, 55:7–9, 57:25) from scales for deeds at Resurrection (21:47). Islahi calls it a just balance of deeds.
  101:8/q01/p2 Mawāzīn are deeds that possess weight and worth before God. — reasons: Al-Farra in Razi treats mawāzīn as a plural of mawzūn and compares ordinary expressions about one thing being the measure or weight of another. Kashshaf gives this as an alternative to weighing scales. Tabatabai assigns weight and standing to faith and obedience, and none to disbelief and sins; Qutb speaks of values with no weight in God's assessment.
  101:8/q01/p3 The scale is a metaphor for divine justice. — reasons: Mujahid in Ibn Atiyya denies a literal balance because the scale is the fairest familiar measure. The representative form of weighing conveys just judgment in Kuranyolu's account.
  101:8/q01/p4 The balances are proofs and arguments. — reasons: Abd al-Aziz ibn Yahya in Qurtubi cites a poet speaking of a balance for every disputant.
  101:8/q01/p5 What is weighed may be records or visible representations of deeds, since vanished deeds cannot themselves be weighed. — reasons: The theologians reported by Razi object that deeds, especially after ceasing to exist, cannot be weighed. Their alternatives are weighing records, light and darkness denoting good and evil, or records embodied in beautiful and hideous forms, publicly disclosing each person's standing.
  101:8/q01/p6 The actual manner of weighing is beyond specification from revelation. — reasons: Kuranyolu says afterlife events are unseen and one cannot speculate beyond what revelation states about how deeds are weighed or measured.
101:8/q02 Why is mawāzīn plural and assigned to a person? [مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the grammatical plural reflects multiple objects, multiple instruments, or individual experiences despite one instrument.)
  101:8/q02/p1 The plural covers the many different things belonging to a person that are weighed. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya explicitly explains the plural and the possessive in this way.
  101:8/q02/p2 There are multiple balances. — reasons: Qurtubi reports this interpretation and cites a poet's line that every event has its balance.
  101:8/q02/p3 A single balance, held by Gabriel, is expressed in the plural. — reasons: Qurtubi reports the one-balance interpretation; Study Quran says most hold that one scale weighs all deeds despite the plural.
  101:8/q02/p4 The plural evokes each person's experience of weighing. — reasons: Study Quran reports this suggestion while noting the majority view of a single scale.
101:8/q03 What makes a person's scales light? [خَفَّتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether lightness describes the balance of bad against good, scarcity or loss of worthy good, following falsehood, or intrinsic lack of divine weight.)
  101:8/q03/p1 Bad deeds outweigh good deeds. — reasons: Wahidi, Baghawi, Razi, Ibn Kathir, Biqai, Bursevi, Alusi, Elmalili, Islahi, and Kuranyolu give the comparative account; Razi and Kuranyolu add that good deeds are few. Biqai calls the scales light or unsteady. Bursevi says both groups sin, but the blessed have fewer, outweighed sins; he cites the Prophet's instruction to Ali to follow a bad deed with a good one and God's forgiving name. Islahi says this outcome leads to Hell under God's just balance.
  101:8/q03/p2 The person followed light falsehood instead of weighty truth. — reasons: Muqatil calls truth heavy and wholesome and falsehood light and vile; Abu Bakr in Wahidi, Kashshaf, and Razi relates their weight to following them in worldly life and says a scale holding only truth should be heavy and one holding falsehood light. Nasafi and Biqai attribute lightness to following falsehood; Biqai adds it felt light in worldly life. Islahi says only truth, meaning good deeds, has weight, and connects loss to denial of God's revelations (7:8–9).
  101:8/q03/p3 There is no good deed of consequential weight. — reasons: Baydawi, Biqai, Bursevi, Alusi, and Elmalili give this as an alternative to bad deeds outweighing good; Biqai links the lack to following falsehood. Kuranyolu allows few or no good acts. Qutb says values without weight have no standing in God's judgment.
  101:8/q03/p4 Good deeds and obedience are few or scarce. — reasons: Tabari says the good deeds weigh little; Tabrisi says few good deeds and little obedience. Ibn Atiyya says faith and deeds make a balance heavy and absence or scarcity makes it light. Razi and Kuranyolu combine few good deeds with wrongs outweighing them.
  101:8/q03/p5 Shirk or corrupt belief nullifies the good deeds, leaving nothing weighty. — reasons: Muqatil says shirk's good deeds turn to wind-scattered ash; shirk is the foulest yet lightest thing in the balance. Islahi says any good deeds are voided by ill intent and heretical belief, while evil deeds carry no weight.
  101:8/q03/p6 Disbelief and sins have no weight before God, unlike faith and obedience. — reasons: Tabatabai says deeds are weighed by their standing with God; faith and obedience have weight and bring felicity, while disbelief and sins lack it and bring misery. Qutb describes the light scales as values without weight in divine assessment.
101:8/q04 Whose scales can be ruinously light? [مَنْ خَفَّتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · referent] (turns on: Whether the verse identifies unbelief as the condition of ruin or states a general bad-over-good criterion.)
  101:8/q04/p1 The disbelievers, especially those whose shirk or denial voids their good deeds. — reasons: Qushayri identifies those light in obedience as disbelievers; Muqatil names shirk and its wind-scattered good deeds; Islahi links the loss to denying revelations and to ill intent and heretical belief. Ibn Atiyya says a believer's balance is never disastrously light, a point repeated in Ibn Atiyya Full.
  101:8/q04/p2 Anyone whose bad deeds exceed their good deeds is in the light-scale group. — reasons: The general descriptions by Wahidi, Baghawi, Razi, Ibn Kathir, Bursevi, Alusi, and others state the bad-over-good criterion without restricting the wording to a named people. Bursevi says both groups commit sins, though in the blessed group sins are fewer and outweighed.
101:8/q05 How does the singular man refer to those being weighed? [مَنْ · grammar]
  101:8/q05/p1 The singular relative expression denotes a plural group. — reasons: Islahi explicitly notes the singular man in the two scale descriptions has plural reference.
101:8/q06 What outcome follows light scales? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the following image emphasizes the abiding destination or the manner of falling into it.)
  101:8/q06/p1 The person's abode or destination is Hawiya, the Fire or Hell. — reasons: Tabari says the person of scant good deeds has Hawiya as abode. Thalabi, Qushayri, Qatada in Tabari and Durr, and Kuranyolu identify the destination as Hell or Fire; Durr's versions call it abode, mother, destination, and master. Biqai describes a very low fiery abyss; Islahi says the next verse names it blazing fire and calls the punishment eternal.
  101:8/q06/p2 They plunge headfirst into Hell. — reasons: Tabari reports Abu Salih and Qatada saying they fall headfirst into Fire, and Tabari himself includes that descent; Thalabi attributes the head interpretation to Qatada and Abu Salih. Ikrima in Mawardi identifies the mother as the head and cites a poem addressed to Amr.
101:8/q07 Why is the following Hawiya called the person's mother? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether mother names a sheltering destination, the head of the falling person, or an idiom of calamity.)
  101:8/q07/p1 Hell is the person's refuge and return, like a child's mother or one's home. — reasons: Ibn Zayd in Tabari and Mawardi says Hell is refuge and return, citing 3:151 and Umayya ibn Abi al-Salt's poem; Ibn Abbas says it is the only home and shelters the person as a woman shelters her child. Biqai likens the shelter to a mother or the earth to which one returns; Qatada in Durr calls it abode, mother, destination, and master. Tabari records a report from al-Ashath about believers' spirits saying the dead were taken to their mother, Hawiya.
  101:8/q07/p2 Mother means the person's head, upon which the person falls. — reasons: Thalabi reports this interpretation from some, attributing its adoption to Qatada and Abu Salih. Ikrima in Mawardi describes the fall on the head and supports the image with a poem addressed to Amr.
  101:8/q07/p3 It is a familiar Arabic exclamation for someone in severe trouble. — reasons: Qatada in Tabari and Thalabi explains hāwat ummuh or hawat ummuh as idiomatic speech about a person in dire predicament; Tabari also reports the wording hāwat ummuh directly.
101:8/q08 Why is the Fire called Hawiya? [* · meaning]
  101:8/q08/p1 It is an abyss into which the person falls through deep reaches. — reasons: Mawardi derives the name from falling into it despite its deep abyss. Biqai depicts the sinner continually falling through its depths and describes it as very low and fiery.
