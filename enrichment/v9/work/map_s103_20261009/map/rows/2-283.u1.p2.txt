2:283/q30 Who is forbidden to conceal testimony? [وَلَا تَكْتُمُوا۟ ٱلشَّهَٰدَةَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the prohibition directly addresses transaction witnesses alone or also the debtor's acknowledgment.)
  2:283/q30/p1 Witnesses who know the transaction must disclose their testimony. — reasons: Tabari and early authorities address summoned witnesses; Jishumi includes those who attended the contract or pledge without formal appointment. Rabi insists even adverse evidence about self or kin is covered.
  2:283/q30/p2 Debtors too must acknowledge their own obligation rather than defeat testimony. — reasons: Baydawi allows debtors testifying against themselves, Elmalılı and Asad include a debtor denying an unsecured loan; Alusi rejects making debtors the primary addressee.
2:283/q31 When must testimony be offered? [وَلَا تَكْتُمُوا۟ ٱلشَّهَٰدَةَ · law] (turns on: Whether only a formal request triggers the duty, or known danger to a right calls for earlier disclosure.)
  2:283/q31/p1 When summoned or when a right risks loss, a witness must testify accurately; the duty may arise before being asked. — reasons: Tabari, Ibn Abbas and Ibn Atiyya speak of summons and risk; Qurtubi treats testimony as collective duty becoming individual for needed witnesses. Al-Qaffal and Ibn Ashur cite 'best witness before asked'; Ibn Ashur adds danger of death, absence or forgetting; KuranYolu reconciles reports criticizing unsolicited testimony as about false testimony or God's rights.
  2:283/q31/p2 One should avoid disclosing testimony in a venue where it will assist falsehood. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya qualifies Ibn Abbas's advice under corrupt conditions when ineffective disclosure may conceal the truth.
2:283/q32 What does concealing testimony mean? [وَلَا تَكْتُمُوا۟ ٱلشَّهَٰدَةَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether concealment is silence when evidence is needed or a false denial of knowledge.)
  2:283/q32/p1 Withholding known evidence, especially failing to speak when needed. — reasons: Jishumi contrasts kitmān with disclosure; Razi says refusal can defeat property rights. Ibn Ashur relates the recurring prohibition to 2:282.
  2:283/q32/p2 Denying that one knows of the event. — reasons: Razi cites the denial about Abraham's family in 2:140 as a parallel.
2:283/q33 How is the prohibition against concealment read? [وَلَا تَكْتُمُوا۟ · readings] (turns on: The opening consonant of the prohibitive verb and whether address remains direct.)
  2:283/q33/p1 Third-person plural, 'let them not conceal.' — reasons: Abu Abd al-Rahman and al-Sulami transmit yāʾ; Samin relates the form to the preceding scribe and witness, while noting a singular expression can mean a group.
  2:283/q33/p2 Second-person plural, directly addressing the hearers. — reasons: Jishumi calls this the apparent reading.
2:283/q34 What is the force of 'his heart is sinful'? [فَإِنَّهُۥٓ ءَاثِمٌۭ قَلْبُهُۥ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the phrase foregrounds a corrupt state of the heart or the sin incurred by this act.)
  2:283/q34/p1 The concealer's heart becomes guilty, wicked or corrupt; concealment is a grave sin. — reasons: Tabari, Ibn Abbas, Suddi and others call the heart wicked. Ibn Abbas ranks concealment with shirk and false testimony; Wahidi mentions a sealed, hypocritical heart. A Prophetic report equates it to false testimony. Elmalılı says it approaches unbelief.
  2:283/q34/p2 It states the heart has incurred the sin of suppressing testimony. — reasons: Mawardi's alternative and Ibn Atiyya stress the guilt attaching to the act.
2:283/q35 Why attribute sin to the heart? [ءَاثِمٌۭ قَلْبُهُۥ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether attribution is rhetorical causal language or grounds for a stronger claim that the heart is the acting subject.)
  2:283/q35/p1 Concealment begins in inward intention and knowledge, and the heart governs outward acts; naming it intensifies blame. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan cite the Prophetic sound-body morsel; Tabrisi cites 58:22; Qurtubi calls it part-for-whole; Ibn Ashur names perception, emotion and intention. Biqai and Bursevi link a corrupted heart to the body. Alusi rejects extra black-mark and body-effect theories.
  2:283/q35/p2 The heart itself is the acting and accountable subject. — reasons: Many theologians infer this from the verse and 26:193.
  2:283/q35/p3 The act can be attributed to an organ because it supplies the act's chief cause. — reasons: Opponents of the theological inference compare the eye seeing and ear hearing; Razi says bodily acts follow heart motives.
2:283/q36 How is 'āthim qalbuhu' parsed? [ءَاثِمٌۭ قَلْبُهُۥ · grammar] (turns on: The governing role of the active participle and whether definiteness permits a specification analysis.)
  2:283/q36/p1 Āthim is the predicate and qalbuhu its nominative agent or subject. — reasons: Nahhas lists this; Kashshaf, Abu Hayyan and Samin report it, and Abu Hayyan and Samin prefer it. It says 'his heart sins.'
  2:283/q36/p2 Āthim is an advanced predicate and qalbuhu a delayed subject. — reasons: Zamakhshari and Abu al-Baqa offer this; Samin notes a Kufan objection to a pronoun pointing to the later noun.
  2:283/q36/p3 Qalbuhu is partitive apposition to the pronoun or to the hidden subject. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya proposes apposition; Basrans object to an unsupported active-participle government in one version, while Farra and Akhfash allow it.
  2:283/q36/p4 An intensive explanation makes qalb an accusative specification. — reasons: Abu Hatim proposes an analogy, but Nahhas rejects it because qalbuhu is definite.
2:283/q37 What variant forms of 'his heart is sinful' are read? [ءَاثِمٌۭ قَلْبُهُۥ · readings] (turns on: Whether the heart is nominative, accusative, or object of a finite verb.)
  2:283/q37/p1 Qalbahu in the accusative. — reasons: Ibn Abi Abla's variant is parsed as apposition, object-like accusative or specification. Makki weakens tamyiz because the noun is definite; Basrans object, Kufans adduce 2:130 and 28:85; Sibawayh allows object-like usage in poetry only.
  2:283/q37/p2 Athama or āthama qalbahu, 'he made his heart sinful.' — reasons: Ibrahim ibn Abi Aylah and Ibn Abi Abla transmit a verbal reading, with the heart as object or as whole-person metonymy.
2:283/q38 To whom does the pronoun in 'indeed he' refer? [فَإِنَّهُۥٓ · referent] (turns on: Whether the pronoun resumes 'whoever' or is impersonal with an explanatory clause.)
  2:283/q38/p1 It refers to the person who conceals testimony. — reasons: Samin considers this apparent, and Alusi says most natural.
  2:283/q38/p2 It is a pronoun of the matter explained by what follows. — reasons: Samin and Alusi report the option but note a grammatical objection: the following participial phrase is not a full explanatory sentence under dominant Basran and Kufan rules.
2:283/q39 What does God's knowledge in the close imply? [وَٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ عَلِيمٌۭ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the closing universal statement is heard only as warning or also as promise.)
  2:283/q39/p1 God knows hidden and public deeds, including testimony, and will judge and repay them; the close threatens concealment. — reasons: Tabari, Ibn Atiyya, Razi, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, Biqai, Ibn Ashur and others draw the warning. Ibn Atiyya says the general wording can also promise reward. Razi connects 2:284; Ibn Ashur argues complete knowledge assures requital.
  2:283/q39/p2 The conclusion also promises reward for truthful action. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says its generality permits promise as well as threat; Alusi states good and evil repayment.
2:283/q40 How is 'what you do' read? [بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ · readings] (turns on: The person prefix of the closing verb.)
  2:283/q40/p1 Third-person plural, 'what they do.' — reasons: Al-Sulami keeps the third-person form used in his other variants.
  2:283/q40/p2 Second-person plural, direct address. — reasons: The verse's apparent wording addresses the hearers.
2:283/q41 How does this verse continue the debt documentation rules? [* · links]
  2:283/q41/p1 It offers pledges when writing or witnessing cannot be done, then allows mutual trust and reminds witnesses to disclose evidence. — reasons: Jishumi and Ibn Ashur connect 2:282's scribe and witnesses to this substitute. Ibn Ashur notes the scribe may also witness and calls the final ban a renewed charge. Tabarisi sees practical divine guidance in both verses; Biqai sees a closure of dealings among people.
2:283/q42 What wordplay and ellipsis mark the debt passage? [* · rhetoric]
  2:283/q42/p1 The passage displays coordinated wordplay, ellipsis and antithesis. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and Samin identify debt/indebtedness, writer/writing, entrustment/trust, concealment/concealer, and the earlier err/remember and small/large pairs; Abu Hayyan inventories implied subjects and objects.

  2:283/q43/p1 Yes; permission to rely on trust supersedes previous requirements. — reasons: Abu Sa'id, Amir, Sha'bi, Hasan, Hakam, Ibn Jurayj and others are reported. Ibn Kathir calls Abu Sa'id's chain good; Abu Ubayd says Hijaz and Iraq scholars agree sale witnesses optional.
