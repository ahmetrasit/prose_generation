6:81/q13 How is 'He sends down' read? [يُنَزِّلْ · readings]
  6:81/q13/p1 Both Form II yunazzil and Form IV yunzil are canonical readings. — reasons: Qiraat-Er says Form II suggests gradual sending, whereas Form IV suggests a single act.
6:81/q14 How is sulṭān vocalized? [سُلْطَٰنًۭا · readings] (turns on: Whether the lām retains sukūn or assimilates to the preceding ḍamma.)
  6:81/q14/p1 Another reading gives the lām ḍamma. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and al-Samin say it may reflect an independent dialect or vowel following, which would not establish an independent pattern. Al-Alusi calls it dialectal harmonization with the preceding ḍamma.
  6:81/q14/p2 Most read sulṭānan with a still lām. — reasons: Al-Samin identifies the majority reading.
6:81/q15 Who are the two parties? [ٱلْفَرِيقَيْنِ · referent] (turns on: Whether the groups are named historically, by their enduring beliefs, or by their misplaced sense of safety.)
  6:81/q15/p1 They are monotheists and polytheists, broadly the party of God and the party of the idols. — reasons: Most commentators identify the parties by their creeds. Al-Biqaʿi explicitly extends them beyond Abraham and his people. Ibn ʿAshur allows future members of Abraham's community, fitting 6:82; al-Wahidi, al-Nasafi, al-Baydawi and others state the general contrast.
  6:81/q15/p2 They are Abraham, perhaps with present or future followers, and his own people. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi explicitly names Abraham and his people. Al-Baghawi says Abraham and adherents of his religion. Ibn ʿAshur says 'party' can describe Abraham alone, present followers, or future members; he cites 29:26 and 6:82.
  6:81/q15/p3 They are those confident where safety belongs and those confident where fear belongs. — reasons: Al-Bursevi reports Abu al-Suʿud's rhetorical description of the same opposing groups.
6:81/q16 Which party deserves security, and on what grounds? [فَأَىُّ ٱلْفَرِيقَيْنِ أَحَقُّ بِٱلْأَمْنِ · interpretation]
  6:81/q16/p1 Those who worship God alone deserve security; idolaters who worship powerless beings without proof do not. — reasons: Ibn Jurayj says Abraham's opponents themselves answer that believers in one Lord deserve security. Ibn Zayd says God answers in 6:82: believers without mixed wrongdoing. Al-Thaʿlabi cites security and guidance for unmixed belief. Ibn Ishaq, Ibn al-Jawzi and Ibn Kathir ground the distinction in God's sole power over harm and benefit; al-Qurtubi and others define the risk as divine punishment. Al-Tabrisi says true knowledge follows proof rather than partisan stubbornness. Islahi says their unsupported association makes them culpable while Abraham did nothing wrong.
6:81/q17 What kind of security is al-amn? [ٱلْأَمْنِ · meaning] (turns on: Whether al-amn is specified as exemption from punishment, a broad absence of fear, or inner calm.)
  6:81/q17/p1 It is safety from God's punishment. — reasons: Ibn Ishaq explicitly includes this world and the next; Ibn Kathir specifies punishment at resurrection. Al-Zajjaj, al-Wahidi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Qurtubi and al-Nasafi give safety from punishment without restricting the time.
  6:81/q17/p2 It is security in general, the opposite of fear. — reasons: Ibn ʿAshur calls al-amn generic security. Al-Alusi's argument contrasts an actually safe position with a dangerous one.
  6:81/q17/p3 It is inward calmness of the soul. — reasons: Al-Jishumi defines security this way, connecting īmān and security from punishment through deeds.
6:81/q18 Why ask 'which of the two parties' rather than 'which of us'? [فَأَىُّ ٱلْفَرِيقَيْنِ · rhetoric]
  6:81/q18/p1 The indirect phrasing avoids Abraham's self-praise while inviting his opponents to make the judgment. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari, al-Razi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan and al-Samin stress modesty; al-Samin contrasts a poem that directly asks which of two men is champion. Al-Biqaʿi says generalizing the parties compels an answer. Al-Alusi says it avoids direct blame, extends security to every monotheist, and uses 'more entitled' gently to draw them from obstinacy. Al-Tabatabai says it avoids provoking pride and marks the foundational division.
6:81/q19 How is 'which party is more entitled to security' constructed and used? [فَأَىُّ ٱلْفَرِيقَيْنِ أَحَقُّ بِٱلْأَمْنِ · grammar]
  6:81/q19/p1 Ayyu al-fariqayn is the subject and ahaqqu bi-l-amn the predicate; the question presses them to state the evident answer. — reasons: Al-Nahhas gives the subject-predicate parsing. Abu Hayyan says a known answer is posed as an open question. Al-Alusi notes the comparative 'more entitled' grants apparent room for an answer to coax them. Al-Itqan contrasts the placement of interrogative ayyu after fa with hamza's initial placement before conjunctions.
6:81/q20 What does 'if you know' ask of them? [إِن كُنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether taʿlamūn has an implicit object or simply means 'are knowledgeable'; the conditional answer is unspoken.)
  6:81/q20/p1 An omitted answer invites them to tell which party is safer if they know or discern. — reasons: Abu Hayyan supplies 'identify which party is safer.' Al-Samin supplies 'then tell me'; Ibn ʿAshur treats it as a new sentence urging a reply. Al-Alusi says it challenges them to answer. Al-Tabrisi asks for reasoning without partisan stubbornness. Kuranyolu says only knowledge enables the correct answer and escape from false beliefs.
  6:81/q20/p2 The object of 'know' is omitted: if you know which group merits security. — reasons: Al-Samin gives the omitted-object analysis; al-Alusi allows 'what they know' as the implied object.
  6:81/q20/p3 The verb is intransitive: if you are knowledgeable. — reasons: Al-Samin gives the objectless analysis, and Abu Hayyan describes knowledgeable and discerning addressees.
6:81/q21 What happened in Abraham's dispute with his people? [* · reports]
  6:81/q21/p1 His people urged him to worship another god and threatened him with their idols; he answered with the fear and security challenge. — reasons: Ibn Jurayj's report in al-Durr supplies the invitation and threat. Al-Tabari and Ibn Abi Hatim report his answer that idols cannot harm or benefit; Mujahid calls the security question his argument. Al-Rabi says God gave him victory in this disputation, linking it to 6:83.
6:81/q22 How does the argument connect with verses 6:82–83? [* · links]
  6:81/q22/p1 Verse 6:82 supplies the answer about unmixed belief and security; verse 6:83 identifies Abraham's God-given argument and victory. — reasons: Ibn Zayd in al-Tabari, Ibn Abi Hatim and al-Durr cites 6:82. Al-Thaʿlabi gives its believers' security and guidance. Ibn ʿAshur uses it to include Abraham's community. Al-Rabi links the dispute's proof and victory to 6:83; Mujahid calls the question Abraham's argument.
6:81/q23 How does this question continue Abraham's preceding reply? [* · links]
  6:81/q23/p1 It continues his rejection of their threat, moving from his lack of fear to their greater reason for fear. — reasons: Al-Tabari calls it his reply; Ibn ʿAtiyya says his speech and decisive proof continue through taʿlamūn. Al-Alusi relates the new question to his preceding denial, and Ibn ʿAshur says their admission of God intensifies the reversal. Al-Biqaʿi compares Abraham's invocation of Lord for protection with Shuʿayb's divine name for a different issue in 7:89; he also links their conditional speech with 18:24.
6:81/q24 What does the argument imply about God's power and other claimed powers? [* · theology]
  6:81/q24/p1 God alone creates, owns, and controls harm and benefit; supposed partners have no independent power. — reasons: Ibn Ishaq and others oppose idols' impotence to God's power. Al-Tabatabai says divine ownership would require any real partnership to be disclosed by revelation or external signs. He also reports an interpretation of God's encompassing knowledge and will that excludes creaturely influence over His acts, even by intercession, citing 2:255.
6:81/q25 What wider purpose does the Abraham account serve in the sūra? [* · links]
  6:81/q25/p1 It presents Abraham and later prophets as heralds of Muhammad's message, calling him to follow their guidance and preach without forcing belief. — reasons: Islahi says Abraham is singled out because Israelite and Ishmaelite descendants both claimed him and ascribed inherited practices to him. He calls Judaism, Christianity, and Ishmaelite polytheism later fabrications unrelated to Abraham, and Muhammad's mission a revival and completion of Abraham's religion.
6:81/q26 What figurative transfers help describe security and related Qur'anic wording? [ٱلْأَمْنِ · rhetoric] (turns on: Which relation licenses the figurative substitution: place and state, instrument and effect, or contextual opposition.)
  6:81/q26/p1 A state can be named through its place, and a place can be described through its people or condition. — reasons: Al-Zarkashi says security or goodness predicated of a town refers to its people or air (14:35, 7:96); 'in God's mercy' means Paradise (3:107), and plotting of night and day occurs during them (34:33). He also lists heart for intellect, mouths for tongues, tongue for language, and village for inhabitants (7:179, 48:11, 14:4, 12:82).
  6:81/q26/p2 Other transfers include an instrument for its effect, an abstract state for its place, and even a word for its opposite. — reasons: Al-Zarkashi gives 'tongue of truth' as good remembrance, eyes as God's sight and a messenger's tongue as his people's language (19:50, 54:14, 14:4); adornment and mosque as place and prayer (7:31); and calling evil's requital evil from the offender's view, or obedience ihsan in reward context (42:40, 55:60). He reports al-Hasan's 'in your eye' for 'in your sleep,' which al-Zamakhshari deems remote and interprets as 'in your vision' (8:43).
6:81/q27 How does the interrogative here compare with hamza? [فَأَىُّ · grammar]
  6:81/q27/p1 Ayyu follows the conjunction fa here, while hamza characteristically precedes conjuncti
