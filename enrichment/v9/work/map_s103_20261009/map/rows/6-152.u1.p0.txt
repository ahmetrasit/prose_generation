## THE CURRENT MAP
6:152/q01 Who is the orphan and why is the orphan's property singled out? [مَالَ ٱلْيَتِيمِ · interpretation]
  6:152/q01/p1 The orphan is a fatherless child whose vulnerable property especially needs protection. — reasons: Mawardi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Abu Hayyan, Qurtubi, Bursevi, Biqai, Ibn Ashur, and Kuran Yolu cite absent parental protection, weak oversight, temptation to seize, and the child's inability to defend property; Mawardi says others' property is equally protected; Qurtubi says God opposes its abuser; Bursevi adds a Persian couplet about an orphan's weeping. Jishumi defines a human orphan as fatherless; Bursevi adds an animal without its mother.
6:152/q02 What does prohibiting even approach to the orphan's property cover? [وَلَا تَقْرَبُوا۟ مَالَ ٱلْيَتِيمِ · interpretation]
  6:152/q02/p1 Every handling, consumption, use, or disposition is barred unless it benefits the orphan. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan say the broad prohibition blocks harmful means; Tabatabai enumerates consumption, use, and disposal; Ibn Ashur says even slight taking is warned against because the child cannot defend himself, citing 2:188; Bursevi addresses guardians and trustees.
6:152/q03 What is the best way to deal with the orphan's property? [إِلَّا بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the exception requires active growth, mere preservation, or permits particular uses; several authors combine preservation and growth.)
  6:152/q03/p1 Preserve and increase the property for the orphan, using beneficial management and investment. — reasons: Muqatil specifies profit; Zajjaj, Thalabi, Mawardi, Wahidi, Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Bursevi, Alusi, Elmalili, Biqai, and others join preservation to growth. Qurtubi prefers this comprehensive reading; Jishumi and Ibn Ashur stress the child's benefit, and Asad says any use must aim at improvement. Qashani excludes waste and use for personal needs.
  6:152/q03/p2 Trade with the property to earn profit for the orphan. — reasons: Mujahid, Atiyya, Said ibn Jubayr, Dahhak, and Suddi report trade; Ibn Atiyya specifies a guardian who has other means. Dahhak bars taking its profits personally; Qurtubi reports Mujahid forbidding purchase from or borrowing the estate.
  6:152/q03/p3 Preserve the property until it can be delivered to its mature owner. — reasons: Kalbi and Ibn al-Saib emphasize guarding principal and handing it over at the endpoint; Tabrisī reports this as another view.
  6:152/q03/p4 Using the orphan's animal or servant can count as permitted management. — reasons: Zajjaj reports the view but says the wording gives no indication of it; Abu Hayyan says the proposal avoids household mixing.
  6:152/q03/p5 Borrowing from the orphan's estate is permitted as its best use. — reasons: Jishumi reports borrowing among the explanations; Abu Hayyan rejects an only-loan interpretation as inconsistent with the child's best interest; Qurtubi reports Mujahid forbidding borrowing.
6:152/q04 May a guardian use the orphan's property for personal support? [إِلَّا بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ · law] (turns on: Whether the guardian's need and useful management make personal consumption part of the best treatment, and whether 4:6 allows food only.)
  6:152/q04/p1 A poor guardian may take a customary reasonable amount, while a wealthy guardian abstains. — reasons: Ibn Zayd and Ibn Abbas cite need and 4:6; Ibn Atiyya says without support the property might be neglected, and Abu Hayyan puts the allowance in trading profit. Several restrict the allowance to food, excluding clothing. Study Quran reports an alternative small amount to repay when possible.
  6:152/q04/p2 The guardian must take nothing for himself, including profit or personal items. — reasons: Ikrima forbids even a cap or turban; Hasan ibn Salih and Dahhak require seeking gain entirely for the orphan, with Dahhak forbidding personal profit. Qashani rules out use for the guardian's needs.
  6:152/q04/p3 Necessary mixing to manage the estate is allowed; mixing merely to consume it is not. — reasons: Al-Sha'bi distinguishes the manager's purpose. The report from Ibn Abbas describes food separation causing waste and 2:220 then permitting beneficial mixing.
6:152/q05 May the guardian buy from the orphan's estate? [مَالَ ٱلْيَتِيمِ · law]
  6:152/q05/p1 The guardian may not buy from the estate. — reasons: Abu Ishaq states the rule, and Qurtubi reports Mujahid forbidding purchase along with borrowing.
6:152/q06 What happened after the warning about orphan property? [مَالَ ٱلْيَتِيمِ · reports]
  6:152/q06/p1 Guardians separated orphan food until leftovers spoiled, then 2:220 allowed beneficial mixing. — reasons: Ibn Abi Hatim transmits Ibn Abbas: God distinguished reformers from corrupters; Ibn Kathir cites Abu Dawud and describes separated food and drink after the warning at 4:10.
6:152/q07 How does the exception except the best manner work grammatically? [إِلَّا بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the omitted feminine referent describes the manner of handling or the guardian.)
  6:152/q07/p1 It excepts the best state or act of handling, with an understood feminine noun. — reasons: Samin allows a circumstantial reading or an omitted verbal-noun adjective and says ahsan demands the best, not merely good. Ibn Ashur supplies state or act and cites 23:96, the idiom al-lutayya wa-allati, and Salmi's line; Alusi considers the alternative of describing the guardian's quality remote.
  6:152/q07/p2 The guardian himself must embody the best quality. — reasons: Alusi mentions this analysis only to call it remote.
6:152/q08 What does until the orphan reaches maturity limit? [حَتَّىٰ يَبْلُغَ أَشُدَّهُۥ · grammar]
  6:152/q08/p1 It limits the implied duty to preserve the estate, which is then delivered; it does not license harmful handling afterward. — reasons: Zajjaj, Wahidi, Abu Hayyan, Samin, Bursevi, Alusi, and Tabatabai read the endpoint semantically. Samin says a literal endpoint to the prohibition would yield an absurd permission; Thalabi and Qurtubi expressly continue the best-treatment rule after maturity. Asad says subsequent borrowing or use needs the new owner's consent.
6:152/q09 What does ashudd mean in sense? [أَشُدَّهُۥ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the term itself names physical vigor or the developed judgment needed in this legal context.)
  6:152/q09/p1 Developed strength, vigor, or youth at maturity. — reasons: Thalabi compares the height of day; Antara's shadd al-nahar line is cited by Thalabi, Jishumi, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, Samin, and Alusi, with another poetic line in Qurtubi. Ibn Qutayba says full youthful strength before decline; Baydawi calls it adulthood; Jawhari gives an eighteen-to-thirty span. Ibn Ashur's context concerns ability to manage property.
  6:152/q09/p2 Mature reason, knowledge, firmness, and competence in managing assets. — reasons: Layth says adult knowledge and judgment; Abu al-Aliya combines understanding with bodily powers; Ibn Atiyya specifies firm financial decisions and distinguishes this from the seasoned reason of age forty in 46:15.
6:152/q10 What is the morphological form and derivation of ashudd? [أَشُدَّهُۥ · grammar] (turns on: Whether an actual singular was heard and whether the af'al pattern can be singular; authorities also dispute its derivation from strength or rising daylight.)
  6:152/q10/p1 It is a plural of shiddah, strength or toughness. — reasons: Abu al-Haytham treats the feminine ending as extra before the af'ul plural; Sibawayh gives shiddah, but Jawhari objects that fa'lah normally does not form af'ul. Abu Muslim compares ni'mah/an'um. Farrā and others derive it from strength.
  6:152/q10/p2 It is a plural of shadd, with different reported vowelings. — reasons: Farrā says shadd is analogically expected but unattested; Abu Ubayd cites poetry for the plural. Basrans give shudd with damma, another analysis shadd with fatha; Nasafi compares shadd/fals. Qurtubi warns that analogical singulars are not heard and cites the analogous proposed ibul for ababil, plus Abu Zayd's shada and ashadda.
  6:152/q10/p3 It is a plural without an attested singular. — reasons: Farrā reports no heard singular despite analogy; Qurtubi compares the lexical dispute over forms; Abu Hayyan lists this among five analyses.
  6:152/q10/p4 It is itself a singular form. — reasons: Ibn al-Anbari reports this and compares al-anuk; Abu Hayyan rejects it because af'al is not established as a singular noun pattern; Samin transmits that objection from the shaykh. Baydawi and Tabrisī note the possibility.
6:152/q11 At what point does the orphan reach ashudd here? [حَتَّىٰ يَبْلُغَ أَشُدَّهُۥ · law] (turns on: Whether ashudd here is a fixed age, puberty, or demonstrated prudence; 4:6's marriage-age and sound-judgment test and 46:15's forty-year context are central evidence.)
  6:152/q11/p1 At puberty, with sound judgment required for delivery of property. — reasons: Sha'bi says moral deeds begin to be recorded then; Rabi'a cites the marriage-age test in 4:6. Zajjaj, Ibn Atiyya, Tabrisī, Razi, Qurtubi, Bursevi, Alusi, Tabatabai, and others require prudent judgment too. Ibn Atiyya prefers Malik's puberty plus absence of foolishness; Tabrisī prefers no fixed age. Zajjaj rejects eighteen if puberty and judgment arrive earlier; Ibn al-Jawzi prefers puberty and doubts that older age reports belong to this verse. Alusi refers readers to his earlier treatment of the disputed handover age.
