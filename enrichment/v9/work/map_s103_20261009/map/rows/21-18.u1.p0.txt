## THE CURRENT MAP
21:18/q01 What does bal turn away from? [بَلْ · links] (turns on: Whether the contrast is read chiefly against the preceding talk of play or the opponents' claims about God.)
  21:18/q01/p1 It rejects the thought that God creates or acts in play, presenting His serious action against falsehood. — reasons: Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Alusi and Ibn Ashur relate it to the preceding denial of amusement; Kashshaf and Razi invoke divine wisdom and self-sufficiency. Tabataba'i objects that a narrow contrast of earnestness and divine amusement would presuppose the amusement just denied.
  21:18/q01/p2 It dismisses the opponents' false claim that God has a child or partner. — reasons: Muqatil sets it against the Najran Christians' claim about Jesus and recalls that God would take a child from angels were He to do so; Wahidi connects it to their description of God, Bagawi and Ibn al-Jawzi call their statement a lie, and Jishumi links the alleged amusement to attribution of child and partner.
21:18/q02 What does naqdhif convey? [نَقْذِفُ · meaning]
  21:18/q02/p1 It means throwing or hurling truth forcefully against falsehood. — reasons: Wahidi and Bagawi give the basic sense; Raghib as cited by Bursevi and Alusi specifies a distant, forceful throw with hard impact. Tabataba'i also gives distance. Ibn Ashur extends the metaphor to proof, warning, destruction, a created force, discernment or empowerment of servants against wrongdoers (8:12).
21:18/q03 What are the truth and falsehood cast against each other? [بِٱلْحَقِّ ٱلْبَٰطِلِ · referent] (turns on: The notes variously identify the pair with revelation and lies, proof and claims, faith and shirk, seriousness and play, or unrestricted truth and falsehood.)
  21:18/q03/p1 The truth includes the Quran or God's revelation, and falsehood includes unbelievers' lies, denial or confusion. — reasons: Yahya, Tabari, Zajjaj, Wahidi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Biqai and Durr identify Quran or Book; Tabari specifies revelation against denial, Wahidi reports Abu Ishaq's Quran against lies, and Durr cites Qatada's confusion. Biqai stresses the Quran's firm argument and lack of frivolity.
  21:18/q03/p2 Truth is broad, including Quran, message, law, faith and all truth; falsehood covers their opposites generally. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya names Quran, message and law; Abu Hayyan reports the same broad view; Bagawi calls truth faith; Tabataba'i takes the rule across belief, practice and creation; Alusi prefers the general reading over restriction to proof and claims about a child. Bursevi includes seriousness, faith and Quran.
  21:18/q03/p3 Truth is Quran and falsehood is Satan or Iblis. — reasons: Tabari reports Qatada; Mawardi reports the alternative; Abu Hayyan reports Mujahid, who says every Quranic use of falsehood means Satan; Alusi also reports Mujahid. Nasafi lists Satan among possible referents.
  21:18/q03/p4 Truth is proof or decisive argument, and falsehood is the opponents' claims, doubt or false descriptions of God. — reasons: Nahhas identifies proofs and arguments against their claim; Tabrisi gives decisive proofs, with argument against doubt as a variant; Abu Hayyan and Alusi mention the claim of a child; Jishumi says proofs distinguish truth from falsehood; Wahidi says truth exposes lies.
  21:18/q03/p5 Truth is God's word that He has no child; falsehood is their claim that He took one. — reasons: Bagawi explicitly offers this alternative; Muqatil applies the clash to the Najran claim about Jesus.
  21:18/q03/p6 Truth is Islam or faith, and falsehood is polytheism or disbelief. — reasons: Mawardi gives Islam against polytheism; Tabrisi gives faith against disbelief; Nasafi gives Islam against idolatry; Yahya identifies falsehood as shirk.
  21:18/q03/p7 Truth is the word to be followed, and falsehood the word to be rejected. — reasons: Mawardi reports this formulation without a named transmitter.
  21:18/q03/p8 Truth is earnestness and falsehood is play or amusement. — reasons: Nasafi lists it; Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi, Bursevi and Alusi use the seriousness/play contrast. Tabataba'i reports and rejects a reading that assumes God was amusing Himself.
  21:18/q03/p9 Truth is God's transcendence, and falsehood is treating Him as immanent in or coexistent with creation. — reasons: Asad argues that finite, perishable creation cannot be a projection of the infinite, eternal Creator.
21:18/q04 What does yadmughuhu mean? [فَيَدْمَغُهُۥ · meaning]
  21:18/q04/p1 It strikes the head or brain, a fatal blow, and thus destroys or defeats falsehood. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, Tabari, Mawardi, Wahidi citing Ibn Qutayba, Bagawi, Ibn Atiyya, Samīn, Jishumi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Baydawi, Tabataba'i, Islahi and Study Quran cite the brain wound; Samīn compares verbs named for the struck body part. Study Quran adds the usage of stamping a form into soft material. Ibn Ashur and Alusi describe breaking a hollow object; Kalbi gives destruction and Tabrisi invalidation.
21:18/q05 How does the casting and crushing image work? [نَقْذِفُ فَيَدْمَغُهُۥ · rhetoric] (turns on: Alusi's distinction is between an explicit overall simile and a concealed comparison through the physical verbs.)
  21:18/q05/p1 It depicts strong truth as a hard projectile crushing weak, hollow falsehood, making abstract defeat vivid. — reasons: Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Biqai and Bursevi give the hard/soft image; Bursevi cites Sahib al-Miftah on physical words transferred to abstract realities; Burhan says both verbs are metaphors. Alusi prefers a full simile of thrown body and soft brain, with truth's height and falsehood's lowliness, to a hidden metaphor. Ibn Ashur says both images transfer physical action to thought. Abu Ishaq, cited by Wahidi, says the defeat makes falsehood disappear in abasement and humiliation.
  21:18/q05/p2 The image may be a hidden metaphor comparing truth and falsehood to the two bodies. — reasons: Alusi states this alternative but prefers the full simile.
21:18/q06 How is fa-yadmaghuhu read and parsed? [فَيَدْمَغُهُۥ · readings] (turns on: The final verb's case or mood and the possible source of the rare mim vowel.)
  21:18/q06/p1 The common reading raises the final ghayn, coordinating the imperfect verb. — reasons: Samīn calls this the common reading and its grammatical coordination; Sibawayh notes more generally that jussive inflection belongs to imperfect verbs and compares it to noun genitive.
  21:18/q06/p2 Isa ibn Umar reads the final ghayn with accusative/subjunctive inflection. — reasons: Kashshaf calls it weak and cites a poetic subjunctive ending fa-astariha; Abu Hayyan and Samīn repeat that judgment. Baydawi parses by meaning or coordination with al-haqq and cites anonymous poetry. Alusi says fa does not govern it here; Abu al-Baqa coordinates it with al-haqq, while Alusi prefers coordination with the underlying sense of throwing then crushing. A proposed indicative with light emphatic nun in the poem is weak, making the verse reading weaker.
  21:18/q06/p3 A rare reading puts a damma on the mim, and another transmits damma on mim and ghayn. — reasons: Abu Hayyan transmits the mim variant from Zamakhshari; Samīn explains it as dialect variation in the imperfect or assimilation from the guttural; Alusi reports both vowels raised.
21:18/q07 What does zahiq mean? [زَاهِقٌۭ · meaning] (turns on: Jishumi lists a separate lexical usage, while the contextual glosses agree on disappearance.)
  21:18/q07/p1 It is departing, vanishing or perishing. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, Zajjaj, linguists cited by Nahhas, Qatada cited by Abd al-Razzaq, Tabari, Mawardi, Wahidi and Tabrisi, and later commentators give these near senses. Ibn Abbas compares departure to an arrow leaving its target; Baydawi and Ibn Ashur recall the departing soul. Ibn Ashur cites 9:55 and 17:81; Study Quran cites Tabari's gloss of destruction.
  21:18/q07/p2 In another Arabic usage, zahiq describes a fat or stout animal. — reasons: Jishumi lists this beside perishing and the usage 'his soul departed'; he does not claim the animal sense is intended here.
21:18/q08 What does fa-idha huwa zahiq add? [فَإِذَا هُوَ زَاهِقٌۭ · rhetoric]
  21:18/q08/p1 The sudden particle and nominal clause make falsehood's complete disappearance seem immediate and startling. — reasons: Bursevi and Alusi identify the suddenness and nominal sentence; Alusi says as though it never existed. Ibn Ashur says clear truth works swiftly and compares 13:17. Tabataba'i sees a surprising reversal; Islahi likens it to immediate death from a brain blow.
21:18/q09 What does wayl mean here? [ٱلْوَيْلُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the valley is the intended referent or merely a way to express deserved punishment.)
  21:18/q09/p1 It denotes woe, ruin, evil, reproach, grief or distress. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba calls it comprehensive evil and quotes Asma'i on condemnation; Itqan repeats Asma'i and cites laments at 18:49 and 5:31. Ibn Atiyya gives disgrace and grief; Bursevi cites Asma'i on ugliness and lament, distinguishes ways as belittling and wayh as compassion. Ibn Qutayba notes diminutive wayh and ways and a separate Arabic word al-alil for groaning; Itqan cites Aisha's report that the Prophet treated wayh/ways as mercy but warned of wayl.
  21:18/q09/p2 It may designate a valley in Hell. — reasons: Wahidi reports Ibn Abbas; Ibn Atiyya allows this as an intended sense if the view is adopted. Bursevi says those calling it a valley mean the condemned have an abode in the Fire, not that the valley is its lexical meaning.
21:18/q10 How does lakum function with al-wayl? [وَلَكُمُ ٱلْوَيْلُ · grammar]
  21:18/q10/p1 The lam has its ordinary genitive form in lakum al-wayl. — reasons: Sibawayh contrasts it with the open lam of vocative ya wayla laka: without ya the lam returns to its normal form.
