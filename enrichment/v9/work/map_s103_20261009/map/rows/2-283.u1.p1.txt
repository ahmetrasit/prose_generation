 claims or uncertain property be pledged? [رِهَٰنٌۭ مَّقْبُوضَةٌۭ · law] (turns on: Whether the collateral can be physically possessed and realized, especially for debt claims and common shares.)
  2:283/q15/p1 Only valuable, saleable and deliverable property; debts and uncertain property cannot be pledged. — reasons: The majority require realizable security and physical possession. A cited Razi argument excludes an undivided share because it cannot be physically received. KuranYolu bars human beings despite pre-Islamic child hostages.
  2:283/q15/p2 A debt or uncertain property can serve as collateral. — reasons: Malik permits debt claims and examples such as runaway slave, stray camel, fetus, fish and unripe fruit; Qurtubi argues anything saleable has value.
2:283/q16 Can an undivided share be pledged? [فَرِهَٰنٌۭ مَّقْبُوضَةٌۭ · law] (turns on: Whether an undivided share can be received as security.)
  2:283/q16/p1 An undivided share cannot be pledged. — reasons: Abu Hanifa and the argument cited by Razi and Elmalılı rely on inability to take definite possession.
  2:283/q16/p2 An undivided property share may be pledged. — reasons: Malik and Shafi'i allow divisible or indivisible shares; Ibn al-Mundhir compares sale and notes a Hanafi joint-creditor exception.
  2:283/q16/p3 An undivided share may be pledged only if it cannot be divided. — reasons: Al-Hasan ibn Salih's intermediate rule.
2:283/q17 Which growth of collateral remains pledged? [رِهَٰنٌۭ مَّقْبُوضَةٌۭ · law]
  2:283/q17/p1 The pledged property's inseparable growth follows the pledge, but separable fruits do not unless stipulated. — reasons: Qurtubi distinguishes fattening and offspring from crops, milk, wool and fruit, citing independent alms treatment of the latter.
2:283/q18 Does a secured creditor have priority over other creditors? [فَرِهَٰنٌۭ · law]
  2:283/q18/p1 The secured creditor has priority over other creditors. — reasons: Malik and many jurists give priority when the debtor has not already been declared insolvent; Qurtubi rejects a report of equal sharing.
2:283/q19 May the creditor use pledged property? [رِهَٰنٌۭ · law] (turns on: Who owns benefits, who pays upkeep, and whether maintenance or the contract permits creditor use.)
  2:283/q19/p1 Benefits belong to the owner, who bears upkeep; creditor use requires permission or is forbidden. — reasons: A report says the owner gets yield and bears loss. Malik, Shafi'i, Sha'bi and Ibn Sirin hold it; Khattabi prefers the explicit wording. Tahawi and Sha'bi argue the permissive report preceded the usury bar or was abrogated; Ibn Abd al-Barr reports agreement and warns of unknown benefit and usury.
  2:283/q19/p2 A creditor who feeds a pledged animal may ride it or drink its milk in proportion to upkeep. — reasons: A more explicit Abu Hurayra report is adopted by Ahmad and Ishaq; Abu Thawr, Awza'i and Layth permit analogous use when creditor maintained neglected collateral.
  2:283/q19/p3 A creditor may stipulate use in a sale or lease pledge, but not in a loan pledge. — reasons: Ibn Khuwayzimandad says use on a loan is interest.
2:283/q20 May a pledge become the creditor's property after nonpayment? [رِهَٰنٌۭ · law]
  2:283/q20/p1 No; pre-Islamic foreclosure at a deadline is barred. — reasons: Tabrisi, Qurtubi and Ibn Ashur cite the Prophetic 'the pledge is not foreclosed.' Qurtubi explains the indicative wording and disputed mursal chains; Zuhayr's verse recalls the earlier practice.
2:283/q21 What happened when the Prophet pledged his armor? [رِهَٰنٌۭ · reports]
  2:283/q21/p1 He pledged his armor to a Jewish seller for food on credit while in Medina. — reasons: Reports variously give twenty or thirty measures of barley, name Abu al-Shahm or leave the seller unnamed, and say the armor was still pledged at death. Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi recount his response that he was trustworthy.
2:283/q22 What does mutual trust permit in a debt transaction? [فَإِنْ أَمِنَ بَعْضُكُم بَعْضًۭا · law] (turns on: Whether the trust clause is a general independent option or a concession confined to inability to document.)
  2:283/q22/p1 The parties may omit writing, witnesses and collateral when they trust one another. — reasons: Sha'bi, Hasan and many exegetes take the clause as a general concession. Ibn Ashur extends it to each party and home or travel; Tabarisi sees evidence the safeguards are advisory.
  2:283/q22/p2 When documentation and witnesses are available, they must still be used; trust is a limited travel concession. — reasons: Tabari accepts a restriction on trust while rejecting restriction on pledges; Dahhak ties trust to travel and absence of a writer. Ibn Ashur finds Tabari's explanation unclear and suggests a traveler unable to pledge may rely on trust.
2:283/q23 Who trusts whom in the trust clause? [أَمِنَ بَعْضُكُم بَعْضًۭا · referent] (turns on: Whether the clause describes only reliance on the debtor or obligations on either party.)
  2:283/q23/p1 Ordinarily the creditor trusts the debtor with property or the debt. — reasons: Wahidi cites the language of security from betrayal and 8:11, 12:64; Ibn Ashur specifies lender and borrower. Biqai says the undefined parties could operate in either direction.
  2:283/q23/p2 Trust can run both ways between creditor and debtor. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says a debtor can trust a creditor holding collateral worth more than the debt; the creditor must return the excess faithfully. Biqai calls trust mutual.
2:283/q24 How is 'if some trust others' alternatively read? [فَإِنْ أَمِنَ بَعْضُكُم بَعْضًۭا · readings]
  2:283/q24/p1 Ubayy reads a passive, 'if some are trusted.' — reasons: Zamakhshari explains public confidence in the debtor; Samin treats baʿḍan as governed by a deleted preposition and an omitted property or debt complement. Sajaawandi reports an alternate reflexive form.
2:283/q25 Who is 'the one entrusted' and what must be given? [ٱلَّذِى ٱؤْتُمِنَ أَمَٰنَتَهُۥ · referent] (turns on: Whether the entrusted person is debtor, pledgee, or the party recording the debt.)
  2:283/q25/p1 The trusted debtor must repay the creditor's debt or property, without denial or delay. — reasons: Jishumi and Razi prefer this over pledgee. Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi cite consensus, judgments and reports. Tabari warns against absconding; Ibn Ashur includes returning the same thing or its equivalent. The name encourages faithful payment.
  2:283/q25/p2 The pledge holder must return collateral after the debt is paid. — reasons: Jishumi and Razi report this alternative because collateral itself is held in trust; Ibn Ashur includes a creditor holding an overvalued pledge as an entrusted party.
  2:283/q25/p3 The entrusted party must write the true sum without increase or decrease. — reasons: Zajjaj equates faithful rendering with justice in recording the obligation.
2:283/q26 What is meant by 'his trust' and whose is it? [أَمَٰنَتَهُۥ · meaning] (turns on: Whether amāna names the owed object or the fiduciary relation, and whether its pronoun belongs to creditor or debtor.)
  2:283/q26/p1 It is the debt or property owed, called a trust because security was waived. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Biqai and Alusi explain the nomenclature; the possessive may refer to debtor or creditor. Ibn Ashur says amāna can mean the object or quality of trust and compares a verbal noun joined to its object.
  2:283/q26/p2 It is the act or obligation of trust, with an omitted 'debt' or a charge to preserve honor by repayment. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya gives the honor nuance; Samin gives a verbal noun with omitted possessive and rejects using it as the verb's absolute object; Abu Hayyan lists both referents.
2:283/q27 How is 'entrusted' formed and pronounced? [ٱؤْتُمِنَ · grammar]
  2:283/q27/p1 It is an eighth-form passive from amāna with an original root hamza. — reasons: Akhfash and Nahhas explain the two hamzas and joining alif; Wāḥidī and Jishumi give the active and passive forms. At a fresh start the joining alif prevents two sounded hamzas.
2:283/q28 Which recitations of 'entrusted' are accepted? [ٱؤْتُمِنَ · readings] (turns on: Whether reported vowel gestures, letter substitution, assimilation and pause length fit transmission and Arabic phonology.)
  2:283/q28/p1 The usual connected reading retains a quiescent hamza after the joining hamza drops. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid, Farisi and Ibn Atiyya reject reported ḍamma indications as incorrect; Farisi argues the first radical of iftaʿala is still and vowel transfer goes backward. Ibn Ashur describes the majority pronunciation.
  2:283/q28/p2 A ḍamma-tinged or rounded hamza is transmitted from Asim, Hamza or Abu Bakr. — reasons: The transmission is described as ishām or a raised initial alif. Abu Ali defends Ahmad ibn Musa's account against Ibn Atiyya; other authorities reject the notation or performance.
  2:283/q28/p3 An assimilated itamin or attamin form is reported, but its acceptability is disputed. — reasons: Zamakhshari and Baydawi reject assimilation because the replacement letter retains hamza's status; Alusi cites Arabic usage, Aisha's speech and a Kufan grammatical defense.
  2:283/q28/p4 Warsh and others turn the hamza into a yāʾ-like sound in connected recitation. — reasons: Samin cites Warsh and Susi; Ibn Ashur explains an intermediary wāw from passive ḍamma followed by yāʾ after the linking hamza disappears. Baydawi accepts a yāʾ substitution.
  2:283/q28/p5 At a pause or initial pronunciation, substitute a matching vowel letter with shortening. — reasons: Samin explains the wāw-like onset; Ibn al-Jazari rejects restored madd or verified hamza, against Abu Shama's analogy; NASHR sets general rules for still hamza and competing madd causes.
2:283/q29 Why add 'fear God, his Lord'? [وَلْيَتَّقِ ٱللَّهَ رَبَّهُۥ · rhetoric]
  2:283/q29/p1 The doubled divine reference solemnly presses the debtor to repay and avoid betrayal. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says repeating God's name rather than a pronoun adds awe; Abu Hayyan no
