22:11/q09 What does the good that befalls him comprise? [خَيْرٌ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether 'good' names material circumstances alone or also the convert's inference about religious truth.)
  22:11/q09/p1 Worldly ease: health, livelihood, fertility, wealth, sons and increased livestock. — reasons: Mujāhid says prosperity; Qatāda adds crops, comforts and pleasures. Al-Wāḥidī includes crops and says the pronoun in 'by it' refers to this good. The reports list horses, foals, sons and charity; Bursawī and al-Ālūsī distinguish outward settlement from believers' inner steadiness.
  22:11/q09/p2 Favorable circumstances are interpreted as proof the new religion is true. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says converts reason that former gods have failed to punish them, while a reverse outcome leads to resentment.
22:11/q10 What does being reassured by that good mean? [ٱطْمَأَنَّ بِهِۦ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the assurance is mere conditional settling or an asserted certainty.)
  22:11/q10/p1 He becomes settled and content in Islam while prosperity continues. — reasons: Mujāhid glosses it as becoming established; al-Māwardī says it may strengthen faith or settle him in his position. Bursawī says the firmness is outward, not the believer's inner assurance. Al-Wāḥidī identifies the pronoun's antecedent as the good.
  22:11/q10/p2 He claims certainty because desired pleasures are coming. — reasons: Qatāda says abundant crops and comfort induce a declaration of certainty, which collapses under hardship.
22:11/q11 What does fitna mean here, and what may it include? [فِتْنَةٌ · meaning] (turns on: The word can name the concrete adverse event or its function as a test; the sources say prosperity too can test.)
  22:11/q11/p1 A test or calamity, commonly illness, drought, constrained livelihood or loss affecting person, family or property. — reasons: Mujāhid adds punishment; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Ṭabrisī gloss test. Al-Rāzī observes that blessing also tests, although ordinary Arabic calls burdensome events fitna. Bursawī says hardship can bring nearness through patience. Al-Biqāʿī says even a small affliction exposes insecurity. The reported migrant faced fever, a daughter, loss of wealth or a delayed alms gift.
  22:11/q11/p2 It is a test of whether hardship overturns profession of faith. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says the path to God includes trials; meeting them with submission strengthens faith and multiplies reward, while despair loses both worlds.
22:11/q12 Why use 'fitna' for hardship rather than call it evil? [فِتْنَةٌ · rhetoric] (turns on: One explanation concerns moral wording, another the smallness suggested by grammatical form.)
  22:11/q12/p1 Hardship is not inherently evil, and 'fitna' names a burdensome test without moral blame. — reasons: Al-Rāzī records Muqātil's question about the contrast with 'good'; Arabic often reserves fitna in this context for what feels hard, although blessings also test. Bursawī says patient acceptance can raise one's rank.
  22:11/q12/p2 The feminine phrasing suggests even a small affliction is enough. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī connects the wording with exposure of hidden instability in bodily or financial trouble.
22:11/q13 What does 'turns back on his face' convey? [ٱنقَلَبَ عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِۦ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the turn refers primarily to apostasy, physical flight or emotional reversal; al-Rāzī addresses how a hypocrite could 'turn back'.)
  22:11/q13/p1 He abandons Islam and returns to disbelief or former idol worship. — reasons: Mujāhid, Abū Isḥāq and others gloss apostasy; al-Mubarrad says 'face' may be his former direction. Al-Baḥr suggests ʿalā can bear the sense ʿan, citing 11:6. Bursawī's Kashifī says he goes back the way he came. Al-Biqāʿī calls it a complete return; al-Rāzī answers the objection that a hypocrite was never inwardly Muslim by saying public praise reverses into denunciation.
  22:11/q13/p2 The image depicts flight, defeat or anxiety when reassurance vanishes. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī offers fleeing or rushing and reports al-Kashf's defeat metonym and another anxiety explanation. Al-Māwardī allows flight to one's people in fear.
  22:11/q13/p3 The face stands for the entire person. — reasons: Asad explicitly calls it metonymy.
22:11/q14 How is the clause 'he lost this world and the next' connected syntactically? [خَسِرَ ٱلدُّنْيَا وَٱلْءَاخِرَةَ · grammar] (turns on: The case and clause relation depend on whether the loss reports a new event, describes the turn, or restates it.)
  22:11/q14/p1 The past-tense clause can begin a fresh statement. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn list a new clause; al-Ālūsī also lists independent speech. On this analysis the prior clause may complete the opening subject.
  22:11/q14/p2 The past-tense clause can be circumstantial without qad. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn permit this even without qad, and the participial reading supports it.
  22:11/q14/p3 The past-tense clause replaces or restates 'turned back'. — reasons: Ibn Jinnī calls it a replacement clause and cites 25:68–69 and poetry; Abū al-Faḍl and al-Samīn permit the same, while al-Ṭabrisī calls it appositional restatement.
22:11/q15 Which readings of 'lost this world and the next' are reported? [خَسِرَ ٱلدُّنْيَا وَٱلْءَاخِرَةَ · readings] (turns on: The variant changes verb to participle and case; the participle may be circumstantial or nominative, with accompanying case differences.)
  22:11/q15/p1 The common reading has the past verb khasira and accusative 'the Hereafter'. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ says it has the same core sense as khāsir; al-Ṭabarī says all regional readers except Ḥumayd al-Aʿraj use it. Jishumī calls it the reading of the seven readers.
  22:11/q15/p2 Khāsir is an accusative active participle in a circumstantial phrase, usually with genitive 'the Hereafter'. — reasons: Reports name Mujāhid, Ḥumayd, al-Aʿraj, Ibn Muḥayṣin, al-Jaḥdarī, Ibn Muqsim and others; Yaʿqūb's Rūḥ and Zayd are transmitted for it. Al-Samīn notes accusative ākhira is also possible if dunyā is accusative and gives a poem for loss of tanwīn at a consonant junction. Ibn Muḥayṣin is also transmitted with accusative. The phrase means turning away while already losing both worlds; no pause falls at wajhihi.
  22:11/q15/p3 A nominative khāsir can be the subject replacing a pronoun or predicate of omitted 'he'. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān reports al-Zamakhsharī's favorable subject parse; al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī include the omitted-subject predicate, and al-Ālūsī mentions tajrīd for emphasis. Al-Nasafī ascribes a nominative variant to Rūḥ and Zayd.
22:11/q16 What is the loss of this world and the Hereafter? [خَسِرَ ٱلدُّنْيَا وَٱلْءَاخِرَةَ · interpretation] (turns on: The notes differ on whether worldly loss means missed hoped-for goods, actual bereavement or death, legal and social exclusion, or moral waste.)
  22:11/q16/p1 He misses the worldly benefit he sought and loses afterlife reward through unbelief or apostasy. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says uncertain worship fails to secure worldly aims and leads to Fire; Yahyā says his cherished worldly life passes and he has no share beyond. Al-Wāḥidī, Ibn Kathīr and Study Quran connect missed goods to rejection of faith. Kashifī adds destruction of deeds. Al-Bayḍāwī says apostasy removes worldly protection and nullifies deeds.
  22:11/q16/p2 He loses worldly goods or loved ones without acceptance, then forfeits the reward of patience. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān identifies property and bereavement; Bursawī prefers loss of family in hardship and Hell after abandoning religion. Iṣlāḥī says ordinary worldly loss could be compensated in the Hereafter if met with submission.
  22:11/q16/p3 He loses worldly standing, spoils, legal protection and social eligibility, and loses reward in the next life. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās and al-Qurṭubī mention divine denunciation, no spoils or praise and no reward. Al-Rāzī enumerates honor, testimony, leadership, judgment and protection of property and blood; al-Bayḍāwī mentions lost protection.
  22:11/q16/p4 His worldly loss is death or separation, while his next-life loss is punishment or hypocrisy. — reasons: Al-Nasafī says death and eternal fire; al-Māwardī and al-Ṭabrisī say separation and hypocrisy. Bursawī prefers worldly family loss with loss of reward and Hell.
  22:11/q16/p5 Worldly loss is abandoning obedience and wasting time; afterlife loss is accumulated liabilities or complacency in Paradise. — reasons: Al-Sulamī transmits both distinct inward accounts: one links disobedience to claims against the person, the other wasted time to contentment in Paradise. Bursawī repeats the first.
  22:11/q16/p6 Sin itself can narrow provision, so the person gains no worldly recovery and loses the afterlife. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī cites 5:66 for provision and a report that sin may deprive a person of it, without naming that report's transmitter.
  22:11/q16/p7 The loss is manifest failure of the self and family on Resurrection Day. — reasons: Muqātil connects it to 39:15.
22:11/q17 What does 'that is the manifest loss' add? [ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ ٱلْخُسْرَانُ ٱلْمُبِينُ · rhetoric]
  22:11/q17/p1 It emphatically identifies the preceding two-world loss as unmistakable ruin. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says no other description fits; Asad calls it the most obvious loss. Al-Wāḥidī says the verb khasira supplies the noun khusrān; al-Ālūsī says the distant demonstrative signals extremity, though it may also point to an unexpressed referent.
