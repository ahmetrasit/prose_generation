## THE CURRENT MAP
2:14/q01 What does meeting mean here? [لَقُوا۟ · meaning]
  2:14/q01/p1 Encountering or seeing someone face to face, nearby. — reasons: Al-Layth says it is facing another; al-Khalil defines proximity and encounter; Ibn al-Sikkit lists liqa, liqyan, liqya, and laqa. Baydawi relates ilqa to putting something where it meets another; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin list fourteen verbal nouns; al-Harali adds reciprocal turning and says intimacy with hypocrites is rare.
2:14/q02 How is the form لقوا derived and pronounced? [لَقُوا۟ · grammar]
  2:14/q02/p1 It contracts from لقيوا by lightening or dropping the yāʾ and resolving adjacent silent letters. — reasons: Al-Akhfash discusses deletion of plural wāw before the following lām; al-Nahhas, al-Wahidi, al-Jishumi, Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, and al-Samin give differing sequences of vowel transfer, yāʾ deletion, and temporary wāw vowel. Al-Nahhas and al-Qurtubi contrast لاقوا, whose preceding fatḥa allows retention.
2:14/q03 How is لقوا read? [لَقُوا۟ · readings] (turns on: The transmitted form and a same-sense variant differ in verbal stem and authority for recitation.)
  2:14/q03/p1 The transmitted reading is لقوا. — reasons: Al-Jishumi says recitation follows widespread transmission from the Prophet and Companions; a report in al-Thaʿlabi gives the same-sense laqu form.
  2:14/q03/p2 A variant reads لاقوا, with the same meeting sense. — reasons: Al-Nahhas and al-Qurtubi trace it to لاقيوا and assign it to Muhammad b. al-Sumayfa; Ibn Atiyya also cites him, while al-Razi and al-Nasafi attribute the form to Abu Hanifa; al-Alusi mentions both.
2:14/q04 What is the function of إذا and the meeting clause? [وَإِذَا لَقُوا۟ · grammar]
  2:14/q04/p1 إذا is a temporal conditional adverb, and قالوا is its response. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi says its answer governs it and notes spatial and surprise usages; Abu Hayyan contrasts temporal, spatial, and particle analyses; al-Samin makes قالوا its governor and parses الذين as the object and آمنا as quoted speech. Al-Nahhas discusses the relative clause and response.
  2:14/q04/p2 إذا portrays their behavior as a repeated, enduring habit. — reasons: Al-Itqan compares the continuing force of the imperfect and cites 4:142.
2:14/q05 Who are the believers they meet? [ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ · referent] (turns on: The notes vary between the Companions generally and Abu Bakr's circle.)
  2:14/q05/p1 The Prophet's Companions, including the Emigrants and Helpers. — reasons: Muqatil calls them Companions affirming Muhammad; Bursevi specifies the Muhajirun and Ansar.
  2:14/q05/p2 Abu Bakr and his companions. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabi and al-Wahidi report this identification.
2:14/q06 What did the hypocrites mean by آمنا? [قَالُوٓا۟ ءَامَنَّا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the verb asserts legal status, inner sincerity, or an omitted object.)
  2:14/q06/p1 They falsely professed Islamic belief outwardly, including belief in Muhammad's revelation, without inward conviction. — reasons: Al-Tabari's Jewish reports specify profession before companions or the Prophet; al-Jishumi and Bursevi describe a protective show; Ibn Ashur says they spoke Islam's formula and favors legal-religious believer status without an omitted object; Abu Hayyan notes ambiguity between false inward faith and mere outward words.
  2:14/q06/p2 They claim sincere inward faith, beyond an already known verbal profession. — reasons: Al-Razi argues that this claim reverses the inward denial they admit to their leaders; al-Alusi rejects the special distinction as unsupported.
  2:14/q06/p3 The omitted object is what was previously specified: God and the Last Day, or whatever the believers believe. — reasons: Al-Alusi says a Moses-only construal is remote; Ibn Ashur prefers taking the verb without any omitted object.
2:14/q07 Why did they profess faith to believers? [قَالُوٓا۟ ءَامَنَّا · interpretation] (turns on: The sources give protective, opportunistic, and derisive motives that can coexist.)
  2:14/q07/p1 To protect themselves and gain worldly benefits while hiding allegiance and obtaining information. — reasons: Al-Tabari, al-Jishumi, Ibn Atiyya, and Abu Hayyan name protection of life, property, and family; al-Razi adds secrets, charity, and spoils; Bursevi adds marriage and intelligence; al-Alusi says they fended believers off. Ibn Atiyya notes kinship clouded evidence and the Prophet feared a public accusation if he killed them.
  2:14/q07/p2 To mock believers and Muhammad's Companions or ingratiate themselves with their leaders. — reasons: Al-Jishumi reports these two proposed motives from Abu Ali; the mockery may include flattery of those they consider foolish.
2:14/q08 What does خلوا mean here? [خَلَوْا۟ · meaning] (turns on: Whether khalaw denotes solitude, motion away, or mockery itself.)
  2:14/q08/p1 They were privately alone with their leaders. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabi equates constructions with bi, ila and maʿa; al-Tabrisi gives the same sense; al-Raghib, cited by al-Alusi, roots khalw in an empty place, space, or time.
  2:14/q08/p2 They went away, withdrew, or returned to their leaders. — reasons: Abu Malik says 'went away'; Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi cite al-Farazdaq's verse to explain the sense of turning or departure; al-Biqaʿi describes a private return.
  2:14/q08/p3 They privately mocked or recounted their mockery to the leaders. — reasons: Al-Razi reports al-Kashshaf's account; al-Alusi rejects an explicit 'mocked' sense for khala as unattested.
2:14/q09 Why is خلوا followed by إلى? [خَلَوْا۟ إِلَىٰ · grammar] (turns on: Whether إلى has its own directional force or stands in for مع or باء.)
  2:14/q09/p1 إلى keeps its endpoint sense: they turned or departed from believers toward their leaders. — reasons: Al-Tabari favors Kufan directionality because a preposition should retain its proper sense without proof; al-Wahidi, Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, al-Alusi and Ibn Ashur explain movement, goal of need, or customary refuge. Ibn Ashur says this makes the Muslim encounter brief; al-Alusi notes an omitted first object.
  2:14/q09/p2 إلى means مع, 'with,' through preposition substitution. — reasons: Al-Akhfash, al-Nadr, and others cite 3:52, 4:2, 2:187, 61:14, a proverb, and poetry; al-Tabari reports the analogy and other preposition shifts, with Yunus and a poet giving Arabic examples. Ibn Atiyya, al-Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, and al-Alusi call it weak or unsupported.
  2:14/q09/p3 إلى substitutes for باء in 'being alone with.' — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says the view is weak and against al-Khalil and Sibawayh; al-Samin attributes permissive substitution to Kufans and Basran resistance; al-Alusi finds no proof.
2:14/q10 Why choose إلى rather than باء with خلوا? [خَلَوْا۟ إِلَىٰ · rhetoric]
  2:14/q10/p1 إلى clarifies a private approach or need; باء can also suggest mockery. — reasons: A Basran grammarian, al-Akhfash, Makkī, al-Jishumi, and Abu Hayyan describe the ambiguity; al-Mawardi calls the wording more eloquent and literal. Al-Alusi disputes that khalā bi explicitly means mocking.
2:14/q11 How is the hamza at خلوا إلى read? [خَلَوْا۟ إِلَىٰ · readings] (turns on: Hamza realization and vowel transfer.)
  2:14/q11/p1 The hamza is fully pronounced and the preceding wāw remains still. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi assigns this to other readers; Abu Hayyan calls it the majority practice.
  2:14/q11/p2 The hamza is omitted and its vowel transferred to the wāw. — reasons: Al-Nahhas calls this a Medinan softening; al-Tabrisi calls it Hijazi, and Abu Hayyan attributes it to Warsh.
2:14/q12 How is خلوا formed? [خَلَوْا۟ · grammar]
  2:14/q12/p1 An earlier wāw becomes alif and is deleted before plural wāw. — reasons: Al-Samin derives خلووا and says the preceding fatḥa signals the deletion.
2:14/q13 What does shayṭān mean generally? [شَيَٰطِينِهِمْ · meaning] (turns on: The breadth of the word's application and the nature of jinn satan.)
  2:14/q13/p1 A rebellious, malicious being, human, jinn, or even animal. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabi cites 37:65, two prophetic reports, and Abu al-Najm's playful verse; al-Wahidi cites 6:112; al-Jishumi reports Abu Ubayda; al-Qurtubi says distance from good encompasses the groups; Elmalili recounts Umar calling a prancing horse a satan; Ibn Ashur distinguishes literal fiery jinn from figurative human corrupters. Asad and Raghib add evil impulses of the soul.
  2:14/q13/p2 A jinn satan also has an immaterial spiritual aspect and material evil forces. — reasons: Elmalili contrasts philosophers' solely immaterial account with Ahl al-Sunna's broader view.
2:14/q14 From what root and pattern is shayṭān derived? [شَيَٰطِينِهِمْ · meaning] (turns on: Root status of nūn, evidence from derived verbs and poetry, and possible borrowing.)
  2:14/q14/p1 From shatana, 'be far,' on fayʿāl with original nūn. — reasons: Al-Mawardi says far from good or far along evil's way; al-Layth, Abu Ali and Ibn Atiyya cite Sibawayh's tashayṭana and Umayya's shāṭin, while Ibn al-Jawzi adds al-Nabigha's shaṭūn. Basrans and Ibn Ashur give distance from mercy or Paradise. Ibn Ashur objects that tashayṭana may be formed from the noun itself and not settle the root.
  2:14/q14/p2 From shāṭa, 'burn' or 'perish,' on faʿlān with added nūn. — reasons: Al-Mawardi prefers burning as naming Satan for an eventual state; al-Aʿsha's yushīṭ and the cited warrior poem attest perishing. The Kufans include failure or burning anger; Elmalili notes implications for declension; Ibn Ashur says derivation alone does not determine declension and cites tanwīn at 15:17.
  2:14/q14/p3 It may be a fixed loanword from an earlier language. — reasons: Ibn Ashur notes similar ancient terms and reported Persian sīṭān; Elmalili reports Persian div and a claimed development into Western dieu with opposite meaning.
