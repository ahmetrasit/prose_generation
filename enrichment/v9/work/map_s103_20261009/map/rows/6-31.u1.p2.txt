6:31/q25 How can 'sāʾa mā yazirūn' be parsed? [سَآءَ مَا يَزِرُونَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether sāʾa functions as an ordinary verb, exclamation, or biʾsa-like blame.)
  6:31/q25/p1 A plain transitive past verb with mā as subject and an omitted object. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya cites a poem; Samīn says mā may be relative, verbal or indefinite and notes the Shaykh will not fix the poem to a single parse. Al-Ālūsī lists the same mā options.
  6:31/q25/p2 An exclamatory expression: how awful what they carry. — reasons: Samīn says this reading is fixed and intransitive, with ḍamma on the second radical; al-Ālūsī accepts it.
  6:31/q25/p3 An intensified verb of blame equivalent to biʾsa. — reasons: Zajjāj compares 7:177, Samīn refers to mā in 2:90; the Shaykh distinguishes its subject rules and clause structure, but Samīn says the distinction needs interpretation. Ibn ʿAṭiyya permits this beside a declarative reading.
6:31/q26 What do the burdens teach about the deniers' two losses? [يَٰحَسْرَتَنَا أَوْزَارَهُمْ · interpretation]
  6:31/q26/p1 They failed to gain the reward they could have prepared for, while earning punishment through sins. — reasons: Al-Rāzī pairs regret for omitted provision with burdens of committed wrong; Bursevī contrasts absent good deeds and physically borne sins; Elmalılı pairs loss of reward with enormous punishment.
6:31/q27 What inward reading is given to denial, the Hour, and burdens? [* · inward] (turns on: Whether the inward burden is embedded traits broadly or the self itself.)
  6:31/q27/p1 The veiled deny meeting the Real; minor resurrection exposes neglected work and attachments embedded in the soul. — reasons: Qāshānī says bodily love, sin and sensory habits become the burdens that obstruct aims and torment the self.
  6:31/q27/p2 The self and its very existence are the chief burden; repentance and effacement free the seeker by God's aid. — reasons: Bursevī cites Ḥāfiẓ against self-regard, a saying that the self cannot escape itself, and Tirmidhī on remembrance softening the heart while ego hardens it. Divine unity and following its people sustain spiritual life.
6:31/q28 What does the pilgrimage dream illustrate about release from burdens? [* · inward]
  6:31/q28/p1 Sound-hearted companionship with the holy benefits the seeker, as following and hearing the Prophet could have relieved deniers' burdens. — reasons: Bursevī tells ʿAlī ibn al-Muwaffaq's dream: after giving up his litter to walk among pilgrims, maidens washed his feet and counted him among them because he loved their company. God guides whom He wills.
6:31/q29 How does this verse connect with its surrounding passage? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the pictured encounter is tied to universal resurrection or each death.)
  6:31/q29/p1 It completes the portrait of people claiming worldly life alone, showing the afterlife loss their denial entails. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls the opening a fresh calamity report; Ibn ʿĀshūr says explicit naming makes their fate an independent statement; Ṭabāṭabāʾī links resurrection, gathering, the Hereafter and meeting God. Study Quran cites 6:29 and locates 4–31 among responses to Makkan excuses and earlier destroyed peoples; Iṣlāḥī contrasts Quran rejecters with afterlife deniers.
  6:31/q29/p2 The scene may portray each unbeliever's encounter with the afterlife after death. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu prefers this explanation: souls already dead see their error now and future dead will do so; the alternative treats prophetic past tense for future resurrection as a mark of certainty.
6:31/q30 What general pattern of human loss does the verse illustrate? [* · theology]
  6:31/q30/p1 Wrong paths defeat the goals for which the created order provides proper causes; apparent unjust gain ends in loss. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī compares trying to sustain life without eating and digestion. Belief in God and the afterlife and practical law are routes to happiness; shirk, seizure of property, violation of honor or life, neglect of worship and deceit damage connected causes in this life and next.
6:31/q31 What encounter with God is reported for a denier? [بِلِقَآءِ ٱللَّهِ · reports]
  6:31/q31/p1 God reminds the denier of His gifts, and the servant admits having forgotten God. — reasons: Ibn Abī Ḥātim transmits the report through Abū Hurayra.
6:31/q32 What is the clause structure around 'when they said'? [إِذَا قَالُوا۟ · grammar]
  6:31/q32/p1 'They said' answers the conditional 'when'. — reasons: Samīn and Ibn ʿĀshūr make the cry the apodosis; al-Ālūsī says the conditional is answered by 'O our remorse'.
6:31/q33 How does the preceding challenge 'Is this not the truth?' relate to denial here? [* · links]
  6:31/q33/p1 The challenge rebukes denial of resurrection and recompense, and the ensuing punishment answers their disbelief. — reasons: Kashshāf explains the previous question and punishment; Kur'an Yolu says after death the denier recognizes the error. Ibn ʿĀshūr says deferred warning in this life yields immediate consequences in the next.
6:31/q34 What do other terms in the adjacent verse mean? [* · links]
  6:31/q34/p1 ʿAql, nahy and ḥijā denote restraint or holding fast, showing how reason restrains wrongdoing. — reasons: Ṭabrisī says nahy may be verbal noun or plural and refers to water held in a low place; ḥijā derives from lingering and is supported by a verse and al-Azharī's taḥajjaytu. Al-Aṣmaʿī's Maʿqala holds water, as medicine restrains the belly.
