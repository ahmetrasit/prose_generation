6:82/q08 What does it mean that they are guided? [وَهُم مُّهْتَدُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether guidance names truth and religion now, entrance to Paradise, or both worldly and final guidance.)
  6:82/q08/p1 They follow truth and God's religion. — reasons: Ibn Abbas says directed to God's religion; Tabarsi says judged to follow truth; Bursawi and Al-Alusi contrast them with all others in error. Ibn Ishaq adds proof through knowledge and uprightness; Islahi says reliance on God in all affairs rather than deities.
  6:82/q08/p2 They are guided to Paradise or the path ensuring safety from eternal punishment. — reasons: Jishumi and Tabarsi report Paradise as a specific gloss; Al-Alusi reports the secure path.
  6:82/q08/p3 They are guided in this worldly life. — reasons: Qurtubi specifies the present world; Ibn Kathir extends guidance to this life and the next.
  6:82/q08/p4 They are guided both in this life and the next. — reasons: Ibn Kathir states both horizons.
6:82/q09 What emphasis does the wording give to security and guidance? [أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ لَهُمُ ٱلْأَمْنُ وَهُم مُّهْتَدُونَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the pronoun in 'they are guided' functions as a separator or the subject of a second sentence.)
  6:82/q09/p1 The demonstrative and nominal clauses strongly establish the promised predicates for the described believers. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says 'those' points to their description as the basis of entitlement, comparing 2:5; the lam marks exclusive settled possession, stronger than merely 'they are safe'. Tabatabai notes several linked nominal clauses.
  6:82/q09/p2 If 'they' is a separator, guidance is exclusive to the monotheists described. — reasons: Ibn Ashur cites 2:55 and 9:104 for the construction.
  6:82/q09/p3 If 'they are guided' is its own nominal sentence, it adds guidance without grammatical exclusivity. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says this makes coordination smoother, with analogies at 64:1 and 57:2.
6:82/q10 Who speaks the answer to Abraham's question? [* · referent] (turns on: Whether this clause is quoted speech continuing the exchange or God's independent verdict, and whether the idolaters could credibly concede Abraham's case.)
  6:82/q10/p1 God declares the ruling that resolves Abraham's dispute. — reasons: Tabari prefers this: idolaters would not concede the monotheism they opposed. Other sources call it a divine verdict or independent resumption; Al-Alusi says a resumption lacks syntactic attachment despite semantic relation and answers Ibn Hisham's objection. Ibn Ashur says it confirms Abraham. Zajjaj allows it. Abu Hayyan also reports God commanding Abraham to say it or the Prophet to answer.
  6:82/q10/p2 Abraham answers his own rhetorical question. — reasons: Ibn Abbas compares this to a teacher who poses and answers a question; Abu Hayyan calls it the apparent speaker, and Ibn Ashur says its undeniable answer silences opponents. Zajjaj allows the attribution. Razi calls it Abraham's answer defining perfected faith and action.
  6:82/q10/p3 Abraham's people answer and inadvertently concede his proof. — reasons: Ibn Jurayj says their answer became evidence against them. Muqatil says they conceded. Ibn Ashur objects that their continued obstinacy and attempted burning make concession unlikely, and Tabari likewise objects that idolaters would not admit monotheism.
6:82/q11 What happened when early Muslims heard this verse? [* · reports]
  6:82/q11/p1 They feared ordinary self-wronging disqualified them; the Prophet explained that the wrong was shirk and cited Luqman 31:13. — reasons: Ibn Masud transmits the recurrent account in Bukhari, Muslim, Ahmad and others; Tirmidhi calls his route hasan sahih. Some routes say clarification was revealed. Ubayy reassured Umar, and Salman reassured Zayd ibn Suhan, with the same explanation.
6:82/q12 Which newly converted people were counted among the secure? [* · reports] (turns on: The reported person's identity and the circumstances of conversion and death.)
  6:82/q12/p1 A traveler or Bedouin accepted Islam, died in an accident after little work, and was declared secure and guided. — reasons: Jarir's versions mention a camel stumbling in a rodent hole, hunger, fruit from Paradise or houris, and niche burial; Ibn Abbas's version describes a destitute man who left family and possessions to learn from the Prophet. The details vary while the convert's prompt accidental death and great reward agree.
  6:82/q12/p2 A former enemy who had killed Muslims converted, fought for them, and died. — reasons: The man asked whether Islam would benefit him despite his past. The narrator thought the verse was revealed about him.
  6:82/q12/p3 A new Muslim fought and was martyred after a question about the verse. — reasons: Ibrahim al-Taymi tells this as an application by the Prophet; Tabatabai notes a similar report.
  6:82/q12/p4 The Prophet himself was told that he belonged among those described. — reasons: Ibn Kathir cites Ibn Mardawayh's report following revelation.
6:82/q13 What conduct is associated with the secure and guided? [* · reports] (turns on: Whether the report describes practical traits of the secure or the monotheistic basis of their worship.)
  6:82/q13/p1 They thank God for gifts, endure deprivation, seek forgiveness after wrong, and pardon others' wrongs. — reasons: The four traits recur in reports from Ibn Umar and others; variant wording speaks of patience in trials and gratitude in ease.
  6:82/q13/p2 They worship God alone and associate nothing with Him. — reasons: Kuranyolu quotes the Prophet on God's right over His servants; Muqatil and Ibn Kathir describe exclusive worship. Islahi stresses trust in God in all affairs.
6:82/q14 What follows for believers who commit major sins? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether faith can coexist with grave sin, whether 'wrongdoing' means shirk or sin generally, and whether lack of assurance entails certain punishment.)
  6:82/q14/p1 A believer may sin gravely; absence of shirk assures eventual escape from the Fire, while lesser punishment remains possible. — reasons: Wahidi explicitly infers ultimate security for one dying without shirk. Razi's school treats faith as compatible with major sin. Tabarsi answers al-Balkhi that the verse's implication does not itself prove security for an unrepentant major sinner and other evidence makes him insecure. Al-Alusi says denying certainty of security does not prove certainty of punishment; Tabatabai holds shirk is unforgivable unlike other sins.
  6:82/q14/p2 A grave sinner lacks the verse's security and faces the threat of punishment. — reasons: The Mu'tazilites infer two conditions, faith plus no wrongdoing. Al-Balkhi argues shirk alone would give the unrepentant major sinner security and conflicts with opposition to irja; Zamakhshari appeals to the verb 'mix' and 9:36. Al-Alusi reports their rejection of the solitary hadith as unable to override categorical proof.
  6:82/q14/p3 Security varies by the spiritual level of the person and the wrong that obstructs faith at that level. — reasons: Tabatabai begins with shirk at monotheism's threshold, then major sins under law, then disliked acts, omitted recommendations and inward obstacles to nearness. Unintentional mistakes do not block faith. The immediate Abrahamic argument still addresses shirk.
  6:82/q14/p4 The verse assures only a particular sinless group; other monotheist sinners remain subject to God's will. — reasons: Tabari reports this solution to the general wrongdoing interpretation, identifying Abraham or the Emigrants as proposed groups and leaving pardon or punishment of other sinners open.
