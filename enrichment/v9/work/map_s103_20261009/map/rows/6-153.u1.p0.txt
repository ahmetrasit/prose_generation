## THE CURRENT MAP
6:153/q01 How is the opening particle of 'and this is My path' read? [وَأَنَّ · readings] (turns on: The transmitted vowels and nūn forms alter whether the clause can be attached to what precedes or opens independently.)
  6:153/q01/p1 With open hamza and doubled nūn. — reasons: Ibn Mujāhid, al-Fārisī, al-Ṭabrisī, al-Nashr, and Ibn ʿĀshūr assign it to most readers, including Ibn Kathīr, Nāfiʿ, ʿĀṣim, and Abū ʿAmr; Ibn ʿĀshūr also names Abū Jaʿfar. Al-Ālūsī also rehearses this tripartite reading as part of the preceding command's syntax.
  6:153/q01/p2 With open hamza and light or quiescent nūn. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀmir is named throughout; several sources add Yaʿqūb, while al-Qurṭubī adds Ibn Abī Isḥāq. Sources differ whether the light particle is reduced emphatic inna or maṣdariyya.
  6:153/q01/p3 With kasra and doubled nūn, beginning a fresh assertion. — reasons: Ḥamza and al-Kisāʾī are consistently named; al-Aʿmash, ʿAlī and Khalaf also appear in some reports. Al-Biqāʿī instead attributes a light kasra reading to Ibn ʿĀmir and Yaʿqūb.
  6:153/q01/p4 With kasra and light nūn. — reasons: Jishumī attributes this form to Ibn ʿĀmir and Yaʿqūb; al-Biqāʿī likewise reports kasra for them, against the usual fatḥa attribution.
  6:153/q01/p5 Without the particle: 'and this is My path'. — reasons: Al-Aʿmash and the codex of Ibn Masʿūd/ʿAbd Allāh are reported; al-Samīn says it supports the independent-sentence analysis.
6:153/q02 What syntactic relation does 'and this is My path' have to its context? [وَأَنَّ هَٰذَا فَٱتَّبِعُوهُ · grammar] (turns on: The open or closed particle, an implied causal lām, and the point of coordination with 6:151 decide the parse.)
  6:153/q02/p1 It gives the reason for the command: follow it because it is straight. — reasons: Al-Khalīl, Sībawayh, Abū ʿAlī, al-Zamakhsharī and others supply a causal lām. Comparisons include 106:1, 23:52 and 72:18. Al-Fārisī and Abū Ḥayyān discuss whether the following fāʾ would then be extra; al-Samīn says Sībawayh disallows that predicate fāʾ. Al-Ālūsī answers a double-conjunction objection by a fronted object or an omitted verb, citing 17:110 and 72:18.
  6:153/q02/p2 It is an object of 'I recite': I recite that this is My path. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ, al-Kisāʾī, Abū al-Baqāʾ and Ibn ʿĀshūr connect it to the recitation beginning at 6:151; al-Ālūsī calls coordination with 'come' very unlikely.
  6:153/q02/p3 It coordinates with the earlier divine prohibition, charge, or its prepositional object. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ makes it genitive after the charge and coordinates with bihi; al-Ḥawfī attaches it to an lā tushrikū; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī attach it to the prior prohibition. Abū al-Baqāʾ objects that the preposition must be repeated and that God would be commanding the path's straightness; al-Samīn replies that the preposition can be understood after a long clause and that commanding its straightness means forbidding departure.
  6:153/q02/p4 With kasra, it is a fresh sentence, and the following fāʾ links the command. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās, Abū ʿAlī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī, and others read it as an independent declaration; the kasra still gives a reason to follow. Some connect the assertion to the Prophet's recitation.
  6:153/q02/p5 The light form has an implicit pronoun of the matter. — reasons: Abū ʿAlī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabrisī and al-Samīn explain the omitted pronoun by al-Aʿshā's poetry and other examples, including 10:10. Al-Naḥḥās allows nominative or accusative; Qirāʾāt-Er instead calls the light particle maṣdariyya.
  6:153/q02/p6 The previous explanatory construction is itself disputed. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says a nominalizing 'that you do not associate' appears to name nonassociation among forbidden things, while an explanatory particle strains to include later positive commands. He rejects a relative-clause solution ascribed to Sībawayh. Al-Zamakhsharī implies prohibitions opposite the positive commands; Abū Ḥayyān supplies an omitted 'what He commanded' or coordinates with 'I recite.' Imruʾ al-Qays's line permits an imperative beside a prohibition.
6:153/q03 How is the consonant of ṣirāṭī pronounced? [صِرَٰطِى · readings] (turns on: The transmitted dialectal or phonological form of the initial consonant.)
  6:153/q03/p1 With ṣād. — reasons: Ibn Mujāhid and later readers make it the reading of those other than the named sīn and intermediate-sound readers.
  6:153/q03/p2 With sīn, sirāṭī. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr and Ibn ʿĀmir are named; Qirāʾāt-Er specifies Qunbul from Ibn Kathīr and calls it a phonological variant of the same root.
  6:153/q03/p3 With an intermediate ṣād–zāy sound. — reasons: Ḥamza's form has variant transmissions in Ibn Mujāhid; Qirāʾāt-Er names Khalaf from Ḥamza and describes ishmām of zāy.
6:153/q04 How is the possessive yāʾ of ṣirāṭī read? [صِرَٰطِى · readings] (turns on: Transmission of the possessive yāʾ vowel, with the same sense in both readings.)
  6:153/q04/p1 The yāʾ is quiescent. — reasons: Most readers leave it still. Ibn Mujāhid distinguishes Nāfiʿ's treatment of this yāʾ from most other added yāʾs and records the related cases of maḥyāy and wajhī; the surah has forty added yāʾs, eight disputed.
  6:153/q04/p2 The yāʾ is opened or vocalized. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀmir is repeatedly named. Ibn Mujāhid also gives his moving yāʾ in maḥyāy and wajhī; al-Nashr and Qirāʾāt-Er specify fatḥa, with no change in meaning.
6:153/q05 What does 'this, My straight path' designate? [هَٰذَا صِرَٰطِى · referent] (turns on: The demonstrative's scope ranges from immediately preceding laws to Islam and the entire sūra; inward or imamic applications identify a particular way of traversing it.)
  6:153/q05/p1 Islam, God's upright religion, including Muhammad's revealed law and Sunnah. — reasons: Muqātil, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Dāmghānī, al-Qurṭubī and others identify it with the religion; al-Dāmghānī cites 6:126, Ibn ʿĀshūr 1:6 and 6:161, and Study Quran 5:15–16. Ibn ʿAṭiyya prefers the whole prophetic law to al-Ṭabarī's narrower commandments.
  6:153/q05/p2 The commands and prohibitions in the two preceding verses, or all ten duties. — reasons: Muqātil is cited for this; al-Rāzī says these summarize the entire prophetic teaching. Al-Ālūsī distinguishes the source and calls the straight path comprehensive. Elmalılı includes preceding tawḥīd; Kur'an Yolu calls the final duty encompassing.
  6:153/q05/p3 The Qurʾān, revelation, or proofs. — reasons: Al-Māwardī and Ibn al-Jawzī record Qurʾān as an alternative to law or Islam. Jishumī adds proofs and the absence of contradiction; al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī stress following revelation and proof.
  6:153/q05/p4 The whole sūra, especially its tawḥīd, prophecy and law. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī report this scope; al-Bursawī uses the sūra's content to explain the causal clause.
  6:153/q05/p5 Abraham's upright monotheistic religion restored by Muhammad. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās calls it the ḥanīf religion and best religion. Iṣlāḥī links it to Abraham's basic teaching, Moses' law, Quraysh's neglected moral duties and Muhammad's restoration after Israelite and Ishmaelite innovations.
  6:153/q05/p6 The divinely guided Imams, particularly ʿAlī, as an application. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī labels Abū Jaʿfar's identification an application; reports through Shiʿi and Sunni routes name ʿAlī. He connects kinship-love in 42:23 to seeking a way to the Lord in 25:57.
  6:153/q05/p7 The heart's route to God, without the self's desire or share. — reasons: Jaʿfar b. Muḥammad speaks of turning from everything other than God; Sahl excludes the self's desire. Al-Qāshānī describes balanced virtues, unity, annihilation and subsistence through divine attributes.
  6:153/q05/p8 Every path ultimately begins from God and turns to Him. — reasons: Al-Bursawī cites a Persian tafsīr, Qūnawī's account of God's encompassing existence or knowledge, and a Persian verse that every road crosses the Beloved's street.
6:153/q06 Whose is the possessive in 'My path'? [صِرَٰطِى · referent] (turns on: Whether the speaking 'I' is God or the Prophet, and where the shift of person falls.)
  6:153/q06/p1 God's; the first person marks divine authorship and certainty. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 42:52–53 and says the later 'His path' shifts person; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports this as the majority view. Al-Ālūsī says God institutes it and the Prophet follows and invites to it.
  6:153/q06/p2 The Prophet's; the path he teaches is God's path. — reasons: Abū al-Baqāʾ takes the speaker as Muhammad. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī prefers this since the Prophet delivers the commands and says the shift of person occurs at 'His path,' not 'My path'; he cites 1:5–6. Ibn ʿĀshūr reports this but says mere prophetic possession would not persuade disbelievers.
  6:153/q06/p3 A variant wording says 'the path of your Lord'. — reasons: The codices of ʿAbd Allāh and Ubayy are cited by al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī; al-Ālūsī notes plural and singular address variants.
6:153/q07 What does calling the way a straight path convey? [صِرَٰطِى مُسْتَقِيمًۭا · rhetoric] (turns on: The road image explains directness, destination, and contrast with branching deviation.)
  6:153/q07/p1 A clear, broad, direct road to Paradise and well-being, free of crookedness. — reasons: Al-Māwardī and al-Bursawī explain the path metaphor by its destination; Jishumī contrasts Satan's route to Hell. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the straight road is visible, easy and quick; Ibn Qutayba cites an Arab road saying. Bursevī compares law to a bridge finer than hair and sharper than a sword. ʿAṭāʾ from Ibn ʿAbbās pictures a traveler leaving the main road for branching tracks.
  6:153/q07/p2 A coherent, balanced religion rooted in human nature. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites 30:30 and says desire-driven routes lack internal coherence; al-Qāshānī sees the balanced middle of virtue after the self's annihilation.
