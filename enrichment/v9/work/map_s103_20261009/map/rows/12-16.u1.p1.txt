12:16/q11 Were the brothers' tears genuine? [يَبْكُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether visible crying represented real remorse over Joseph or a performance supporting the wolf story.)
  12:16/q11/p1 Their weeping was staged to make a false account seem true. — reasons: Ibn Abī Ḥātim's al-Ḥasan calls them lying sinners. Al-Wāḥidī says they used it to seem innocent of the alleged wolf attack. Al-Ṭabrisī, al-Bayḍāwī, Bursevī and al-Ālūsī say the crying was feigned; Bursevī's Persian gloss calls it deliberate, and his quotation from the Mathnawī says the displayed grief concealed envy and malice. Al-Biqāʿī defines crying as tears flowing in sadness yet says that tears can be feigned. Al-Ālūsī says liars imitate sorrow, and another reported explanation says they hid joy at success in darkness. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says they wept to disguise their plot and gain trust.
  12:16/q11/p2 They may have sincerely regretted harming Joseph while hiding the reason for their grief. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī allows genuine regret, though they blamed a wolf when speaking to Jacob.
12:16/q12 Do tears establish that a person's claim is true? [يَبْكُونَ · interpretation]
  12:16/q12/p1 Tears do not establish truth, and a judge must decide by the prescribed standard or truth. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī reports Mujālid from al-Shaʿbī: Shurayḥ answered a crying woman by citing the brothers. Al-Kashshāf, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Rāzī, Bursevī and al-Ālūsī recount forms of this court example; al-Rāzī calls the brothers oppressive liars, while al-Kashshāf and Bursevī state the duty to follow the prescribed Sunna. Al-Ṭabrisī and al-Biqāʿī state the general inference. Al-Nasafī and Abū Ḥayyān transmit al-Aʿmash's warning not to believe a weeper; al-Ālūsī gives his stronger quip that even one's father should not be believed. Al-Qushayrī and the Study Quran report that a complete hypocrite can control tears at will. Al-Ālūsī cites a couplet proposing tears crossing the cheek as a way to distinguish genuine from imitated weeping.
12:16/q13 What spiritual condition is shown by a liar's ability to weep at will? [يَبْكُونَ · inward]
  12:16/q13/p1 It is a sign of divine abandonment or complete hypocrisy. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī calls the enabling of a liar's voluntary tears God's abandonment and reports that fully developed hypocrisy lets a person command his eyes; the Study Quran repeats the hypocrisy report.
12:16/q14 What is omitted in the narrative transition to the brothers' return? [وَجَآءُوٓ · rhetoric] (turns on: The two reconstructions supply different unspoken stages of the surrounding story.)
  12:16/q14/p1 Jacob granted the brothers' request and sent Joseph with them. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says this connective clause is omitted because context supplies it.
  12:16/q14/p2 The brothers arrived without Joseph, Jacob asked about him, and they gave their wolf claim. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān supplies this unspoken exchange from the context after their arrival.
12:16/q15 How is the answer to the preceding 'when they took him away' construction analyzed? [* · grammar] (turns on: Whether an answer is omitted or an extra wāw permits a later explicit clause to serve as the answer.)
  12:16/q15/p1 The answer is omitted and recoverable from context. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī says the grave plot they intended is understood after the oath; he reports the Basran rejection of an inserted wāw for lack of evidence or analogy. Abū Ḥayyān reports several Basran estimates for the omitted answer, citing discussions of 37:103 and 39:73.
  12:16/q15/p2 The answer is explicit in the later speech or 'We inspired him,' with wāw treated as additional. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī reports the Kūfan analysis of the wāw in 'and they agreed' as inserted and says Kūfans cite poetry; Abū Ḥayyān reports an explicit-answer analysis allowing an extra conjunction in Kūfan usage. Basrans argue against the insertion.
12:16/q16 How does the following speech continue without a conjunction? [* · rhetoric]
  12:16/q16/p1 An implied question, 'What did they say?', is answered by 'O our father.' — reasons: Al-Burhān says the apparent sequence is a resumption answering the hearer's imagined question after their arrival crying.
12:16/q17 What other nearby circumstantial clauses and attachments are noted? [* · grammar]
  12:16/q17/p1 'We were racing' is circumstantial, while 'they did not realize' may attach to the promise or revelation. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī gives both possible attachments for the latter clause while parsing the neighboring account.
12:16/q18 How can recoverable omission and oblique expression serve the passage's style? [* · rhetoric]
  12:16/q18/p1 A recoverable object can be omitted for graceful, balanced, deliberately indirect speech. — reasons: Ibn Jinnī illustrates object omission with 'two women watering' in 28:23, where their camels are understood, and with poetry. He cites Dhū al-Rumma on speech avoiding both rambling and excessive brevity. He interprets 'the best speech is laḥnan' as deliberate obliqueness rather than grammatical error, supported by the Prophetic phrase 'more adept in his case.'
12:16/q19 What wider point does the Qur'anic Joseph story illustrate? [* · interpretation]
  12:16/q19/p1 God directs human affairs beyond what people can fathom. — reasons: Asad contrasts the Qur'anic story with Genesis' romantic account and cites 2:216: people can dislike what benefits them and love what harms them.
