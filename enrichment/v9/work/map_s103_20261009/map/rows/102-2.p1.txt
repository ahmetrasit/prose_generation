[IBNATIYYA:102:2/r1] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · قوم | reports | One group says people kept boasting of their fathers and predecessors, counting the dead and their decayed bones among their accomplishments.
[IBNATIYYA:102:2/r2] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | holds | Another explanation is that people died and physically visited their graves, allowing competition over numbers to consume their lifespans.

[IBNATIYYA:102:2/r7] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | holds | Ibn ʿAṭiyya criticizes people who turn graves into displays by piling stones and marble, raising and ornamenting them, or building mausoleums.

[RAZI:102:1/r20] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Razi sees the grave-visiting wording as matching a past rivalry; the rebuke asks what it would profit people even if they were more numerous.
[RAZI:102:1/r21] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Visiting graves means going to a place, especially to soften the heart and remove love of worldly life; here grave-visitation perversely results from hard hearts and absorption in worldly love.
[RAZI:102:1/r22] RAZI, d. 606 · رسول الله | reports | The Prophet says he had forbidden grave visits and then permits them, since visiting graves is a reminder.
[RAZI:102:1/r24] RAZI, d. 606 · author | reports | On the wealth interpretation, visiting graves is a euphemism for dying; the verse says that pursuit of wealth occupied people until death arrived.
[RAZI:102:1/r25] RAZI, d. 606 · جرير | reports | Jarīr's verse uses visiting graves to mean that Abū Mālik died, serving as poetic evidence for the euphemism.
[RAZI:102:1/r26] RAZI, d. 606 · author | reports | An objection is that a visitor leaves after a while whereas the dead remain in their graves; Razi answers that a visitor must eventually depart and grave-dwellers likewise leave for the place of reckoning.
[RAZI:102:1/r27] RAZI, d. 606 · author | reports | To explain the past tense for a future death, one possibility is that the address concerns people nearing death from old age, already standing at the brink of the grave.
[RAZI:102:1/r28] RAZI, d. 606 · author | reports | Another answer reports the fate of earlier people as admonition to their successors, treating it as though it were news of the current audience because they follow the same way.
[RAZI:102:1/r29] RAZI, d. 606 · أبو مسلم | reports | Abū Muslim says God addresses unbelievers with this sura on Judgment Day, when their visits to graves have already occurred.
[RAZI:102:1/r30] RAZI, d. 606 · author | reports | A third view says greed for wealth prevented people from paying financial obligations until death, when they finally bequeathed money for zakat and pilgrimage.
[RAZI:102:1/r31] RAZI, d. 606 · author | reports | A fourth view says rivalry turns hearts to stone, leaving only the small amount of religious feeling associated with visiting graves; Razi compares this with the rebuke that people give little thanks. | mentions 7:10
[RAZI:102:1/r35] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Read with the following grave-visitation clause, the verse means that rivalry made people forget the grave until they actually visited it.
[RAZI:102:2/r1] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Ilhāʾ is turning someone toward diversion; lahw is turning aside toward what desire calls for, thereby neglecting something else.
[RAZI:102:2/r10] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | The virtuous seek bodily health as a means to gain enduring spiritual goods through knowledge and action.
[RAZI:102:2/r11] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Pursuing wealth, rank, helpers, and relatives privileges the lowest goods over knowledge and action, so it reverses the proper order and is blameworthy.
[RAZI:102:2/r12] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | The condemned rivalry includes competition in numbers, wealth, status, relatives, allies, armies, and worldly pleasures generally.
