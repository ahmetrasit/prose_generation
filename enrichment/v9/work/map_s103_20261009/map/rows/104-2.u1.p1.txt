  104:2/q10/p2 Admiration of amassed wealth makes the slanderer imagine himself superior and belittle others. — reasons: Al-Rāzī and Bursawī explain why this vice is singled out. Al-Biqāʿī says a culture that boasts of profit calls it success, while the verse exposes his loss; Ibn al-Zubayr links his imagined completeness and faultfinding to 103:2. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī links perceived self-sufficiency and tyranny to 96:6–7; Quṭb portrays a recurring contemptuous type.
  104:2/q10/p3 Trust in wealth to ward off death, judgment or hardship is delusion. — reasons: Quṭb says the hoarder thinks money secures life and evades God's decree; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says brief bodily needs and endless hope drive the illusion. Corpus Coranicum contrasts this hoarding with 90:6's showy spending, both seeking self-made immortality. Asad reads it as a safeguard.
  104:2/q10/p4 The obsession and ridicule reveal a degraded character whom the warning and punishment answer. — reasons: Quṭb says the warning defends believers and pairs arrogance with the crushing Fire, reaching the heart, enclosure and pillars; harsh sound and an awe-inspiring question intensify it. He sees the Qur'an strengthening believers and striking opponents.
104:2/q11 What inward lesson is drawn from collecting and counting wealth? [ٱلَّذِى جَمَعَ مَالًۭا وَعَدَّدَهُۥ · inward] (turns on: The notes draw different spiritual lessons from wealth dependence and counting.)
  104:2/q11/p1 Wealth, status, kin or even obedience confer no true richness; relying on God alone does. — reasons: Al-Sulamī's anonymous sayings call the money-rich poor, status-rich contemptible, obedience-rich bankrupt and kin-rich lowly. Al-Qushayrī repeats dependence on wealth, rank and works as poverty or bankruptcy and says reliance on God gives nobility.
  104:2/q11/p2 Accumulating, loving and hoarding wealth reveal spiritual ignorance, hypocrisy and unbelief. — reasons: An anonymous saying reported by al-Sulamī assigns these three diagnoses respectively; al-Tustarī says the miser makes wealth a servant of worldly life.
  104:2/q11/p3 Ignorance is the root vice, and entrenched immersion in it leads to punishment that overwhelms the heart. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī calls ignorance a vice of the royal faculty and says the immersed merit eternal punishment; he also says counting wealth falsely assumes it can withstand calamity.
104:2/q12 What episode is reported behind the counting and condemnation? [ٱلَّذِى جَمَعَ مَالًۭا وَعَدَّدَهُۥ · reports] (turns on: These are separate reported encounters; the notes do not say they describe the same event.)
  104:2/q12/p1 The man boasts that his wealth cannot be exhausted, is warned its Giver can take it, inventories it, then boasts to Muhammad; the sūra is revealed. — reasons: Muqātil reports the oath to distribute wealth through Quraysh, the warning of poverty, the inventory of gold, silver, land, gardens and slaves, and a boast that even as bread it would not run out.
  104:2/q12/p2 Al-Ḥasan rebukes a wealthy visitor who saves thousands for hardship rather than helping others. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf reports al-Ḥasan asking why the money ransoms no one and is given to no generous person; the visitor says it is for hardship, and al-Ḥasan predicts an ungrateful heir and an unforgiving Lord.
104:2/q13 How large was al-Akhnas's fortune in the reports? [مَالًۭا · reports] (turns on: The reports give different numbers without a stated criterion for choosing one.)
  104:2/q13/p1 Four thousand dinars. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī preserve this report as one of two amounts.
  104:2/q13/p2 Ten thousand dinars. — reasons: The same sources give this competing figure.
104:2/q14 How does this verse connect to surrounding passages and Qur'anic themes? [* · links] (turns on: The notes compare the verse with different neighboring and broader scriptural passages.)
  104:2/q14/p1 The wealth hoarder contrasts with al-ʿAṣr's call to truth, patience and recognition of human loss. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī's Ibn al-Zubayr cites 103:2; al-Iṣlāḥī cites 103:3 and says hoarders taunt those who press duties.
  104:2/q14/p2 Other Qur'anic passages condemn hoarding, withholding and mockery of givers. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī cites 50:25 or 68:12 and 70:18; Ibn Kathīr cites 70:18; Study Quran cites 9:34–35's branding of hoarded gold and silver. Al-Iṣlāḥī reads 9:79 as hypocrites mocking poor donors while giving nothing.
  104:2/q14/p3 The sūra's woe and vice catalogue resemble other Qur'anic warnings and prophetic woe speeches. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum cites 107:4–7, 83:1–6, 83:10–11, 52:11–12, 51:60 and Micah 2:1–3, highlighting the wealth theme and rhetorical question in 83:1–6.
  104:2/q14/p4 The sūra condemns the hoarder in a Meccan nine-verse warning. — reasons: Quṭb identifies its place and length; Study Quran states its focus on torment for those who hoard in worldly life.
104:2/q15 Why render the perfect verbs as present actions? [جَمَعَ وَعَدَّدَهُۥ · grammar]
  104:2/q15/p1 Their perfect form is aspectual and describes a continuing type, warranting a German present tense. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum cites 96:1–2 for comparable aspectual use.
104:2/q16 Why might a translation repeat the opening woe at this verse? [* · rhetoric]
  104:2/q16/p1 It treats the attitude in verses 2–3 as a separate blameworthy object beside the two kinds named in verse 1. — reasons: Asad explains his inserted repetition on this basis.
104:2/q17 What is reported about the seven named readings and their preservation? [* · sciences]
  104:2/q17/p1 The seven named readings are sound choices of reciters within the ʿUthmānic codex tradition, not the Companions' seven aḥruf. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr transmits al-Qurṭubī citing al-Dāwūdī and Ibn Abī Ṣafra; reciters allowed one another's readings, choosing what seemed apt, and regional Muslims relied on soundly transmitted written documentation, fulfilling preservation.
104:2/q18 Who was Abū Zayd named among the collectors of the Qur'an? [* · sciences] (turns on: Anas's kinship and tribal claim and whether the named collector appears separately in al-Shaʿbī's list; Ibn Ḥajar finds an explicit report for Qays ibn al-Sakan.)
  104:2/q18/p1 Qays ibn al-Sakan of Banū ʿAdī ibn al-Najjār. — reasons: Ibn Ḥajar prefers the Ibn Abī Dāwūd report; Anas calls him his maternal uncle, says he died without descendants and Anas inherited him. Ibn Abī Dāwūd gives his lineage, ʿAqaba and Badr participation, and near-Prophetic death.
  104:2/q18/p2 Saʿd ibn ʿUbayd ibn al-Nuʿmān of Banū ʿAmr ibn ʿAwf. — reasons: Al-ʿAskarī says Saʿd was the only Aws collector; Ibn Ḥabīb lists him as collecting in the Prophet's life. The identification is challenged because Anas was Khazrajī, called Abū Zayd an uncle, and al-Shaʿbī listed Saʿd separately.
  104:2/q18/p3 Qays ibn Abī Saʿṣaʿa, a Khazrajī also called Abū Zayd. — reasons: Ibn Ḥajar offers this possibility because it fits Anas's kin claim.
  104:2/q18/p4 Saʿd ibn al-Mundhir ibn Aws ibn Zuhayr, another Khazrajī collector. — reasons: Ibn Ḥajar names him but finds no explicit evidence of the kunya Abū Zayd.
  104:2/q18/p5 Thābit, Aws or Muʿādh. — reasons: Al-Suyūṭī lists these further proposed names without grounds.
104:2/q19 When were Qur'anic verses and surahs divided and ordered? [* · sciences] (turns on: Whether the complete surah sequence was fixed by revelation or partly arranged during codex production; the notes distinguish verse placement from surah order.)
  104:2/q19/p1 Surahs and verse placement were established during the Prophet's lifetime. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās reports instructions to place a revealed verse in a named surah; Ibn ʿĀshūr cites known surah sizes, prophetic prayer recitations and marriage for memorized surahs. Al-Dānī says Gabriel showed placements, and Zayd reports arranging sheets before the Prophet, though al-Bayhaqī restricts this to verses.
  104:2/q19/p2 The Prophet fixed the order of surahs. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī lists this possibility; al-Dānī attributes verse and surah placement to Gabriel.
  104:2/q19/p3 Some surah order was set in the Prophet's life, with remaining order fixed or preserved by Zayd and ʿUthmān. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya transmits al-Bāqillānī's strong view of Zayd and ʿUthmān; Ibn ʿĀshūr finds long opening surahs and Mufaṣṣal already placed, the rest uncertain. Zayd and ʿUthmān sought to preserve known prophetic recitation.
104:2/q20 How was the Qur'an collected into sheets and a codex? [* · sciences]
  104:2/q20/p1 Abū Bakr collected separate sheets, which passed to ʿUmar and Ḥafṣa; ʿUthmān made a codex and sent copies. — reasons: Ibn ʿUmar gives the succession of sheets; a report through ʿUmāra ibn Ghaziyya says Zayd wrote on leather and palm stalks under Abū Bakr and joined them under ʿUmar. Ibn ʿĀshūr explains the name 'sheets' and later provincial copies; al-Bukhārī's al-Fatḥ judges a single codex under Abū Bakr sounder.
104:2/q21 Why divide a book into surahs? [* · rhetoric]
  104:2/q21/p1 Division is more elegant and renews the reader's energy by marking progress. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr transmits al-Zamakhsharī's analogy of a traveler passing a mile or league and cites 2:23.
104:2/q22 Who else may gather around and count the wealth? [جَمَعَ وَعَدَّدَهُۥ · referent]
  104:2/q22/p1 The slanderer's poorer companions may gather around him and count his wealth while aspiring to acquire their own through slander. — reasons: Elmalılı describes many in the humaza-lumaza circle as owning little, clustering around the wealthy man and counting his assets.
