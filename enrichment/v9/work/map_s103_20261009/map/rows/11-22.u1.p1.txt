11:22/q04 Who are 'they' in this judgment? [أَنَّهُمْ · referent]
  11:22/q04/p1 The disbelievers just described, including those who denied the afterlife and relied on fabricated objects of worship. — reasons: Muqatil identifies them as these unbelievers. Al-Biqa'i calls them afterlife deniers. Ibn Ashur connects the class to 11:18 and to those who lost themselves by failing to use guidance, citing 6:12; he says their claims included idol intercession and relief from hardship, with 46:28 illustrating the mockery of idols 'straying' from worshipers. Islahi says they squandered their abilities on false worship; Study Quran says fabricated idols, ideas and beliefs forsake them, citing 6:24, 6:54, 10:30, 16:87 and 28:75.
11:22/q05 What comparison does 'in the afterlife, the greatest losers' imply? [فِى ٱلْءَاخِرَةِ ٱلْأَخْسَرُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the superlative compares them with other afterlife inhabitants or compares their enduring afterlife loss with their worldly loss; al-Tabataba'i favors the first from the verse's apparent restriction.)
  11:22/q05/p1 Among the inhabitants of the afterlife, they are the most ruined, with no prospect of salvation while they persist in defiance. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i treats the superlative as comparing them with other people there and invokes 6:12, 36:9-10 and 45:23. He prefers this reading because the verse appears to restrict the greatest losers to them in the afterlife. Al-Baghawi says all are losers but these are the greatest; al-Baydawi says no one is more plainly or greatly a loser there. Al-Alusi reads degree or amount, and Ibn Ashur says they combine forms of misery dispersed among deluded nations.
  11:22/q05/p2 Their afterlife loss exceeds their loss in this brief worldly life, since the former lasts forever; they lose both lives. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i reports this as another possible comparison and cites 46:35, while preferring the comparison with afterlife inhabitants. Al-Razi says the worldly object chosen over faith perishes; Abu Hayyan distinguishes their endless loss from sinners whose punishment ends. Al-Tabataba'i also says deeds intensify through their afterlife consequences, citing 17:72; Ibn Ashur says they lose this world and the next.
11:22/q06 What does هم do in the sentence? [هُمُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether hum is parsed as separator, emphatic pronoun, or the subject of a new sentence; all accounts give it an emphatic effect.)
  11:22/q06/p1 It is a separating pronoun that emphasizes and restricts the superlative judgment to them. — reasons: Al-Samin lists the separator analysis. Al-Biqa'i says the repeated pronoun and 'they' intensify the claim. Al-Alusi says the definite predicate conveys exclusivity and the separator strengthens it. Ibn Ashur says the restriction is rhetorical: they reach maximal loss as if none shared it.
  11:22/q06/p2 It is the subject of a fresh clause whose predicate is الأخسرون, reinforcing the judgment. — reasons: Al-Samin gives this parse. Al-Alusi says taking hum as subject, as with separator, further emphasizes the superlative claim.
  11:22/q06/p3 It is an emphatic pronoun repeating the people already named. — reasons: Al-Samin lists the independent emphatic-pronoun analysis. Al-Biqa'i attributes superlative emphasis to repeated pronominal reference.
11:22/q07 Why are they called the greatest losers? [ٱلْأَخْسَرُونَ · interpretation]
  11:22/q07/p1 Their rejection brings the fullest, enduring loss and punishment in the afterlife. — reasons: Al-Tabari says they exchanged their Paradise homes for the homes of Paradise's people in Hell. Ibn Kathir expands the exchange: Paradise's heights and delights for Hell's depths and punishments, and God's nearness and vision for anger. Al-Razi says the worldly thing chosen instead of noble faith cannot last. Al-Nasafi says they themselves turned away and led others astray. Abu Hayyan contrasts their endless loss with sinners' finite punishment and eventual relief. Ibn Ashur calls loss a trader metaphor: they expected gain and suffered harm; he says they combine punishments scattered across deluded peoples and suffer misery precisely where they expected joy, losing both worlds, citing 18:103-104. Al-Tabataba'i says disbelief destroys themselves with no salvation while defiance persists, and reports that their afterlife loss exceeds worldly loss because it lasts forever; he adds that deeds intensify through their afterlife consequences, citing 17:72. Al-Alusi says greatest can indicate degree or amount, while an alternative cutting analysis makes their loss uninterrupted. Al-Baghawi observes that others also lose, but these lose most. Muqatil, al-Baydawi and al-Biqa'i affirm the superlative. Islahi traces their ruin to capacities squandered on false worship and the failure of imagined divine supports.
11:22/q08 How does this judgment sum up the preceding account? [* · links]
  11:22/q08/p1 It concludes the account of their rejected guidance, false worship, failed claims, and inability to avert punishment by naming their final loss. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says the final sentence sums up their exposure, punishment, refusal to hear warnings and neglect of evidence for God's unity, linking it to 11:18; he also treats the related preceding sentence as a new sentence reinforcing 11:18 and a class who lost themselves, citing 6:12. He identifies the claims as idol intercession and protection from hardship and sees the idols' disappearance as mockery, citing 46:28. Al-Suddi, reported in al-Durr, says those who lost themselves cheated or deprived themselves. Al-Biqa'i explains the immediately preceding inability to thwart as inability to prevent God's will, doubling as increase by an equal or greater amount, and capacity as bodily power. Islahi says invented gods and hoped-for support are useless and leave worshipers without insight. Study Quran says fabricated idols, ideas and beliefs ultimately abandon them, citing 6:24, 6:54, 10:30, 16:87 and 28:75.
11:22/q09 How does this verse lead into the account of believers? [* · links]
  11:22/q09/p1 The completed account of disbelievers' conduct and fate gives way to believers' good outcome, completing the contrast. — reasons: Al-Alusi says the discourse turns from the disbelievers to believers and completes a contrast with the preceding praise of believers.
