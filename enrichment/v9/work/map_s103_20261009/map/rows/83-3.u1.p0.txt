## THE CURRENT MAP
83:3/q01 How do the pronouns in كَالُوهُمْ and وَّزَنُوهُمْ function? [كَالُوهُمْ وَّزَنُوهُمْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the suffix names recipients, their due goods, or the measuring agents; the disputed evidence is Arabic transitivity, discourse contrast, and the missing alif in the codex.)
  83:3/q01/p1 They are attached accusative objects referring to the people for whom goods are measured or weighed; the benefactive lām is omitted. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ, al-Akhfash, al-Kisāʾī, al-Ṭabarī, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Ṭabrisī, Ibn ʿĀshūr and others cite Ḥijāz usage such as 'I measured you your food'; Ibn ʿĀshūr also mentions neighboring Qays and lightening of speech. Abū ʿUbaydah cites hunting poetry; al-Farrāʾ cites a Bedouin woman; Majāz cites Khuffāf; al-Zamakhsharī and al-Bayḍāwī cite poetry; al-Naḥḥās cites Abū Zayd's جنيتك أكموءا وعساقلا and lines with أمرتك الخير, نبيت عبد الله and أستغفر الله ذنبا; Ibn Qutaybah's cited Ḥumayd, Ḥātim, Labīd, Ṭarafah, al-Farrāʾ and al-Muthaqib show other permissible implicit referents or complements. Al-Naḥḥās reports Abū ʿAmr, al-Kisāʾī and al-Akhfash with Sībawayh's كَلْتُكَ and صَدْتُكَ analogy. Al-Samīn notes one object is direct and another may lose its preposition, with disagreement over the basic verb. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Zajjāj, al-Naḥḥās, Abū ʿUbayd, al-Jishumī, al-Samīn, al-Bursawī and al-Itqān cite the absence of a separating alif in the written form. Al-Zamakhsharī objects that spelling is not uniformly conventional and alif has no spoken value; al-Rāzī answers that early spelling practice should be preserved. Al-Samīn additionally prefers this parse for maintaining the contrast of taking from and giving to others. Al-Qurṭubī says a nominative parse leaves the first verb without a predicate; al-Ālūsī reports that nominative emphasis would not distinguish merchants from servants and would call for a connective fa. Al-Biqāʿī says the object can represent the customers' due rights and foregrounds their presence. Abū Ḥayyān says the commodity itself remains unexpressed. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says direct-object and lām constructions are both possible.
  83:3/q01/p2 The attached accusative pronoun stands for the measured or weighed amount due to the recipients, with an omitted noun understood. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī explain replacement of the omitted measured noun by its genitive pronoun. Al-Qurṭubī includes this beside preposition deletion. Al-Bayḍāwī names the omitted measured amount. Al-Ālūsī reports an omitted goods analysis. Al-Biqāʿī calls the object the customers' due rights; al-Samīn describes the goods as unexpressed.
  83:3/q01/p3 هم is nominative, a separate subject or emphasis of the verbs' plural subject; the goods and recipients are understood. — reasons: ʿĪsā ibn ʿUmar, sometimes with a reading attributed to Ḥamzah, pauses after each plural wāw; accounts vary between a new subject and emphasis of the verbal subject. Al-Naḥḥās also reports an unnamed emphatic analysis like قاموا هم. Abū Ḥayyān and the Shaykh cited by al-Samīn defend it: emphasis leaves the action in focus and the recipients remain understood since cheats do not short themselves. Al-Zamakhsharī objects that it shifts the contrast from treatment of recipients to identity of performers; al-Qurṭubī objects that the first verb then lacks a predicate. Al-Zajjāj and others object from the missing alif; al-Zamakhsharī and al-Samīn report that spelling argument as weak, since the alif is silent and manuscript usage varies.
83:3/q02 Where may a reciter pause in كَالُوهُمْ or وَّزَنُوهُمْ? [كَالُوهُمْ وَّزَنُوهُمْ · readings] (turns on: Whether syntactic separation licenses a recited stop within a written word, and whether a sound transmission supports that stop.)
  83:3/q02/p1 Each written verb and pronoun is one unit, so a pause comes after the pronoun. — reasons: Abū ʿUbayd and the joined-reading advocates cite manuscript spelling without an alif and ordinary transitive Arabic. Al-Jishumī says readers unanimously use the joined reading. Ibn al-Jazarī in al-Nashr regards the established performance rule as keeping written-together words together; al-Aʿmash through Abū Bakr from ʿĀṣim and Sūrah through al-Kisāʾī treat each as one word, while al-Dānī associates Nāfiʿ with adherence to the script. Al-Jaʿbarī says a connected pair takes a stop after the second word. Al-Zajjāj forbids pausing after kālū on the object analysis.
  83:3/q02/p2 A pause may be made after each plural wāw, treating هم as a separate word. — reasons: ʿĪsā ibn ʿUmar is repeatedly reported to stop after kālū or wazanū, with Ḥamzah sometimes associated with him. Al-Jaʿbarī permits pausing at an independent word and choosing according to spelling or linguistic origin; al-Nashr says al-Kisāʾī allowed division by meaning and Khalaf preferred following script, but the alleged Ḥamzah transmission comes only through ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ṣāliḥ al-ʿIjlī and al-Dānī says performance specialists oppose it.
83:3/q03 Why is the entitlement lām omitted from the verbs? [كَالُوهُمْ وَّزَنُوهُمْ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the omitted lām carries a deliberate implication of denied entitlement or reflects common transitive usage.)
  83:3/q03/p1 Its omission portrays the cheaters' refusal to give customers what is due to them. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī says the lām carries entitlement in both verbs; removing it in kālūhum and wazanūhum suits their withholding of the recipients' rights.
  83:3/q03/p2 Its omission is an idiomatic lightening of a normal Arabic construction. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the verbs ordinarily take a measured object and the lām may be dropped frequently, as with common verbs such as thanking someone. Al-Sāmarrāʾī acknowledges both a direct object and an entitlement lām as possible usages.
83:3/q04 Why is measuring alone mentioned for taking, but both measuring and weighing for giving? [كَالُوهُمْ وَّزَنُوهُمْ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the wording mirrors actual trade methods, uses an implied parallel, argues from lesser to greater harm, or states a general moral contrast.)
  83:3/q04/p1 Cheaters take by measure because filling a vessel enables theft, but short others through both measures and scales. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān reporting al-Zamakhsharī, al-Samīn reporting him, and al-Ālūsī connect the asymmetry to practical fraud: overfilling when taking and shorting by either means when giving. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī objects that claiming the original cheats actually bought only by measure lacks evidence.
  83:3/q04/p2 Merchants bought large or customary measured quantities, then sold smaller lots by measure or weight. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports the bulk-buying and retail-lot explanation but objects that it is not universal: Baghdad in his time weighed everything. Ibn ʿĀshūr says buyers generally took measured produce and paid weighed coin, while merchants sold through both methods. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects the bulk-grain account as weak.
  83:3/q04/p3 Mentioning full measure implies full weight too, while both forms of shortchanging are named when giving. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj says trade uses both measure and weight, and al-Ṭībī describes one half of the parallel as implying the other. Al-Ālūsī is unpersuaded. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls the inference arbitrary because the same reasoning could equally omit measuring.
  83:3/q04/p4 The measure example implies an even worse possible fraud in weighed goods because a small weight difference can cost more. — reasons: Sayyid Ṣibghat Allāh al-Ḥaydawī argues weighed goods are often more valuable, so mentioning the lesser harm implies the greater. Al-Ālūsī replies that reversing the verse's contrast could support an inverse implication equally well, with ellipsis like antimetabole.
  83:3/q04/p5 The verse condemns unfair taking and giving in general without specifying commodities or every method on each side. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī attributes this to other exegetes. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the rival attempts to account for the exact trading methods are weak; the pair of verses concerns unequal reciprocal rights.
83:3/q05 What does يُخْسِرُونَ mean here? [يُخْسِرُونَ · meaning]
  83:3/q05/p1 They cause the other party loss by reducing the amount, weight, or right due to them. — reasons: Muqātil, Ibn Qutaybah, al-Ṭabarī, al-Zajjāj, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Māwardī, al-Baghawī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Bursawī and others gloss it as shorting the recipient. Al-Zamakhsharī compares a scale that loses or causes loss; al-Zajjāj reports both akhsara and khasara al-mīzān for diminution, while al-Muʾarrij calls the expression Quraysh usage. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn distinguish intransitive khasira from causative akhsara; al-Samīn calls يُخْسِرُونَ the answer to إذا and supplies 'people of their goods' as omitted object. Ibn ʿĀshūr specifies monetary loss. Al-Bursawī says the shortfall violates the equality intended by measures and scales. Al-Zajjāj accepts a form yukh'sirūn in Arabic but knows of no one reciting that form here.
83:3/q06 What kind of shortfall is meant by cheating in measure? [يُخْسِرُونَ · interpretation]
  83:3/q06/p1 It may be a tiny, easily hidden shortfall in another person's due measure or weight. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī traces muṭaffif to something scant; al-Jishumī says ṭafīf means tiny and that even subtle theft or a slight shortfall counts. Abū Ḥayyān calls it an inconspicuous small reduction. Al-Jishumī reports one derivation from the small missing amount and another characterization as covert small theft. His related usage includes an underfilled vessel and descendants of Adam being near a full measure.
