## THE CURRENT MAP
70:21/q01 What does al-khayr denote here? [ٱلْخَيْرُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the wording is taken as material wealth, health, worldly standing, or good fortune broadly.)
  70:21/q01/p1 Wealth, provision, or material abundance. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī identifies receiving wealth; Tustarī, Sahl, Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Baghawī, and others contrast poverty with wealth. Abū Ḥayyān explicitly glosses al-khayr as wealth. Al-Biqāʿī calls it provision. Al-Ālūsī includes abundance. Al-Samarrāʾī cites the wealthy person's contrasting behavior in health and illness.
  70:21/q01/p2 Health. — reasons: Al-Rāzī and al-Nasafī pair illness with health as an alternative to poverty and wealth. Al-Ālūsī likewise allows health; al-Būrsawī says health can increase stinginess and commends charity while healthy. Elmalılı and Kur'an Yolu include health among blessings, and Study Quran reports illness versus health.
  70:21/q01/p3 Good or beneficial fortune generally. — reasons: Al-Rāzī gives poverty/wealth and illness/health as possibilities; Study Quran reports general misfortune versus good fortune as well as particular examples. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says people pursue what they perceive as beneficial.
  70:21/q01/p4 Status or security among worldly blessings. — reasons: Elmalılı names status alongside wealth and health; Kur'an Yolu names security alongside wealth and health.
70:21/q02 What does manūʿan say the person does? [مَنُوعًا · meaning] (turns on: Whether withholding is expressed as refusing benefits and due rights or, in the ethical gloss, failing to thank God.)
  70:21/q02/p1 He intensely withholds the good he receives, hoarding it and refusing others their share, including God's due rights. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī, Ibn al-Jawzī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Biqāʿī, and Study Quran specify God's due in wealth; Study Quran also names other people's rights and charity. Al-Nasafī says he hoards and denies others; Elmalılı adds that he forgets earlier distress and blocks good. Al-Būrsawī attributes the refusal to ignorance of divine apportionment and generosity's reward, adding that health can intensify stinginess and charity then is better. Al-Biqāʿī attributes it to love of immediate gain. Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḍaḥḥāk, Sahl, and others describe withholding upon prosperity; al-Ālūsī calls it intense. Kur'an Yolu says blessings can be treated as personal achievement, with no spending for God or help to others. Al-Samarrāʾī cites Ibn Kathīr: blessing leads to denying others and God's due.
  70:21/q02/p2 In blessing, he fails to give thanks. — reasons: Abū ʿUbayda and Thaʿlab pair lack of thanks in prosperity with lack of patience in affliction.
70:21/q03 How are manūʿan and its paired adjective construed grammatically? [مَنُوعًا · grammar] (turns on: Whether the two accusatives are circumstantial states, adjectives of halūʿ, predicates of an omitted verb, or accusatives by proximity.)
  70:21/q03/p1 The two words are circumstantial states of the pronoun in halūʿan, or all three words are circumstantial states. — reasons: Al-Samīn prefers overlapping states of the pronoun in halūʿan. Al-Ālūsī reports the analysis treating all three words as circumstantial; they may describe traits actual after maturity or present potentially from creation.
  70:21/q03/p2 The two words qualify halūʿan as adjectives or revealing descriptions. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās allows adjectives of halūʿ; al-Ālūsī reports revealing descriptions while halūʿ itself is circumstantial. Makkī allows this but calls it distant because the time phrase would precede its adjective. Al-Samīn rejects that objection: an adverbial phrase can precede its governor; the forbidden order is moving an adjective's governed element before the adjective.
  70:21/q03/p3 An omitted kāna or ṣāra makes each adjective a predicate: he becomes distressed or withholding when touched. — reasons: Makkī supplies the omitted verb; al-Naḥḥās also allows the sense that he becomes so when harm or good touches him.
  70:21/q03/p4 The accusatives arise by proximity to halūʿan, rather than sharing its grammatical function. — reasons: Abū Muslim gives the proximity analysis as reported by al-Jishumī.
70:21/q04 What does each idhā govern in the paired clauses? [وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ مَنُوعًا · grammar]
  70:21/q04/p1 Each idhā time phrase governs the corresponding following description. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī says each idhā governs its subsequent quality; al-Ālūsī repeats that account. Al-Samīn relates the two time phrases to the respective circumstantial states.
70:21/q05 What disposition does this clause help define as halūʿ? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether halūʿ is emphasized as fretful greed, as eagerness for perceived benefit, or as failures of patience and gratitude.)
  70:21/q05/p1 Extreme impatience and alarm in hardship, joined to greed and withholding in ease. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan says this is how humanity was created. Al-Farrāʾ derives the explanation from the pattern of jazaʿ; Abū ʿUbayda calls halāʿ the verbal noun for the worst impatience. Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḍaḥḥāk, Sahl, and others use the two reactions to explain it. Al-Rāzī includes intense acquisitiveness and little patience, citing al-Farrāʾ and al-Mubarrad's 'fretful' gloss and Aḥmad ibn Yaḥyā's appeal to the next verses. Thaʿlab tells Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ṭāhir that God's explanation is clearest; Abū Ḥayyān reports the same exchange. Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls halʿ anxious agitation amid fear and desire. Al-Samarrāʾī quotes Fatḥ al-Qadīr citing al-Ṣiḥāḥ for extreme greed and excessive panic, and Ibn Kathīr for terror and despair of future good under harm. Al-Iṣlāḥī says people even complain at slight divine punishment for their deeds.
  70:21/q05/p2 Intense eagerness for what one perceives as good, whose misdirection produces panic and selfish withholding. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī prefers 'intense eagerness'; self-love seeks what seems useful and resists harm, while limited judgment makes a person withhold benefits. Al-Rāzī's gloss also includes strong acquisitiveness.
  70:21/q05/p3 Failure to be patient in affliction and grateful in blessing. — reasons: Abū ʿUbayda and Thaʿlab give this ethical explanation as reported by al-Jishumī.
70:21/q06 Why do the two clauses follow the description halūʿ? [* · rhetoric]
  70:21/q06/p1 They explain and remove ambiguity from halūʿ, completing its sense. — reasons: Al-Burhān says explanatory clauses are common in the Qur'an. Al-Itqān cites the rhetoricians' definition of explanation as removing ambiguity and reports Abū al-ʿĀliya and others applying it here. Ibn Jinnī, cited in both works, says one should not pause before the explanatory clause because it completes the preceding expression like an adjective or relative clause. Thaʿlab's reply and Ibn ʿAbbās's glosses likewise appeal to the following clauses as God's own explanation.
70:21/q07 Whom does the human trait described here encompass? [* · referent] (turns on: Whether 'the human' is the species subject to an exception or specifically the unbeliever.)
  70:21/q07/p1 Human beings generally, with worshippers excepted by the following verses. — reasons: Al-Nasafī says 'the human' denotes the species, permitting the exception for those who pray. Al-Rāzī reports this general reading. Al-Ḥasan and several interpreters present the behavior as human temperament. Study Quran says those with the eight qualities listed in 70:22–35 escape the blameworthy state. Al-Ālūsī gives connected and disconnected readings of the following exception.
  70:21/q07/p2 The disbeliever or associator. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī takes the intensely anxious person to be the disbeliever; Ibn ʿAbbās specifies associators; al-Ḍaḥḥāk also says the disbeliever. Al-Rāzī reports this as one view.
70:21/q08 Is the trait innate, and what makes its expression blameworthy? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether creation of the tendency is literal or figurative, and whether the initial tendency or its accountable expression is morally at issue.)
  70:21/q08/p1 A disposition can be created in humans, while blame attaches to chosen conduct after accountability. — reasons: Al-Qāḍī says God cannot blame His own act and believers can struggle against the trait. Al-Rāzī distinguishes involuntary inward anxiety created by God from speech and conduct that people can restrain. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī say the qualities can be actual after maturity or potential from creation; al-Ālūsī affirms God can create the disposition and points to infants panicking at hunger and pain. He says accountable expression, rather than creation itself, is blamed. Ibn Kaysān says people were made to love pleasure and flee pain, then commanded to give what they love and endure what they dislike. Ibn ʿĀshūr stresses the wording rebukes hoarding rather than excusing it.
  70:21/q08/p2 The wording is figurative: panic and withholding are so entrenched that the person seems born with them. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī attributes this reading to al-Zamakhsharī. Abū Ḥayyān likewise says the traits are so entrenched that humanity is described as created bearing them, comparing 21:37. Al-Ālūsī objects that infants visibly panic at hunger and pain, and separates creation from culpability.
  70:21/q08/p3 Innate self-love and eagerness are useful; excess, deficient judgment, or surrender to appetite turn them into vice. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says eagerness enables pursuit of happiness. Children first follow instinctive ideas of good and resist sharing; reason later corrects judgments. Continued appetite can reject truth and others' rights. Ignoring reason turns a God-given means of happiness into misery and hoarding. Al-Qāshānī instead describes attachment to the ego's dark nature as producing cowardice and greed.
