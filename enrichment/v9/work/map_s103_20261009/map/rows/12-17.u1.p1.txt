12:17/q10 Why did the brothers choose a wolf for their story? [فَأَكَلَهُ ٱلذِّئْبُ · rhetoric]
  12:17/q10/p1 They reused Jacob's earlier stated fear of a wolf, though the excuse was weak and exposed their deception. — reasons: Mawradi, Qurtubi, and Islahi say they borrowed his own warning; Ibn Kathir calls the admission of likely disbelief calculated tact, since the coincidence sounds unlikely. Islahi calls it unintelligent; Biqai cites their suspicious behavior and telltale signs, despite no prior proven lie.
12:17/q11 How is ٱلذِّئْبُ read? [ٱلذِّئْبُ · readings] (turns on: Whether to realize or soften the hamza, including the analogy Kisai raises with the fish wording.)
  12:17/q11/p1 The hamza is articulated: al-dhiʾbu. — reasons: Ibn Jinni reports Hamza ibn Habib's reading against Kisai's; Tabarsi attributes articulation to readers other than his listed softeners. Qiraat ER calls it the standard connected reading; in pause the final consonant becomes silent. Kisai asks why Hamza hamzates wolf but not the fish at 37:142; the excerpt gives no reply. Ibn Jinni notes that early reading arguments began with analogy of forms or roots and later added grammar and citations.
  12:17/q11/p2 The hamza is softened or dropped, yielding al-dhību. — reasons: Ibn Jinni reports Kisai's unhamzated form; Tabarsi lists Kisai, Khalaf, Abu Jafar, Warsh, al-Asha, and al-Yazidi in all three occurrences. Qiraat ER describes compensatory lengthening. Tabarsi also gives plurals adhuʾub, dhawāʾib, and dhuʾbān and the wind verb tadhāʾabat.
12:17/q12 What does مُؤْمِنٍۢ لَّنَا mean? [مُؤْمِنٍۢ لَّنَا · meaning]
  12:17/q12/p1 Someone who believes, credits, or accepts their account. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba derives iman from affirmation and cites ordinary speech, 40:12, and prayer called iman at 2:143; Bursevi distinguishes verbal declaration of a speaker's truth from inward acceptance and defines truth against falsehood. Tabatabai notes the verb takes li or bi, citing 29:26; Ibn Ashur cites 29:26 and 10:83 for li before a person. Wahidi points to discussion at 2:3. Tabari's al-Suddi glosses it this way, while Tabari rejects reading the brothers as confessing lies or accusing Jacob of disbelief in known truth.
12:17/q13 What other senses and theological uses of īmān and al-Muʾmin are brought to bear? [مُؤْمِنٍۢ · theology] (turns on: Whether the divine name is explained through affirmation or the granting of safety.)
  12:17/q13/p1 A human believer affirms; God is al-Muʾmin by confirming His promise or accepting a servant's faith. — reasons: Both Ibn Qutayba works give this distinction. Gharib further warns that the divine attribute cannot be conjugated at will but must follow a transmitted form from Prophet or imams.
  12:17/q13/p2 The divine name al-Muʾmin may derive from granting security: none is safe unless God makes them safe. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba offers this alternative etymology alongside confirmation.
12:17/q14 Why say مُؤْمِنٍۢ rather than مُصَدِّق? [مُؤْمِنٍۢ · rhetoric]
  12:17/q14/p1 Muʾmin combines believing with giving security or reassurance, outweighing muṣaddiq's verbal echo of ṣādiqīn. — reasons: Burhan says the added security sense better suits their request. Itqan says meaning takes priority over sound embellishment and the verse keeps muʾmin despite the available rhyme.
12:17/q15 Why did they expect Jacob not to believe them? [وَمَآ أَنتَ بِمُؤْمِنٍۢ لَّنَا · interpretation] (turns on: The sources point to prior distrust, absence or contradiction of evidence, and the rhetorical use of an anticipated refusal; these reasons can coexist.)
  12:17/q15/p1 His love for Joseph and prior suspicion of the brothers would make this particular account unbelievable to him. — reasons: Zajjaj says a prophet cannot reject known truth. Tabari says Jacob suspects them, not that they confess lying or accuse him of rejecting truthful people. Wahidi, Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Bursevi, Alusi, and others emphasize love and distrust; Tabarsi adds jealousy and resentment. Ibn Kathir says they acknowledged his earlier warning. Study Quran links his suspicion to 12:11–12 and notes Genesis instead says he believed them. Asad says envy led him to infer harm but 12:83 shows he was unsure Joseph had died.
  12:17/q15/p2 They provided no convincing evidence, and the available signs worked against their claim. — reasons: Wahidi's Abu Bakr says objective truth alone would not secure belief without signs; Baghawi likewise says no evidence was offered. Razi allows this alternative. Qurtubi says evidence contradicted their accusation; Biqai sees signs of fabrication and Jacob's insight. Islahi calls the story weak.
  12:17/q15/p3 Their claim of expected disbelief is a rhetorical excuse that signals their asserted truth or their expectation of rejection. — reasons: Tabatabai calls it a formula used when means are exhausted, indirectly claiming truth; Ibn Ashur says it shows they were not seeking credence. Ibn Kathir calls it calculated tact. Biqai says they conceded Jacob's disbelief; Burhan says even manifest innocence would not sway a father favoring Joseph, but their contrived proof betrayed them.
12:17/q16 What is the force of وَلَوْ كُنَّا صَٰدِقِينَ? [وَلَوْ كُنَّا صَٰدِقِينَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether ṣādiqīn describes their present claim, their general reputation, or a concessive assertion within their lie.)
  12:17/q16/p1 Even if the brothers were normally known as honest, Jacob would doubt them about Joseph. — reasons: Mawradi attributes this to Ibn Jarir; Ibn Jawzi and Qurtubi attribute it to Zajjaj, Tabari, and others. Ibn Atiyya says Jacob might have believed them before this disaster but not amid grief and old suspicion, while finding al-Rummani's analogy unsound. Alusi says this avoids their admitting a lie by contrast; Bursevi allows that Jacob regards them as truthful.
  12:17/q16/p2 Even if their present account were in fact true, Jacob would not believe it. — reasons: Ibn Ishaq is cited by Mawradi and Ibn Jawzi; Abu Bakr in Wahidi says they may believe themselves truthful but need proof. Baghawi says they could be truthful before God yet lack evidence. Abu Hayyan says the clause may limit the truth claim to this incident; Ibn Atiyya says it continues their lie.
  12:17/q16/p3 They are asserting 'although we have told the truth' as part of the invented story. — reasons: Mawradi cites Ibn Ishaq's wording; Ibn Atiyya reports Mubarrad's continuation of their assertion and questions an analogy to 7:88 and al-Rummani's account. Qurtubi notes the readings 'even if' and 'even though'. Tabatabai says the excuse indirectly asserts truth. Alusi resists making it a direct confession of actual truth, since contrast would imply they were lying.
12:17/q17 How does the concessive construction وَلَوْ كُنَّا work? [وَلَوْ كُنَّا صَٰدِقِينَ · grammar]
  12:17/q17/p1 It is an elliptical concessive or circumstantial clause: Jacob would still not believe them, even under that condition. — reasons: Nahhas paraphrases it with wa-in kunna; Tabarsi supplies the omitted result. Samin calls it a circumstantial clause. Ibn Ashur identifies the waw as circumstantial and law as the remotest possible condition; he says no deleted opposite condition or contrary conclusion is implied, unlike a restrictive conditional, and cites Maʿarri's verse and 2:170 and 3:91. Burhan observes that law and lammā take past-form verbs as exceptions to the usual future orientation of condition verbs.
12:17/q18 How are نَسْتَبِقُ and وَتَرَكْنَا related syntactically to ذَهَبْنَا? [ذَهَبْنَا نَسْتَبِقُ وَتَرَكْنَا · grammar]
  12:17/q18/p1 Nastabiq is circumstantial, and taraknā is another circumstantial clause, with qad understood by some grammarians. — reasons: Samin al-Durr gives both analyses and notes the optional implicit qad.
12:17/q19 What is the grammatical role of بِـ in بِمُؤْمِنٍۢ? [بِمُؤْمِنٍۢ · grammar]
  12:17/q19/p1 It precedes the predicate in a negative clause, a recurrent Quranic construction. — reasons: Bint al-Shati lists this verse alongside 2:167, 11:29, 26:114, 11:83, 16:46, 40:56, 14:22, 6:134, 12:44, 12:103, 26:137–138, 27:81, 30:53, 35:22, 37:162, 52:29, and 68:2.
12:17/q20 How do the plural pronominal endings in the verse mark their speakers? [ذَهَبْنَا كُنَّا · grammar]
  12:17/q20/p1 Plural and dual suffixes use following letters for number and gender rather than the singular vowel alone. — reasons: Sibawayh says masculine dual and plural suffixes add mim after alif or waw; -tumā and -tumū retain ḍamma on taʾ and kaf because following material marks number and gender, and no marker beyond dual is added since dual already counts as plural. Feminine plural verb endings avoid a doubled nun unlike -tun or -kunna, to prevent four or five successive vowels.
12:17/q21 When can a hearer ask for identification after hearing a plural reference? [ذَهَبْنَا · grammar]
  12:17/q21/p1 A definite named person cannot be queried as though unidentified, but an unfamiliar plural group can be. — reasons: Sibawayh says asking man after a specifically named Abd Allah is inappropriate; one may instead ask who he is or which man. He reports Arabs asking maʿa manīn after 'we went with them' or mannā after 'I saw him' when the people are not personally known, treating the report like 'I saw a man'.
