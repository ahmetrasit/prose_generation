  102:1/q18/p2 Some Companions called it al-Mughīra. — reasons: Saʿīd b. Abī Hilāl transmits this name through Ibn Abī Ḥātim and al-Durr.
  102:1/q18/p3 Some Companions called it al-Maqbara, the Graveyard Sūra. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī and Ibn ʿĀshūr both attribute this to Saʿīd b. Abī Hilāl, although other transmissions here give al-Mughīra.
102:1/q19 How many verses does the sūra have and where is it placed in revelation order? [* · sciences]
  102:1/q19/p1 It has eight verses. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says this is unanimous; several sūra headings agree.
  102:1/q19/p2 On the Meccan chronology, it is sixteenth, after al-Kawthar and before al-Māʿūn. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr gives both neighbors; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ calls sixteenth a common dating and names al-Kawthar; Study Quran places it after al-Kawthar.
102:1/q20 What merit or recitation practice is reported for this sūra? [* · sciences] (turns on: The reports describe different merits and circumstances; the weeping promise is explicitly graded weak.)
  102:1/q20/p1 Reciting it counts like reciting a thousand verses. — reasons: Reports from Ubayy, Ibn ʿUmar, and ʿUmar give the merit; Ibn al-Milq suggests its teaching of return to God is one of six Qurʾānic aims and matches one sixth of roughly 6,200 verses, but al-Ālūsī says the true basis lies beyond that calculation.
  102:1/q20/p2 A report promises Paradise to whoever weeps at its recitation, or feigns tears. — reasons: Al-Durr records the invitation and repeats the promise at a second recitation; al-Bayhaqī weakens the report.
  102:1/q20/p3 The Prophet recited the sūra in prayer or in a companion's hearing. — reasons: ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Shikhkhīr says he found the Prophet reciting it, including one version in prayer through its end.
102:1/q21 How does al-Takāthur connect with al-Qāriʿah and nearby sūras? [* · links] (turns on: The proposed al-Qāriʿah link is either thematic in the canonical order or also treated as a direct sequential explanation; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ disputes chronological succession.)
  102:1/q21/p1 It follows al-Qāriʿah's account of the Hour by naming the worldly rivalry that causes ruin and distracts from preparing for it. — reasons: Jishumī says the preceding chapter ended with disbelievers in Hell; al-Biqāʿī says this sūra identifies their cause of misery, and Ibn al-Zubayr says rivalry in kin and numbers diverts from al-Qāriʿah's terrors. Islāḥī pairs the two around the afterlife's scale of good deeds versus worldly treasure.
  102:1/q21/p2 The link to al-Qāriʿah is thematic in muṣḥaf order, not a chronological succession. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports al-Nīsābūrī's link but rejects chronological succession because Takāthur precedes Qāriʿah by thirteen sūras in revelation; she allows shared warning tone.
  102:1/q21/p3 The following al-ʿAṣr recalls time wasted by striving for increase. — reasons: Study Quran says time is one of the blessings for which people will be questioned, connecting 102:1 and 102:8 with the following sūra.
  102:1/q21/p4 An extended sequence links accumulation to slander, mockery, and the pride associated with the Elephant. — reasons: Ibn al-Zubayr, as cited by al-Biqāʿī, connects 102:1, 104:1, and 105:1.
102:1/q22 What is the proper lesson and practice of visiting graves in relation to this rebuke? [* · law] (turns on: Whether literal visitation serves remembrance and reform or becomes the very boastful practice condemned by the passage.)
  102:1/q22/p1 A grave visit should awaken reflection on death and judgment, prayer to God, and resolve for good deeds. — reasons: Elmalılı cites the hadith that visiting graves reminds of the afterlife. He describes greeting the dead, praying to Allah for living and dead, reciting Qurʾān and dedicating reward, and reflecting on resurrection, scales and destinations (101:4–11); he compares a visit to reading a book and learning from the deceased's good example. Ibn ʿĀbidīn says benefit varies with visitors' insight.
  102:1/q22/p2 Using graves to boast of ancestors defeats their lesson and is rebuked. — reasons: Elmalılı says the dead's deeds are theirs and ours ours (2:141), so competing over them is pointless. Love of the good should follow God and the Prophet (2:165, 3:31); a person rises with those loved, but worship and aid are sought from Allah, not the dead. He prefers reading the passage as rebuke rather than encouragement of literal visiting.
102:1/q23 How does the opening warning return at the sūra's end? [* · links]
  102:1/q23/p1 Questioning over blessings exposes the cost of indulgence and calls for gratitude. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says even lawful pleasure is restrained by knowing the majestic King will question it, more so disliked or forbidden pleasure. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says ingratitude leads to loss and punishment; Study Quran says verses 3–8 promise certain knowledge of Hellfire.
  102:1/q23/p2 The warning can be illustrated through five ordinary blessings. — reasons: The Ibn ʿAbbās report in al-Thaʿlabī names a full stomach, cold drink, sleep, shade, and sound constitution after discussing wrongful accumulation, graves, and judgment.
102:1/q24 What does the sūra's basmala contribute to its theme? [* · links]
  102:1/q24/p1 It presents divine majesty, general provision and guidance, and completed mercy for those God loves. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī gives this reading in both transmitted segments.
102:1/q25 How does takāthur relate to God's nature? [ٱلتَّكَاثُرُ · theology]
  102:1/q25/p1 Increase and decrease do not apply to God, whom no number or place circumscribes. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī says God's holy name precedes time and cannot be spatially limited; the incapacity of number to encompass Him excludes numerical change.
102:1/q26 What other uses of the l-h-w word family are noted? [أَلْهَىٰكُمُ · meaning] (turns on: These are distinct lexical uses of related forms, rather than competing glosses of the verse's verb.)
  102:1/q26/p1 The root is illustrated by stopping a conversation when thunder is heard. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī and Ibn al-Jawzī tell this of Ibn al-Zubayr; Rāzī names al-Zubayr and glosses it as abandoning conversation.
  102:1/q26/p2 Lahwa can denote a miller's handful or a gift by analogy. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī reports the noun and plural lahā; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ suggests the miller's occupied hand as an image of continuing diversion.
  102:1/q26/p3 Lahāt denotes a fleshy part by the throat or, alternatively, the back of the mouth. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī records the two anatomical glosses and plural lahā.
102:1/q27 What unrelated lexical gloss is preserved with these notes? [* · meaning]
  102:1/q27/p1 Kāda means 'nearly' or 'came close'. — reasons: Al-Zarkashī refers to his earlier discussion of verbs; the note supplies no connection to the verse's vocabulary.
