## THE CURRENT MAP
7:124/q01 What does the cutting verb convey about the extent of the threat? [لَأُقَطِّعَنَّ أَيْدِيَكُمْ وَأَرْجُلَكُم · grammar] (turns on: Whether the forms mark multiplied acts, the number of people threatened, or the scope of the definite plural.)
  7:124/q01/p1 Its intensive form conveys repeated or multiplied cutting across the magicians' limbs. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī relates the intensive form to the plural hands. Al-Jishumī glosses taqṭīʿ as multiplying cuts.
  7:124/q01/p2 The verb forms imply that large numbers of sorcerers, or sorcerers with many followers, were threatened. — reasons: Asad reads numerical scale from the Arabic forms. He supports the possible large following with a Biblical report of many Egyptians joining Israel's exodus.
  7:124/q01/p3 The definite plural extends the threat to every hand and foot of every sorcerer. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr draws this scope from the definite plural, alongside the omitted object of the preceding threat.
7:124/q02 How are the cutting and crucifixion verbs read? [لَأُقَطِّعَنَّ لَأُصَلِّبَنَّكُمْ · readings] (turns on: Whether the transmitted form is intensified or the lighter triliteral, and how the latter is vocalized.)
  7:124/q02/p1 Both verbs have lighter, triliteral readings in place of the intensified forms. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf mentions lightening the lām in both verbs. Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports alternate vocalizations from Ḥumayd al-Makkī, Ibn Muḥayṣin and Mujāhid, including a report of kasra on the second verb's lām. Al-Samīn names the same readers and notes two imperfect-vowel patterns for ṣalaba.
  7:124/q02/p2 The crucifixion verb is read with a doubled lām by the Qurʾānic readers. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī states this while deriving ṣalb from fastening to wood; the other sources also record lighter readings, so his statement is retained as his formulation.
7:124/q03 What does 'min khilāf' mean and how does it attach to the cutting? [مِّنْ خِلَٰفٍۢ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether khilāf identifies opposite sides of the body or the magicians' opposition, and whether min is circumstantial or causal.)
  7:124/q03/p1 One hand and the opposite foot are cut, conventionally the right hand and left foot, or the reverse; min describes the crosswise manner. — reasons: Muqātil, al-Ṭabarī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabrisī explicitly allow either right hand with left foot or left hand with right foot. Al-Kalbī, Ibn al-Jawzī and Bursawī give the right-hand/left-foot example; ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz ibn Yaḥyā also gives the right-hand/left-leg example. Al-Samīn calls min circumstantial, and al-Ālūsī agrees. Ibn ʿĀshūr says one hand and the opposite foot leaves a person able to walk or lean on a stick and points to 5:33. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī also describes one hand and opposite foot.
  7:124/q03/p2 Min is causal: the punishment is because of the magicians' opposition or perverseness. — reasons: Al-Samīn reports the causal analysis but says direct attachment to the verb is remote. Al-Ālūsī likewise calls it remote. Asad renders min khilāf 'because of perverseness' and refers to his note on 5:33.
7:124/q04 What does crucifixion mean here? [لَأُصَلِّبَنَّكُمْ · meaning] (turns on: Whether fastening to the support itself kills, serves as a nonfatal display, or names the general act of binding.)
  7:124/q04/p1 Ṣalb is fastening a person to wood or a raised support. — reasons: Al-Jishumī and al-Ṭabrisī define it as binding to wood. Al-Ālūsī gives that as the original sense. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī describes fastening to raised wood for public warning. Al-Biqāʿī allows a cross-shaped display.
  7:124/q04/p2 Crucifixion can mean hanging a person until death. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says the word came to mean hanging by the neck until death. His Ibn ʿAbbās report describes binding beneath the arms and hanging until death. Ibn ʿĀshūr discusses killing on a cross with reference to 4:157.
  7:124/q04/p3 Crucifixion can be a painful display without killing on the support. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr describes suspension after amputation as a public warning. Al-Biqāʿī allows torture until bodily fluid runs.
7:124/q05 Why does Pharaoh threaten crucifixion? [لَأُصَلِّبَنَّكُمْ · interpretation]
  7:124/q05/p1 Its public display would disgrace the magicians and deter others. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī name disgrace and deterrence. Bursawī places the display on palm trunks by the Nile for that purpose. Ibn ʿĀshūr and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī call the suspension a public warning. Al-Biqāʿī says the drawn-out threat frightens the magicians and wavering spectators.
7:124/q06 Where and on what were the victims to be crucified? [لَأُصَلِّبَنَّكُمْ · reports]
  7:124/q06/p1 On the Nile bank in Egypt, with reports specifying date-palm trunks there. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī and al-Kalbī locate the threatened crucifixion on the Nile bank. Al-Ṭabrisī reports date-palm trunks along the Nile; Bursawī and the Study Quran specify palm trees there. Ibn Kathīr and the Study Quran cite the parallel wording at 20:71 for palm trunks.
7:124/q07 What sequence does 'then' imply between cutting and crucifixion? [ثُمَّ لَأُصَلِّبَنَّكُمْ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether crucifixion kills or displays a surviving amputee, and what temporal force the parallel conjunctions carry.)
  7:124/q07/p1 Cutting precedes crucifixion, with an interval before the second punishment. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says the delayed 'then' prolongs terror. Ibn ʿĀshūr says a nonfatal suspension after amputation allows time for healing. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says al-Aʿrāf grants respite whereas the waw of 26:49 in al-Shuʿarāʾ gives none and suits Pharaoh's hotter anger; his other treatment expressly contrasts the two wordings.
  7:124/q07/p2 The 'then' is compatible with the waw used in parallel passages because waw can also permit an interval. — reasons: Al-Samīn supplies the grammatical reconciliation of the two conjunctions.
  7:124/q07/p3 If crucifixion means killing, the punishments target different groups rather than following each other for each victim. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr argues that cutting someone crosswise before killing them on a cross would make the crosswise detail pointless; he points to 4:157 for the killing sense.
7:124/q08 What force does 'all' have, and whom does it cover? [أَجْمَعِينَ · grammar]
  7:124/q08/p1 It emphatically includes all the magicians addressed by Pharaoh. — reasons: Al-Samīn says ajmaʿīn is emphatic even without a preceding kull. Muqātil, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Bayḍāwī and others explicitly describe the threat as extending to all. Ibn ʿAṭiyya distinguishes the universal threat from reports that only some were actually cut and crucified.
7:124/q09 Did Pharaoh carry out the threatened punishment? [* · reports] (turns on: Whether the execution reports or the prayer to die as Muslims establish the outcome; the verse itself only records Pharaoh's words.)
  7:124/q09/p1 He did execute or mutilate the converts, though one report says only some suffered it. — reasons: Al-Suddī reports killing and cutting. Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports cutting and crucifying some despite the threat to all; Ibn ʿAbbās and others say the converts were sorcerers in the morning and martyrs by evening. Al-Rāzī calls the Ibn ʿAbbās account of cross-amputation more apparent, as an example to deter others. He also reports an argument from the officials' later warning about Moses and his people in 7:127, while noting the reply that the magicians could fall under 'his people.'
  7:124/q09/p2 He did not carry out the threat; the magicians' death came from God. — reasons: Al-Rāzī reports others' inference from their prayer to die as Muslims and calls that inference close.
  7:124/q09/p3 The verse states a threat but does not establish whether it was carried out. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya distinguishes the text from reports of execution. Al-Rāzī likewise says the verse itself leaves the outcome open; his discussion of 7:127 records both an argument for execution and the reply that the magicians may be included among Moses' people.
7:124/q10 Was Pharaoh the first to impose crosswise amputation or crucifixion? [* · reports]
  7:124/q10/p1 Pharaoh was the first to use crosswise amputation, crucifixion, or both. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās is cited for both acts by al-Ṭabarī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ibn al-Jawzī, Ibn Kathīr and al-Ālūsī. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr is cited by Ibn Abī Ḥātim, al-Thaʿlabī and al-Wāḥidī for crosswise amputation, with Ibn Abī Ḥātim also naming crucifixion. Al-Kashshāf reports the combined claim without naming a transmitter. Al-Ṭabrisī says a report gives both and specifies palm trunks. Al-Nasafī and the Study Quran report the first use of this punishment and crucifixion. Al-Bayḍāwī and Bursawī transmit the first institution of crucifixion specifically. Al-Ālūsī's Ibn ʿAbbās report describes binding under the arms and hanging until death.
7:124/q11 How is Pharaoh's punishment related to the penalty for highway robbery? [* · law] (turns on: Whether the link is a report about the later institution of the penalty or a comparison with its Qurʾānic form.)
  7:124/q11/p1 God prescribed crucifixion for highway robbers after Pharaoh's first use of it, because their crime is grave warfare against God and His messenger. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī reports this view and says the penalty is understood sequentially from divine mercy. Bursawī repeats the prior institution and later prescription. Al-Ālūsī invokes 5:33 and the gravity of the crime.
  7:124/q11/p2 The threatened punishment resembles the Qurʾānic penalty for warfare against God and His messenger and spreading corruption. — reasons: The Study Quran points to 5:33; Ibn ʿĀshūr also cites that verse when discussing opposite-side amputation.
