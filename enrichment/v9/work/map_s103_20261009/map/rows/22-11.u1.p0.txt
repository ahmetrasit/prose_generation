## THE CURRENT MAP
22:11/q01 Why say 'from the people' rather than use a pronoun? [وَمِنَ ٱلنَّاسِ · rhetoric]
  22:11/q01/p1 The fresh noun evokes the group's instability and vacillation. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī ties the wording to their precarious worship.
22:11/q02 How does the definite class noun in 'from the people' work? [ٱلنَّاسِ · grammar]
  22:11/q02/p1 The article can mark a class applicable to each member. — reasons: Sībawayh illustrates definite class names through epithets, stars, days, place names and duals. A common epithet may become an individual's name; al-Khalīl distinguishes named persons from descriptive definite names. Definite known pairs differ from arbitrary pairs. Fixed places retain definite names, while personal name pairs are indefinite; lost derivations do not change the rule.
22:11/q03 Who is the conditional worshipper, and what occasion is reported? [مَن يَعْبُدُ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ · reports] (turns on: Whether the verse is tied to a particular migrant or incident, a group of conditional converts, hypocrites, or weak converts; Ibn ʿĀshūr objects that concealed disbelief cannot explain reassurance after conversion.)
  22:11/q03/p1 Bedouin or other Arab migrants judged Islam by health, children, animals, harvests, charity and wealth, remaining during prosperity and leaving after misfortune. — reasons: Muqātil specifies Asad ibn Khuzayma and Ghaṭafān; reports vary between one newcomer and groups, foals and horses, sons versus daughters or miscarriages, fever, drought and delayed alms. Ibn Kathīr cites Bukhārī for Ibn ʿAbbās's individual report. Ibn ʿĀshūr treats this as evidence for a Medinan occasion; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ notes two routes. Ibn Jurayj adds nearby villagers; some feared loss of Jewish allies' protection if Muhammad failed.
  22:11/q03/p2 A Jewish convert suffered loss of sight, wealth and children, asked to leave Islam, and was refused. — reasons: Abū Saʿīd's report has the Prophet compare Islam's testing to fire purifying iron, silver and gold; wording also says Islam does not release one. Al-Ālūsī says Ibn Ḥajar weakened the report. Study Quran reports the refining saying without the full identification.
  22:11/q03/p3 Shayba ibn Rabīʿa is identified as the person. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās's version says he accepted Islam before Muhammad's mission and apostatized when revelation began; another account connects conditional belief to requests for wealth, animals and children.
  22:11/q03/p4 The verse concerns hypocrites whose profession depends on outward benefit. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan says worship is only on the tongue; Ibn Zayd says worldly ease governs worship. Ibn ʿĀshūr reports ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy among them but objects that concealed unbelief ill fits reassurance after conversion; Jishumī distinguishes weak Muslims who can apostatize from hypocrites who were always unbelievers.
  22:11/q03/p5 Weak or uncertain Muslims, rather than established hypocrites, are described. — reasons: Jishumī's distinction is that weak Muslims might actually apostatize; Ibn ʿĀshūr describes entrants neither secure in Islam nor wholly rejecting it, weighing the new faith against old gods.
  22:11/q03/p6 Banū Asad ibn Khuzayma demanded gifts and threatened to return to polytheism if denied. — reasons: Jishumī transmits this as another report, distinct in its demand for gifts.
  22:11/q03/p7 Al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith is another named identification. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī lists it among alternatives.
  22:11/q03/p8 ʿUyayna ibn Badr, Aqraʿ ibn Ḥābis and ʿAbbās ibn Mirdās are named. — reasons: Elmalılı reports they would accept Muhammad's religion if it brought good and otherwise call it false.
  22:11/q03/p9 The ʿUrayniyyūn exemplify the wavering converts. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says they migrated, disliked Medina, recovered with charity-camel milk and urine, then killed a shepherd and stole camels before capture and execution; Ibn ʿAṭiyya also invokes them.
22:11/q04 What does worshipping God 'on an edge' mean? [يَعْبُدُ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ · interpretation] (turns on: The literal edge image can point to doubt, one-sided conditional worship, mere outward profession, or a specific inward dependence on reward; several sources explicitly connect these explanations.)
  22:11/q04/p1 It is worship based on doubt or weak conviction, liable to collapse. — reasons: Mujāhid, Qatāda and others give the contextual gloss 'doubt'; al-Wāḥidī distinguishes it from the literal edge. Weak grasp of evidence means a small objection can unsettle one. The edge or cliff and army flank image represents lack of firm footing; gratitude in ease and patience in hardship would stabilize worship.
  22:11/q04/p2 It is worship on one side only: when worldly wishes are met, not in adversity. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba calls it one way or creed; al-Azharī prefers the prosperity/adversity pair, and al-Wāḥidī says worship in both shows acceptance of the Creator's direction. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ says the following clauses explain the image. The soldier or cliff stays for booty and flees at danger. Ibn ʿĀshūr notes that the worship remains directed to God alone, as 22:12 contrasts it with calling on others.
  22:11/q04/p3 It is outward worship by the tongue without the heart. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan says religion has tongue and heart and this confession lacks the second; others describe a marginal or verbal Muslim whose worship serves immediate aims.
  22:11/q04/p4 It is a wager resting on one's own pledged effort and hoped-for reward. — reasons: Al-Wāsiṭī likens the worshipper to someone resting on a staked pledge; other inward accounts describe expecting payment proportional to deeds and failing to perceive God's favor in enabling worship.
  22:11/q04/p5 It is ambivalent service that tries to keep God and former deities together. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says such people obey only agreeable directives, seek benefits from ancestral deities during calamity, and try to stand with both God and Satan; genuine monotheism requires total submission. His 'edge' is distance from full entry into Islam, with 2:208 and 4:176 cited.
  22:11/q04/p6 It is worship with a single aim of worldly gain. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ says religion is used for gain, while Kur'an Yolu portrays a person seeking a god who grants wishes.
22:11/q05 What does the word ḥarf literally denote here? [حَرْفٍ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the root image is physical boundary or a transferred sense of mode; Abū al-Fatḥ disputes derivation from inclination.)
  22:11/q05/p1 An edge, side, margin, end or boundary. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī and Jishumī cite the sword's edge and related turning aside; Abū al-Fatḥ derives the root from sharpness or limits and adduces pungent food or plants and distancing; another derivation is inclination from the center. Linguists also use it for a letter as part of a word; al-Rāghib calls letters edges joined together.
  22:11/q05/p2 It can mean a face, aspect, way or condition. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba uses one way or school of thought; al-Dānī adduces this verse as an analogy for a linguistic mode in the seven aḥruf tradition.
22:11/q06 How should one answer the challenge 'on which letter do you worship'? [يَعْبُدُ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ · theology]
  22:11/q06/p1 One worships God alone without partner. — reasons: Muqātil gives this reply to a questioner.
22:11/q07 How is the seven-aḥruf tradition related to the word ḥarf here? [حَرْفٍ · readings] (turns on: Whether ḥarf means content aspect, language, or a transmitted reading form, and whether the cited reports are sound.)
  22:11/q07/p1 Seven aḥruf are seven modes or aspects, including kinds of content or divine names, distinct from permitted variant recitations. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya describes paths including permission and prohibition; Ubayy's prophetic report permits examples among divine names but not swapping punishment for mercy. Ibn al-Ṭayyib treats them as aspects of names. Al-Bāqillānī and Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr distinguish content categories from variant readings. Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr calls Ibn Masʿūd's report weak; al-Bayhaqī's disconnected chain is called good though Abū Salama never met Ibn Masʿūd. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says any original name-substitution permission was later abrogated and is now impermissible.
  22:11/q07/p2 The aḥruf mean seven distinct languages. — reasons: A reported view takes the number literally as languages; al-Qāḍī Abū Bakr objects that ʿUmar, Ubayy, Hishām and Ibn Masʿūd shared one language yet disputed readings.
  22:11/q07/p3 Every word that has variants must possess seven forms, some unattested. — reasons: One group's reasoning is that otherwise the seven-aḥruf hadith would not be fulfilled; missing forms may simply have no transmitted report.
  22:11/q07/p4 It suffices if one or two words have seven forms. — reasons: Another group says this fulfills the hadith's wording.
  22:11/q07/p5 Seven aḥruf are seven linguistic modes or vocalic allowances. — reasons: Al-Dānī uses ḥarf as a face or aspect, or a part naming the whole reading because a single letter may change. Ibn Qutayba says replacing a lifelong dialect is difficult, so God permitted latitude.
  22:11/q07/p6 The seven-content-category explanation is invalid. — reasons: Aḥmad ibn Abī ʿImrān and al-Ṭaḥāwī say it absurdly collapses all content into one category; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says categories are not letters and rulings cannot change; al-Māwardī notes the report concerns substitution of a letter, not a legal verse with a parable.
22:11/q08 What do the faʿla noun forms cited alongside this verse indicate? [فِتْنَةٌ · grammar]
  22:11/q08/p1 Faʿla can name a manner or kind of act, and its regular use can name one occurrence; lexical exceptions must be recognized. — reasons: Sībawayh distinguishes qatlah and ṭaʿmah for kinds from one event; the direct verbal base supplies the regular single-occurrence form because augmented verbal nouns are not uniformly productive.
