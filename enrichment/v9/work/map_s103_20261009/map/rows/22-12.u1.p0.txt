## THE CURRENT MAP
22:12/q01 Who is the person invoking besides God? [يَدْعُوا۟ · referent] (turns on: Whether the surrounding apostasy describes the caller specifically, and whether invocation of idols excludes a Jewish referent.)
  22:12/q01/p1 A worshipper who turns back to unbelief after a trial and then invokes idols. — reasons: Al-Tabari connects this with the precarious worshipper's trial and apostasy in 22:11; Ibn Zayd calls it disbelief after belief. Al-Wahidi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Qurtubi, al-Nasafi and Bursawi describe the same return to unbelief; Ibn Ashur makes this clause circumstantial to انقلب in 22:11.
  22:12/q01/p2 An idol worshipper or polytheist. — reasons: Muqatil and Yahya identify the caller through idol worship; al-Razi prefers a polytheist reading because the objects called upon are idols. Al-Alusi likewise says the idol reading makes a Jewish referent unlikely, although Jews took rabbis and monks as lords.
  22:12/q01/p3 Polytheists who attached themselves to the Messenger hypocritically. — reasons: Al-Razi offers this as the likely setting for the passage.
  22:12/q01/p4 People whose profession of faith lacks full commitment and who credit other forces with power over destiny. — reasons: Asad includes real or imagined forces in the failure of commitment.
  22:12/q01/p5 A Jew. — reasons: Al-Razi finds this unlikely because of idol worship; al-Alusi agrees even while noting that Jews took religious authorities as lords.
22:12/q02 What does يدعو mean here? [يَدْعُوا۟ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the verb describes the worshipful act, a plea for aid, or the act of naming or addressing.)
  22:12/q02/p1 To worship or serve the object. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya gives worship as one sense; al-Alusi prefers supplication as worship in this context. The many explanations of worship if served and abandonment if unserved use this sense.
  22:12/q02/p2 To invoke, plead with or seek help from the object, especially in distress. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya includes distress invocation; Ibn Kathir specifies help, victory and provision. Al-Alusi describes calling on a lifeless thing to rescue one from affliction. Islahi includes praying, pleading, beseeching, and seeking help or mercy; Antara's line illustrates calling or addressing someone.
  22:12/q02/p3 To call or name a thing by a name. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi offers this alternative and cites a verse of Ibn Ahmar in which something is called by a name.
22:12/q03 What is invoked besides God? [مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ مَا · referent] (turns on: Whether the object is identified concretely as an idol, extended to leaders, or generalized to perceived powers.)
  22:12/q03/p1 Idols, stones or other inert false gods. — reasons: Al-Farra names idols; al-Wahidi specifies stones. Al-Zajjaj says the idol neither hears nor sees. Al-Baydawi and al-Alusi stress inertness; the Study Quran cites 6:71, 10:18, 10:106, 20:89, 21:66, 25:55 and 26:73 for this common reading. Al-Razi prefers idols and thereby disfavors a Jewish referent.
  22:12/q03/p2 Idols or human leaders whom the worshipper follows. — reasons: Al-Jishumi reports both objects as the possible destination of the worshipper's turn; the leaders are associated with nearer harm than benefit.
  22:12/q03/p3 Real or imagined forces assigned decisive influence over destiny. — reasons: Asad applies the verse to forces to which the wavering believer grants divine qualities.
22:12/q04 What does invoking something besides God imply? [مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ · theology]
  22:12/q04/p1 It wrongly transfers worship, trust or control over harm and benefit away from God. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i says all besides God are beneath the possessor of every perfection; worship is owed through chosen action rather than compelled natural behavior. He also contrasts this reliance with the exclusive plea for divine help in 1:5. Islahi says only God governs harm and benefit and all reliance belongs to Him; Asad calls assigning decisive influence to other forces a grant of divine qualities. Al-Tabataba'i says the idol lacks awareness and will.
22:12/q05 Why is harm mentioned before benefit? [لَا يَضُرُّهُۥ وَمَا لَا يَنفَعُهُۥ · rhetoric]
  22:12/q05/p1 The order reflects the wavering person's wish to escape perceived harm from Islam and imagined anger from idols. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says the ensuing wording mocks the mistake of seeking relief from powerless idols.
22:12/q06 Why repeat ما before the denial of benefit? [مَا لَا يَضُرُّهُۥ وَمَا لَا يَنفَعُهُۥ · rhetoric]
  22:12/q06/p1 The repetition reinforces the object's inertness and treats the negations of harm and benefit separately. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i details both directions: it cannot cause harm or block benefit, and cannot provide benefit or avert harm; blame need not require the absence of both qualities together. Bursawi and al-Alusi say the repeated ma points to an inanimate object.
22:12/q07 In what sense can the invoked object neither harm nor benefit? [مَا لَا يَضُرُّهُۥ وَمَا لَا يَنفَعُهُۥ · interpretation]
  22:12/q07/p1 The object has no power to punish neglect or reward service, nor to remove distress or grant advantage. — reasons: Muqatil and al-Tabari locate ineffective punishment in this world and ineffective reward in the Hereafter. Al-Tha'labi, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Nasafi and Bursawi explain disobedience versus obedience or abandonment versus worship. Al-Zajjaj notes lack of hearing and sight; Ibn Ashur says seeking relief or advantage from it is mistaken. Al-Biqa'i adds that abandoning it cannot prevent God's beneficence and worship cannot secure its aid. Al-Tabataba'i grounds impotence in lack of awareness and will. Al-Suddi distinguishes the idol's impotence from harm arriving in the afterlife.
22:12/q08 What does ذلك point back to, and what does its wording emphasize? [ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ · rhetoric]
  22:12/q08/p1 It points to the invocation, emphatically identifying that act as the distant error. — reasons: Ibn Ashur identifies the antecedent as invocation and says the demonstrative, separating pronoun and restriction work as in 22:11. Asad gives the literal wording as 'this, this is the far-away straying' and refers to his explanation at 14:18. Samarra'i counts huwa 13 times in al-Hajj versus 7 in Luqman, including 8 versus 3 uses as a separating pronoun, to account for its strong stylistic presence here.
22:12/q09 What does the distant error mean? [ٱلضَّلَٰلُ ٱلْبَعِيدُ · meaning]
  22:12/q09/p1 An extreme, long or far departure from truth and guidance, pictured as being lost far into a wilderness. — reasons: Al-Farra calls it long error; al-Tabari, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi and Ibn al-Jawzi express distance from God's path, truth or guidance. Al-Baydawi, Bursawi and al-Alusi compare it to a traveler astray in a wilderness. Ibn Ashur says idolatry is an extreme degree of error because its objects have no utility; al-Biqa'i calls reliance on them gross folly.
22:12/q10 How does the clause about invocation connect to the preceding account? [يَدْعُوا۟ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the clause is syntactically dependent on the earlier turning back or starts an explanatory or additional sentence.)
  22:12/q10/p1 It is a circumstantial clause describing the person who turned back. — reasons: Ibn Ashur attaches it to the subject of انقلب in 22:11. Al-Alusi reports this analysis with the preceding material treated as parenthetical.
  22:12/q10/p2 It resumes as a new sentence exposing a further fault of the apostate. — reasons: Bursawi describes it as resuming the account of the apostate polytheist's great loss; al-Alusi lists a new sentence exposing another fault.
  22:12/q10/p3 It explains the scale of the preceding loss. — reasons: Al-Alusi gives this as a further possible connection.
22:12/q11 How can 22:12 deny harm and benefit while 22:13 says harm is nearer? [مَا لَا يَضُرُّهُۥ وَمَا لَا يَنفَعُهُۥ · links]
  22:12/q11/p1 The idol itself has no power, but worshipping it harms its devotee, especially through loss in the afterlife. — reasons: Kuran Yolu gives this explanation. Al-Tabataba'i attributes the harm and loss to the worshipper's own act, not the unaware idol; al-Suddi places the harm in the afterlife; Ibn Kathir says worldly harm is nearer than any benefit and next-world harm is certain. Kuran Yolu also reports unspecified linguistic explanations for the apparent difference.
22:12/q12 What inward lessons are drawn from reliance on other than God? [مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ · inward] (turns on: Which aspect of exclusive dependence on God is foregrounded.)
  22:12/q12/p1 Reliance on anything besides the Real is reliance on harm; reliance on God is reliance on the source of everything. — reasons: Ibn Ata gives the contrast as an inward application of the verse.
  22:12/q12/p2 God's decree of humiliation cannot be reversed by another, and the garment of Truth never changes. — reasons: Al-Sulami reports this from al-Sayyari.
22:12/q13 How do other verses and the surrounding passage illuminate this error? [* · links] (turns on: The notes draw different connections: parallel idol texts, the appeal in 1:5, continuing worship after apostasy, and the passage's contrast with the righteous.)
  22:12/q13/p1 Other passages expose the impotence of false objects of worship and the danger of seeking refuge with them. — reasons: Islahi invokes 22:73: such beings cannot create a fly or recover what it steals, and escape from God's trial ends in Hell. Samarra'i links this passage's denunciation to 22:13, 22:31, 22:71 and 22:73. The Study Quran supplies parallels at 6:71, 10:18, 10:106, 20:89, 21:66, 25:55 and 26:73.
  22:12/q13/p2 The appeal to other beings reverses the exclusive request for God's help. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i expressly compares the caller's reliance with 1:5.
  22:12/q13/p3 After turning from God, people still seek an object of worship and supplication, even one useless or harmful. — reasons: Kuran Yolu states this as the passage's human pattern.
