## THE CURRENT MAP
2:250/q01 What does barazū mean at the encounter? [بَرَزُوا۟ · meaning]
  2:250/q01/p1 They emerged from cover onto open, level ground, visible to the opposing army. — reasons: Al-Tabari relates tabarraza, going out to relieve oneself, to open ground and taghawwata to low ground. Al-Razi and al-Samin explain that mubāraza names exposed face-to-face combat; al-Biqa'i stresses leaving concealment. Al-Jishumi and al-Tabrisi gloss the root as appearing; al-Tabataba'i applies it to emerging for battle.
2:250/q02 Who came out to face Jalut? [بَرَزُوا۟ لِجَالُوتَ وَجُنُودِهِۦ · referent]
  2:250/q02/p1 Talut and the believers with him faced Jalut and his forces. — reasons: Al-Baghawi calls Jalut's forces polytheists; al-Biqa'i and Ibn Kathir stress the believers' small, weak force against a numerous, strong foe. Al-Bursevi says the frightened became brave before the collective prayer. Muqatil calls them the companions of the chamber.
2:250/q03 How does the lām in li-Jālūt work? [لِجَالُوتَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the prepositional phrase depends on the stated verb or an omitted circumstantial expression.)
  2:250/q03/p1 It attaches directly to barazū, expressing their advance toward Jalut. — reasons: Al-Samin reports this as one possible analysis.
  2:250/q03/p2 It belongs to an implied circumstantial phrase: they advanced intending to meet Jalut. — reasons: Al-Samin reports an omitted sense of intended encounter.
2:250/q04 Who was Jalut? [جَالُوتَ · referent] (turns on: The sources identify his people differently: Amalekites, Canaanites, or Philistines.)
  2:250/q04/p1 He was the king and commander of the Amalekites. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi give this identification. Al-Kashshaf traces him to ʿAmliq son of ʿAd and says his helmet weighed three hundred ratls; al-Qurtubi reports a one-mile shadow and a claimed Berber descent from him. Ibn Atiyya calls Jālūt a non-Arabic name adapted to Arabic.
  2:250/q04/p2 He was a Canaanite king in Syria during the Israelites' time. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i gives this identification.
  2:250/q04/p3 He commanded the powerful Philistines and was exceptionally large and strong. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu gives this description as the source of Israelite fear and demoralization.
2:250/q05 How large was Jalut's army? [جُنُودِهِۦ · reports] (turns on: Two transmitted counts give different numbers of troops.)
  2:250/q05/p1 It numbered three hundred thousand horsemen. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya cites a report; al-Qurtubi reports the same number and contrasts ʿIkrima's smaller figure.
  2:250/q05/p2 It numbered ninety thousand. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi transmits this figure from ʿIkrima in contrast to the three hundred thousand report.
2:250/q06 What is conveyed by calling God rabbanā? [رَبَّنَآ · rhetoric]
  2:250/q06/p1 It acknowledges their servitude and invokes God's nurturing lordship to reform and support them. — reasons: Al-Samin and al-Bursevi draw on the sense of rabb; al-Alusi emphasizes God's nurturing lordship.
2:250/q07 What does 'pour patience upon us' convey? [أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًۭا · meaning]
  2:250/q07/p1 It asks God to grant patience in a full, abundant outpouring, as though emptying a vessel over them. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, al-Zajjaj, al-Wahidi, al-Jishumi, al-Tabrisi and al-Razi explain the vessel image and the root's sense of emptiness; al-Jishumi cites 28:10. Muqatil compares pouring molten metal at 18:96. Al-Razi says the image implies all contents poured and made to remain. Al-Samin says ʿalā makes patience encompass them; al-Alusi adds cooling after withheld water and the indefinite noun magnifies patience. Ibn Ashur cites Abu Kabir al-Hudhali's poetry; al-Tabataba'i says divine imparting fits each person's capacity.
2:250/q08 What patience did the fighters seek? [صَبْرًۭا · interpretation]
  2:250/q08/p1 They sought self-restraint and endurance in battle, especially restraint from fleeing. — reasons: Al-Jishumi defines sabr as restraining the self from what attracts it and says the fighters sought endurance in jihad. Al-Tabrisi says restraint from flight is rewarded. Al-Bursevi names battle's hardships; al-Biqa'i wants patience sufficient for effective blows. Ibn Abi Hatim transmits ʿAli b. al-Husayn's broader report: the patient enter Paradise before reckoning for enduring obedience and avoiding sin.
2:250/q09 What does making their feet firm mean? [وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether firm feet chiefly pictures inner resilience or also refers to the ground underfoot.)
  2:250/q09/p1 It means steadfast hearts, courage and resolve so they hold their ground and do not flee. — reasons: Ibn Abi Hatim reports Ibn Ishaq's gloss. Al-Jishumi derives thabāt from remaining in place and refers to God's firm word supporting truth. Ibn Atiyya says firm feet evoke stability; Ibn Ashur and al-Tabataba'i explain the metaphor through slipping in fear versus planted feet. Al-Biqa'i adds forceful blows, and al-Bursevi and al-Alusi reject limiting it to physical immobility.
  2:250/q09/p2 It includes secure physical footing on slippery battlefield ground. — reasons: Abu Hayyan explicitly includes firm footing on slippery terrain among the requests at face-to-face battle.
2:250/q10 By what means might God steady them? [وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا · interpretation]
  2:250/q10/p1 God may strengthen the believers through courage and by weakening, frightening, dividing, or disabling their enemy. — reasons: Al-Wahidi allows strong hearts, enemy discord, fear and similar means. Al-Kashshaf names courage and terror; al-Nasafi names the two sides' hearts. Al-Qadi adds calamity, disease, a leader's death and guiding some enemies to see their error, causing disagreement.
2:250/q11 What aid do they ask for in 'help us'? [وَٱنصُرْنَا · meaning]
  2:250/q11/p1 They ask God for assistance and success against the unbelieving enemy, ending in its defeat and rout. — reasons: Al-Jishumi defines nasr as assistance and describes success in combat. Ibn Abi Hatim transmits Ibn Ishaq's gloss; al-Tabrisi describes aid against an enemy. Al-Alusi specifies the enemy's defeat and rout, and Ibn Atiyya traces the root of defeat from parts folding into one another to an army's retreat.
2:250/q12 How can divine victory be given? [وَٱنصُرْنَا · interpretation] (turns on: The notes give distinct ways divine aid can operate, one through battlefield conditions and another through angels.)
  2:250/q12/p1 God can strengthen believers and undermine the enemy through fear, weakness, apparent numbers, or division. — reasons: Al-Jishumi specifies stronger believing hearts, weaker unbelieving hearts, terror and discord. Al-Tabrisi adds exposing weakness and imagined numbers, calling victory a divine لطف while distinguishing it from other لطف.
  2:250/q12/p2 God can support believers through angels. — reasons: Al-Jishumi gives Badr as the parallel and cites 3:123.
2:250/q13 Who are 'the unbelieving people'? [ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلْكَٰفِرِينَ · referent]
  2:250/q13/p1 They are Jalut's people and troops. — reasons: Al-Jishumi identifies the group directly; the battle context supplies the same referent in al-Baghawi and al-Alusi.
2:250/q14 Why call the enemy 'the unbelieving people'? [ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلْكَٰفِرِينَ · rhetoric]
  2:250/q14/p1 It presents the battle as for God's cause and gives the reason the believers seek victory over them. — reasons: Al-Qushayri says they sought victory against God's enemies rather than revenge for personal loss; acting for and by God brought victory. Al-Biqa'i contrasts God's neglected rights with personal advantage and notes the enemy's power. Al-Alusi says the noun phrase instead of a pronoun states why victory is deserved.
2:250/q15 Why are patience, firm feet, and victory requested in this order? [أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًۭا وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا وَٱنصُرْنَا · rhetoric]
  2:250/q15/p1 Patience is the foundation, steadfast resistance follows, and victory is the final aim. — reasons: Al-Baydawi says victory usually follows firm footing. Al-Bursevi says firmness arises from patience. Al-Alusi adds that patience alone may lack resistance and courage without victory is fruitless; his reported alternative notes that wāw rather than fāʾ lets the mind infer the order, citing al-Sakkākī. Al-Razi relates the three requests to courage, enabling equipment and circumstances, and strength to overcome.
2:250/q16 Does the prayer make God the creator of patience and steadfastness? [أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًۭا وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا · theology] (turns on: Whether the requests name God as creator of the inner dispositions themselves or as creator of external causes that foster them.)
  2:250/q16/p1 Yes: it directly asks God for human resolve and the resulting steadfastness. — reasons: Al-Razi reports the Ashʿarite argument and adopts the apparent attribution: patience is resolve and firmness is stillness; departure from that apparent sense needs evidence. He argues that even God-given causes either change motives or do not, and either way the Muʿtazilite account cannot escape divine determination.
  2:250/q16/p2 The prayer asks God to create the external causes of patience and firmness. — reasons: Al-Qadi lists enemy fear, division, calamities, disease, a leader's death and some enemies' recognition of error as causes that embolden believers. Al-Razi says this figurative reading abandons the verse's apparent attribution without evidence; his motives argument is directed against it.
