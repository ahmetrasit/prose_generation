# 96:17/q01 Who is challenged to call for help? [فَلْيَدْعُ · referent]
turns on: Whether the command is restricted to the historical prohibitor or generalized from him.
- 96:17/q01/p1 Abū Jahl is the challenged man.
  reasons: Al-Māwardī identifies him directly. Al-Ṭabarī and later exegetes identify him from his ban on Muḥammad's prayer and boast of the valley's largest council; Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿIkrima and Abū Hurayra transmit this setting.

# 96:17/q02 What does the command dare him to do, and when? [فَلْيَدْعُ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the command addresses Abū Jahl's earthly boast in sequence or is read after the seizure and angelic summons in the hereafter.
- 96:17/q02/p1 He is dared now to summon his human supporters against Muḥammad or to rescue himself; God's angels will overpower or seize them.
  reasons: Al-Ṭabarī links his boast to God's counter-summons in 96:18. Al-Wāḥidī cites the threatened valley full of cavalry and men. Ibn ʿAbbās says angels would seize him immediately if he called. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī call the command taunting; Ibn ʿĀshūr says neither he nor his hearers dared act.

# 96:17/q03 What rhetorical force does the imperative and its fāʾ carry? [فَلْيَدْعُ · rhetoric]
turns on: The notes vary in whether the taunt stresses his failure to act, the impotence of his group, or the ironic claim of noble defenders.
- 96:17/q03/p1 It is a taunting challenge that exposes his inability to summon effective help.
  reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says God will not enable him and angels would seize him openly; al-Ālūsī quotes this and Ibn ʿĀshūr says 96:19's kallā confirms he will not carry it out. Al-Rāzī says the fāʾ marks a miracle because Abū Jahl dared not accept the challenge; Ibn ʿĀshūr likewise calls the unaccepted public challenge a Qurʾānic miracle.

# 96:17/q04 How is the command read in the reported variant? [فَلْيَدْعُ نَادِيَهُۥ · readings]
- 96:17/q04/p1 The reading attributed to Ibn Masʿūd says to call to his council and changes the following response to 'then I will call'.
  reasons: Al-Farrāʾ reports ʿAbd Allāh's reading with ilā before nādiyah and fa-sa-adʿū for the answering call; Ibn ʿAṭiyya independently reports Ibn Masʿūd's 'to his council'.

# 96:17/q05 Who or what is his nādī? [نَادِيَهُۥ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the term denotes the present assembly and its people, a specifically named social body or companion, or a symbolic inward resource; the lexical notes also distinguish consultation from generosity associations.
- 96:17/q05/p1 It is the people of his gathering, his companions, supporters, clan or wider faction whom he expects to help.
  reasons: Al-Farrāʾ says Arabs name a man's group by council, assembly, gathering or witness, and cites a poet. Abū ʿUbayda, Ibn Hishām and others distinguish the venue from its people: the place bears the name while they meet there. Ibn Hishām cites 29:29, 19:73, 12:82 and verses by ʿUbayd, Salama and al-Kumayt. Jarīr, Zuhayr and an unnamed poet supply council and station parallels; Abū Ḥayyān observes Jarīr's adjectives describe attendees, not masonry. Muʾarrij gives Quraysh usage; Ibn ʿĀshūr notes a daytime gathering, unlike night sāmir (23:67). Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites 68:21, 40:32, 29:29 and 19:73 for related root uses. Elmalılı compares an assembly, congress or parliament and proposes Turkish kurultay. Al-Iṣlāḥī says a linked communal group or party. The cited poetic wording and attribution vary, including Jarīr versus Dhū al-Rumma.
  holders (123 notes): MUJAHID(مجاهد); MUQATIL; FARRA; MAJAZ; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); IBNHISHAM(ابن هشام); A
