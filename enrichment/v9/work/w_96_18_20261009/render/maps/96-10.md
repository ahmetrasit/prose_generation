# 96:10: 20 questions

## 96:10/q01 Who is the praying servant, and who forbids him?  [عَبْدًا · referent]
turns on: The historical identification and Meccan chronology, versus the indefinite wording's general reach.
- 96:10/q01/p1 The servant is Muhammad and the forbider is Abu Jahl. — Mujahid, Qatada, Ibn Abbas, and many commentators identify this pair; Ibn Atiyya calls it a consensus. Tabari places the prayer at the Maqam, and Ibn Kathir at the House. Razi says the prior context and following verses point to this specific man. Ibn Ashur says the threat could have been meant to reach Muhammad rather than said face to face. [61 notes: MUQATIL; TAB(ابن عباس,قتادة,مجاهد); TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,قتادة,مجاهد); ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(مجاهد); THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(ابن عباس وقتادة); BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA(المفسرين); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(المفسرون); ABUHAYYAN(ابن عطية); IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; DURR(ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); ITQAN; SUYUTI-LUBAB(ابن عباس); ALUSI(ابن عطية); ELMALILI(İbn Atıyye); IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES(classical commentators); BINTSHATI(جمهرة المفسرين); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Müfessirlerin çoğunluğu); ALUSI-FULL(المفسرون)+; NASAFI-FULL+; NASAFI+; QURTUBI-FULL+; QURTUBI+; RAZI+; TAB+]
- 96:10/q01/p2 The servant is Salman and the forbider is Umayya ibn Khalaf. — Al-Hasan reports this pair. Alusi and Elmalili object that Salman converted in Medina after the Hijra, whereas the sura is Meccan; Alusi allows that the verse's general ruling could still cover such an episode. [9 notes: RAZI(الحسن); ABUHAYYAN(الحسن); ALUSI(الحسن); BINTSHATI(الحسن البصري); ALUSI(الحسن)-; ALUSI-FULL(الحسن)-; ELMALILI(Hasan)-]
- 96:10/q01/p3 The description covers any servant of God opposed in prayer, including Muhammad and other Muslims. — Jishumi reports a general alternative to naming Abu Jahl; Razi says a specific occasion can have general application. Islahi says multiple Quraysh ruffians stopped Muhammad and other servants. Ibn Ashur makes the individual generic to focus attention on the act; Qashani reads the servant as one present and steady in worship. [6 notes: JISHUMI(أبو جهل); RAZI; QASHANI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNASHUR-FULL+; IBNASHUR+]
- 96:10/q01/p4 The servant may be the preacher himself. — Neuwirth offers this as a conjecture. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Neuwirth)]

## 96:10/q02 What does ʿabd signify here?  [عَبْدًا · meaning]
turns on: Whether social subordination is included alongside religious servanthood.
- 96:10/q02/p1 God's servant, devoted to worship. — Nöldeke and Schwally insist on God's servant rather than merely a human being; their social reading also draws on inner-textual signs of unequal rank at 96:17. Razi and Biqai emphasize complete devotion and service to the divine Master. [3 notes: RAZI; BIQAI-FULL; CORPUSCORANICUM(Nöldeke and Schwally)+]
- 96:10/q02/p2 The word admits both a worldly slave and a worshipper of God. — Corpus Coranicum contrasts the worldly view of a slave with the believer's recognition that obedience to God can outrank obedience to an earthly master, with 96:19 as context. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 96:10/q03 Why call him an indefinite 'servant' rather than address Muhammad directly?  [عَبْدًا · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the indefiniteness primarily magnifies Muhammad, generalizes the worshipper, or has no special rhetorical force.
- 96:10/q03/p1 The indefinite servant magnifies Muhammad's perfect devotion and the outrage of obstructing prayer. — Baydawi, Biqai, Bursevi, and Alusi say the form heightens the ugliness of forbidding a servant's service to his Master. Razi says an indefinite yet uniquely identifiable servant magnifies the Prophet, citing 97:1, 17:1 and 72:19; his account of a learned Jew and Ali cites 28:60 and 68:4. Razi also says it may portray the forbider's habit toward anyone. Bursevi notes the avoidance of direct 'you'. [10 notes: RAZI; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI(قيل)]
- 96:10/q03/p2 The generic designation makes the act itself astonishing regardless of which worshipper is targeted. — Ibn Ashur says the wording deliberately generalizes Muhammad to anyone devoted to God, citing 17:5; its wonder lies in forbidding prayer itself. [2 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:10/q03/p3 The nunation may simply retain its ordinary indefinite sense. — Alusi records this alternative without a further argument. [1 notes: ALUSI(بعض)]

## 96:10/q04 What prayer is meant by 'when he prays'?  [إِذَا صَلَّىٰٓ · referent]
turns on: The reported noon occasion, the date when five prayers became obligatory, and the verse's unrestricted wording.
- 96:10/q04/p1 The noon prayer. — Mawardi holds this; al-Tabrizi is cited by Abu Hayyan and others. Elmalili says the report calls it noon prayer without claiming it was obligatory, and associates a first public prayer after Umar's conversion with it. [14 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(المفسرون); ABUHAYYAN(التبريزي); ALUSI(أبو حيان); ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان); ELMALILI(Tebrizî); MAWARDI+]
- 96:10/q04/p2 An early, publicly performed prayer with prostration, before the five daily prayers were obligated. — Ibn Ashur prefers an unregulated pre-obligation prayer; al-Harbi says the Prophet prayed before sunset and sunrise before the Night Journey, with Yahya ibn Sallam and al-Suhayli giving related chronology. [3 notes: IBNASHUR(الحربي,يحيى بن سلام); IBNASHUR+]
- 96:10/q04/p3 Prayer generally, whether prescribed or voluntary. — Elmalili says the verse's unrestricted wording covers every legally prescribed or voluntary prayer, even if the occasion was noon prayer. [1 notes: ELMALILI]

## 96:10/q05 What happened around this act of forbidding prayer?  [إِذَا صَلَّىٰٓ · reports]
turns on: The reports differ in the attempted act and what Abu Jahl perceived; some describe verbal confrontation, others a thwarted physical approach.
- 96:10/q05/p1 Abu Jahl threatened to trample Muhammad at prayer and recoiled from a fiery trench, terror, and wings; angels would have seized him. — Abu Hurayra supplies the full threat, retreat and angelic warning; Abu Jahl swears by al-Lat and al-Uzza in Mawardi, Alusi and Elmalili. Baghawi and Ibn Kathir note uncertainty whether the revelation notice belongs to Abu Hurayra's report. Mawardi cites Tirmidhi's grade hasan sahih gharib; Ibn Kathir lists Ahmad, Muslim, Nasa'i and Ibn Abi Hatim through Mu'tamir. Some accounts say neck and face; Bursevi says Abu Jahl saw wings but not angels' persons. Variants place him at the Maqam or Kaaba, and describe his attempted action as approach, threat, or physical harassment. [50 notes: FARRA; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); IBNHISHAM(أبو جهل); MUSNAD-AHMAD(ابن عباس); TAB(قتادة); TAB-FULL(أبو هريرة,ابن عباس,قتادة); ZAJJAJ; THALABI(أبو هريرة,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); MAWARDI(النبي); WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI(أبو هريرة); BAGHAWI-FULL(أبو هريرة); KASHSHAF-FULL(أبو جهل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو هريرة); RAZI(أبو جهل); QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو هريرة); BAYDAWI(أبو جهل); BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو هريرة); DURR(أبو هريرة); DURR-FULL(قتادة); BURSEVI(أبو جهل); ALUSI(أبو هريرة); ALUSI-FULL(أبو هريرة); ELMALILI(Ebû Hüreyre); IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو هريرة); BINTSHATI(الطبري)]
- 96:10/q05/p2 Abu Jahl confronted the Prophet at prayer, forbade him verbally, boasted of his council, and was warned of God's guards. — Ibn Abbas's versions have Abu Jahl ask whether he had not forbidden the Prophet, threaten to summon cavalry or footmen, boast of his clan, or vow to kill him at the Maqam; angels would seize him if he advanced. Tirmidhi authenticates the explicit speech report, according to Alusi. Some versions extend revelation through 96:16 or to 96:18. [24 notes: MUSNAD-AHMAD(ابن عباس); TAB(ابن عباس); TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); THALABI(ابن عباس); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس); DURR(ابن عباس); ALUSI(ابن عباس); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); ELMALILI(Tirmizî)]
- 96:10/q05/p3 Abu Jahl attacked or intended to attack the praying Prophet by other means. — Ibn Abbas says he threw camel entrails on Muhammad during prostration. Another report in Bursevi's al-Takmila says he meant to drop a stone at noon prayer and fled after seeing a fiery trench and angels' wings. [8 notes: DURR(ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); BURSEVI]
- 96:10/q05/p4 Abu Jahl saw dark battalions between himself and Muhammad when trying to kill him. — Ibn Abbas says Abu Jahl threatened to kill Muhammad at the prayer place; the battalions would have been angels who seized him. [1 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس)]

## 96:10/q06 Was the prayer a first congregation, and who attended?  [إِذَا صَلَّىٰٓ · reports]
turns on: The attendance list differs, and Alusi questions Abu Talib's presence because of the date of obligatory prayer.
- 96:10/q06/p1 It may have been Islam's first congregational prayer, with Muhammad and Ali; Abu Talib brought Jaʿfar to support them. — Jaʿfar ibn Muhammad's account in Mawardi has Muhammad praying with Ali; Abu Talib passes with Jaʿfar, urges him to join, leaves rejoicing, and composes verses praising Ali and Jaʿfar's loyalty. [4 notes: MAWARDI(أبو طالب,جعفر بن محمد)]
- 96:10/q06/p2 It was Islam's first congregation, attended by Muhammad, Abu Bakr, Ali, and other early believers; Abu Talib and Jaʿfar passed by. — The Abu Hayyan, Alusi, and Tabrizi accounts add Abu Bakr and sometimes other early Muslims. Abu Talib urges support or Jaʿfar's joining and recites verses vowing not to abandon the Prophet; versions differ as to who rejoiced. [17 notes: ABUHAYYAN(أبو طالب,قيل); ALUSI(أبو حيان,أبو طالب,قيل); ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان); ELMALILI(Ebû Tâlib,Tebrizî); ALUSI-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 96:10/q07 What is the force and form of forbidding in this verse?  [عَبْدًا إِذَا صَلَّىٰٓ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the narrated threat itself or explicit speech constitutes the prohibition.
- 96:10/q07/p1 The forbider attempts to stop prayer, including an explicit verbal prohibition. — Jishumi glosses forbidding as refraining from an act; Alusi says the wording suggests Abu Jahl had power only to forbid, while Ibn Abbas's authenticated report has him explicitly say he had forbidden the Prophet. Ibn Ashur says the prohibited act is omitted because prayer supplies it. [9 notes: JISHUMI; ALUSI(ابن عباس,قيل); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES]
- 96:10/q07/p2 The forbidding is a threat meant to reach Muhammad, even without face-to-face prohibition. — Ibn Ashur describes Abu Jahl's threatened trampling at the Kaaba as a communicated threat rather than direct speech. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 96:10/q08 What does the phrase 'when he prays' add?  [إِذَا صَلَّىٰٓ · rhetoric]
- 96:10/q08/p1 It locates the prohibition precisely while the servant is engaged in prayer. — Alusi says the temporal restriction suggests interruption of an ongoing prayer. [1 notes: ALUSI]
- 96:10/q08/p2 It emphasizes the evil of obstructing the servant's highest act of worship. — Biqai says prayer joins nearness to God and withdrawal from creation, giving God His due; obstructing it can arise from envy or rebellion. Islahi calls prayer a person's foremost duty to God. [3 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 96:10/q09 Why use the imperfect verb for a past act of forbidding?  [عَبْدًا إِذَا صَلَّىٰٓ · rhetoric]
- 96:10/q09/p1 It makes the past scene vivid and strange as though happening now. — Alusi and Ibn Ashur explain the present-tense form this way; Ibn Ashur also notes the object of forbidding is left implicit from prayer. [3 notes: ALUSI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 96:10/q10 What does the denunciation of forbidding prayer teach?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes distinguish the immediate Abu Jahl occasion from the wording's broader moral reach and Qashani's inward reading.
- 96:10/q10/p1 Anyone who obstructs prayer, obedience, or other worship shares the warning. — Razi and Alusi say Abu Jahl's occasion is particular but the warning general; Bursevi cites Abu al-Layth and Ibn al-Shaykh. Jishumi extends blame to stopping obedience or guidance and infers enjoining right. Alusi and Elmalili include verbal and practical obstruction, even distracting a worshipper; Elmalili includes prescribed and voluntary prayers and people like Bilal. Biqai sees envy and rebellion in denying God's due; Asad applies it to privatizing religion or dismissing metaphysical guidance, and Kuranyolu to tyrants obstructing social goodness, truth, and justice. Qashani calls the forbider veiled and self-satisfied; Islahi relates it to earlier rebellion. [23 notes: QUSHAYRI; JISHUMI; RAZI; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; QASHANI; BURSEVI(أبو الليث,ابن الشيخ); ALUSI; ELMALILI; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BINTSHATI(الفخر الرازي); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR+]
- 96:10/q10/p2 The immediate rebuke addresses Abu Jahl's audacity and denial despite God's favors. — Tabari says the man opposing Muhammad rejects God's favor; Ibn Kathir says God begins with gentle admonition. Razi notes Abu Jahl's nickname Abu al-Hakam makes his idolatry and obstruction ironic, and contrasts his authority with the true Creator. Bursevi says pagan people called him wise but Islam named him Abu Jahl; Muhammad had prayed for Islam through Abu Jahl or Umar, and Umar's conversion exposes the contrast. Ibn Ashur says the preceding transgression prepares for this incident. [14 notes: TAB; TAB-FULL; RAZI; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:10/q10/p3 The rebuke presents a veiled, wealthy and self-satisfied opponent resisting attentive worship. — Qashani gives this inward description of the ignorant forbider, whose wealth and clan veil him, and the worshipper present and steady before God. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 96:10/q11 Does this warning forbid objecting to every prayer practice?  [إِذَا صَلَّىٰٓ · law]
- 96:10/q11/p1 One may object to an accompanying wrong, such as a usurped place or disliked time, while avoiding direct prohibition of prayer itself. — Ibn al-Shaykh, Alusi and Elmalili distinguish prayer from its unlawful circumstance; Ibn al-Shaykh says an invalid attribute does not invalidate underlying prayer. Ali avoided forbidding people praying before Eid, saying only that he had not seen the Prophet do so; Abu Hanifa likewise gave Abu Yusuf the prescribed praise after bowing without expressly forbidding his proposed forgiveness formula. [16 notes: RAZI(أبو حنيفة,علي); BURSEVI(أبو حنيفة,ابن الشيخ,علي); ALUSI(أبو حنيفة,أمير المؤمنين,علي); ELMALILI(Hazret-i Ali,İmâm-ı A‘zam Ebû Hanîfe)]

## 96:10/q12 How do the questions and clauses around this phrase function rhetorically?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: How much unstated warning or staged progression the repeated questions carry.
- 96:10/q12/p1 They invite astonished attention, rebuke the forbider, and warn that God sees him. — Wahidi says repeated 'have you seen?' intensifies amazement; Kashshaf paraphrases it as 'tell me about' the forbider. Farra contrasts the guided worshipper with the denier; Ibn Atiyya says the closing 'Does he not know?' supplies a compressed threat for three conditional formulations. Qutb describes quick, broken touches that make listeners witness escalating ugliness; Islahi compares 'just look at him'. Alusi says separated clauses give each point independent force. [15 notes: TAB; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-QT; KASHSHAF; ABUHAYYAN(ابن عطية,الفراء); ALUSI; QUTB-ZILAL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BINTSHATI]
- 96:10/q12/p2 An implied question asks whether the forbidding man is safe from punishment. — Qurtubi explicitly supplies this ellipsis. [3 notes: QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL]
- 96:10/q12/p3 The questions successively expose tyranny, lost guidance, and deserved punishment. — Isam al-Din assigns these stages to 96:9–14. Alusi distances himself from the elaboration and leaves the intended meaning to God. [1 notes: ALUSI(عصام الدين); ALUSI-]

## 96:10/q13 Who is addressed by the surrounding 'have you seen?' questions?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether consistent pronouns favor the Prophet or the discourse turns to the forbider or a general audience.
- 96:10/q13/p1 The Prophet is addressed. — Razi relates his earlier prayer for Islam through Abu Jahl or Umar. Abu Hayyan prefers the Messenger because the pronouns stay consistent; Alusi cites al-Bahr's preference. [5 notes: RAZI; ALUSI(الإمام); ABUHAYYAN+]
- 96:10/q13/p2 Anyone capable of seeing or understanding is addressed. — Bursevi takes seeing as visual; Alusi records a group who address any capable hearer and another view addressing humanity as at 96:8. Ibn Ashur leaves the addressee unspecified and allows visual witnessing of the event. [4 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI(جمع,قيل); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:10/q13/p3 The disbelieving forbider is addressed. — Abu Hayyan reports this reading: if the worshipper's prayer is guidance and a summons to piety, would the disbeliever still forbid him? The conditional pronouns then refer to the forbider; Alusi says others allow it. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN(قيل); ALUSI]

## 96:10/q14 To whom do the conditional pronouns in the next verses refer?  [* · grammar]
turns on: Whether the conditional subject continues the worshipper or turns ironically to the forbider.
- 96:10/q14/p1 The guided and pious one is the worshipper, while the denier is the forbider. — Ibn Atiyya and Farra take 'if he is on guidance' of the worshipper; Farra links prayer with piety because Muhammad reforms himself in prayer and others through calling to God. Qatada identifies Muhammad as guided and Abu Jahl as denier; Ibn Ashur says context assigns the different antecedents and leaves the object of denial implicit. [14 notes: ABUHAYYAN(ابن عطية والفراء,الفراء); DURR(قتادة); DURR-FULL(قتادة); IBNASHUR]
- 96:10/q14/p2 The conditions ironically consider the forbider's possible guidance and his actual denial. — Zamakhshari asks whether the forbider, if he were guided and commanded what he considers piety, or instead denied and turned away, knows that God sees and requites him. Alusi's Imam considers whether the sensible, wealthy man would have been better choosing guidance and piety. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN(الزمخشري); ALUSI(الإمام)]

## 96:10/q15 How is the repeated 'have you seen?' construction parsed?  [* · grammar]
turns on: Whether 'seeing' is visual or cognitive, whether a second object is required, and whether a conditional interrogative answer requires fa.
- 96:10/q15/p1 The first relative clause is an object, with later objects and final interrogative supplied by ordinary ellipsis. — Abu Hayyan makes the relative clause the first object's content, recovers omitted later objects from the servant or forbider, and says omission of clauses is not syntactic competition. Alusi similarly recovers pronouns or demonstratives and the final 'Does he not know?' as second object, calling it semantic government rather than tanazuʿ. Abu Hayyan argues the second object must be interrogative, citing 53:33–35, 19:77–78 and 56:58–59. [9 notes: ABUHAYYAN; ALUSI; BINTSHATI; ABUHAYYAN+; ALUSI+; ALUSI-]
- 96:10/q15/p2 The relative and conditional clauses fill two objects, one repeated question is emphatic, and a later clause supplies the conditional answer. — Zamakhshari offers this syntax; Bint Shati reports his treatment of the verse 13 conditional as the second object and verse 14 as answer. Abu Hayyan and Bint Shati object that an interrogative conditional answer requires fa except in poetic necessity; Alusi rejects the claim that the second object must always be interrogative, citing Sibawayh's reported view. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN(الزمخشري); BINTSHATI(الزمخشري); ALUSI(قيل); ABUHAYYAN-; BINTSHATI-]
- 96:10/q15/p3 Interrogative 'have you seen?' need not have an express or inferred second object. — Bint Shati cites Meccan examples at 107:1, 19:77, 25:43, 45:23 and 53:33: the question directs attention to a lesson or warning. She urges attention to Quranic usage even when school grammar would force a conjectural object. [3 notes: BINTSHATI]
- 96:10/q15/p4 The first seeing is visual and takes no second object, while the later two are inward and take conditional objects. — Alusi reports this alternate analysis; another reported view takes the opening and verse 13 as inward seeing. Ibn Atiyya calls the opening an interrogative challenge rather than two-object knowledge vision. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA; ALUSI(قيل)]

## 96:10/q16 How does this scene fit the sura's revelation sequence and earlier verses?  [* · links]
turns on: The dating of the Night Journey and what kinds of prayer existed before the five daily prayers.
- 96:10/q16/p1 The Abu Jahl episode follows the opening revelation, while the preceding account of human transgression prepares for it. — Ibn Ashur says a short pause followed the first verses and that adjacent recited verses normally preserve revelation order absent clear proof otherwise. He sees 96:6–8 as preparation for the Abu Jahl scene at 96:9. Asad places the later passage at least a year after 96:1–5; Bint Shati says later public preaching and prayer suit the episode. [5 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; BINTSHATI; IBNASHUR+]
- 96:10/q16/p2 The prayer reference creates a chronological difficulty for immediate revelation after the first five verses. — Some reason from the later Night Journey obligation of five prayers to an intervening revelation or to denying that this sura was first. Bint Shati says the Abu Jahl occasion presupposes public preaching and threats. Ibn Ashur reports both alternatives without deciding for them. [3 notes: IBNASHUR(بعضهم); IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI]
- 96:10/q16/p3 Earlier non-five-daily prayer could explain the episode before the Night Journey. — Elmalili objects to Alusi's chronology: the Night Journey established five daily prayers, but a night vigil or noon prayer could have been prescribed earlier. Ibn Ashur prefers an unregulated earlier prayer including prostration; al-Harbi and Yahya ibn Sallam describe prayer before sunset and sunrise. [5 notes: ELMALILI; IBNASHUR(الحربي,يحيى بن سلام); ELMALILI+; IBNASHUR+]
- 96:10/q16/p4 Abu Talib's presence in a purported first congregation conflicts with common Night Journey and death chronologies. — Alusi says obligatory prayer began on the Night Journey, usually placed one year before Hijra, while Abu Talib died roughly three years before it, three or five days before Khadija. Ibn Hazm claims consensus at one year; Ibn Faris gives one year three months and al-Suddi one year five months. Al-Zuhri instead dates it five years after the mission, favored by Nawawi and Qurtubi but contested. Elmalili rejects the inference that no earlier prayer existed. [11 notes: ALUSI(ابن حزم,ابن فارس,الزهري,السدي); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI(Âlûsî); ELMALILI-]
- 96:10/q16/p5 The move from recitation to prayer is apt because recitation is a pillar of prayer. — Elmalili says prayer is the foundation of worship and the pillar of religion, and takes the movement as evidence that the later verses followed legislation of prayer. [1 notes: ELMALILI]

## 96:10/q17 What ancillary reports or literary observations accompany this verse?  [* · reports]
turns on: These are distinct ancillary observations rather than competing accounts.
- 96:10/q17/p1 Abu Jahl is called this community's Pharaoh. — Qatada reports that every nation has a Pharaoh; al-Hasan attributes the comparison to the Prophet. [6 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB(قتادة); TAB-FULL(قائل غير مسمى); MAWARDI(الحسن)]
- 96:10/q17/p2 The sura's reciter receives a reward like reciting all the short suras. — Baydawi Full attributes this virtue report to the Prophet. [1 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 96:10/q17/p3 A verse ending need not finish a semantic unit; its sense may depend on adjacent wording. — Samarrai states that meaning can cross a fāṣila in either direction. [2 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR-MUH; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 96:10/q18 What does the preceding kalla repudiate?  [* · links]
- 96:10/q18/p1 It rebukes the one who forbids a servant's prayer, placing the repudiated act after the particle. — Ibn Ashur says kalla can open before its target although it ordinarily follows rejected speech; he cites an initial negative particle in Imru al-Qays as a parallel. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 96:10/q19 Which passage is reported to have been revealed about Abu Jahl's forbidding?  [* · sciences]
turns on: The reported starting point of the later revelation.
- 96:10/q19/p1 The passage starting 'Have you seen the one who forbids' was revealed about his threat. — Ibn Ashur cites Tabari for this start point. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(الطبري)]
- 96:10/q19/p2 All but the sura's opening passage was revealed after Abu Jahl forbade Muhammad's prayer at the Maqam. — Qutb attributes this to sound reports. [1 notes: QUTB-ZILAL(الروايات الصحيحة)]

## 96:10/q20 How do the later assembly and guards relate to the forbidding episode?  [* · links]
- 96:10/q20/p1 Abu Jahl's assembly is his clan, while the summoned guards are angels. — Mujahid makes both identifications; Qatada identifies the assembly as Abu Jahl's people and gives zabaniya the Arabic sense of police. Repeated Durr extracts preserve these details across the sura. [6 notes: DURR(مجاهد)]
