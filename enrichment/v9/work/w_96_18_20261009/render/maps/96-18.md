# 96:18: 20 questions

## 96:18/q01 How is 'We will summon' read?  [سَنَدْعُ · readings]
turns on: The verb's agent and voice, and the wording of the preceding challenge.
- 96:18/q01/p1 The common reading has active first-person plural sanadʿu: God summons the zabāniya. — Abū Ḥayyān calls this the majority reading; al-Samīn calls the plural majestic. Al-Farrāʾ and the codex discussions cite its consonantal spelling. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN(الجمهور); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; FARRA]
- 96:18/q01/p2 Ibn Abī ʿAbla reads the verb passively, sayudʿā or sutdaʿā: the zabāniya are summoned. — Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī specify nominative al-zabāniya; al-Rāzī reports the passive form. [9 notes: KASHSHAF(ابن أبي عبلة); KASHSHAF-FULL(ابن أبي عبلة); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN(ابن أبي عبلة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن أبي عبلة); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI(ابن أبي عبلة); ALUSI-FULL(ابن أبي عبلة)]
- 96:18/q01/p3 A reading attributed to Ibn Masʿūd has fa-sa-adʿū, 'then I will call,' after fal-yadʿu ilā nādīhi. — Al-Farrāʾ reports the fuller variant; Ibn ʿAṭiyya records the added ilā in the preceding challenge. [2 notes: FARRA(عبد الله); IBNATIYYA(ابن مسعود)]

## 96:18/q02 What is the grammatical mood of sanadʿu?  [سَنَدْعُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the preceding imperative governs God's answering verb.
- 96:18/q02/p1 It is indicative: no subjunctive or jussive governor precedes it. — Al-Ālūsī rejects the proposed jussive response to the command as weak; he and his full version note the independent clause. [2 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-]
- 96:18/q02/p2 It is jussive as the answer to the taunting command to summon the council. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the challenged man's summons is answered by God's counter-summons, and the future prefix strengthens the response. [2 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 96:18/q03 Why does the muṣḥaf write sanadʿu without its final wāw?  [سَنَدْعُ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the spelling reflects pronunciation, economy, rhetorical force, or the pause form.
- 96:18/q03/p1 The spelling follows loss of wāw in connected pronunciation before a quiescent consonant. — Al-Farrāʾ cites yadʿu al-insān and three poetic parallels; Abū Ḥayyān, Ibn Khālawayh as quoted by Bursawī, al-Ālūsī and Ibn ʿĀshūr explain the meeting of two vowelless letters. Ibn ʿĀshūr notes it is no verse-end pause. [8 notes: FARRA; ABUHAYYAN(الجمهور); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI(ابن خالويه); ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:18/q03/p2 The wāw is omitted for abbreviation, lightness, or conformity to the preceding fal-yadʿu. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya says abbreviation; Bursawī and al-Ālūsī mention parallelism. Biqāʿī observes all ʿUthmānic codices omit it and sees no phonetic necessity. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; BURSEVI; BIQAI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 96:18/q03/p3 The omission suggests swift, easy divine action, an immediate response, and violent seizure. — Al-Zarkashī compares 54:50; al-Marrākushī generalizes omitted letters as speed and force, and al-Suyūṭī applies it to the guards. The severe end of the threat is left unstated. [3 notes: BURHAN; ITQAN(المراكشي)]
- 96:18/q03/p4 The omission emphasizes that angels have no independent power; strength belongs to God. — Biqāʿī presents this tentatively as one explanation of the ʿUthmānic spelling. [2 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL]
- 96:18/q03/p5 The spelling evokes an imperative, making the summons and response inevitable. — Biqāʿī links the prefixed sīn with certainty; Bursawī likewise suggests inevitability as one possibility. [3 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 96:18/q03/p6 The lightest call suffices to release irresistible angels. — Biqāʿī says a strengthened summons would imply an even more overwhelming force. [2 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL]
- 96:18/q03/p7 The omitted letter is explained by the pause form. — Al-Zarkashī notes that Yaʿqūb's pause retaining wāw shows the underlying letter. [2 notes: BURHAN(يعقوب)]

## 96:18/q04 How is the omitted wāw treated when recitation stops?  [سَنَدْعُ · readings]
turns on: Whether a stop follows written spelling, underlying form, or is discouraged.
- 96:18/q04/p1 Readers normally pause according to the written form, without restoring wāw. — Ibn al-Jazarī says all readers follow the rasm in four forms with omitted wāw before a still letter; his discussion of plural mīm uses sanadʿu as a comparable connected spelling. Al-Suyūṭī lists exceptional stop spellings. [4 notes: NASHR(جميع القراء); ITQAN; BURHAN(يعقوب)]
- 96:18/q04/p2 A route from Yaʿqūb pauses with the underlying wāw. — Al-Dānī transmitted it from two teachers; Ibn al-Jazarī says he read the individual route. Al-Zarkashī uses Yaʿqūb's pause as evidence for the underlying wāw. [2 notes: NASHR(يعقوب عن أبي الفتح وأبي الحسن); BURHAN]
- 96:18/q04/p3 Makkī and others discourage pausing on these forms. — They feared violating either rasm or underlying form; Ibn al-Jazarī rejects the restriction because emergency stops and sound transmitted readings are permissible. He likewise rejects Makkī's analogous prohibition at hāʾum as error. [1 notes: NASHR(مكي وغيره); NASHR-]

## 96:18/q05 What does the future prefix in sanadʿu convey?  [سَنَدْعُ · rhetoric]
- 96:18/q05/p1 It announces a certain divine promise to avenge and help the Prophet, even after a delay. — Al-Rāzī says future sīn does not imply doubt; he cites the Prophet's assurance, 'I will help you, even after a time.' Biqāʿī also sees certainty and establishment in sīn. [5 notes: RAZI(النبي); RAZI-FULL; BIQAI]

## 96:18/q07 Where does the name zabāniya come from?  [ٱلزَّبَانِيَةَ · meaning]
turns on: The Arabic pushing root versus proposed foreign origins and uncertain attestation.
- 96:18/q07/p1 It derives from zabn, forceful pushing or repelling. — Ibn Qutayba, Jishumī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Qurṭubī and others say they drive sinners into Hell. Evidence includes a camel kicking its milker, war pushing people in poems and ʿUtba's saying, muzābana sales, and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ's fierce zābin. [50 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB(غيره); TUSTARI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيدة); JISHUMI(ابن عباس وقتادة ومجاهد والضحاك); BAGHAWI(ابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن عباس); KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA(عتبة بن أبي سفيان); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الشاعر,عتبة بن أبي سفيان); TABRISI(أبو عبيدة والكسائي والأخفش,الشاعر); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة وابن دريد); RAZI(أبو عبيدة والمبرد,قتادة); RAZI-FULL(أبو عبيدة والمبرد,قتادة); QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL(قتادة); BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN(عتبة بن أبي سفيان); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(عيسى بن عمر); SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI(الأخفش); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 96:18/q07/p2 It may be a loanword connected with Syriac guides of the dead. — Jeffery proposes this derivation; Corpus Coranicum considers foreign etymologies less convincing. [2 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Jeffery; Eilers)]
- 96:18/q07/p3 It may come from a Persian word for flame. — Jeffery also proposes this; Corpus Coranicum rates the foreign proposals less convincing. [2 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Jeffery; Eilers)]
- 96:18/q07/p4 It may come from Middle Persian for jailers. — Eilers proposes this; Corpus Coranicum considers it less convincing than an ancient Arabic poetic attestation. [2 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Jeffery; Eilers)]
- 96:18/q07/p5 The word's origin and exact meaning remain uncertain. — Corpus Coranicum marks the traditional hell-warden sense as an interpretation and cites competing derivations. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 96:18/q08 Who or what are the zabāniya?  [ٱلزَّبَانِيَةَ · referent]
turns on: Whether the term names the verse's punitive angels, its worldly analogues, or demon-like beings in older poetry.
- 96:18/q08/p1 They are stern, powerful angels of punishment and guardians of Hell. — Mujāhid, Qatāda, al-Ḍaḥḥāk and later exegetes identify them as angels; al-Zajjāj invokes 66:6. ## 96:18/q09 How are the zabāniya's strength and form described?  [ٱلزَّبَانِيَةَ · interpretation]
turns on: The reports supply different concrete descriptions of their size and action.
- 96:18/q09/p1 Their feet reach earth while their heads reach heaven. — ʿAbd Allāh ibn Abī al-Hudhayl, ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Ḥārith, Muqātil and reports in al-Qurṭubī give this vast stature; some say the lowest heaven. - 96:18/q09/p2 They are exceptionally huge and strong, perhaps the largest and strongest angels. — Al-Māwardī calls them large and forceful; al-Qurṭubī reports the superlative view. - 96:18/q09/p3 They use feet as well as hands to push and seize. — Al-Farrāʾ compares a camel's kick at its milker; Abū al-Layth al-Samarqandī explains their name through this way of working. [5 notes: FARRA; TUSTARI; QURTUBI(أبو الليث السمرقندي); QURTUBI-FULL(أبو الليث السمرقندي)]

## 96:18/q12 What did Abū Jahl see or do when the threat became immediate?  [* · reports]
turns on: The differing sensory reports of fire and wings, dark ranks, a horseman, or a blocked horizon.
- 96:18/q12/p1 He recoiled from a trench of fire, dread or awe, and wings between him and the Prophet. — Abū Hurayra's report says the Prophet warned angels would tear him limb by limb. Ibn Kathīr cites Aḥmad, Muslim, al-Nasāʾī and Ibn Abī Ḥātim through Muʿtamir, but doubts whether the report itself includes the revelation context from 96:6 onward. [8 notes: TAB(أبو هريرة); TAB-FULL(أبو هريرة); IBNKATHIR(أبو هريرة); IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو هريرة); DURR(أبو هريرة); DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 96:18/q12/p2 He saw dark battalions or ranks filling the space between them and did not advance. — Ibn ʿAbbās reports that angels would seize him if he moved; some versions put the Prophet's prayer at the Maqām. [7 notes: TAB(ابن عباس); TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR(ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس); DURR(ابن عباس)]
- 96:18/q12/p3 He fled after hearing of the zabāniya and said he feared them or saw a threatening mounted angel. — Al-Tustarī says he admitted fear despite not knowing them; al-Qurṭubī's version adds the apparent horseman. [4 notes: TUSTARI; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 96:18/q12/p4 The sky's horizon was blocked from him after the Prophet recited the sūra. — Al-ʿAbbās reports Abū Jahl's vow to trample the prostrating Prophet; Muhammad then prostrated at the sūra's end. [2 notes: DURR(العباس بن عبد المطلب)]

## 96:18/q13 How does God's summons answer the man's assembly?  [* · links]
turns on: The balance between Abū Jahl's supporters and the divine force summoned against them.
- 96:18/q13/p1 His earthly clan cannot prevail against God's powerful guards; the challenge exposes the outcome. — Al-Farrāʾ contrasts the stronger zabāniya with the men summoned; Ibn Kathīr describes a contest between God's party and Abū Jahl's. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ and Corpus Coranicum stress human helplessness; Quṭb says the result is already known. [12 notes: FARRA; TAB; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; MUQATIL]
- 96:18/q13/p2 The taunting imperative and its answer constitute a miracle: he dared not summon his supporters. — Al-Rāzī sees the fāʾ as inviting a test whose result would be God's counter-summons; Kuran Yolu says Abū Jahl never dared act after hearing the warning. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the next kallā rebuts the challenge. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Rivayete göre); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:18/q13/p3 The answer supplies the implied result of his proposed summons: God calls the guards to drag him away. — Al-Biqāʿī makes the implied question explicit; al-Qushayrī says God commands them to destroy him. [2 notes: BIQAI; QUSHAYRI]

## 96:18/q14 Who is the council opposed by the zabāniya?  [* · referent]
- 96:18/q14/p1 It is Abū Jahl's people, clan, or supporters; nādī can name their gathering place and its assembled people. — Mujāhid and Qatāda gloss the neighboring verse; al-Nasafī explains the place-for-people usage. Ibn ʿĀshūr distinguishes daytime nādī from nighttime sāmir at 23:67 and describes Quṣayy's Quraysh council house by the Sacred Mosque. [25 notes: IBNABIHATIM(مجاهد); DURR(أبو هريرة,قتادة,مجاهد); DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,قتادة,مجاهد); NASAFI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 96:18/q15 What happened to Abū Jahl at Badr, according to the extended account?  [* · reports]
- 96:18/q15/p1 Ibn Masʿūd killed the wounded Abū Jahl and brought evidence of his death to the Prophet. — Bursawī first recounts Abū Jahl bloodying Ibn Masʿūd's ear after his Sūrat al-Raḥmān recitation. Gabriel foretells an outcome; at Badr Ibn Masʿūd spears, overcomes, and decapitates him, dragging the head by its ear. Abū Jahl remains defiant, and the Prophet compares him unfavorably with Pharaoh. [8 notes: BURSEVI(أبو جهل,ابن مسعود,النبي عليه السلام)]

## 96:18/q16 Does the verse specify the angels' manner of punishment?  [* · rhetoric]
- 96:18/q16/p1 It leaves the manner unspecified so the imagination can encompass its terror. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says the open threat intensifies dread. [1 notes: BINTSHATI]

## 96:18/q17 How is the warning read inwardly?  [* · inward]
- 96:18/q17/p1 Abū Jahl represents the commanding soul, his council its dark desires, and the zabāniya forceful majestic manifestations driving them toward abandonment and loss. — Bursawī explicitly gives this inward reading. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 96:18/q18 What other teachings or merits are reported alongside these notes?  [* · reports]
- 96:18/q18/p1 The nineteen letters of the basmala protect against nineteen zabāniya; teaching or reciting it brings deliverance and blessing. — Ibn Masʿūd links each letter to a garden against one guard; Thaʿlabī's Prophetic report praises teachers and liberation from Fire for child, parents and teacher; Jābir reports cosmic signs, healing and Paradise when the basmala descended. [3 notes: THALABI(النبي,جابر,عبد الله بن مسعود)]
- 96:18/q18/p2 Reciting the sūra earns a reward as though one recited the group of shorter sūras. — Al-Bayḍāwī's full version reports the promise from the Prophet. [1 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 96:18/q18/p3 Seekers of knowledge and wealth are both insatiable, but the first gains God's approval while the second transgresses. — Ibn Masʿūd's report cites 35:28 and 96:6–7. [1 notes: DURR(ابن مسعود)]

## 96:18/q19 What fate does an extended report picture for Abū Jahl after his persecution of Muhammad?  [* · reports]
- 96:18/q19/p1 He is dragged face-first over Hell's embers, regrets harming Muhammad, and begs to return to make his clan believe. — Muqātil says the zabāniya are angrier at him than Banū Makhzūm are at Muhammad and quotes a Prophetic scene of regret. [1 notes: MUQATIL]

## 96:18/q20 What other letter omissions illustrate the speed conveyed by this spelling?  [سَنَدْعُ · rhetoric]
- 96:18/q20/p1 Omitted letters elsewhere also suggest the swiftness of God's act and the response to a call. — Al-Suyūṭī cites the omitted alif in God's effacing falsehood (42:24) for rapid annihilation and in the caller's call (54:6) for a rapid call and answer. [2 notes: ITQAN]
