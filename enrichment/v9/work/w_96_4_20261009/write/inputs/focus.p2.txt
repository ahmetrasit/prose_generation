# 96:4/q10 What images does poetry use for the pen and writing? [ٱلْقَلَمِ · rhetoric]
- 96:4/q10/p1 Poems picture dark, swift pens as serpents or figures animated by white nibs, ink as venom or honey, and the written page as exalted.
  reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī quotes black-legged figures and white blades; Abū Ḥayyān says speckled swift snakes; al-Ṭabrisī and al-Ālūsī quote serpent saliva and gathered honey. Al-Qurṭubī's modern poet likens the stained nib to an old suitor and says pages ascend to God. Al-Ālūsī adds a jinn and eager hands. Ibn ʿĀshūr quotes Abū Ḥayya's Jewish hand and a poet boasting of literacy.

# 96:4/q11 How does the pen teaching relate to the surrounding verses? [* · links]
turns on: Whether the sequence is read as a rational-to-transmitted progression, reassurance during the first recitation, or a broader presentation of divine favors.
- 96:4/q11/p1 Creation provides rational evidence of lordship; pen teaching supplies transmitted knowledge and evidence of prophethood.
  reasons: Al-Rāzī argues lordship can be known without prophecy but prophecy presupposes lordship. # 96:4/q12 What does the verse imply about Muhammad's literacy and the Qur'an's origin? [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether later instruction about writing implies acquired literacy, and how 29:48 bears on the time before and after revelation.
- 96:4/q12/p1 His unlettered recitation, while the verse praises writing, confirms that the Qur'an came from God.
  reasons: Al-Qurṭubī says the Prophet knew writing least among Arabs and 29:48 strengthens the miracle; Qutb notes he could not write the book he proclaimed. Elmalılı says he recited and dictated without writing and God promised retention at 87:6; Ibn ʿĀshūr says reading and pen teaching imply his unlettered state.
  holders (8 notes): QURTUBI(علماؤنا); QURTUBI-FULL(علماؤنا); ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI

# 96:4/q14 What rule about teaching women writing is reported? [* · law]
- 96:4/q14/p1 A report discourages giving women upper rooms or teaching them writing.
  reasons: Al-Qurṭubī attributes this via Ibn Masʿūd to the Prophet without grading it. His scholars say upper rooms allow looking at men and risk temptation; writing could enable desired correspondence, since it conveys the absent person's hand and inner thought. They cite a saying commending women remaining unseen by men.
