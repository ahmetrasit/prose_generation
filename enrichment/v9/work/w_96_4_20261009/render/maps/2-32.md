# 2:32: 33 questions

## 2:32/q01 Who says “They said,” and to what challenge do they reply?  [قَالُوا۟ · referent]
- 2:32/q01/p1 The speakers are the angels, answering God's challenge to name what Adam learned. — Muqātil and Jishumī identify the angels. Muqātil says God confronted their claim to knowledge of unseen, uncreated things by asking about visible things they did not know. Ibn Masʿūd's report relates the challenge to their earlier prediction of human corruption. Al-Ālūsī and Bursawī read this as a fresh answer to whether they fulfilled the task. [6 notes: MUQATIL(الله عز وجل); JISHUMI; DURR-FULL(ابن مسعود وناس من الصحابة); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:32/q02 Why does “they said” lack a linking particle?  [قَالُوا۟ · grammar]
- 2:32/q02/p1 Direct, successive dialogue needs no conjunction when its sequence is clear. — Abū Ḥayyān cites the dialogue in 2:30, 2:32, 5:27, 2:259, 2:260 and 26:10; al-Ālūsī notes frequent Qurʾānic usage, especially al-Shuʿarāʾ. Ibn ʿĀshūr specifically explains the absence of fa as direct dialogue. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 2:32/q03 What does “Subḥānaka” mean?  [سُبْحَٰنَكَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the expression is explained by disavowal and transcendence or a movement metaphor.
- 2:32/q03/p1 It glorifies God by declaring Him transcendent, free of evil, defect, or blame. — Majāz cites al-Aʿshā's poetic repudiation of ʿAlqama's boast; Akhfash uses the same line for disavowal. Ibn ʿAbbās says free of evil; ʿAlī calls it a word God loves; Maymūn calls it a divine name of magnification; al-Ḥasan says humans cannot claim it. Al-Zajjāj reports philologists' agreement on freedom from defect. Abū Ḥayyān contrasts taqdīs as purification, supported by Bayt al-Maqdis, holy land, quds vessel and qidās string of pearls; he also records al-Zamakhsharī's travel derivation and an anonymous poetic qidās. [18 notes: MAJAZ; AKHFASH; TAB-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس,الحسن,علي,ميمون بن مهران); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو الخطاب الكبير); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس,علي,ميمون بن مهران); ITQAN(ابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 2:32/q03/p2 It denotes rapid movement toward God. — Al-Naḍr transmits a dream interpreter's gloss, relating it to a horse's swift swimming motion. [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(النضر)]

## 2:32/q04 What is the form and derivation of subḥān?  [سُبْحَٰنَكَ · grammar]
turns on: Its morphological source, lexicalized status, and the evidence of rare poetic forms.
- 2:32/q04/p1 It is a verbal noun or noun standing for tasbīḥ, normally accusative in an understood glorification construction. — Akhfash, Ṭabarī, al-Khalīl, Sībawayh and others give an implicit verb; Sībawayh distinguishes the verbal noun tasbīḥ from its substitute subḥān. Bayḍāwī compares ghufrān and maʿādh Allāh and notes an exceptional predicate use. Ibn ʿĀshūr considers a lighter sabbaḥa derivation; al-Itqān says the verb fell out of use. Samīn records both verbal noun and ism maṣdar analyses. [20 notes: AKHFASH; TAB-FULL; NAHHAS(الخليل وسيبويه); THALABI(الخليل); WAHIDI-BASIT(الخليل والفراء,سيبويه); JISHUMI(الخليل); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(سيبويه); QURTUBI-FULL(الخليل وسيبويه); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 2:32/q04/p2 It is a conventional proper name for glorification or transcendence. — Abū Ḥayyān and Samīn discuss standalone poetic subḥān: tanwīn may be poetic declension or indefiniteness, and the untanwinned form diptote through properness plus alif-nūn. Al-Ālūsī cites the al-ʿAlqama poem for naming and the counterclaim of omitted Allāh; Ibn ʿĀshūr cites al-Nābigha's analogous names, while Sībawayh explains loss of tanwīn by definiteness. Bursawī and Elmalılı also call it a proper name. [11 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(جماعة); BURSEVI; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL(سيبويه)]
- 2:32/q04/p3 An unusual derivation links it to raising the voice in praise. — Al-Mufaṣṣal derives it from sabaḥa and cites a poem of Taghlib and pilgrims praising God. [1 notes: ITQAN(المفصل)]
- 2:32/q04/p4 It is a reduplicated form denoting repeated sanctification. — Abū Ḥayyān rejects this because it requires unattested singular subḥā; al-Ālūsī objects to the required dual or nominative form and unexplained fixed nūn. [0 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL(بعض)-]

## 2:32/q05 How is “Subḥānaka” governed and what does its suffix mark?  [سُبْحَٰنَكَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether it is an accusative cognate noun, vocative, or governed by the quotation verb; whether its suffix denotes object or subject.
- 2:32/q05/p1 It is accusative with an implied glorification verb, and the kāf is its object. — Al-Khalīl and Sībawayh supply 'we glorify You'; Abū Ḥayyān says the cognate verb must be omitted and favors object kāf. Samīn also reports annexation to the object. [8 notes: NAHHAS(الخليل وسيبويه); THALABI(الخليل); JISHUMI(الخليل); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(سيبويه); QURTUBI-FULL(الخليل وسيبويه); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]
- 2:32/q05/p2 It is an annexed vocative, ‘O Your Glory.’ — Al-Kisāʾī proposes the vocative; Abū Ḥayyān rejects it for lacking a transmitted vocative particle, Samīn says most grammarians reject it, and al-Ālūsī finds no proof. [5 notes: NAHHAS(الكسائي); THALABI(قيل); JISHUMI(قيل); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الكسائي); QURTUBI-FULL(الكسائي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الكسائي)-; SAMIN-DURR(الكسائي)-; ALUSI-FULL(الكسائي)-]
- 2:32/q05/p3 Its accusative is governed by the verb ‘they said.’ — Samīn explicitly names the verb of saying as the governing ʿāmil. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]
- 2:32/q05/p4 The kāf can be the subject of the annexation: ‘You are transcendent.’ — Samīn reports the subject annexation; Abū Ḥayyān allows that some take the suffix as subject, while favoring object. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 2:32/q05/p5 Al-Kisāʾī explains its accusative by its lack of an adjective. — Naḥḥās transmits this additional grammatical explanation alongside al-Kisāʾī's vocative allowance; Thaʿlabī says it lies outside ordinary description. [2 notes: NAHHAS(الكسائي); THALABI(الكسائي)]
- 2:32/q05/p6 Standalone subḥān is cut from annexation and therefore takes tanwīn. — Abū Ḥayyān says grammar works reject this account; an alternative likens the untanwinned standalone form to qabl and baʿd. [0 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]

## 2:32/q06 What are the angels doing by opening with “Subḥānaka”?  [سُبْحَٰنَكَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the opening is primarily disavowal, apology for the prior question, or courtly praise.
- 2:32/q06/p1 They clear God of objection, error, or lack of wisdom and disclaim their own claim. — Ṭabarī and Ibn ʿAbbās relate it to God alone knowing the unseen; Thaʿlabī, Wāḥidī, Jishumī, Ṭabrisī and Abū Ḥayyān relate it to His judgment and governance. Abū ʿAlī stresses reverent speech even before knowing the wisdom. Bursawī says God is free of purposeless acts. [17 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس,قيل); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(أبو علي,ابن عباس); TABRISI(ابن عباس,قيل); QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; QASHANI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 2:32/q06/p2 They apologize or repent for their earlier question or presumption. — Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān and Ibn ʿĀshūr describe apology and courtesy, comparing Moses' 7:143 and Jonah's 21:87; Bursawī notes the same formulas. Those who think angels sinned see repentance; al-Ālūsī says it resembles repentance for a less preferable act without breaching infallibility. Qatāda and al-Ḥasan report the angels had privately claimed superiority. [8 notes: RAZI-FULL(الذين اعتقدوا أن الملائكة أتوا بالمعصية); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(من اعتقد أن الملائكة غير معصومين); ALUSI-FULL(من زعم عدم العصمة); DURR-FULL(قتادة والحسن); BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 2:32/q06/p3 They begin with reverent praise before acknowledging inability. — Qushayrī says praise precedes excuse; Ibn ʿĀshūr emphasizes majesty. Wāḥidī refers back to the angels' tasbīḥ in 2:30. [3 notes: QUSHAYRI; IBNASHUR-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT]

## 2:32/q07 What do the angels mean by “We have no knowledge except what You taught us”?  [لَا عِلْمَ لَنَآ إِلَّا مَا عَلَّمْتَنَآ · interpretation]
turns on: Their prior question in 2:30, whether it was culpable, and what God had previously taught them.
- 2:32/q07/p1 Their knowledge is received from God, and the requested names were not among what He taught them. — Muqātil, Ṭabarī, Ibn Isḥāq and many others read it as admitting the limit of angelic knowledge. Jishumī and Ṭabrisī specify the names; al-Ālūsī adds inability for the succession task. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the statement admits incapacity rather than informing God. Study Quran stresses limited comprehensive scope. [33 notes: MUQATIL; AKHFASH; TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNABIHATIM(محمد بن إسحاق); THALABI; QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون وأهل المعاني); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL(الذين أنكروا معصيتهم); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(من اعتقد بعصمتهم); IBNKATHIR-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMARRAI-ASRAR; STUDYQURAN]
- 2:32/q07/p2 They ask tactfully to be taught and repent for their uninformed question about Adam. — Wāḥidī reports that their ignorance of names reveals greater ignorance of unseen outcomes and warns against rating their own obedience; the reading ties back to 2:30. [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(قيل)]
- 2:32/q07/p3 They express astonishment at being asked about something unknown. — Ṭabrisī reports this further reading. [1 notes: TABRISI(قيل)]
- 2:32/q07/p4 They repent because their earlier speech was a sin. — Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān transmit this from those who deny angelic infallibility; al-Ālūsī reports and qualifies it. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL(الذين اعتقدوا أن الملائكة أتوا بالمعصية); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(من اعتقد أن الملائكة غير معصومين); ALUSI-FULL(من زعم عدم العصمة)]
- 2:32/q07/p5 They make an innocent inquiry: God had told them of future corruption but not the names. — Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān distinguish the earlier divinely supplied prediction from the names; Bayḍāwī, Bursawī and Elmalılı deny an explicit claim to succession or sinful objection. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL(الذين أنكروا معصيتهم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(من اعتقد بعصمتهم); BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI]

## 2:32/q08 How does the wording “no knowledge except what You taught us” add to a bare admission of ignorance?  [لَا عِلْمَ لَنَآ إِلَّا مَا عَلَّمْتَنَآ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the exception acknowledges knowledge already granted or is read hypothetically of future teaching.
- 2:32/q08/p1 The exception praises God, thanks Him for teaching, and denies independent knowledge. — Wāḥidī says 'we do not know this' would omit recognition; Jishumī and Ṭabrisī say a bare denial would miss gratitude and duty. Al-Ālūsī says the broad wording avoids self-assertion and a narrow denial of names. Abū Ḥayyān points to lā with an indefinite noun excluding every kind of untaught knowledge. [5 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون وأهل المعاني); JISHUMI; TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 2:32/q08/p2 The wording allows future learning without affirming past teaching. — Abū Ḥayyān sketches a strained disconnected exception with conditional mā: 'whatever You teach us, we shall know'; he says it conflicts with reports of prior divine teaching or knowledge from the Preserved Tablet, unless the angels discount that specific information. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]

## 2:32/q09 How is “lā ʿilma lanā” constructed?  [لَا عِلْمَ لَنَآ · grammar]
turns on: Whether lā is the genus-negating particle or operates like laysa.
- 2:32/q09/p1 Lā negates an indefinite noun generally; lanā is its omitted locative predicate. — Naḥḥās compares lā rayba fīhi; Ṭabrisī supplies an omitted element governing lanā. Abū Ḥayyān says the indefinite denies every category of knowledge. [3 notes: NAHHAS; TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 2:32/q09/p2 Lā can instead function as laysa. — Naḥḥās gives the alternative analysis of 'we have no knowledge.' [1 notes: NAHHAS]

## 2:32/q10 What does ʿilm mean here?  [عِلْمَ · meaning]
- 2:32/q10/p1 It can mean the thing known, so the sentence says they know no object except what God taught. — Nasafī explicitly glosses ʿilm as maʿlūm; Samīn uses that sense to explain an omitted resumptive pronoun in the relative clause. [2 notes: NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]

## 2:32/q11 What is the grammatical role and sense of “mā” after “except”?  [مَا عَلَّمْتَنَآ · grammar]
turns on: Whether mā is relative, verbal-noun, or conditional; whether the exception is connected, and what can govern mā.
- 2:32/q11/p1 It is relative, ‘that which You taught us,’ most plausibly nominative in apposition. — Abū Ḥayyān prefers nominative relative mā; Samīn compares lā rayba fīhi and supplies an omitted resumptive if ʿilm means maʿlūm. Naḥḥās compares lā ilāha illā Allāh; al-Ālūsī reports the majority's relative analysis, allowing apposition or accusative exception. Al-Burhān notes relative mā after illā. [10 notes: NAHHAS; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الزهراوي); TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(الجمهور); BURHAN]
- 2:32/q11/p2 It is a verbal noun, ‘Your teaching of us,’ taking exception case or nominative apposition. — Qurṭubī gives the verbal-noun sense. Samīn allows accusative as exception and denies government by the noun of lā, which would then have to decline. Bursawī makes the phrase nominative as a substitute for the position of lā ʿilm; Naḥḥās permits accusative after a complete clause. [6 notes: NAHHAS; QURTUBI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; BURHAN]
- 2:32/q11/p3 It is nominative as a substitute for lā's predicate. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya analogizes lā ilāha illā Allāh; Samīn objects that exception comes from the judged subject rather than its predicate. [1 notes: NAHHAS; SAMIN-DURR(ابن عطية)-]
- 2:32/q11/p4 It is the accusative object of the following ʿallamtanā. — Al-Zahrawī's proposal is transmitted by Ibn ʿAṭiyya; Abū Ḥayyān and Samīn object that a relative clause cannot govern its antecedent. Al-Ālūsī also transmits a remote disconnected-exception rescue. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الزهراوي); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عطية عن الزهراوي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزهراوي نقلا عن ابن عطية)-; SAMIN-DURR(الزهراوي)-]
- 2:32/q11/p5 It can be conditional in a disconnected exception, with an omitted answer. — Abū Ḥayyān offers this as strained, and Samīn reports the shaykh's rescue; al-Ālūsī repeats it. It gives 'but whatever You taught us, we knew' or future learning, with no prior knowledge affirmed. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عطية عن الزهراوي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]

## 2:32/q12 What does God's teaching imply about the source of knowledge?  [عَلَّمْتَنَآ · theology]
turns on: Whether establishing evidence counts as God's teaching or teaching requires His production of the resulting knowledge.
- 2:32/q12/p1 God produces acquired knowledge in creatures. — Rāzī presents the Ashʿarite inference and argues evidence alone is not teaching since the learner's own consideration would produce knowledge. He says God alone can produce knowledge in another and cites 55:2. Ṭabrisī also says all knowledge traces to God, though by necessary creation and proof for inference. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL(أصحابنا)+]
- 2:32/q12/p2 God teaches by instruction or by establishing evidence from which a learner reasons. — Rāzī reports the Muʿtazilite account but rejects evidence alone as sufficient teaching. Ṭabrisī describes necessary knowledge produced by God and inferential knowledge based on proofs He establishes. An astrologer could answer that planetary motions are divine signs, showing the breadth of this account. [2 notes: TABRISI; RAZI-FULL(المنجم); RAZI-FULL(المعتزلة)-]

## 2:32/q13 May God be called ‘Teacher’ on the strength of ‘You taught us’?  [عَلَّمْتَنَآ · theology]
- 2:32/q13/p1 God truly teaches, but ordinary naming custom restricts the unqualified title ‘Teacher.’ — Rāzī says custom reserves teacher for professional instruction, as in a bequest excluding schoolmasters from 'learners'; apart from custom, the title is fitting because God produces knowledge, as 55:2 states. Bayḍāwī likewise allows attribution of teaching but avoids al-Muʿallim as a divine title. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL]

## 2:32/q14 Can the angels or humans know the unseen independently?  [لَا عِلْمَ لَنَآ إِلَّا مَا عَلَّمْتَنَآ · theology]
turns on: Whether created celestial signs meet the verse's requirement of divine instruction.
- 2:32/q14/p1 No: unseen knowledge belongs to God and reaches creatures only through His teaching or revelation. — Ṭabarī's Ibn ʿAbbās says only God knows the unseen except what He teaches Adam and others. Rāzī cites 6:59 and 72:26–27 and argues humans are still less able than angels. Ṭabarī rebukes claims by diviners and astrologers without report or proof. [9 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL(أهل الإسلام); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); JISHUMI(ابن عباس); TABRISI(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 2:32/q14/p2 Celestial signs could count as God's instruction of unseen conditions. — Rāzī reports an astrologer's reply to a Muʿtazilite: God created planetary motions as signs of terrestrial events, so interpretation could be called divine teaching. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(المنجم)]

## 2:32/q15 What is the scope and growth of angelic knowledge?  [عِلْمَ لَنَآ · theology]
turns on: Whether angels can acquire further knowledge or their rank and perfections are fixed.
- 2:32/q15/p1 Angelic knowledge is limited to what God reveals and lacks independent inference. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says angels receive inspired or commanded meanings and cannot derive new ones. Elmalılı says they act on revealed text rather than human-style analogy; their limited knowledge avoids compounded ignorance. Study Quran stresses lack of universal comprehensive scope. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ELMALILI; STUDYQURAN]
- 2:32/q15/p2 Angels can gain knowledge and perfection. — Bayḍāwī opposes philosophers who infer fixed angelic rank from 37:164; Adam's teaching episode permits increase. [1 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL]
- 2:32/q15/p3 Angelic perfections accompany existence; their answer shows they cannot acquire human ranks of knowledge. — Qāshānī takes their admission as testimony that they fall short of human perfection. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 2:32/q16 What does “al-ʿAlīm” mean here?  [ٱلْعَلِيمُ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the name's scope is stated generally or applied to the angels' hidden thoughts.
- 2:32/q16/p1 God knows comprehensively without being taught, including hidden, past, and future things. — Ṭabarī says God needs no teacher; Ibn ʿAbbās calls His knowledge perfect. Ibn Isḥāq specifies what angels conceal; Baghawī says His creation. Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ṭabrisī, Rāzī, Ibn al-Jawzī and Qurṭubī emphasize the intensive faʿīl form. Rāzī says only God merits it absolutely. Bayḍāwī says nothing is hidden; Bursawī connects it with 2:30. [25 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNABIHATIM(محمد بن إسحاق); THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(ابن عباس,قيل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 2:32/q16/p2 It specifically points to God's knowledge of the angels' concealed claim. — Ibn Isḥāq glosses the name by what they hide; Bayḍāwī says God knows the hidden conditions of heaven and earth, including theirs. [2 notes: IBNABIHATIM(محمد بن إسحاق); BAYDAWI-FULL]

## 2:32/q17 What does “al-Ḥakīm” mean, and what sort of divine attribute is it?  [ٱلْحَكِيمُ · meaning]
turns on: Whether ḥikma denotes judging, knowledge, precise action, restraint, or a perfection combining knowledge and action.
- 2:32/q17/p1 The wise judge or ruler, perfect in judgment and just decree. — Ṭabarī gives Ibn ʿAbbās's perfected judgment and another judge gloss. Wāḥidī cites Ibn al-Muẓaffar on ḥukm as knowledge or just judgment, 19:12, and al-Nābigha's poetic decision. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Qurṭubī note intensive faʿīl. This can be an attribute of essence when wisdom means knowing. [16 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,قيل); IBNABIHATIM(أبو العالية,محمد بن جعفر بن الزبير); THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن المظفر,النابغة); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة); QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; TABRISI(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 2:32/q17/p2 The one who perfects, orders, and makes acts precise, an attribute of action. — Thaʿlabī, Wāḥidī and Abū Ḥayyān explain faʿīl as causative mufʿil, citing samīʿ and alīm parallels. Mawardī, Jishumī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Qurṭubī and others call this action; Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 2:117 and the poetic najība for adjective-pattern variation, resisting a forced possessive gloss. Rāzī prefers this to avoid repeating al-ʿAlīm. [21 notes: THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(قوم); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الخطابي); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن الأنباري); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; RAZI-FULL; TABRISI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 2:32/q17/p3 The one who knows or possesses wisdom, an essential attribute. — Thaʿlabī, Jishumī, Ṭabrisī, Rāzī and al-Ālūsī distinguish knowledge or possessed wisdom from action. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites al-Ghazālī on God's eternal, exact knowledge of the noblest object and Lutf Allāh on knowledge joined to perfect governance. [9 notes: THALABI; JISHUMI; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان); IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو حامد الغزالي,الشيخ لطف الله الأرضرومي)]
- 2:32/q17/p4 The one who restrains or prevents corruption. — Mawardī compares a horse's bridle; Wāḥidī cites al-Aṣmaʿī, al-Azharī, Jarīr, Labīd and al-Nakhaʿī on restraint of horses, fools, or orphans. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Qurṭubī add Jarīr and Zuhayr; Jishumī, Ṭabrisī and Samīn say wisdom prevents ignorance and flawed action. [23 notes: THALABI(جرير); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الأزهري,الأصمعي,النخعي,جرير); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(جرير,قوم); TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL(جرير,زهير,قوم); SAMIN-DURR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 2:32/q17/p5 Wisdom adds a perfection beyond knowledge: right, objection-free action and governance. — Bayḍāwī says a mere duplicate of 'All-Knowing' would be redundant; Ibn ʿĀshūr says wisdom exceeds knowledge as 'eloquent poet' adds to poet. Qushayrī says God's acts are right, without folly. Rāzī prefers this sense; Ibn Kathīr extends it to creation, command, giving and withholding instruction. ## 2:32/q18 How is “anta” parsed and what does it convey?  [أَنتَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether anta is a separator, accusative emphasis, or an independent nominative subject.
- 2:32/q18/p1 It is a separating pronoun, emphasizing and restricting the divine predicates. — Naḥḥās reports the Kūfan ʿimād; Ṭabrisī, Nasafī, Bayḍāwī, al-Ālūsī and Ibn ʿĀshūr allow faṣl. Samīn notes dispute over its case: al-Farrāʾ follows the preceding word, al-Kisāʾī the following. Ibn ʿĀshūr says it may correct presumed angelic knowledge or confine complete knowledge and wisdom to God. [11 notes: NAHHAS; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الزهراوي); TABRISI; NASAFI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 2:32/q18/p2 It emphasizes the second-person pronoun governed by inna. — Naḥḥās treats it as accusative emphasis; Bayḍāwī notes dependent emphasis is allowed where a standalone object is not; Samīn and al-Ālūsī report this parse. [5 notes: NAHHAS; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الزهراوي); BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]
- 2:32/q18/p3 It is a new subject, with al-ʿAlīm (and al-Ḥakīm) as predicate; the clause predicates inna. — Naḥḥās, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ṭabrisī, Nasafī, Bayḍāwī, Samīn and al-Ālūsī report this full-clause analysis. [9 notes: NAHHAS; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الزهراوي); TABRISI; NASAFI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; JISHUMI]

## 2:32/q19 How does “al-Ḥakīm” relate syntactically to “al-ʿAlīm”?  [ٱلْعَلِيمُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the second divine name is attributive or predicative.
- 2:32/q19/p1 It is an adjective modifying al-ʿAlīm. — Naḥḥās states the modifying analysis; Samīn lists it as an option and says both forms carry intensive force. [2 notes: NAHHAS; SAMIN-DURR]
- 2:32/q19/p2 It is a second predicate. — Samīn and Nasafī allow the two names as predicates after anta or after inna if anta is a separator. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR; NASAFI-FULL]

## 2:32/q20 Why does “al-ʿAlīm” precede “al-Ḥakīm”?  [ٱلْعَلِيمُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the order follows conceptual dependence, the immediate verbal context, or both.
- 2:32/q20/p1 Knowledge is prior because wisdom depends on it. — Samīn, al-Burhān and al-Ālūsī say sound judgment follows knowledge; al-Burhān adds that knowledge may lead as an essential attribute. [3 notes: SAMIN-DURR; BURHAN; ALUSI-FULL]
- 2:32/q20/p2 It echoes the preceding talk of knowledge and God's command to inform Him. — Al-Burhān points to 'we have no knowledge'; Samīn and al-Ālūsī cite the same local context, al-Ālūsī also the preceding 'inform Me.' [3 notes: SAMIN-DURR; BURHAN; ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:32/q21 Where does Ibn ʿĀshūr defer further discussion of wisdom?  [ٱلْحَكِيمُ · links]
- 2:32/q21/p1 He defers the fuller discussion to 2:269. — Ibn ʿĀshūr gives 2:269 as the later locus for the discussion of wisdom. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 2:32/q22 What is the force of “Indeed You are the All-Knowing, the Wise” in this reply?  [إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْعَلِيمُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the closing titles express a general divine perfection or point specifically to Adam's appointment.
- 2:32/q22/p1 It confirms their limited knowledge by affirming God's comprehensive knowledge and wise decision. — Abū Ḥayyān says Adam's learning shows wisdom in the vicegerency; al-Ālūsī says the clause confirms their denial and corrects a possible misunderstanding. Ibn ʿĀshūr says inna gives an explanation: God grants knowledge according to creatures' capacity. Qushayrī says His acts are beyond blame. Al-Ālūsī prefers unrestricted objects of both names over a narrow restriction to commands and decrees. [7 notes: QUSHAYRI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 2:32/q22/p2 It names the particular decision to make Adam vicegerent. — Ibn ʿAbbās, as quoted by Rāzī, takes the close to refer to God's decree of Adam's earthly succession. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس)]

## 2:32/q23 What conduct does the angels' admission teach a person asked about what they do not know?  [لَا عِلْمَ لَنَآ · interpretation]
- 2:32/q23/p1 Admit ignorance humbly, say ‘I do not know’ or ‘God knows best,’ and accept correction. — Qurṭubī cites a prophetic warning that ignorant opinion misleads, the Prophet's consultation with Gabriel and Michael on places, Abū Bakr on inheritance, ʿAlī and Ibn ʿUmar on 'God knows best,' Qāsim and Mālik's practice, and the poem of Yazīd. His reports of ʿUmar, ʿAlī and Bakkār show fairness in accepting correction; Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr calls fairness a condition of learning. Bursawī adds Abū Yūsuf and the pulpit scholar; one should also not conceal knowledge one has. [22 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(أبو بكر الصديق,ابن عبد البر,ابن عمر,ابن هرمز,الصادق,القاسم بن عبيد الله,امرأة,علي,علي بن أبي طالب,عمر,قاسم بن أصبغ,مالك ابن أنس,مالك بن أنس,يحيى بن سعيد,يزيد بن الوليد بن عبد الملك); BURSEVI(أبو يوسف,عالم)]
- 2:32/q23/p2 Seeking status through disputation undermines honest admission of ignorance. — Qurṭubī judges his age worse than Mālik's, reporting Mālik's remark that fairness was scarce; rivalry hardens hearts and harms piety. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(مالك ابن أنس)]

## 2:32/q24 Does the command to name the things prove God may impose an impossible task?  [لَا عِلْمَ لَنَآ إِلَّا مَا عَلَّمْتَنَآ · theology]
- 2:32/q24/p1 Yes: God asked the angels to report names He knew they could not know. — Thaʿlabī explicitly draws this inference; Qushayrī says no one can blame God for testing the unable. Ṭabrisī says the test displayed dependence on His teaching and elevated Adam. [3 notes: THALABI; QUSHAYRI; TABRISI]

## 2:32/q25 What did the angels' inability demonstrate about Adam and vicegerency?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether knowledge of names by itself establishes fitness, or deeper human representation is needed.
- 2:32/q25/p1 Adam's taught knowledge showed his fitness and rank over the angels. — Ṭabrisī says God raised Adam by the names; Bayḍāwī calls knowledge central to vicegerency and superior to worship, citing 39:9. Nasafī says names knowledge surpasses worship, even more so divine law. Elmalılı and al-Sāmarrāʾī say the comparison tested Adam's fitness and prepared angelic submission. [8 notes: TABRISI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ELMALILI; SAMARRAI-ASRAR]
- 2:32/q25/p2 The angels' failure alone cannot establish unfitness for succession; a deeper representative capacity matters. — Elmalılı argues that ignorance of all names would imply no speech, while ignorance of descendants' or new names would not disqualify them. Human representative speech and direct angelic cognition differ; humans can also err by confusing words or concepts with truth. [10 notes: ELMALILI]
- 2:32/q25/p3 Adam's comprehensive inward and outward constitution makes him uniquely representative of God. — Bursawī says he gathers spiritual, bodily, celestial and earthly realms, uniquely received God's spirit (17:70), and mirrors divine attributes. Angels know God yet not themselves and all attributes; the human inner lamp images the spirit's manifestation in just and merciful action, citing 38:26, 9:128, 48:29. [4 notes: BURSEVI]

## 2:32/q26 What does the scene imply about the origin of language and the angels' speech?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether 'teaching names' concerns prior divine designation or how angelic communication itself occurs.
- 2:32/q26/p1 God instituted the names and their meanings before Adam used them. — Bayḍāwī reasons that teaching words with meanings presupposes prior designation, and no creature before Adam could designate them. [1 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL]
- 2:32/q26/p2 Angelic dialogue is direct illumination or knowledge, unlike human indirect representative speech. — Elmalılı says angels' speech cannot be ordinary naming whether they lacked all names or just some; God first addressed them by direct reality and their glorification likewise expresses illumination. Adam's speech conveys knowledge indirectly via signs or deputies. [3 notes: ELMALILI]

## 2:32/q27 How does this passage connect to God's previous claim to know what the angels did not?  [* · links]
turns on: How the scene functions in the surrounding narrative and later address.
- 2:32/q27/p1 The names test and its answer display the hidden knowledge and wisdom asserted in 2:30. — Bayḍāwī says the follow-up challenge restates the 2:30 claim in fuller form and exposes hidden conditions. Bursawī links al-ʿAlīm to that claim; Abū Ḥayyān says Adam's learning reveals why God appointed him. [3 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 2:32/q27/p2 The story prepares the audience for the call to the Children of Israel. — Elmalılı says divine lordship and embedded human gifts lead into 2:40; Ṭabarī says the account recalls favors to their ancestors and warns of Iblis-like punishment for continued rebellion. [2 notes: ELMALILI; TAB-FULL]

## 2:32/q28 What does recounting this unseen dialogue establish for Muhammad's audience?  [* · links]
- 2:32/q28/p1 Revelation of inaccessible events proves Muhammad's prophethood and the divine source of his message. — Ṭabarī connects the information in 2:30–31 with a proof to Jewish hearers that Muhammad knew unseen events only by revelation. [1 notes: TAB-FULL]

## 2:32/q29 What report explains the angels' prior private claim and subsequent repentance?  [* · reports]
- 2:32/q29/p1 They expected no creature more knowledgeable or honored; Adam's knowledge and commanded prostration corrected them. — Abū Ḥayyān transmits their expectation. Al-Durr reports Qatāda and al-Ḥasan that the angels privately claimed superiority and repented when unable to answer. Al-Baghawī's report says prostration was ordered after Adam's spirit was breathed, citing 15:29. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; DURR-FULL(قتادة والحسن); BIQAI-FULL(البغوي)]

## 2:32/q30 What inward lesson is drawn from the angels' answer?  [* · inward]
turns on: Whether the inner emphasis lies on humility before God or the limit of the angelic spiritual station.
- 2:32/q30/p1 Reliance on God purifies the self of pretension and brings recognition of one's limits. — Qushayrī links reliance to purity and confessed ignorance. Abū ʿUthmān al-Maghribī, with a parallel from Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, says creatures suffer from pretensions: angels claimed worship then admitted ignorance. [3 notes: QUSHAYRI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو عثمان المغربي,جعفر الصادق)]
- 2:32/q30/p2 The angels' fixed perfections disclose their shortfall from human spiritual perfection. — Qāshānī reads their answer as testimony that they cannot acquire the ranks of human knowledge. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 2:32/q31 What broader truths does the episode establish about human knowledge and speech?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the episode is used to stress knowledge's rank or the risks of representation.
- 2:32/q31/p1 Knowledge is central to Adam's honor and human vicegerency. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī says God has comprehensive knowledge, angels know what He teaches, and Adam receives what equips him for succession. Bayḍāwī cites 39:9 for knowledge's superiority to worship. [3 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR; BAYDAWI-FULL]
- 2:32/q31/p2 Human speech can carry truth but can also enable illusion and evil. — Elmalılı says speech represents reality indirectly; identifying concepts with reality leads to error and compounded ignorance and gives Satan a route to mislead. [3 notes: ELMALILI]

## 2:32/q32 What does the verse teach about claims to hidden knowledge?  [* · interpretation]
- 2:32/q32/p1 Claims about unseen events require reliable transmitted report or proof. — Ṭabarī rebukes soothsayers, diviners, fortune tellers and astrologers who claim unseen knowledge without either. Rāzī argues that angels' ignorance makes independent human access still less plausible. [2 notes: TAB-FULL; RAZI-FULL]

- 2:32/q33/p1 The cited Study Quran note says Eve's origin is not narrated, though primordial Adam represents male and female humanity. — It notes that the Qurʾān does not explain the move from representative Adam to the first couple, referencing 2:30–34 and 7:11. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]
