# 96:4: 26 questions

## 96:4/q01 How does 'who' attach to the surrounding sentence?  [ٱلَّذِى · grammar]
turns on: Whether the relative clause qualifies an earlier relative or serves in the new explanatory sentence.
- 96:4/q01/p1 It modifies the earlier 'who' as an adjective. — Al-Naḥḥās takes this relative clause as a description of the first alladhī. [1 notes: NAHHAS]
- 96:4/q01/p2 The teaching clause is an explanatory predication in the sentence about 'your Lord'. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says rabbuka is the subject, with either this clause or 'taught humanity what it did not know' as predicate; al-Samīn says the paired teaching clauses admit the same analyses as the paired creation clauses. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]

## 96:4/q02 What does God teach here?  [عَلَّمَ بِٱلْقَلَمِ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the pen refers to ordinary writing, heavenly scripture, or a wider kind of knowledge; the expanded reading explicitly supplies 'writing'.
- 96:4/q02/p1 God teaches human beings writing and the knowledge it records through a pen. — Muqātil also joins writing to teaching the Qur'an. Al-Kalbī allows 'writing or the written book'; Abū Isḥāq specifies humans writing. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Zajjāj, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Māwardī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, al-Nasafī, and others give the straightforward script or handwriting gloss. Al-Jishumī prefers it for preservation and instruction. Ibn al-Zubayr's expanded wording supplies 'writing'; al-Qurṭubī and al-Biqāʿī link it to divine generosity. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾi allows teaching reading or writing. [45 notes: MUQATIL; TAB; ZAJJAJ; THALABI; MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق,الكلبي,صاحب النظم); WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(أبو مسلم); BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; BURHAN; BIQAI; ITQAN(ابن فارس); BURSEVI; ALUSI(بعضهم); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI(ابن عباس,الزمخشري,الطبري); CORPUSCORANICUM; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; BINTSHATI+; JISHUMI+]
- 96:4/q02/p2 The pen stands for writing that conveys otherwise absent or hidden matters. — Al-Rāzī explicitly calls this a metonym, contrasted with his other direct gloss of teaching book writing; both show writing's excellence. Corpus Coranicum also allows 'teaching with a pen' as distinct from teaching writing. [3 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 96:4/q02/p3 The teaching is revealed religious knowledge or God's speech recorded in the heavenly Tablet. — Al-Rāzī contrasts the rational sign of creation with transmitted rulings and prophethood. Abū ʿAlī says angels read God's speech from the Preserved Tablet and convey it; Neuwirth takes the pen as heavenly and invokes 85:22, 68:1, and 80:13–16. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports al-Nīsābūrī's similar symbolic contrast but sets it aside. [6 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; JISHUMI(أبو علي); BINTSHATI(الفخر الرازي,النيسابوري); CORPUSCORANICUM(Neuwirth); BINTSHATI-]
- 96:4/q02/p4 The teaching encompasses all human learning, including necessary knowledge and reason. — Al-Qushayrī includes necessary and acquired knowledge. Al-Jishumī reports alternatives of all religious and legal knowledge, all human learning, and necessary sciences and reasoning that perfect intellect. [2 notes: QUSHAYRI; JISHUMI]
- 96:4/q02/p5 The teaching is the names taught to Adam and inherited by his descendants. — Al-Jishumī reports this explanation; Ibn Fāris also links letters of script to the names God taught Adam. [2 notes: JISHUMI; ITQAN(ابن فارس)]

## 96:4/q03 Why are the objects of 'taught' unstated?  [عَلَّمَ · grammar]
- 96:4/q03/p1 They are omitted so the statement centers God's act of teaching; context supplies writers and writing. — Abū Ḥayyān says both objects of ʿallama are omitted for this focus. Ibn ʿĀshūr supplies 'writers' or 'people to write' and notes the Arabs' regard for literacy. Ṣāḥib al-naẓm supplies humanity and its unknown knowledge from verse 5; repetition emphasizes teaching. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(صاحب النظم)]

## 96:4/q04 What does the bāʾ in 'by the pen' express?  [بِٱلْقَلَمِ · grammar]
- 96:4/q04/p1 It marks the pen as the instrument or means of teaching and writing. — Al-Burhān defines instrumental bāʾ through writing with a pen and compares bism Allāh. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls it causal while still treating the pen as means, and allows a circumstantial or fresh statement. [2 notes: BURHAN; TABATABAI]

## 96:4/q05 What does 'pen' denote here?  [ٱلْقَلَمِ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the pen is the earthly writing tool or a heavenly medium.
- 96:4/q05/p1 The ordinary writing instrument, specifically a trimmed reed nib and, by extension, writing tools. — Ibn ʿĀshūr describes the smooth reed, slit tip, ink, and paper, and recalls the pens in 3:44. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ compares the oath in 68:1. Kur'an Yolu extends its functional sense to modern tools of information exchange. [7 notes: BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 96:4/q05/p2 A heavenly pen or the written speech of God on the Preserved Tablet. — Abū ʿAlī says angels read and transmit the Tablet's writing. Neuwirth invokes 85:22, 68:1, and 80:13–16; Corpus Coranicum allows that but says later plural uses at 31:27 and 3:44 also support earthly pens. Al-Qāshānī inwardly reads the pen as the supreme first spirit. [3 notes: JISHUMI(أبو علي); CORPUSCORANICUM(Neuwirth); QASHANI]

## 96:4/q06 Why is a pen called qalam, and where does the word come from?  [ٱلْقَلَمِ · meaning]
turns on: The notes give an Arabic derivation from cutting and a borrowing history through Ethiopic.
- 96:4/q06/p1 It is named from cutting or trimming. — Al-Māwardī and al-Qurṭubī compare trimming a fingernail; al-Bursevī refers to the cut nib. [5 notes: MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 96:4/q06/p2 The word is borrowed through Ethiopic from Greek kalamos. — Corpus Coranicum gives the Ethiopic and Greek derivation. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 96:4/q07 Is 'writing' part of a variant reading of the verse?  [عَلَّمَ بِٱلْقَلَمِ · readings]
turns on: Whether an addition differing from the codex consonants counts as a reading or only explains the received text.
- 96:4/q07/p1 A reading attributed to Ibn al-Zubayr explicitly adds 'writing' after 'taught'. — Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Bayḍāwī, and al-Ālūsī cite it to make the writing gloss explicit; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ gives a similar attribution to Ibn ʿAbbās. Corpus Coranicum calls it a likely later clarification. [7 notes: KASHSHAF(ابن الزبير); KASHSHAF-FULL(ابن الزبير); BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI(ابن الزبير); BINTSHATI(ابن عباس); CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 96:4/q07/p2 The extra word is an explanatory gloss, not Qur'anic text. — Abū Ḥayyān rejects it as a reading because it differs from the codex consonantal text; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports his judgment. [3 notes: BINTSHATI(ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن الزبير)+; ABUHAYYAN(ابن الزبير)+; KASHSHAF(ابن الزبير)-; KASHSHAF-FULL(ابن الزبير)-; BAYDAWI-FULL-; ALUSI(ابن الزبير)-]

## 96:4/q08 Who is taught by the pen?  [عَلَّمَ بِٱلْقَلَمِ · referent]
turns on: Whether the unstated learner is all writers, an original writer, prophets, or Muhammad.
- 96:4/q08/p1 Everyone who writes, or human beings generally. — Al-Māwardī says all writing comes from God's teaching; al-Qurṭubī prefers this third view because creation and instruction are joined. Abū Ḥayyān calls it the apparent sense. Al-Ālūsī notes that the person is not named in the verse. [7 notes: MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; QURTUBI; ABUHAYYAN; ALUSI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; QURTUBI-FULL+]
- 96:4/q08/p2 Adam, the first writer. — Kaʿb al-Aḥbār gives Adam; al-Bursevī relays that he wrote Arabic, Syriac, and all books in clay three hundred years before death, later recovered by Idrīs. Al-Ālūsī doubts the special scripts attributed to him. [8 notes: MAWARDI(كعب الأحبار); MAWARDI-FULL(كعب الأحبار); TABRISI(كعب); QURTUBI(كعب الأحبار); QURTUBI-FULL(كعب الأحبار); ABUHAYYAN(قيل); ALUSI(كعب); BURSEVI(كعب الأحبار)]
- 96:4/q08/p3 Idrīs, the first writer. — Al-Ḍaḥḥāk gives Idrīs; Ibn Ḥibbān's Ṣaḥīḥ also calls him the first to write with a pen. Al-Ālūsī doubts special alphabet inscriptions attributed to him; al-Bursevī says he recovered Adam's clay books and invented sand writing. [9 notes: MAWARDI(الضحاك); MAWARDI-FULL(الضحاك); TABRISI(الضحاك); QURTUBI(الضحاك); QURTUBI-FULL(الضحاك); ABUHAYYAN(الضحاك); ALUSI(الضحاك); ITQAN(ابن حبان); BURSEVI(كعب الأحبار)]
- 96:4/q08/p4 Every prophet who wrote with a pen. — Al-Ṭabrisī and al-Ālūsī report this extension on the ground that God taught each writer. [2 notes: TABRISI(قيل); ALUSI(قيل)]
- 96:4/q08/p5 Muḥammad is the particular human taught. — Al-Jishumī relates this to 'He taught you what you did not know' in 4:113; al-Bursevī says God taught the Prophet without writing even while people learn through pens. [2 notes: JISHUMI; BURSEVI]

## 96:4/q09 What does this teaching show about knowledge and writing?  [* · interpretation]
- 96:4/q09/p1 Writing is a divine favor that preserves and communicates knowledge and sustains religious and worldly life. — Qatāda says religion and livelihood cannot stand without it. Al-Zamakhsharī lists sciences, wisdom, former peoples' accounts, sayings, and revealed books; al-Bayḍāwī stresses distant communication. Al-Rāzī calls the pen a hunter of knowledge and likens its far silent reach to Zechariah's quiet prayer (19:3 or 3:38); Solomon's ifrit contrasts passing speech with writing that binds it. Ibn Kathīr distinguishes mental, oral, and written knowledge: writing entails the other two; an athar urges binding knowledge in writing. Al-Bursevī urges writers to consider what they will see at Judgment. Ibn ʿĀshūr adds transmission across generations, oral teaching, and discovery. Qutb calls the pen humanity's deepest teaching tool, whose full value was not yet apparent. [55 notes: TAB(قتادة); TAB-FULL(قتادة); IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); JISHUMI(أبو مسلم,قتادة); KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(قتادة); RAZI(سليمان عليه السلام); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI(علماؤنا,قتادة); QURTUBI-FULL(علماؤنا,قتادة); BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN(الزمخشري); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; DURR(قتادة); DURR-FULL(قتادة); BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI(الزمخشري,الفخر الرازي); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; IBNKATHIR]
- 96:4/q09/p2 The pen and writing themselves display God's fine wisdom, ordering, and generosity. — Al-Zamakhsharī says pen and script suffice as a sign of subtle governance; Abū Ḥayyān repeats him. Al-Biqāʿī says knowledge raises humans from a low rational state; God grants the unlettered community unusual gifts despite shorter lives. Al-Qurṭubī says teaching moves people from ignorance's darkness to knowledge's light. Al-ʿĀlūsī says God's generosity lacks ulterior purpose and alone merits the unrestricted 'Most Generous'. [16 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; QURTUBI; ABUHAYYAN(الزمخشري); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI]
- 96:4/q09/p3 Written preservation of the Qur'an secures its exact guidance for all humanity. — Iṣlāḥī contrasts earlier orally transmitted prophetic teachings and the present Torah, where divine and narrator wording cannot be distinguished, with the Qur'an preserved word for word under God's scrutiny; this especially favors unlettered Arabs. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the first verses signal concern for writing revelation and the Prophet appointed scribes early. [4 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNASHUR; ELMALILI]

## 96:4/q10 What images does poetry use for the pen and writing?  [ٱلْقَلَمِ · rhetoric]
- 96:4/q10/p1 Poems picture dark, swift pens as serpents or figures animated by white nibs, ink as venom or honey, and the written page as exalted. — Al-Zamakhsharī quotes black-legged figures and white blades; Abū Ḥayyān says speckled swift snakes; al-Ṭabrisī and al-Ālūsī quote serpent saliva and gathered honey. Al-Qurṭubī's modern poet likens the stained nib to an old suitor and says pages ascend to God. Al-Ālūsī adds a jinn and eager hands. Ibn ʿĀshūr quotes Abū Ḥayya's Jewish hand and a poet boasting of literacy. [13 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(بعضهم); QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL(الشعراء المحدثون); ABUHAYYAN(بعضهم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI(الزمخشري,قيل); ALUSI-FULL(شاعر غير مسمى); IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو حية النميري)]

## 96:4/q11 How does the pen teaching relate to the surrounding verses?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the sequence is read as a rational-to-transmitted progression, reassurance during the first recitation, or a broader presentation of divine favors.
- 96:4/q11/p1 Creation provides rational evidence of lordship; pen teaching supplies transmitted knowledge and evidence of prophethood. — Al-Rāzī argues lordship can be known without prophecy but prophecy presupposes lordship. Al-Bayḍāwī describes movement from humanity's low beginning to its high end, from rational to sensory evidence. Elmalılı says recitation and writing carry transmitted proof while creation yields rational proof; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports al-Nīsābūrī alongside al-Rāzī but sets symbolic readings aside. [8 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ELMALILI(Beyzâvî); BINTSHATI(الفخر الرازي,النيسابوري); BINTSHATI-]
- 96:4/q11/p2 The clause reassures the unlettered Prophet that the God who teaches writing can also teach him recitation. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says without the hadith it answers an implied question about reading; with the hadith it answers 'I cannot read' through dictation, instruction, or inspiration, as with Adam's names. Its position between subject and predicate emphasizes this reassurance. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:4/q11/p3 The paired clauses bind creation, generosity, reading, and teaching into God's favors and attributes. — Al-Samīn parallels create/create humanity with teach/teach humanity. Ibn ʿĀshūr says repeated 'your Lord' signals care, al-Akram intensifies giving without comparison, and lordship, creation, teaching, and generosity imply divine oneness, acts, and perfection. Al-Kashshāf and Ibn Kathīr call these opening verses a supreme gift. Al-Itqān calls the opening the Qur'an's title because it previews reading, God's name, law, and teaching. [12 notes: SAMIN-DURR; IBNASHUR-FULL; KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ITQAN; IBNKATHIR]
- 96:4/q11/p4 The opening's praise of knowledge is answered later by warning against wealth. — Al-Ālūsī reports that the closing verses urge religion and learning over riches and worldly life. [1 notes: ALUSI(قيل)]
- 96:4/q11/p5 The repetition of 'taught' resembles a repeated word at 106:1–2. — Corpus Coranicum observes the formal parallel. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 96:4/q12 What does the verse imply about Muhammad's literacy and the Qur'an's origin?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether later instruction about writing implies acquired literacy, and how 29:48 bears on the time before and after revelation.
- 96:4/q12/p1 His unlettered recitation, while the verse praises writing, confirms that the Qur'an came from God. — Al-Qurṭubī says the Prophet knew writing least among Arabs and 29:48 strengthens the miracle; Qutb notes he could not write the book he proclaimed. Elmalılı says he recited and dictated without writing and God promised retention at 87:6; Ibn ʿĀshūr says reading and pen teaching imply his unlettered state. [8 notes: QURTUBI(علماؤنا); QURTUBI-FULL(علماؤنا); ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI]
- 96:4/q12/p2 He may have gained knowledge of writing after prophethood without harming the earlier miracle. — Elmalılı reports the common view that he did not read others' writing and had to ask ʿAlī which word to erase at Ḥudaybiyya; accounts of his directions to Muʿāwiya on the pen and basmala could be special revelation or later learning. He holds that he did not himself write or study books; 29:48 concerns the period before revelation. [4 notes: ELMALILI]

## 96:4/q13 May the Prophet's sayings be written down?  [* · law]
- 96:4/q13/p1 Yes; the Prophet authorized writing what was heard from him and cited God's teaching by the pen. — Al-Thaʿlabī names ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr; al-Qurṭubī's version names Ibn ʿUmar. [3 notes: THALABI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); QURTUBI(عبد الله بن عمر); QURTUBI-FULL(عبد الله بن عمر)]

## 96:4/q14 What rule about teaching women writing is reported?  [* · law]
- 96:4/q14/p1 A report discourages giving women upper rooms or teaching them writing. — Al-Qurṭubī attributes this via Ibn Masʿūd to the Prophet without grading it. His scholars say upper rooms allow looking at men and risk temptation; writing could enable desired correspondence, since it conveys the absent person's hand and inner thought. They cite a saying commending women remaining unseen by men. [6 notes: QURTUBI(عبد الله بن مسعود,علماؤنا); QURTUBI-FULL(عبد الله بن مسعود,علماؤنا)]

## 96:4/q15 What pens and acts of divine or angelic writing do reports describe?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports describe distinct acts: first creation and decree, God's written mercy, angelic recording, and four creations made by His hand.
- 96:4/q15/p1 The first created pen was commanded to record every destiny until Resurrection. — Al-Qurṭubī calls it an established Prophetic report and places the record above the Throne; al-Suyūṭī says it begins with forgiveness for whoever repents. [3 notes: QURTUBI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); BURSEVI(السيوطي)]
- 96:4/q15/p2 God wrote above the Throne that His mercy overcomes His wrath. — Al-Qurṭubī calls Abū Hurayra's report sound. [2 notes: QURTUBI(أبو هريرة); QURTUBI-FULL(أبو هريرة)]
- 96:4/q15/p3 The fetus's decree and human deeds are written by angels. — Ibn Masʿūd's authentic report says after forty-two nights an angel shapes the embryo and records sex, lifespan, and provision; 82:10–11 names guardian scribes. Al-Qurṭubī's scholars classify divine, angelic, and human pens by what each records. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ lists Ibn Qayyim's twelve pens, from primordial decree and revelation through juristic, medical, political, accounting, judicial, testimonial, dream, historical, linguistic, and polemical writing. [5 notes: QURTUBI(عبد الله بن مسعود,علماؤنا); QURTUBI-FULL(عبد الله بن مسعود,علماؤنا); BINTSHATI]
- 96:4/q15/p4 God created the pen, Throne, Garden of Eden, and Adam with His hand. — Mujāhid's report from Ibn ʿUmar says all other creatures were then brought forth by 'Be'. [4 notes: MAWARDI(ابن عمر); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عمر); QURTUBI(مجاهد); QURTUBI-FULL(مجاهد)]

## 96:4/q16 Who first developed or transmitted writing among peoples?  [* · reports]
turns on: The accounts differ over a first divinely taught writer and later human transmission of Arabic scripts.
- 96:4/q16/p1 Writing was taught by God and was known to Adam or other prophets. — Al-Burhān cites this verse and 68:1, and Ibn Fāris includes letters among Adam's divinely taught names. Kaʿb's account gives Adam Arabic and Syriac clay books; reports also name Idrīs as inventor of sand writing, Tahmurth of Persian, and Joseph of papyrus. [3 notes: BURHAN; ITQAN(ابن فارس); BURSEVI(كعب الأحبار)]
- 96:4/q16/p2 Ishmael first established Arabic writing. — Ibn ʿAbbās gives this report; al-Burhān says accounts of origins are numerous and conflicting. [1 notes: BURHAN(ابن عباس)]
- 96:4/q16/p3 Arabic script reached the Hijaz through al-Hira and al-Anbar, with Harb ibn Umayya bringing it to Mecca. — Al-Kalbī and al-Haytham trace Harb through Aslam ibn Asdara or Sidra to inventor Marāmir ibn Murra. Al-Ālūsī says writing was ancient among non-Arabs but comparatively recent in Arabia; Ibn ʿĀshūr adds the earlier Himyarite Musnad. One poem boasts of learning to write. [6 notes: ALUSI(الكلبي والهيثم بن عدي,غير واحد); ALUSI-FULL(الكلبي، الهيثم بن عدي); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:4/q16/p4 Himyarites used a separate, unjoined Musnad script; writing systems have many branches. — Al-Ālūsī says Musnad was prestigious and restricted to authorized learners. He allows a count of twelve scripts only as root families, because derivatives are nearly innumerable. [3 notes: ALUSI(بعضهم,قيل); ALUSI-FULL]
- 96:4/q16/p5 Early Arab and Muslim writing had established letter names, grammar, and orthographic conventions. — Al-Burhān answers claims of ancient ignorance by tracing letter names to Adam. He says Abū al-Aswad revived grammar and al-Khalīl prosody, while codex spelling of wāw, yāʾ, and hamza shows early orthographic knowledge. Al-Farrāʾ prefers codex spelling where Arabic usage supports it; Mālik requires preserving first codex orthography even for extra unpronounced wāw or alif. [5 notes: BURHAN(الفراء,مالك)]

## 96:4/q17 What happened when the opening revelation came to Muhammad?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports differ in whether the delivery includes a written silk sheet or the opening and washing of his chest.
- 96:4/q17/p1 At Hira after true dreams and retreat, Gabriel repeatedly ordered him to recite, embraced him three times, and delivered the opening verses. — ʿĀʾisha's account has Khadīja supply provisions; Muhammad answered that he could not read and returned trembling. Al-Wāḥidī notes transmission in Bukhārī and Muslim; Ibn Kathīr cites both Ṣaḥīḥs through al-Zuhrī; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the retention shows immediate recitation and Khadīja's consultation of Waraqa confirms it. Ibn Shaddād likewise reports the squeezing. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says the words remained fixed in his heart. [25 notes: TAB(عائشة,عبد الله بن شداد); WAHIDI-ASBAB(عائشة رضي الله عنها); BAGHAWI(عائشة); IBNKATHIR(عائشة); DURR(عائشة,عبد الله بن شداد); DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); BINTSHATI(عائشة أم المؤمنين); IBNASHUR-FULL(عائشة)]
- 96:4/q17/p2 Gabriel or an angel brought an inscribed silk or brocade sheet with the opening verses. — Ibn Hishām's ʿUbayd report places the annual Ramadan seclusion at Hira, feeding the poor, and a return circumambulation of the Kaaba; Gabriel presents written silk and embraces Muhammad three times. Al-Zuhrī and ʿAmr b. Dīnār, ʿAmr b. Jābir, and al-Itqān also report an inscribed sheet. [5 notes: IBNHISHAM(عبيد); DURR(الزهري وعمرو بن دينار,عمرو بن جابر); ITQAN(الزهري,عبيد بن عمير)]
- 96:4/q17/p3 Gabriel appeared vast, opened and washed Muhammad's chest, taught five verses, and weighed him against men. — A second ʿĀʾisha account says Michael predicted the community would follow. [1 notes: DURR(عائشة)]

## 96:4/q18 How did Khadija, Waraqa, and Gabriel respond after the first revelation?  [* · reports]
- 96:4/q18/p1 Khadija reassured Muhammad and Waraqa identified Gabriel as Moses' angel and predicted opposition. — Khadīja cited kinship, truthfulness, hospitality, bearing burdens, and aid in adversity. Waraqa, a Christian familiar with Arabic Gospel writing, promised support if alive. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ adds that Muhammad saw Gabriel in the sky. Ibn Shaddād reports Khadīja's referral to Waraqa. [15 notes: TAB(عائشة,عبد الله بن شداد); WAHIDI-ASBAB(عائشة رضي الله عنها); BAGHAWI(عائشة); IBNKATHIR(خديجة,ورقة بن نوفل); BINTSHATI(عائشة أم المؤمنين)]
- 96:4/q18/p2 After Waraqa's death revelation paused; Gabriel reassured Muhammad during grief, and al-Duha supplied reassurance. — Ibn Kathīr marks the mountain-peaks account 'as has reached us'; al-Baghawī attributes the addition to al-Zuhrī. Ibn Shaddād connects the pause to 93:1–3. [7 notes: TAB(عبد الله بن شداد); BAGHAWI(الزهري); IBNKATHIR]

## 96:4/q19 Was this the first revelation, and how much of the sura came first?  [* · sciences]
turns on: The reports variously call the first unit the sura or specify its first five verses; verse counts also differ.
- 96:4/q19/p1 The opening five verses through 96:5 were the first Qur'anic revelation at Hira; the rest came later. — ʿĀʾisha and several transmitters identify Iqra as first; Ibn ʿAbbās and scholars quoted by al-Wāḥidī and al-Durr specify five verses. Ibn ʿĀshūr dates it to Ramadan 17, year 40 after the Elephant; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites al-Ṭabarī and biography reports stopping at verse 5 and Abū Ḥayyān's rejection of claims for al-Muddaththir or al-Fātiḥa. Ibn ʿĀshūr notes the next subject starts at verse 6. [34 notes: TAB(أبو رجاء العطاردي,عائشة,عبيد بن عمير,عطاء بن يسار,مجاهد); WAHIDI-ASBAB(بعض علمائهم); JISHUMI(جماعة من المفسرين,عائشة ومجاهد وعطاء بن يسار); BAGHAWI(أكثر المفسرين); BAGHAWI-FULL(أكثر المفسرين); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURHAN(عائشة); DURR(ابن عباس,بعض علمائهم); DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ITQAN(عبيد بن عمير); IBNASHUR(عائشة,عائشة، أبو موسى الأشعري); IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI(أبو حيان,الفخر الرازي); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 96:4/q19/p2 The whole Iqra sura was first revealed. — ʿUbayd ibn ʿUmayr, Abū Rajāʾ, ʿAṭāʾ, Mujāhid, and ʿĀʾisha are reported as calling Iqra the first sura; some reports may be using its name for the opening passage. Mujāhid adds that Nūn and the Pen followed. [11 notes: TAB(أبو رجاء العطاردي,عائشة,عبيد بن عمير,عطاء بن يسار,مجاهد); JISHUMI(عائشة ومجاهد وعطاء بن يسار)]
- 96:4/q19/p3 The sura is Meccan, with differing verse counts. — Al-Jishumī counts seventeen; al-Durr counts nineteen. [2 notes: JISHUMI; DURR-FULL]

## 96:4/q20 Which passages followed the opening revelation?  [* · sciences]
- 96:4/q20/p1 Nūn and the Pen, al-Muddaththir, and al-Duha are named as later revelations. — ʿĀʾisha gives these in the aftermath of Iqra; one Mujāhid transmission specifically says Nūn and the Pen followed. Ibn Shaddād connects al-Duha to a pause and reassurance. [6 notes: TAB(عائشة,عبد الله بن شداد,مجاهد)]

## 96:4/q21 What is said of reciting this sura?  [* · sciences]
- 96:4/q21/p1 A report promises reward as though the reciter had read all the shorter suras. — Al-Bayḍāwī transmits this attribution to the Prophet. [1 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 96:4/q22 What other reports identify Idris and his family?  [* · reports]
- 96:4/q22/p1 Idris is called Khanuq, Noah's grandfather, a prophet and messenger, and the first pen writer. — Wahb calls Khanuq Syriac; another derivation links Idrīs to extensive study of scriptures. A weak al-Mustadrak report says he was raised to the sixth heaven after seeing earthly injustice, linked to 19:57; Ibn Qutayba gives age 350 and Ibn ʿAbbās says a thousand years separated him from Noah. [6 notes: ITQAN(ابن حبان,ابن عباس,ابن قتيبة,سمرة,وهب بن منبه)]

## 96:4/q23 What ancillary genealogical claims appear with the account of early writers?  [* · reports]
- 96:4/q23/p1 The cited material gives variant names, ancestry, birth intervals, and lifespans for Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac. — Al-Jawālīqī treats Ibrahim as a non-Arabic name with variant forms and a seven-reader form Ibrāhām; another claim gives Syriac 'merciful father', while al-Kirmānī derives it from intense looking. The genealogy runs from Azar/Tarah through Nahor, Sharukh, Raghu, Falikh, Abir, Shalikh, Arfakhshadh, and Shem to Noah. Al-Wāqidī dates Abraham's birth two thousand years after Adam. Abū Hurayra gives circumcision at 120 and death at 200; al-Nawawī reports 175. Ishmael is eldest, his name has a final-nūn variant, and Isaac is said to be born fourteen years later, live 180 years, with a Hebrew name meaning laughter. [12 notes: ITQAN(أبو علي بن مسكويه,أبو هريرة,الجواليقي,الكرماني,النووي وغيره,الواقدي)]

## 96:4/q24 What adjacent examples of letters and grammatical particles do sources give?  [* · grammar]
turns on: The sources give separate analogies about letters and bāʾ while explaining language associated with pen and writing.
- 96:4/q24/p1 Opening letters and the oath by them honor the alphabet, whose abbreviations follow Arab speech habits. — Ibn Qutayba says letters build revealed books, divine names and attributes, speech, and worship; shorthand substitutes related words or nearby letters, as in مدهته for مدحته and جدث/جدف. [2 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL]
- 96:4/q24/p2 A prepositional phrase with bāʾ implies a verb, and bāʾ has several grammatical functions. — Al-Rāzī compares bi-l-qalam with 'I wrote with the pen', oath bi-llāh, and travel 'in God's name'; omitting the verb widens implication. Sībawayh contrasts the bāʾ's kasra with the kāf's fatḥa. Al-Rāzī lists original or extra bāʾ, adhesion, partitive according to al-Shāfiʿī, strengthening negation (41:46), transitivity (2:17), and meaning 'in', supported by poetry and 46:9. [9 notes: RAZI-FULL(الشافعي,سيبويه)]

## 96:4/q25 How should the title 'Most Generous' be understood or used?  [* · rhetoric]
- 96:4/q25/p1 God's unrestricted superlative generosity is unique, an intensive attribute without self-interest. — Al-Ālūsī defines karam as fitting benefit without ulterior motive, links it to teaching by pen, and reports scholars criticizing the unrestricted human honorific in correspondence, especially for Jews and Christians; he urges reverence and gratitude. Ibn ʿĀshūr says al-Akram is an intensifier, not comparison. [4 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 96:4/q25/p2 The second 'Read' introduces assurance that answers Muhammad's excuse. — Al-Ālūsī reports analysis taking it as a new command, with 'Your Lord is most generous' as a circumstantial clause rather than mere emphasis. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 96:4/q26 What is knowledge in this account of divine teaching?  [* · interpretation]
- 96:4/q26/p1 Knowledge is apprehending a thing as it truly is, and God's first revealed verses are an initial mercy and blessing. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ defines knowledge against ignorance and refers to her discussion of 102:3; Ibn Kathīr calls these first verses God's first mercy and blessing to people. [2 notes: BINTSHATI; IBNKATHIR]
