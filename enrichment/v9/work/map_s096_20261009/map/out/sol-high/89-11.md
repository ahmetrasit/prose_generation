# 89:11: 10 questions

## 89:11/q01 Who are 'those who transgressed'?  [ٱلَّذِينَ · referent]
turns on: Whether the plural relative reaches back over all the named peoples or attaches to the nearest antecedent, Pharaoh and his people.
- 89:11/q01/p1 The description covers all three previously named peoples: ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Pharaoh with his people or troops. — Muqātil, al-Ṭabarī, al-Baghawī and his full text, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Biqāʿī, Ibn ʿĀshūr, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, and Study Quran connect the plural to the preceding examples. Al-Biqāʿī says Pharaoh's name represents his troops because a collective follows its leader; the reference also includes the followers of ʿĀd and Thamūd (89:6, 89:9, 89:10). Al-Rāzī prefers this comprehensive scope over the nearer antecedent. [15 notes: MUQATIL; TAB; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI; BAYDAWI; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN; RAZI+]
- 89:11/q01/p2 The description can refer specifically to Pharaoh and his people, the nearest preceding group. — Al-Rāzī gives proximity to Pharaoh as the reason for this possible scope but prefers all the named peoples. Ibn ʿĀshūr and his full text also allow Pharaoh and his people alone. [3 notes: RAZI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 89:11/q02 How is 'those who transgressed' parsed?  [ٱلَّذِينَ طَغَوْا۟ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the relative expression remains a modifier without ellipsis or is detached as censure, and, if detached, whether its implied construction is accusative or nominative.
- 89:11/q02/p1 It is accusative as an expression of censure or blame, with an implied verb such as 'I mean.' — Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī call this the best parse; al-Qurṭubī prefers it. Al-Bursawī reports al-Zamakhsharī's view that this implied reproach better suits the meaning. Al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Ālūsī, and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ record it as possible; al-Ālūsī says the same parsing carries into 89:12. Al-Ālūsī's full text says the detached censure can mean each tyrant. [16 notes: BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI(المفسرون واللغويون); KASHSHAF-FULL+; KASHSHAF+; QURTUBI-FULL+; RAZI+]
- 89:11/q02/p2 It is genitive, a modifier of the previously named ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Pharaoh. — Al-Bursawī prefers this because it requires no ellipsis, although he acknowledges the force of the accusative reproach. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says all three parses are formally valid but rhetorically unequal and favors this connected modifier. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn, and al-Ālūsī also allow the construction; al-Ālūsī extends the parallel parsing to 89:12. [16 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI(المفسرون واللغويون); BINTSHATI+; BURSEVI+]
- 89:11/q02/p3 It is nominative after an omitted subject, 'they are,' as a separate statement of blame. — Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Ālūsī, and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ give this as a possible parse; al-Ālūsī applies the parallel analysis to 89:12. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ accepts its formal validity but prefers the connected modifier. [14 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI(المفسرون واللغويون)]

## 89:11/q03 What does 'they transgressed' mean here?  [طَغَوْا۟ · meaning]
- 89:11/q03/p1 They overstepped God's bounds through rebellious sin, disbelief, arrogance, injustice, and corruption, including oppression of prophets and other people. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Biqāʿī, and al-Nasafī give the basic sense as crossing limits; al-Ṭabarī specifies exceeding what God permitted and committing prohibited disbelief. Al-Jishumī compares corruption beyond its limit to water beyond its bank (69:11); Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says the water sense precedes the metaphor of tyrants' arrogance, and points to Moses' charge to Pharaoh (79:17). Corpus Coranicum refers the root ṭ-ġ-w/y to 96:6. Muqātil and al-Naḥḥās mention sins and defiance of God's command; al-Baghawī and his full text add arrogance. Al-Kalbī as reported by al-Ṭabrisī says they opposed prophets and spread killing and sin. Ibn al-Jawzī and al-Rāzī add tyranny over prophets, and al-Rāzī includes believers. Al-Qurṭubī says injustice and aggression; Ibn Kathīr says insolence, corruption, and harm to people. Al-Biqāʿī's full text specifies unbelief and injustice that others imitated. Almalılı says pride in strength and desire led to violations of justice and the rights of people and their Creator. Ibn ʿĀshūr and his full text call it extreme disobedience and injustice. Iṣlāḥī describes fearlessness toward God and the conceit that resources were self-earned and beyond accountability. Kur'an Yolu describes unrestrained rebellion and a country turned into sedition and corruption through disregard of guidance and justice. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī and Study Quran couple tyranny with spreading corruption. [26 notes: MUQATIL; TAB; NAHHAS; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA; TABRISI(الكلبي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI; QURTUBI; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]

## 89:11/q04 Which lands does 'in the lands' cover?  [فِى ٱلْبِلَٰدِ · referent]
turns on: Whether the plural is limited distributively to each people's own country or also encompasses territory within their potential coercive reach.
- 89:11/q04/p1 Each people transgressed in its own land; the plural distributes the act among their respective territories. — Al-Ṭabarī calls them their lands, and al-Naḥḥās says they lived there. Al-Bursawī assigns ʿĀd to Yemen, Thamūd to Syria, Egyptians to Egypt, and Nimrod to Sawād. Al-Ālūsī says each tyrant transgressed in his own land. Ibn ʿĀshūr and his full text read the definite article as pointing to the already mentioned peoples' lands, or as contextually restricted to them, and take the plural distributively; he adds that local oppression spreads through the lands. [8 notes: TAB; NAHHAS; BURSEVI; ALUSI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 89:11/q04/p2 Their transgression reached the lands they ruled and those they could dominate by force. — Al-Biqāʿī expressly includes both actual rule and potential coercive reach; his full text says Pharaoh, his army, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and their followers exceeded limits in the lands they ruled. [2 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL]

## 89:11/q05 What effects does tyranny have on a society?  [طَغَوْا۟ فِى ٱلْبِلَٰدِ · interpretation]
- 89:11/q05/p1 It spreads corruption by damaging rulers, the oppressed, shared values, social bonds, and the ability to resist harm. — Sayyid Quṭb says the tyrant becomes enslaved to desire and claims God's place, as Pharaoh did (79:24); the oppressed lose dignity, creativity, and ambition while resentment and degraded desires grow; rulers falsify values because sound standards threaten their power. Ibn ʿĀshūr says tyranny teaches rights violations, breeds hatred and fear, divides society, and weakens resistance to external enemies; oppression beginning locally spreads through the lands. Al-Biqāʿī's full text says unbelief and injustice become imitated precedents. [6 notes: QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; BIQAI-FULL]

## 89:11/q06 What lesson do the transgressors' histories teach about God's response?  [* · interpretation]
- 89:11/q06/p1 God watches powerful, corrupt nations, grants them time, then punishes persistent rebellion; their strength cannot prevent judgment, which also warns individuals of accountability. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says the cited ʿĀd, Iram, Thamūd, and Pharaoh carry this core lesson: transgression and corruption met punishment under God's watch. She says exegetes disagree widely about additional historical details and further sin catalogues are unnecessary. Iṣlāḥī says God controls persons and nations, sets their respite, permits reform or a final communication of truth, then seizes those still rebellious even at their zenith; the destruction anticipates each person's full reward or punishment. Kur'an Yolu says material strength cannot avert punishment for denial and rebellion, and the warning extends to rulers and other responsible people who abandon guidance and justice. It says the first audience likely knew the stories by report or ruins, and knew the peoples for strength and corruption. [9 notes: BINTSHATI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 89:11/q07 How do these historical examples relate to the surah's oath answer?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the historical lessons themselves evidence an omitted oath answer or prepare for the answer stated later.
- 89:11/q07/p1 They supply evidence for an unspoken answer to the opening oaths. — Ibn ʿĀshūr's full text gives this as one way the historical lessons function in the surah. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 89:11/q07/p2 They delay the stated answer, 'Your Lord is indeed watching,' building suspense and preparing the audience for it. — Ibn ʿĀshūr's full text gives this as the alternative relation of the examples to the later answer. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 89:11/q08 What earlier poetic parallel is noted for the catalogue of punished peoples?  [* · links]
- 89:11/q08/p1 A verse attributed to the early poet Zuhayr lists Pharaoh among people God destroyed and resembles Qur'anic catalogues of punishment. — Corpus Coranicum notes that Zuhayr names individuals where the Qur'anic catalogue names collectives, and suggests the Qur'anic historical cycles may develop related ideas. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 89:11/q09 How is the nearby 'bi-l-wādī' read?  [* · readings]
turns on: Whether the yāʾ is retained or omitted in connected reading and at a pause.
- 89:11/q09/p1 The final yāʾ is retained both in connected reading and at a pause. — Al-Ṭabrisī reports this for al-Qawwās, al-Bazzī, and Yaʿqūb. [1 notes: TABRISI(القواس، البزي، يعقوب)]
- 89:11/q09/p2 The final yāʾ is retained in connected reading and dropped at a pause. — Al-Ṭabrisī reports this for Warsh. [1 notes: TABRISI(ورش)]
- 89:11/q09/p3 The final yāʾ is omitted both in connected reading and at a pause. — Al-Ṭabrisī attributes this to the other readers. [1 notes: TABRISI(ورش)]

## 89:11/q10 How is the subsequent 'scourge' described?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the scourge is explained as the specific whip or as the punishment it signifies.
- 89:11/q10/p1 It is a kind of punishment poured upon the transgressors. — Al-Thaʿlabī reports Qatāda's gloss of the scourge in the ensuing punishment verse. [1 notes: THALABI(قتادة)]
- 89:11/q10/p2 It is the familiar whip, perhaps so named because its strike mixes flesh. — Al-Samīn gives this lexical explanation alongside his parsing of 'those who transgressed.' [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]
