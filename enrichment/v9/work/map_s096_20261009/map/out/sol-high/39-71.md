# 39:71: 42 questions

## 39:71/q01 Who are those who disbelieved?  [ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوٓا۟ · referent]
turns on: Whether the reference is all deniers or the idolaters specifically.
- 39:71/q01/p1 They are those who reject God's oneness or signs. — Muqatil specifies denial of God's oneness. Biqa'i says they cover the light of intellect and become confused. Tabataba'i associates them with obstinate rejection of God's signs. [3 notes: MUQATIL; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 39:71/q01/p2 They are the idolaters addressed in this passage. — Islahi says associating partners with God is disbelief because only pure monotheism is acceptable. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 39:71/q02 What does their being driven to Hell convey?  [وَسِيقَ إِلَىٰ جَهَنَّمَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether sawq denotes coercion, general conveyance, or the inward force of deeds.
- 39:71/q02/p1 They are urged or forced toward Hell, in humiliation. — Tabataba'i cites al-Majmaʿ for sawq as urging onward. Thalabi says they are dragged on their faces; Ibn Zaydan cites 52:13, 107:2 and 19:86 for pushing. Nasafi compares prisoners or rebels led to confinement or execution. Ibn Kathir adds thirst, threats, deafness, dumbness, blindness and some walking on their faces (17:97; 19:85–86). Ibn Ashur explains a driver goes ahead and beside one forced along, comparing 8:6. Biqa'i says God's command moves them swiftly after judgment; Bursevi adds the Persian gloss, their disgrace, and Hell's scowl. Study Quran connects heavy and light scales (23:102–103; 101:6–9) and 52:13. Abu Hayyan says driving normally implies urging and force. [19 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; RAZI-FULL(ابن زيدان); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(المجمع); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 39:71/q02/p2 All souls are driven; their destinations distinguish them. — Samarra'i compares 50:21, where every soul comes with a driver and witness: unbelievers go to Hell and the God-fearing to Paradise. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 39:71/q02/p3 Inwardly, their deeds and lower selves drive them. — Qashani reads the veiled as guided by desire and downward inclination. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 39:71/q03 What are the zumar, and how are the groups formed?  [زُمَرًا · meaning]
turns on: Whether the word denotes succession, group membership or size, derives from sound, or evokes a sounding form of driving.
- 39:71/q03/p1 Separate groups follow one another. — Majaz cites al-Akhtal; Mawrdi cites Khuffaf ibn Nudba; Qurtubi cites two unnamed poets and notes zumrah as singular, with a pattern comparison. Abu Hayyan and Samin cite a line on group after group. Nasafi and Samin call zumar circumstantial accusative. Ibn Ashur says succession is intrinsic, rather than one isolated group. Muqatil and others add that each nation's unbelievers may be separate. [34 notes: MUQATIL; MAJAZ; TAB-FULL(قتادة); THALABI(أبو عبيد والأخفش); MAWARDI-FULL(الأخفش,الحسن,السدي,خفاف بن ندبة); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL(أبو عبيدة والأخفش); IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو عبيدة); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الأخفش وأبو عبيدة); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو عبيد والأخفش); SAMIN-DURR(أبو عبيدة والأخفش); BIQAI-FULL(أبو عبيد); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(الصحاح); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 39:71/q03/p2 The groups are distinct communities or peoples. — Al-Kalbi calls them communities; Muqatil and Thalabi say each nation's unbelievers are gathered separately. [3 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(الكلبي); MUQATIL; THALABI]
- 39:71/q03/p3 They are classes arranged by kind or degree of error and sin. — Biqa'i reports Abu Ubayd: like people are gathered with like. Baydawi, Bursevi, Alusi and Ibn Ashur distinguish degrees of misguidance or wickedness. Ibn Ashur specifies Quraysh's treatment of Muslims and the Prophet, or polytheists' kinds of shirk; Islahi says the gates receive groups classified by sins. [6 notes: BIQAI-FULL(أبو عبيد); BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 39:71/q03/p4 A zumrah is a small group. — Al-Raghib relates the sense to sparse hair and scant manliness; Baydawi and Samin report the same derivation. Samin also mentions an ostrich's cry and derived music and wanton-woman terms. [4 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الراغب); ALUSI-FULL(الراغب); BURSEVI]
- 39:71/q03/p5 The word is connected with a group's sound. — Baydawi, Biqa'i, Bursevi and Alusi connect zamr to the sound or noise a gathering makes; Biqa'i compares a flute, and Samin reports al-Raghib's related sound words. [5 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الراغب); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 39:71/q03/p6 It can suggest being pushed and rebuked with a pipe-like sound. — Mawrdi compares the phrase about David's pipes; Qurtubi records a gloss connecting the sound to mizmar. [2 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]

## 39:71/q04 How are the passive vowels of sīqa and jīʾa read?  [وَسِيقَ جَآءُوهَا · readings]
turns on: The transmitted passive-vowel pronunciations.
- 39:71/q04/p1 The initial letters are pronounced with kasra. — Ibn Atiyya attributes this to the majority. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الجمهور)]
- 39:71/q04/p2 The vowels incline toward ḍamma. — Ibn Atiyya names al-Hasan, Ibn Waththab, Asim and al-Aʿmash; Qiraat-ER says the vowel texture changes without changing the consonantal verb or procession. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن وابن وثاب وعاصم والأعمش); QIRAAT-ER]

## 39:71/q05 When and how do Hell's gates open?  [حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَا جَآءُوهَا فُتِحَتْ أَبْوَٰبُهَا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the scene is explained as a physical prison-like opening or an inward account of Hell's affinity.
- 39:71/q05/p1 They stay shut until the disbelievers arrive, then open for entry. — Muqatil adds that folded records are spread. Nahhas contrasts Paradise's already-open gates. Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, Biqa'i, Bursevi, Alusi and Samarra'i compare a prison: the wait humiliates, terrifies or spiritually punishes them; Abu Hayyan and Alusi say the doors close after entry. Ibn Kathir says opening on arrival starts punishment at once; Biqa'i says heat and flames rush out. Study Quran compares 16:29. Bursevi cites 15:44 for the seven gates. [22 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; NAHHAS(بعض أهل العلم); THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURHAN(أبو علي); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(أسئلة الحكم); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; STUDYQURAN]
- 39:71/q05/p2 Inwardly, Hell opens because it longs for those suited to it. — Qashani interprets Hell's eagerness as the reason for opening at arrival. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 39:71/q06 How many gates does Hell have?  [أَبْوَٰبُهَا · interpretation]
- 39:71/q06/p1 Hell has seven gates. — Abu Hayyan and Bursevi cite 15:44; Qurtubi refers to discussion in Surat al-Hijr. Tabataba'i says the seven gates open for entry. [9 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 39:71/q07 What are the seven gates or levels called and ordered?  [أَبْوَٰبُهَا · reports]
turns on: The reports reverse the order of the named levels.
- 39:71/q07/p1 From lowest upward: Jahannam, Lahza, Hutama, Saqar, Jahim, Sa'ir, Hāwiya. — Tabataba'i reports this from Ali, describing seven stacked gates or levels. [1 notes: TABATABAI]
- 39:71/q07/p2 Hāwiya is lowest and Jahannam highest. — Tabataba'i reports this reversed order from al-Kalbi. [1 notes: TABATABAI]

## 39:71/q08 What do Hell's seven gates signify inwardly?  [أَبْوَٰبُهَا · inward]
- 39:71/q08/p1 They signify seven blameworthy traits through which entrants pass. — Bursevi lists pride, stinginess, greed, lust, envy, anger and rancor; the soul should be purified of them. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 39:71/q09 How does the construction with ḥattā and idhā work?  [حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَا جَآءُوهَا فُتِحَتْ أَبْوَٰبُهَا · grammar]
turns on: Whether the expressed opening completes the conditional directly or an implied repeated arrival does.
- 39:71/q09/p1 The answer to idhā is 'its gates were opened'. — Nahhas contrasts Paradise's omitted answer. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan say the explicit answer makes arrival the condition of opening; Baydawi says ḥattā introduces and depicts the following sentence. Bursevi calls it a narrative clause; Ibn Ashur calls ḥattā a new start and idhā a future time adverb with conditional force; Samarra'i says omitting mā presents an ordinary arrival-opening sequence, unlike 41:20. [10 notes: NAHHAS; JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 39:71/q09/p2 The full implied construction repeats 'they arrived' before the doors opened. — Burhan says ḥattā requires completion, so an implied repetition of jāʾūhā completes it. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 39:71/q10 How is futiḥat read, and what does the form suggest?  [فُتِحَتْ · readings]
turns on: Whether the form is geminated to mark repeated or emphatic action, or light for a single opening.
- 39:71/q10/p1 The tāʾ is doubled, conveying repeated, abundant or intensified opening. — Ibn Mujahid and Ibn al-Jawzi name Ibn Kathir, Nafiʿ, Abu ʿAmr and Ibn ʿAmir; Ibn Atiyya calls this the majority. Ibn Khalawayh says each door's opening may recur and cites 12:23 and 38:50; Thalabi and Baghawi describe repeated or abundant action. Qiraat-ER allows multiple gate access or emphatic opening. [10 notes: IBNMUJAHID(ابن كثير ونافع وأبو عمرو وابن عامر); IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA; THALABI(أهل الكوفة); JISHUMI(عاصم، وحمزة، والكسائي); BAGHAWI-FULL(أهل الكوفة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الجمهور); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن كثير ونافع وأبو عمرو وابن عامر); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; QIRAAT-ER]
- 39:71/q10/p2 The tāʾ is light, describing a single opening. — Ibn Mujahid and Ibn al-Jawzi name Asim, Hamza and al-Kisa'i; Ibn Atiyya also lists Talha and al-Aʿmash. Thalabi, Baghawi, Baydawi and Nasafi call it the Kufan reading. Ibn Khalawayh prefers it because this action is not repeated. [11 notes: IBNMUJAHID(عاصم وحمزة والكسائى); THALABI(أهل الكوفة); JISHUMI(عاصم، وحمزة، والكسائي); BAGHAWI-FULL(أهل الكوفة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(عاصم وحمزة والكسائي وطلحة والأعمش); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(عاصم وحمزة والكسائي); BAYDAWI-FULL(الكوفون); NASAFI-FULL(كوفى); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA+]

## 39:71/q11 Why is there no waw before Hell's gate opening, unlike Paradise's?  [فُتِحَتْ أَبْوَٰبُهَا · rhetoric]
turns on: The grammatical force of waw and whether the gates stand open before arrival.
- 39:71/q11/p1 Hell's arrival triggers opening; Paradise's gates are already open. — Nahhas reports the Basran view that waw is meaningful coordination, unlike the Kufan claim that it is extra. Ibn Atiyya compares open homes ready for joyful visitors. Abu Ali rejects the waw-of-eight account, taking Paradise's waw as circumstantial. Samarra'i compares open Paradise with a closed prison and cites 38:50. [7 notes: NAHHAS(بعض أهل العلم); IBNATIYYA-FULL; BURHAN(أبو علي); SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 39:71/q11/p2 The Paradise waw is extra and the answer is its gates opening. — Jishumi reports the construction joining arrival and opening; Nahhas assigns the extra-waw account to Kufans and says Basrans reject it. [2 notes: NAHHAS; JISHUMI+; BURHAN(أبو علي)-]
- 39:71/q11/p3 Paradise has eight gates, Hell seven; the waw is a waw of eight. — Jishumi reports this proposed explanation. Abu Ali explicitly rejects it. [1 notes: JISHUMI(قيل); BURHAN(أبو علي)-]
- 39:71/q11/p4 Paradise's answer is omitted because its blessings exceed expression. — Nahhas states the Paradise answer is omitted. Samarra'i says language cannot encompass the blessings and the answer lies within Paradise. [2 notes: NAHHAS; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 39:71/q11/p5 The Paradise waw can coordinate an appositional expression. — Jishumi adduces 21:48, where Moses and Aaron's Criterion and light exemplify conjunction understood as apposition. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 39:71/q12 Who are Hell's keepers?  [خَزَنَتُهَآ · referent]
turns on: Literal angelic custodians and Qashani's symbolic account.
- 39:71/q12/p1 They are angels appointed to guard and punish Hell's people. — Nasafi and Bursevi specify their punitive charge; Ibn Kathir calls them the harsh and powerful Zabāniya. Qurtubi gives khāzin as the singular of khazana, like sādin and sadana. Study Quran says there are nineteen, citing 74:30; Tabataba'i calls them angels. [8 notes: TAB-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]
- 39:71/q12/p2 Inwardly, Mālik and the Zabāniya symbolize bodily nature and the earthly angelic realm. — Qashani says that realm is assigned to lower selves. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 39:71/q13 What is the point of the keepers' question?  [وَقَالَ لَهُمْ خَزَنَتُهَآ أَلَمْ يَأْتِكُمْ رُسُلٌۭ · interpretation]
- 39:71/q13/p1 It rebukes and humiliates the condemned for rejecting a warning they received. — Thalabi, Baghawi, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Ibn Kathir, Abu Hayyan, Biqa'i, Alusi and Islahi call it reproach; Biqa'i says it compounds punishment. Jishumi says it shows their acts came from themselves. Study Quran cites parallel rebukes at 40:50 and 67:8–9. Kuranyolu says it also warns living readers while change remains possible. [14 notes: THALABI; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]

## 39:71/q14 How is 'did messengers come to you' read?  [أَلَمْ يَأْتِكُمْ · readings]
turns on: The gender agreement of the verb with messengers.
- 39:71/q14/p1 The verb has masculine yāʾ: yaʾtikum. — Ibn Atiyya calls this the majority; Abu Hayyan likewise contrasts it with a feminine reading. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الجمهور والأعرج); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن هرمز)]
- 39:71/q14/p2 The verb has feminine tāʾ: taʾtikum. — Ibn Atiyya names al-Aʿraj; Abu Hayyan, Samin and Alusi name Ibn Hurmuz. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الجمهور والأعرج); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن هرمز); SAMIN-DURR(ابن هرمز); ALUSI-FULL(ابن هرمز)]

## 39:71/q15 Who are the messengers 'from among you'?  [رُسُلٌۭ مِّنكُمْ · referent]
turns on: Whether 'from among you' stresses common humanity or the same community.
- 39:71/q15/p1 They are human messengers from the condemned people's own kind or peoples. — Muqatil calls them from among the people. Ibn Atiyya, Ibn Kathir, Abu Hayyan, Biqa'i, Bursevi and Alusi say shared speech, access and understanding strengthened the proof. Tabataba'i explicitly says human messengers; Nasafi calls them children of Adam. [15 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 39:71/q15/p2 The messengers are specifically from the unbelievers' own communities. — Tabari phrases the question as messengers from among the disbelievers and sent to their peoples; Muqatil says from among the people addressed. [2 notes: TAB-FULL; MUQATIL]

## 39:71/q16 How does minkum attach grammatically?  [مِّنكُمْ · grammar]
- 39:71/q16/p1 It may qualify 'messengers' or attach to the action of coming. — Samin gives both analyses. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 39:71/q17 Is there a reading other than rusul?  [رُسُلٌۭ · readings]
- 39:71/q17/p1 A reported variant has nudhur, 'warners', instead of rusul. — Alusi reports the variant without naming its reciter. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 39:71/q18 What are the Lord's signs recited by the messengers?  [ءَايَٰتِ رَبِّكُمْ · referent]
- 39:71/q18/p1 They are revealed scriptures, including God's Quranic signs and proofs. — Muqatil identifies Quranic signs. Tabari adds God's revealed Book and proofs. Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan and Bursevi identify scriptures revealed to prophets; Abu Hayyan says they brought glad tidings and warnings. Alusi notes they were revealed for the hearers' benefit; Ibn Kathir says their recital established proof. [7 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 39:71/q19 Why call the signs those of 'your Lord'?  [ءَايَٰتِ رَبِّكُمْ · rhetoric]
- 39:71/q19/p1 The wording softens admonition and recalls the good news they could have received. — Biqa'i says it makes acceptance likelier, and links 'Lord' to the omitted majesty in the second half of an iḥtibāk. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 39:71/q20 What does 'reciting to you' add?  [يَتْلُونَ عَلَيْكُمْ · interpretation]
- 39:71/q20/p1 It denotes repeated reminder and delivery of signs. — Biqa'i relates continual reminder to the sura's concern with worship and deficient souls' need for reminders; the other exegetes describe recitation of revelation as completing proof. [4 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]

## 39:71/q21 How is yatlūna related grammatically to rusul?  [يَتْلُونَ عَلَيْكُمْ · grammar]
- 39:71/q21/p1 It is a second adjective qualifying messengers. — Samin gives this syntactic analysis. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 39:71/q22 What meeting is meant by 'your day'?  [لِقَآءَ يَوْمِكُمْ هَٰذَا · referent]
turns on: Whether 'day' names Resurrection as a whole or specifically the time of entering Hell.
- 39:71/q22/p1 It is the time of their entry into Hell. — Kashshaf and Razi say Arabic calls hardship a 'day'; Bursevi adds Resurrection is not exclusive to unbelievers. Alusi prefers this as the time of actual punishment but allows Resurrection to include it or their particular punishment. Baydawi and Nasafi agree. [7 notes: TAB-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 39:71/q22/p2 It is the Day of Resurrection or Judgment. — Muqatil and Qurtubi name Resurrection or Judgment; Tabari associates the warning with what they meet that day and cites 6:130. Abu Hayyan says the warning included that day and its punishment; Thalabi calls it meeting God then. Alusi permits Resurrection as including entry or particular punishment. [7 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; THALABI; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 39:71/q23 Why emphasize warning of this day?  [وَيُنذِرُونَكُمْ لِقَآءَ يَوْمِكُمْ هَٰذَا · rhetoric]
- 39:71/q23/p1 Warning frightens and restrains; 'this' makes its threatened outcome present. — Bursevi explains frightening them with consequences. Biqa'i says warning restrains more forcefully and the demonstrative makes the meeting concrete; his iḥtibāk reads omitted glad tidings beside it. [3 notes: BURSEVI; BIQAI-FULL]

## 39:71/q24 What is the reported reading of the warning verb?  [وَيُنذِرُونَكُمْ · readings]
- 39:71/q24/p1 A reported form is nadhar, 'we warn', with ḍamma on nūn. — Kashshaf reports this reading. [1 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL]

## 39:71/q25 What do the condemned acknowledge with balā?  [قَالُوا۟ بَلَىٰ · interpretation]
- 39:71/q25/p1 They affirm that messengers came, recited, and warned, establishing proof against them. — Ibn Atiyya explains balā answers a negative question affirmatively; naʿam would affirm that none came. Qurtubi and Abu Hayyan say it acknowledges completed proof. Bursevi and Study Quran say confession comes too late to help or avert punishment; Study Quran cites 10:33, 22:18, 36:7, 40:6. [15 notes: TAB-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]

## 39:71/q26 Should recitation stop at balā?  [بَلَىٰ · readings]
- 39:71/q26/p1 Continuing without a stop is preferable. — Burhan says five instances are disputed and the continuation links backward and forward. Itqan likewise lists the five in al-Baqara, al-Zumar, al-Zukhruf, al-Hadid and al-Mulk. Burhan notes the broader discipline of stopping and resuming guides recitation, interpretation and inference, naming al-Zajjaj, Ibn al-Anbari, Ibn Abbad, al-Dani and al-Umani. [3 notes: BURHAN; ITQAN; BURHAN+]

## 39:71/q27 What does ḥaqqat mean?  [حَقَّتْ · meaning]
- 39:71/q27/p1 It became binding, due, or came to pass. — Baghawi says it became binding; Itqan gives 'became due' or 'already happened'. [2 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL; ITQAN]

## 39:71/q28 What is the 'word of punishment'?  [كَلِمَةُ ٱلْعَذَابِ · referent]
turns on: Whether the phrase denotes a specific divine utterance, prior decree, or punishment consequent on deeds.
- 39:71/q28/p1 It is God's declaration that He will fill Hell with Satan, his followers, jinn and humankind. — Muqatil and Bursevi cite God's statement to Satan at 38:85; Thalabi cites 32:13; Baghawi, Qurtubi, Baydawi and Abu Hayyan cite 11:119; Ibn al-Jawzi cites 7:18. Tabataba'i instead cites 2:39, the declaration that sign-deniers abide in Fire. Islahi calls the decree comprehensive: whoever follows Satan enters with him. The scriptural references and wording vary. [12 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL(قيل); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 39:71/q28/p2 It is God's prior judgment that these people are wretched and will remain in Hell. — Ibn Atiyya invokes 38:85 for eternal Fire; Baydawi specifies wretchedness and Hell; Razi says one under this decree cannot escape, so the blessed do not become wretched or vice versa. Biqa'i says it was established in eternity. Alusi allows this decree or the declaration to Iblis. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 39:71/q28/p3 It is the inevitable consequence of unrepented sinful choice and rejection. — Tabari reports Qatada's emphasis on deeds; Kashshaf, Nasafi, Ibn Kathir, Bursevi, Alusi, Asad and Islahi identify denial, error, opposition or bad choice as what makes the sentence due. Ibn Kathir cites 67:8–11; Nasafi cites 23:106. Bursevi says they claimed God sent nothing. [12 notes: TAB-FULL(قتادة); KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; DURR-FULL(قتادة); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]

## 39:71/q29 Why name 'the disbelievers' again instead of using a pronoun?  [عَلَى ٱلْكَٰفِرِينَ · rhetoric]
- 39:71/q29/p1 The explicit noun identifies disbelief as the quality causing their punishment. — Baydawi says it specifies who is condemned; Abu Hayyan, Biqa'i and Alusi say it names the trait deserving punishment. Alusi says the reply admits guilt rather than excusing it. [4 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 39:71/q30 Does the exchange make revelation a condition of obligation?  [أَلَمْ يَأْتِكُمْ رُسُلٌۭ مِّنكُمْ · theology]
turns on: Whether the rebuke establishes that no obligation binds before revealed law.
- 39:71/q30/p1 Yes; liability follows the arrival of messengers and their proof. — Razi argues the rebuke would be pointless if revelation were not a condition. Baydawi and Bursevi likewise ground accountability in delivery of scripture; Alusi reports this use of the verse. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 39:71/q30/p2 The inference is disputed; rational duties may still bind before revelation. — Alusi says the argument assumes a disputed generality; rational-duty proponents can say post-message disbelief deserves sharper rebuke. The reciting and warning verbs preclude making the messengers mere intellect. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 39:71/q31 Is fixed decree or rejection of prophets the basis of the condemnation?  [حَقَّتْ كَلِمَةُ ٱلْعَذَابِ · theology]
turns on: Whether entry is explained by an inescapable decree or their rejection of delivered proofs.
- 39:71/q31/p1 The divine sentence takes effect and cannot be escaped. — Razi says the wretched cannot become blessed; Biqa'i calls the decree eternal. Alusi allows a decree of wretchedness, while also citing their bad choice. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 39:71/q31/p2 Their arrogant rejection of prophets and proofs makes them liable. — Razi reports the Muʿtazila's objection: if fixed decree alone determined entry, the angels' rebuke would be pointless. Jishumi says the rebuke proves their acts arose from themselves; Asad calls punishment the consequence of unrepented sin. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL(المعتزلة); JISHUMI; ASAD-NOTES]

## 39:71/q32 What happens after the keepers' exchange?  [وَقَالَ لَهُمْ خَزَنَتُهَآ · interpretation]
- 39:71/q32/p1 The condemned are ordered into Hell. — Abu Hayyan notes the order follows the exchange. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 39:71/q33 How does this scene relate to the preceding judgment and the closing contrast?  [* · links]
- 39:71/q33/p1 It enacts the full recompense announced earlier and contrasts Hell with Paradise. — Razi begins with 39:70, every soul repaid, and follows punishment with reward. Qurtubi says each soul receives its due. Ibn Ashur cites 39:69–70, judgment and recompense. Islahi and Kuranyolu describe the fates of the two groups. Study Quran says the call to repent at 39:54–67 leads into the final contrast; its note on the gates compares 16:29. [9 notes: RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]

## 39:71/q34 Why does the conclusion describe Hell's people first?  [* · links]
- 39:71/q34/p1 Warning dominates here, so the account starts with those facing wrath. — Biqa'i notes the warning-heavy setting. Ibn Ashur says renewed warning addresses people unmoved by repeated admonition, whereas the saved have already responded and their mention repeats good news and praise. Islahi says the closing fates awaken those complacent about their hopes. [3 notes: BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 39:71/q35 How should the afterlife scene be understood?  [* · theology]
- 39:71/q35/p1 Its realities belong to faith; the descriptions make them vivid and call for belief and action. — Kuranyolu says resurrection, judgment, scales, path, Paradise and Hell are unique, unrealized events beyond human knowledge. Figurative descriptions evoke helpful associations; exegeses are personal, not binding certainties. One should believe Qur'an and authentic hadith and follow their purpose. [4 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 39:71/q36 What does the passage imply about the Qur'an as proof?  [* · links]
- 39:71/q36/p1 The Qur'an's clear Arabic warning establishes compelling proof; rejecting its signs merits punishment. — Baqillani cites 26:192–195 for revelation through the Trustworthy Spirit to Muhammad. He says sūras with disconnected letters establish inimitability; 40:4–6 call disputing God's signs disbelief and recount prior punishment; 40:7 praises believers through the angels' prayer. [4 notes: BAQILLANI]

## 39:71/q37 How are the related singular and plural forms written and paused on?  [كَلِمَةُ · readings]
- 39:71/q37/p1 Singular readers pause by their usual hāʾ or tāʾ practice, whereas plural readers stop with tāʾ. — Nashr says codices generally write these forms with tāʾ; al-Dani notes an Iraqi-copy exception in Yunus. The Ghafir spelling may indicate either number, though tāʾ copies there are read plural as far as known. [2 notes: NASHR]

## 39:71/q38 What does a report say Hell does to its people?  [إِلَىٰ جَهَنَّمَ · reports]
- 39:71/q38/p1 It lashes them with a neck that strips flesh from their bones down to the ankles. — Durr reports this from Abu Hurayra. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(أبو هريرة)]

## 39:71/q39 What wider warning does their confession recall?  [قَالُوا۟ بَلَىٰ · links]
- 39:71/q39/p1 Other passages describe the deniers' admission that they rejected revelation despite a warner. — Islahi cites 67:8–10, where they regret not listening or reasoning. Study Quran cites 67:9–10 for denial of revelation and the missed chance to avoid the Blaze. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]

## 39:71/q40 Why is the sūra called al-Zumar?  [زُمَرًا · sciences]
- 39:71/q40/p1 Its name comes from both disbelievers and believers being driven in throngs to their destinations. — Study Quran gives the title's source. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 39:71/q41 What further comparison does the gate opening invite?  [فُتِحَتْ أَبْوَٰبُهَا · links]
- 39:71/q41/p1 The opening of Hell's gates is compared with 16:29. — Study Quran gives this cross reference. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 39:71/q42 How does greeting at Paradise's gates compare with this scene?  [خَزَنَتُهَآ · reports]
- 39:71/q42/p1 Paradise's people receive greetings at three ranks: from keepers, from angels at every gate, or from God. — Al-Sulami reports Ibn Ata's ranks: the lowest are greeted by keepers, the middle by angels entering through every gate, and the highest by God; he cites 39:73, 13:23 and 36:58. [1 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء)]
