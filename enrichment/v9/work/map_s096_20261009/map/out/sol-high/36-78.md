# 36:78: 22 questions

## 36:78/q01 Who posed the bone challenge, and what happened?  [وَضَرَبَ لَنَا مَثَلًۭا قَالَ مَن يُحْىِ ٱلْعِظَٰمَ · reports]
turns on: The competing transmitted identifications and their chains; the Meccan setting and the report that Abd Allah ibn Ubayy lived in Medina are used to test one attribution.
- 36:78/q01/p1 Ubayy ibn Khalaf brought and crumbled the bone before the Prophet. — Muqatil names Ubayy. Mujahid, Qatada, Ikrima and other transmitted routes describe a withered or dry bone crushed and sometimes scattered into the wind; some place the encounter in Mecca and say the closing verses of Ya Sin were revealed. The Prophet affirms revival, Ubayy's death and resurrection, and Hell; Tabari says Ubayy was killed at Uhud, Abu Hayyan specifies the Prophet's spear pierced his neck, and Biqai describes single combat. Kashshaf reports Abu Jahl, al-Asi and al-Walid with him; Kuran Yolu finds a report of a prior public challenge most plausible. Ibn Atiyya cites Ibn Wahb from Malik, Ibn Ishaq and others for this as the sound account; Abu Hayyan calls it strongest. Tabataba'i notes al-Ayyashi from al-Sadiq and numerous routes in al-Durr al-Manthur; Ibn Kathir lists Mujahid, Ikrima, Urwa, Suddi and Qatada. The response also points to the first creation and God's knowledge, according to Burhan and Itqan. [25 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(مجاهد); ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB-FULL(قتادة,مجاهد); IBNABIHATIM(عكرمة,مجاهد); KASHSHAF-FULL(أبي بن خلف); IBNATIYYA-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(مجاهد، وعكرمة، وعروة بن الزبير، والسدي، وقتادة); BURHAN(أبي بن خلف); BIQAI-FULL(أبي بن خلف الجمحي); ITQAN(أبي بن خلف); ELMALILI; TABATABAI(أبي بن خلف,ابن عباس وعروة بن الزبير وقتادة والسدي وعكرمة); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(IK, JJ, Q, W); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(مالك)+; IBNATIYYA-FULL+; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR+]
- 36:78/q01/p2 Al-As ibn Wa'il was the challenger. — Sa'id ibn Jubayr's report in Tabari says he crumbled a dry bone; Mawardi attributes an account to Ibn Abbas, and Ibn Kathir's Ibn Abbas report through Sa'id puts the bone at al-Batha and includes the Prophet's warning of resurrection and Hell. Ibn Kathir notes that Ibn Jarir's chain through Sa'id omits Ibn Abbas. Abu Hayyan, Tabataba'i and Kuran Yolu list this attribution too. [8 notes: TAB-FULL(سعيد بن جبير); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن جرير,ابن عباس); TABATABAI(ابن عباس وعروة بن الزبير وقتادة والسدي وعكرمة); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 36:78/q01/p3 Abd Allah ibn Ubayy was the challenger. — Tabari and Ibn Atiyya attribute this to Ibn Abbas; Ibn Kathir calls the al-Awfi route munkar because Ya Sin is Meccan and Ibn Ubayy was in Medina. Ibn Atiyya adds that he never openly challenged the Prophet this way and transmitters likely confused his name with Ubayy; Kuran Yolu endorses that criticism. Tabataba'i records this among alternate Ibn Abbas reports. [4 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس); TABATABAI(ابن عباس وعروة بن الزبير وقتادة والسدي وعكرمة); IBNATIYYA-FULL-; IBNKATHIR-FULL(العوفي عن ابن عباس)-; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(İbn Atıyye)-]
- 36:78/q01/p4 Umayya ibn Khalaf was the challenger. — Ibn Atiyya attributes this to Mujahid, Qatada and Hasan and says Rummani recorded Hasan's report; Abu Hayyan likewise cites Mujahid and Qatada and allows that multiple candidates may have made the challenge. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(مجاهد وقتادة والحسن); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(مجاهد وقتادة)]
- 36:78/q01/p5 An unnamed polytheist or leading opponent brought the bone. — Jishumi says a polytheist brought an old crumbled bone and that the passage from here to the sura's end followed. Kuran Yolu reports the same gesture and the Prophet's reply that God would kill, resurrect and cast him into Hell. [3 notes: JISHUMI(قيل); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Resûl-i Ekrem)]
- 36:78/q01/p6 Abu Jahl was the interlocutor. — Kuran Yolu lists him among reported names; Kashshaf instead includes him in the group accompanying Ubayy. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 36:78/q01/p7 Al-Walid ibn al-Mughira was the interlocutor. — Kuran Yolu lists him among reported names; Kashshaf instead includes him in the group accompanying Ubayy. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 36:78/q01/p8 Similar challenges may have occurred more than once. — Abu Hayyan allows that al-Asi, Umayya and Ubayy all may have made the objection; Kuran Yolu cites Ibn Ashur for the possibility of repeated incidents. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(مجاهد وقتادة); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 36:78/q02 What does his making a mathal mean here?  [وَضَرَبَ لَنَا مَثَلًۭا · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether mathal names an implied analogy, a striking story, or the opponent's sarcastic enacted example; several commentators allow more than one sense.
- 36:78/q02/p1 He made a comparison from human inability to God's supposed inability to resurrect. — Tabari, Kashshaf, Baydawi and Ibn al-Jawzi say he measures God by creatures who cannot revive bones; Kashshaf calls it a challenge despite proof in first creation. Biqai specifies the powerless idols he worships as the model, although nothing resembles the Creator. Asad sees an elliptical allusion to refusal to conceive a transcendent God beyond sense and imagination, citing 42:11 and 112:4. Alusi gives human comparison as one possibility. [8 notes: TAB-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES]
- 36:78/q02/p2 Mathal denotes his strange, striking story or assertion. — Kashshaf and Baydawi call the denial astonishing; Abu Hayyan says the implied story is denial of God's power where humans cannot revive the dead. Bursevi cites Ibn al-Shaykh: a strange event is likened to the oddity of a known proverb. Alusi calls it an absurd fable, and Bursevi says the claim that a possible thing is impossible is irrational. Itqan records Ja'far ibn Shams al-Khilafa listing the clause among proverbially used Qur'anic expressions. [7 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI(ابن الشيخ); ALUSI-FULL; ITQAN(جعفر بن شمس الخلافة)]
- 36:78/q02/p3 He mocked resurrection by crumbling and scattering a bone as an example. — Muqatil as cited by Wahidi says the gesture ridiculed anyone promising revival; Nasafi and Ibn al-Jawzi describe the crumbling, and Islahi says the expression may mean citing an example or wise saying but its sarcastic sense is intended here. Kuran Yolu says Makkan polytheists used mockery to influence others while defending established worship. [5 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); NASAFI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ISLAHI-TADABBUR+]

## 36:78/q04 What does forgetting his creation mean?  [وَنَسِىَ خَلْقَهُۥ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether nasiya describes simple loss of awareness or culpable failure to consider evidence; Ibn Atiyya, Biqai and Alusi allow both.
- 36:78/q04/p1 He lost sight of the fact that God first created him, originally from nothing or a drop. — Muqatil says he was once nonexistent; Tabari recalls a drop becoming a complete, articulate disputant. Yahya, Baghawi, Baydawi, Qurtubi, Nasafi, Bursevi, Abu Hayyan, Ibn Kathir, Tabataba'i and others link his forgotten beginning to the possibility of recreation. Qurtubi describes the drop as lifeless; Nasafi calls its development more remarkable than restoring bone. Islahi names clay as the remembered origin. Study Quran permits the rendering 'having forgotten his own creation.' [21 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 36:78/q04/p2 He failed or refused to reflect on his own creation as evidence. — Mawardi distinguishes failing to use creation as evidence from failing to reflect on it; Ibn Atiyya allows deliberate neglect as well as forgetfulness. Abu Hayyan calls it stubborn suppression; Biqai allows heedlessness or abandonment of reflection. Alusi adds acting as if forgotten. Kuran Yolu says he neglects reflection while arrogantly judging the Creator and Prophet by reason alone. [8 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; BIQAI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 36:78/q05 How is khalqahu read?  [خَلْقَهُۥ · readings]
turns on: The reading changes the form from the noun 'creation' to an active participle referring to the Creator.
- 36:78/q05/p1 The majority read khalqahu, 'his creation' or origin. — Abu Hayyan reports the majority reading and its meaning. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور)]
- 36:78/q05/p2 Zayd ibn Ali reads khaliquhu, 'his Creator,' with an active participle. — Abu Hayyan transmits Zayd's reading. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(زيد بن علي)]

## 36:78/q06 How does 'his creation' function grammatically?  [وَنَسِىَ خَلْقَهُۥ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the whole phrase is coordinated or circumstantial; the annexation of the verbal noun explains its internal construction.
- 36:78/q06/p1 The verbal noun is annexed to its object: God's creation of him. — Nasafi supplies God as the creator and the pronoun as the object; Alusi likewise glosses God's creation of him. [2 notes: NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 36:78/q06/p2 The phrase 'he forgot his creation' is circumstantial to the subject of 'he made.' — Tabataba'i identifies a circumstantial clause; Alusi allows it as an alternative to coordination. [2 notes: TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 36:78/q06/p3 The phrase coordinates with the preceding clause. — Alusi offers coordination as the other syntactic analysis. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 36:78/q07 Why is the question introduced by 'he said'?  [قَالَ · grammar]
- 36:78/q07/p1 It answers an implied question asking what parable he posed. — Alusi supplies an implicit 'What parable did he offer?' or 'What did he say?'; Tabataba'i says this explains the example and therefore is left unjoined. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 36:78/q08 What is the force of 'Who will revive the bones'?  [مَن يُحْىِ ٱلْعِظَٰمَ وَهِىَ رَمِيمٌۭ · interpretation]
- 36:78/q08/p1 It is a skeptical denial that completely decayed bones can be raised. — Mawardi, Wahidi and Alusi see disbelief expressed as a question. Biqai says bone is the hardest material and least receptive to life, especially when crumbled; Bursevi says skin, flesh, veins and nerves are gone. Ibn al-Jawzi says the objector wrongly judges divine power by human power; Biqai calls the analogy foolish even by a speaking donkey's standard. The Study Quran lists parallel bodily resurrection objections at 13:5, 17:49, 17:98, 23:35, 23:82-83, 27:67, 37:16-17, 37:53, 50:3, 56:47-48 and 79:11. [9 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; STUDYQURAN]

## 36:78/q09 How does the reply to 'Who?' match its question?  [مَن يُحْىِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the point of the short answer is an elided verb or its distinct answering and restrictive force; both are discussed as aspects of question and answer form.
- 36:78/q09/p1 A complete answer keeps the verbal clause; a short answer can imply an omitted verb. — Burhan reports Ibn Malik's analysis of a bare name such as 'Zayd' answering 'Who read?' as a verbal clause with omitted verb, while the resurrection answer retains the verb. Itqan cites 36:79 and 5:4 for reconstructing a missing verb and mentions 43:9. [2 notes: BURHAN(ابن مالك); ITQAN]
- 36:78/q09/p2 A bare answer also singles out the named answer as the one meant. — Burhan contrasts 'Zayd' answering 'Who came?' with 'Zayd came': the former unmistakably answers the question and conveys restriction, while the latter can stand as an unrelated sentence. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 36:78/q10 Does 'the bones' mean bones themselves or their owners?  [ٱلْعِظَٰمَ · referent]
turns on: Whether an omitted possessive must be supplied or the reference to bones can remain literal.
- 36:78/q10/p1 The bones themselves are meant literally. — Qurtubi says no omitted possessive is needed; Ibn al-Arabi points to sensation in bones as a sign of life, supporting a literal reading. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(ابن العربي); QURTUBI-FULL+]
- 36:78/q10/p2 'Bones' stands for their owners through an omitted possessive. — Qurtubi records this suggestion but says the ellipsis is unnecessary. [0 notes: QURTUBI-FULL-]

## 36:78/q11 Does reviving bones imply life in bones and impurity after death?  [يُحْىِ ٱلْعِظَٰمَ · law]
turns on: Whether life and sensation inhabit bone, and whether 'revive' is literal of bone or means restoring it within a living body; the notes disagree on school attributions.
- 36:78/q11/p1 Bones contain life, so death affects them and makes dead bones impure. — Kashshaf records this inference without adopting it; Baydawi says bones have life like other members. Qurtubi attributes it to Abu Hanifa and some Shafi'i associates and cites Ibn al-Arabi's sensory perception in bone as evidence. Bursevi instead attributes this inference to Shafi'i, Malik and Ahmad; the source attributions thus differ. [6 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو حنيفة وبعض أصحاب الشافعي,ابن العربي); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 36:78/q11/p2 Bones lack life and remain pure; reviving them means restoring them fresh and moist in a living body. — Kashshaf reports this as Abu Hanifa's school view, including sinews and nerves; Nasafi adopts it for bones, hair and sinews, adding that death cannot affect what had no life. Bursevi ascribes the same to Hanafis. Qurtubi instead assigns absence of life to Shafi'i, showing conflicting school attributions. [5 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(أصحاب أبي حنيفة); QURTUBI-FULL(الشافعي); NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; NASAFI-FULL+]

## 36:78/q12 How is a human corpse's purity treated in the related legal discussion?  [يُحْىِ ٱلْعِظَٰمَ · law]
turns on: Whether death renders the whole human body impure; Malik's reported rulings also separate bodily purity from mosque funeral prayer.
- 36:78/q12/p1 A human becomes impure at death, then washing purifies him as an honor; mosque funeral prayer is disliked. — Bursevi attributes this to Abu Hanifa and explains the impurity by blood. [1 notes: BURSEVI(أبو حنيفة)]
- 36:78/q12/p2 A human does not become impure at death and mosque funeral prayer is permitted. — Bursevi attributes this to Shafi'i and Ahmad; Malik has differing reports, with purity the stronger view, though mosque prayer remains disliked in his well-known position. [1 notes: BURSEVI(الشافعي وأحمد)]

## 36:78/q13 What does ramim mean?  [رَمِيمٌۭ · meaning]
- 36:78/q13/p1 It means worn away, rotten or crumbled remains. — Muqatil, Yahya, Ibn Qutayba, Abu Ubayd, Ibn al-A'rabi, Baghawi, Ibn Atiyya, Ibn al-Jawzi, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, Tabataba'i and others give decay, with stages from old bone to fragments. Ibn Qutayba gives ramim or rumam for an old bone, like rufat and futat; Abu Ubayd compares rimma, and Ibn al-A'rabi gives the verbs rammat and arammat. Ibn al-Jawzi cites ramma of a bone wearing away. Bursevi cites ramma-yarimm and rimma. Wahidi transmits Abu Ubayda's 'fragments or dust'; Biqai specifies dust blown by wind. Alusi suggests the root can evoke eating: earth consumed the decayed material. [19 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيد,أبو عبيدة,ابن الأعرابي); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 36:78/q14 What form is ramim, and why has it no feminine ending?  [وَهِىَ رَمِيمٌۭ · grammar]
turns on: Whether ramim is a substantive, a shifted form, or an active or passive adjective, and whether its gender is explained by form, grammatical femininity, or singular pattern.
- 36:78/q14/p1 It is a noun for decayed remains, so feminine agreement is not required. — Kashshaf denies that it is an active or passive participle; Nasafi, Abu Hayyan citing Zamakhshari, and Bursevi concur. Nasafi contrasts adjectives such as ramma and rufat. Al-Mutli' compares rimma and rufat, but Alusi objects that ramim has a verb and adjectival pattern. Samīn reports Zamakhshari's rejection of participial analyses. [6 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(المطلع)-]
- 36:78/q14/p2 It is shifted from a regular active or feminine form, so its expected feminine ending drops. — Wahidi says it is transferred from an active participle and therefore does not take ordinary verb-derived agreement; Jishumi, Baghawi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Qurtubi, Burhan citing Baghawi, and Alusi citing Muhyi al-Sunna report this explanation. Baghawi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Qurtubi and Alusi compare baghiy without ta in Maryam 19:28, shifted from baghiya. Jishumi says shifted inflection and gender are permitted. [9 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI(قيل); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; BURHAN(البغوي); ALUSI-FULL(محي السنة)]
- 36:78/q14/p3 It is adjectival, either active or passive, with gender neutral form or irregular agreement. — Baydawi allows either participle and says it became a fixed noun without feminine marking. Samīn reports active participle with irregular missing feminine marking and a separate passive-participle view. Alusi favors adjective, active from intransitive ramma or passive if ramma is transitive; the latter evokes earth eating the remains. Burhan cites qarib as a comparable fa'il form used for both genders. [6 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(قيل); ALUSI-FULL; BURHAN(قيل); ALUSI-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري)-]
- 36:78/q14/p4 Masculine agreement is allowed because the femininity of bones is grammatical, not biological. — Jishumi reports this distinct explanation. [1 notes: JISHUMI(قيل)]
- 36:78/q14/p5 The plural bones follows a singular masculine pattern for adjectival agreement. — Alusi cites al-Azhari's comparison with kitab and qirab but calls it unusual. [0 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الأزهري)-]

## 36:78/q15 What is conveyed by the spelling of 'revive'?  [يُحْىِ · rhetoric]
- 36:78/q15/p1 The omitted ya in the standalone form and its retention with the object pronoun distinguish inner from outward life. — Burhan says inner life is clearer to knowledge and stronger in perception than outward life; more generally it associates omitted ya with absence from perception and submission to God in the station of excellence. [2 notes: BURHAN]

## 36:78/q16 Why is the damma not pronounced on the ya of yuhyi?  [يُحْىِ · grammar]
- 36:78/q16/p1 The damma is suppressed because it is heavy, and assimilation would produce two adjacent sukuns. — Nahhas gives both phonological reasons. [1 notes: NAHHAS]

## 36:78/q17 What does the verse teach about God's power to restore life?  [* · theology]
- 36:78/q17/p1 First creation proves that God can recreate dead people and bones. — Yahya, Tabari, Wahidi, Nasafi, Bursevi, Ibn Kathir, Tabataba'i and Kuran Yolu reason from the original making of a person from nothing or a drop; Tabari stresses a fully formed disputant and Nasafi calls that origin more remarkable. Wahidi says recalling it would prevent denial. Bursevi compares bringing inert matter to life in both acts. Kuran Yolu adds that pagan Arabs admitted God created the universe. Tabataba'i reports the Prophet arguing that creation without prior model permits restoration. Rummani calls the reply—first creation and God's knowledge of every creation—supremely persuasive; Burhan calls the exchange the strongest argument. Abu Hurayra's divine saying, reported by Yahya, calls denial of restoration a denial of God and claiming a child an insult. Asad says materialists' denial of resurrection ultimately denies creative power and existence. Kuran Yolu cites Razi that the general denial matters more than which man caused revelation; Burhan counts the challenge among literal Qur'anic signs of God's existence, oneness and transcendence, alongside 59:22, 27:60-64 and 56:58, 63, 68, 71. [17 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(أبو هريرة); TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; NASAFI-FULL; RUMMANI; BURSEVI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURHAN; TABATABAI(النبي صلى الله عليه وآله وسلم); ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî)]

## 36:78/q18 What related reports describe the restoration of bodily remains?  [* · reports]
- 36:78/q18/p1 Souls remain in their stations while bodily remains are kept in earth, reassembled after resurrection rain and reunited with souls. — Tabataba'i transmits this account from the Prophet. [1 notes: TABATABAI(النبي صلى الله عليه وآله وسلم)]
- 36:78/q18/p2 God reassembled a man who ordered his body burned and scattered, then forgave him because he feared God. — Ibn Kathir cites Ahmad's report from Hudhayfa; Uqba ibn Amr confirms hearing the Prophet tell it. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(حذيفة)]

## 36:78/q19 Why is the resurrection reply exemplary bayān?  [* · rhetoric]
- 36:78/q19/p1 It brings decisive meaning into a fitting, supremely persuasive expression. — Rummani defines bayan as distinguishing a thing in perception by speech, state, gesture or sign; meaningful speech must communicate, while intelligibility alone is insufficient. He contrasts a rustic's ugly but intelligible answer about breeding his donkey and Baqil's failed gesture of eleven fingers and tongue that let a gazelle escape. Highest verbal bayan has balanced composition, pleasing sound, easy utterance, acceptance by the soul and proportion to need. Meaning can arise through noun, attribute or construction, as in ghulam Zayd for ownership; a derived form like qatala can imply killing and victim without saying both. Names and attributes are finite but compositions limitless, so the Qur'an's challenge by composition proves its miracle; all its text has consummate bayan. He specifically calls the response to the denier, with first creation and knowledge of all creation, supremely persuasive. Burhan calls the exchange the strongest argument. [10 notes: RUMMANI; BURHAN]

## 36:78/q20 What inward lessons are drawn from the verse?  [* · inward]
- 36:78/q20/p1 Reviving dead bones figures the revival of hardened hearts into surrender and trust. — Sulami reports al-Wasiti's reading of hearts dead through hardness and turning away, restored to turning toward God. Bursevi reports al-Baqli that God revives the ignorant heart and that the human, gathering both worlds, mirrors divine reality to knowers; knowing oneself is knowing one's Lord. [2 notes: SULAMI(الواسطي); BURSEVI(البقلي)]
- 36:78/q20/p2 The parable guides monotheists and worshippers toward divine inspiration. — Sulami reports al-Wasiti: a little of God's inspiration is better than much of their own practice. [1 notes: SULAMI(الواسطي)]

## 36:78/q21 How does this verse fit the sura's argument and other resurrection objections?  [* · links]
- 36:78/q21/p1 It continues the reassurance of verse 76 and returns to the sura's themes of monotheism and the Hereafter. — Islahi says arrogant Quraysh mock resurrection and insult the Prophet, while the Prophet awakens morally living hearers rather than dead hearts. Earlier provision in 36:71 and 36:73 entails gratitude, monotheism and accountability, which opponents evade. Kuran Yolu says resistance to prophethood and resurrection protects established idol worship. Study Quran compares the common bodily resurrection objection in 13:5, 17:49, 17:98, 23:35, 23:82-83, 27:67, 37:16-17, 37:53, 50:3, 56:47-48 and 79:11. [5 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]

## 36:78/q22 What is the divine creative command behind the promised revival?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether creative command is an eternal attribute, a command present at each creation, or God's effective act itself.
- 36:78/q22/p1 Divine takwin is eternal in God's essence while each part of the world comes into being at its appointed time. — Burhan reports the Hanafi view and distinguishes the timeless attribute from an utterance of kun at creation. [1 notes: BURHAN(الحنفية)]
- 36:78/q22/p2 The command kun exists whenever a thing is created, along with the act of bringing it into existence. — Burhan attributes this to Fakhr al-Islam Shams al-A'imma. [1 notes: BURHAN(فخر الإسلام شمس الأئمة)]
- 36:78/q22/p3 God's command and will are an effective act of creating, not audible speech or a creaturely inward intention. — Tabataba'i cites Nahj al-Balagha: an eternal audible command would imply a second deity. Safwan's report from Abu al-Hasan contrasts human inward intention followed by action with God's direct bringing into existence. Tabataba'i says numerous Imams' reports treat divine will as an attribute of action. [3 notes: TABATABAI(أبو الحسن عليه السلام,نهج البلاغة)]
