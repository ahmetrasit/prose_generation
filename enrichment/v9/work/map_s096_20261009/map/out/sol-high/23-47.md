# 23:47: 19 questions

## 23:47/q01 Who spoke, and how is their speech tied to arrogance?  [فَقَالُوٓا۟ · interpretation]
- 23:47/q01/p1 Pharaoh's people or chiefs spoke among themselves; their statement expresses the arrogance just described. — Bursawī and Ālūsī say 'they said' is joined to their arrogance, with an intervening explanatory parenthesis, and describe the speech as mutual counsel. Ibn ʿĀshūr specifies that some elites spoke but all assented; Pharaoh listened, then separately claimed to know no deity besides himself (28:38). Rāzī describes their arrogance as rank or numerical and military strength. Ibn ʿĀshūr says Pharaoh was treated as divine, as a son of gods despite human appearance. [7 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; BAGHAWI-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 23:47/q02 What does the question 'shall we believe?' do?  [أَنُؤْمِنُ · rhetoric]
- 23:47/q02/p1 It is a denying question: the speakers declare that they will not believe the two humans. — Burhān distinguishes questions seeking no information from genuine questions, and divides report-like questions into denial and affirmation; it classifies this denial alongside the Thamūd-like objection. Itqān likewise calls denial the first rhetorical use. Itqān equates istifhām with istikhbār, while reporting Ibn Fāris's narrower distinction: istikhbār asks first and istifhām asks again after incomplete understanding. Ibn Mālik says genuine questioning requires doubt and a knowable answer; other authorities say divine interrogatives can imply known affirmation or denial and can be figurative beyond hamza, contra al-Ṣaffār. Bursawī says belief should not issue from the speakers. Ibn ʿĀshūr adds that they rejected the messengers' challenge to their idols. Ālūsī contrasts rejecting a human prophet with accepting stone as divine. [11 notes: BURHAN; ITQAN(ابن فارس,ابن مالك,بعض الأئمة); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 23:47/q03 What does the lām in 'believe two humans' express?  [لِبَشَرَيْنِ · grammar]
- 23:47/q03/p1 Believing someone with lām means trusting that speaker about his message, with an underlying causal sense. — Ibn ʿĀshūr contrasts āmana li-Muḥammad, trusting his report, with āmana bi-Muḥammad, believing its content; one says āmana bi-Allāh, not li-Allāh. He cites 29:26 and 44:21 for the causal force. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 23:47/q04 Who are the 'two humans'?  [لِبَشَرَيْنِ · referent]
- 23:47/q04/p1 They are Moses and Aaron. — Yaḥyā, Baghawī, Ṭabrisī, Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr, Biqāʿī, Elmalılı, and Ṭabāṭabāʾī identify the messengers directly or in explaining the objection. [8 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI]

## 23:47/q05 What does bashar mean and why is a human called that?  [بَشَرَيْنِ · meaning]
- 23:47/q05/p1 Bashar means a human, named for exposed skin. — Jishumī contrasts human exposed skin with animals' hair or wool. Ṭabrisī adds feathers and other coverings and reads the difference as divine kindness: humans can reason out how to cover themselves, unlike animals. [2 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI]

## 23:47/q06 Why are bashar dual and mithl singular here?  [بَشَرَيْنِ مِثْلِنَا · grammar]
turns on: Whether mithl is treated as an invariant verbal noun, or its singular form is explained by the scope or implied number of the likeness.
- 23:47/q06/p1 Bashar permits singular or collective use and can take a dual; mithl stays singular because it behaves like a verbal noun applicable across number and gender. — Bayḍāwī and Abū Ḥayyān cite Mary's use of bashar (19:26); Ālūsī also cites 19:17. Samīn says bashar can apply to one, two, or a group of either gender, yet may agree in number, as in 3:47. Nasafī compares mithl with ghayr. Samīn cites 3:13 and 47:38 for mithl across number; Ibn ʿĀshūr says mithl can also agree with its noun, citing 7:194. [8 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 23:47/q06/p2 Mithl is singular because the likeness concerns shared humanity, not number, or because one form stands for two. — Samīn reports these as two alternative grammatical explanations, without choosing between them. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR(قيل)]

## 23:47/q07 What effect does the dual for the messengers and singular 'like us' have?  [بَشَرَيْنِ مِثْلِنَا · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the form is explained as Arabic brevity or as emphasis on the two messengers’ fewness and alleged likeness.
- 23:47/q07/p1 The concise phrasing avoids repeating dual and plural descriptions. — Rāzī reports al-Kashshāf's explanation that Arabs prefer brevity: the wording avoids saying ‘like the two of us’ and ‘peoples like them,’ with parallels at 4:140 and 3:110. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(صاحب الكشاف)]
- 23:47/q07/p2 The dual stresses the messengers' fewness against the many chiefs, while singular 'like us' intensifies their alleged complete resemblance. — Ālūsī says the chiefs depict Moses and Aaron as separated from their large group, yet as resembling it so fully that the chiefs seem one. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 23:47/q08 What objection rests on the messengers being 'like us'?  [أَنُؤْمِنُ لِبَشَرَيْنِ مِثْلِنَا · interpretation]
- 23:47/q08/p1 The speakers deny that ordinary humans like themselves could have prophetic superiority or merit their belief. — Muqātil and Ibn ʿAbbās as reported by Wāḥidī emphasize flesh-and-blood humanity without visible superiority; Ṭabrisī says the two were created like their opponents. Yaḥyā says the speakers demanded angels. Rāzī identifies this as one of two objections. Bayḍāwī and Ālūsī call it a mistaken analogy; Ibn Kathīr says earlier peoples likewise rejected human messengers. Ibn ʿĀshūr says elites expected a messenger of another kind, even imagining hybrid forms for gods, while overlooking soul and intellect. Qurṭubī points to an earlier discussion without further explanation. [13 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس، مقاتل); TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 23:47/q09 How do the sources answer the objection from shared humanity?  [بَشَرَيْنِ مِثْلِنَا · theology]
- 23:47/q09/p1 Human beings differ greatly in knowledge and spiritual capacity, so shared humanity does not disqualify a prophet. — Bayḍāwī contrasts people whose thought yields nothing with those who perceive with little study, citing the human recipient of revelation in 18:110. Biqāʿī argues from obvious differences in knowledge and ability. Ālūsī contrasts purified prophetic souls, able to connect subtle and material worlds, receive revelation and guide others while meeting human needs, with ignorant people worse than cattle (7:179). Ibn ʿĀshūr points to differences of soul and intellect. [6 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 23:47/q10 Who are 'their people'?  [وَقَوْمُهُمَا · referent]
- 23:47/q10/p1 They are the Children of Israel, the people of Moses and Aaron. — Wāḥidī reports Ibn ʿAbbās and Muqātil; Yaḥyā, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, Bursawī and others identify Israel. Kur'an Yolu says the Israelites lived in Egypt for four centuries before Moses led them out, as second-class citizens. [16 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; MAWARDI-FULL(الحسن); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس,ابن عباس، مقاتل); BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI(الحسن); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]

## 23:47/q11 What does calling their people 'ʿābidūn to us' mean?  [عَٰبِدُونَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether ʿābidūn denotes political subjection and slave service or literal worship of Pharaoh; the Wajīz wording gives a different people as the obedient group.
- 23:47/q11/p1 It means obedient, submissive or subjected servants of Pharaoh's people, including slave labor, rather than necessarily religious worship. — Māwardī reports Ibn ʿĪsā's 'obedient,' Ibn Shajara's 'submissive,' and Yaḥyā's 'enslaved or subjected.' Yaḥyā explicitly excludes worship and adds tribute. Wāḥidī reports Ibn ʿAbbās's 'obedient'; Abū ʿUbayda cites the subjects of Persian kings in al-Ḥīra as royal ʿābids. Al-Mubarrad derives ʿabd from obedience and cites obeying Satan in 19:44. Baghawī, Rāzī and Nasafī repeat the Arab usage for a king's subject; Ibn ʿAṭiyya likens subdued people to a levelled muʿabbad path and explains al-Ḥīra's al-ʿIbād as those who entered Khosrow's obedience. Ṭabrisī compares slaves obeying masters. Ibn ʿĀshūr says Israelites labored for the Copts, citing 26:22. Kur'an Yolu describes centuries of lower status; Study Quran takes Egyptian ownership as literal and connects demanded subservience with 79:24. Ālūsī prefers this sense for both Pharaoh and his chiefs. [25 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; MAWARDI-FULL(ابن شجرة,ابن عيسى,يحيى بن سلام); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيدة,ابن عباس,المبرد); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 23:47/q11/p2 The Israelites actually worshipped Pharaoh, whose own worship was directed to idols. — Māwardī and Ṭabrisī report al-Ḥasan's formulation. Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān report the possibility based on Pharaoh's claim to divinity. Bursawī reports Kashifī's account from some commentaries and says such obedience could count as real worship. [5 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(الحسن); TABRISI(الحسن); RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI(الكاشفي); ALUSI-FULL-]
- 23:47/q11/p3 The people described as obedient and submissive are the Egyptians. — Wāḥidī's Wajīz wording identifies Egyptians as those obedient to Pharaoh's people. [1 notes: WAHIDI-WAJIZ]

## 23:47/q12 Why does Ālūsī resist the literal-worship reading?  [لَنَا عَٰبِدُونَ · interpretation]
- 23:47/q12/p1 The speech appears to be from Pharaoh's chiefs, and Pharaoh's claim to divinity would not establish Israelite worship. — Ālūsī says chiefs speaking in the king's voice is possible but strained. He adds that Pharaoh's claim does not show that Israelites worshipped him or that he claimed they did; reports say he often showed the opposite of what he concealed. He prefers service and subjection. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 23:47/q13 What role does 'their people are servants to us' play in the sentence?  [وَقَوْمُهُمَا لَنَا عَٰبِدُونَ · grammar]
turns on: The notes address separate features of the same clause: its circumstantial function and its plural agreement.
- 23:47/q13/p1 It is a circumstantial clause reinforcing the speakers' refusal. — Samīn identifies the ḥāl construction. Ālūsī says it reinforces a false analogy between religious and worldly leadership, which the speakers ground in wealth and social standing. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]
- 23:47/q13/p2 The plural predicate agrees with the collective sense of 'people'. — Iṣlāḥī explains the plural form in the claim that the messengers' people are slaves. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 23:47/q14 Why is 'to us' placed before 'servants'?  [لَنَا عَٰبِدُونَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the fronting serves the sound pattern or restricts whom the people serve.
- 23:47/q14/p1 It may be fronted to suit the verse ending. — Ālūsī reports rhyme as one possible purpose. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 23:47/q14/p2 It may restrict the service to the speakers: they serve us rather than the messengers. — Ālūsī gives restriction as another possible purpose. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 23:47/q15 How does the people's subordination support the rejection?  [وَقَوْمُهُمَا لَنَا عَٰبِدُونَ · interpretation]
- 23:47/q15/p1 The elites take their mastery over the messengers' people as proof of their own superiority and the messengers' unfitness to lead them. — Bursawī says the claim lowers the two messengers' rank beyond the objection to their humanity. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the elites attack the brothers' origins after attacking their humanity, assuming servants' kin should not rule. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says their reasoning implies Moses ought to worship them; he cites Pharaoh's threat to imprison Moses (26:29). Ālūsī says they confuse religious authority with wealth and social position and compares Quraysh's demand for revelation to a great man of the two towns (43:31), ignoring God's choice. Kur'an Yolu says accepting the religion of Israelites would dishonor the Egyptians. [12 notes: MUQATIL; RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 23:47/q16 How are Moses and Aaron's signs in the nearby verse explained?  [* · links]
turns on: The referents of 'signs' and 'clear authority,' including whether the latter is a particular miracle or its demonstrative force.
- 23:47/q16/p1 The signs are Moses's miracles, with nine signs enumerated. — Rāzī reports Ibn ʿAbbās's nine: staff, hand, locusts, lice, frogs, blood, sea's parting, drought years and reduced fruits; Rāzī prefers the miracle sense because messenger narratives use 'signs' this way. He adds that Moses's miracles belonged to Aaron because their prophethood was shared. Ibn Kathīr describes compelling signs, proofs and decisive arguments sent to Pharaoh and his council. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR-FULL; RAZI-FULL+]
- 23:47/q16/p2 The signs mean God's religion, distinct from the clear authority. — Rāzī reports al-Ḥasan's reasoning that taking both 'signs' and 'clear authority' as miracles would coordinate one thing with itself. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(الحسن)]
- 23:47/q16/p3 The clear authority is the staff singled out from the signs for its many miracles. — Rāzī lists its becoming a serpent, swallowing sorcery, splitting the sea and producing springs. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 23:47/q16/p4 The clear authority is the miracles' forceful proof of Moses's truth and stature. — Rāzī reports this second answer. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 23:47/q16/p5 The clear authority is Moses's dominance in proving the Creator and prophethood. — Rāzī reports this third answer despite the opponents' view of Moses as worthless. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 23:47/q17 What is reported about Jesus and his mother in the surrounding passage?  [* · reports]
- 23:47/q17/p1 Mary bore Jesus without a father, and the sign made of them was a lesson. — Durr reports the fatherless birth from Qatāda and the sign-as-lesson explanation from al-Rabīʿ b. Anas. [2 notes: DURR-FULL(الربيع بن أنس,قتادة)]

## 23:47/q18 How does this verse connect with the surrounding messenger accounts and sūra?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes trace different links: repetition of rejection, expansion of the preceding sūra, and transition to religious division.
- 23:47/q18/p1 It repeats the pattern of earlier rejection of human messengers and of powerful opponents to prophets. — Ibn Kathīr says the rejecters' hearts were alike. Study Quran compares the disdain to Noah's notables in 23:34 and points to repeated powerful opposition in the sūra and elsewhere, citing 7:59–62 and 7:136. [3 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 23:47/q18/p2 This messenger sequence expands stories only briefly told in the preceding sūra. — Iṣlāḥī cites 22:42–45 and notes that Noah alone is named among earlier messengers, while Moses, Aaron and Jesus are named among the later ones. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 23:47/q18/p3 After brief mention of Moses and Jesus, the sūra turns to humanity's division into religious groups. — Study Quran describes this transition in the sūra. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 23:47/q19 What general lesson is drawn from the account?  [* · interpretation]
- 23:47/q19/p1 The histories show believers' success and the eventual destruction of deniers; apparent respite is a trial. — Iṣlāḥī calls this God's established practice and says respite tightens the deniers' noose. Kur'an Yolu connects Egyptian destruction to arrogant pride and warns Meccan pagans and all readers against judging truth by wealth, status or lineage. [4 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
