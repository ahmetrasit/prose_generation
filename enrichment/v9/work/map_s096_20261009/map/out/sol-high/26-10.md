# 26:10: 19 questions

## 26:10/q01 What governs the opening إذ?  [وَإِذْ · grammar]
turns on: Whether an omitted command is supplied, and if so whether it is اذكر or اتل, or إذ is read as a temporal adjunct; the explicit recital command at 26:69 is cited for the first two analyses.
- 26:10/q01/p1 An understood command to remember the event governs it. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Nasafī, al-Burūsawī and Ibn ʿĀshūr supply اذكر; Ibn ʿĀshūr points to 26:69 and says the recollection consoles the Messenger. Al-Qurṭubī compares 46:21, 38:45 and 19:16. Abū Ḥayyān says remembering here amounts to recounting; al-Samīn and al-Bayḍāwī allow this analysis. Al-Ālūsī favors remembrance addressed to the Prophet's people on the evidence of 26:69, rejecting the need for a private address inferred from stories beginning كذبت. [12 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(وقيل); SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(بعض,شيخ الإسلام); IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 26:10/q01/p2 An understood command to recite the story governs it. — Al-Zajjāj argues from the explicit command to recite Abraham's account at 26:69; al-Wāḥidī, al-Ṭabrisī and Abū Ḥayyān transmit his argument, and al-Qurṭubī transmits al-Naḥḥās's parallel formulation. Ibn al-Jawzī spells out the implied address as reciting the story to the Prophet's people. Al-Samīn records al-Zajjāj's estimate. [8 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج); TABRISI(الزجاج); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL(النحاس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزجاج); SAMIN-DURR]
- 26:10/q01/p3 إذ functions as a temporal introduction to what follows. — Al-Bayḍāwī allows it as a temporal adjunct; al-Qurṭubī records a construal introducing what happened when God called Moses. Al-Ālūsī explicitly rejects attaching it as a time adverb to a later قال and regards an additional omitted command before اذكر as needless. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 26:10/q02 Whom does ربك address, and what is he to do with this account?  [رَبُّكَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the implied remembering is directed privately to the Prophet or is a public recounting to his people; al-Ālūsī invokes 26:69.
- 26:10/q02/p1 Muhammad is addressed and is to remember or recount Moses' call to his people. — Muqātil identifies the addressee. Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī formulate the invitation as recalling or recounting the event. Al-Burūsawī adds warning his people of Pharaoh's fate. Al-Ālūsī prefers public remembrance because 26:69 commands recitation to them. [9 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(شيخ الإسلام); TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 26:10/q02/p2 Muhammad is to remember privately that earlier messengers met rejection. — Al-Ālūsī reports this proposal as reassurance in the face of denial despite clear signs, but argues that the explicit public recital in 26:69 makes a private address unnecessary. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعض); ALUSI-FULL-]

## 26:10/q03 What does نادى mean here?  [نَادَىٰ · meaning]
turns on: Whether nidāʾ is glossed by the act of addressing Moses or by the command conveyed in that address.
- 26:10/q03/p1 God called or summoned Moses, addressing him vocatively. — Al-Wāḥidī and al-Qurṭubī explain the vocative as saying 'O Moses'; al-Jishumī takes the summons to precede the instruction. Al-Nasafī and al-Ālūsī gloss it as calling or summoning, while Abū Ḥayyān reports this gloss. Ibn ʿAṭāʾ, reported by al-Sulamī, describes a secret summons. Al-Burūsawī defines munādāt as raising the voice and associates its root with moisture and eloquent speech from a moist mouth. [8 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; SULAMI(ابن عطاء); JISHUMI; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(وقيل); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 26:10/q03/p2 God's call means his command to Moses. — Muqātil glosses the call as commanding him; al-Dāmghānī and Ibn al-Jawzī report command as a sense of nidāʾ, and Abū Ḥayyān records it as another gloss. [4 notes: MUQATIL; DAMGHANI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(بعض المفسرين); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(وقيل)]

## 26:10/q04 Where and when did the call to Moses occur?  [نَادَىٰ مُوسَىٰٓ · reports]
- 26:10/q04/p1 At the mountain, when Moses encountered the fire and tree on his return from Midian. — Al-Suddī, transmitted by Ibn Abī Ḥātim and al-Suyūṭī, places Moses on the right side of the Mount; Ibn Kathīr also places the private divine speech there. Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī and al-Baghawī associate the call with his sight of the tree and fire. Al-Burūsawī specifies his return from Midian and the burning tree; Iṣlāḥī names the sacred valley of Ṭuwā and his appointment there. Study Quran cites the fuller fire, approach, speech and commission in 20:9–48 and 28:29–35. [10 notes: IBNABIHATIM(السدي); THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; DURR-FULL(السدي); BURSEVI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]

## 26:10/q05 What was the nature of the speech Moses heard?  [نَادَىٰ · theology]
turns on: Whether the audible divine speech itself is without letters and sounds, as al-Ashʿarī says, or the heard call consists of letters and sounds, as al-Māturīdī and the Muʿtazilites say.
- 26:10/q05/p1 Moses heard God's eternal speech, audible without letters or sounds. — Al-Rāzī reports Abū al-Ḥasan al-Ashʿarī's comparison: God's speech is unlike created speech just as God is unlike created things. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(أبو الحسن الأشعري)]
- 26:10/q05/p2 The call Moses heard consisted of letters and sounds. — Al-Rāzī reports this from Abū Manṣūr al-Māturīdī and the Muʿtazilites. Al-Māturīdī argues that visibility can be extended across existents through the common cause of existence, but hearing has no parallel proof that every existent is audible. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL(أبو منصور الماتريدي,المعتزلة)]

## 26:10/q06 Did Moses receive the call directly, and how was it recognized?  [نَادَىٰ مُوسَىٰٓ · theology]
- 26:10/q06/p1 Moses heard God's revelation directly without an angel intermediary. — Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies the call as revelation through speech heard directly. Al-Rāzī reports the Muʿtazilite claim that its miraculous character lay in Moses knowing it came from God and addressed him, without need for an intermediary. Ibn Kathīr describes God speaking privately with Moses. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL(المعتزلة); IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL]

## 26:10/q07 How is أن ائت connected grammatically to نادى?  [أَنِ ٱئْتِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether أن merely explains the quoted command, forms an infinitival complement through an omitted preposition, or the command is described as the call's object.
- 26:10/q07/p1 أن is explanatory, introducing the content of the call or command. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya compares explanatory أي and says it has no syntactic position; al-Nasafī and al-Burūsawī likewise gloss it as 'that is.' Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn, al-Ālūsī and Ibn ʿĀshūr allow or adopt this reading. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the command explains the call; al-Bayḍāwī allows أن to stand in for the command. [9 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 26:10/q07/p2 أن is infinitival, with an omitted preposition before it. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya supplies bi-an; al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī allow an omitted preposition governing the command. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn allow the masdariyya analysis; al-Samīn paraphrases it as 'that you come.' [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]
- 26:10/q07/p3 The command to go is the object or message of the calling. — Al-Ṭabrisī explicitly makes the directive to approach the unjust people the object of 'called,' namely the message Moses was summoned to deliver. [1 notes: TABRISI]

## 26:10/q08 What does ائت convey lexically?  [ٱئْتِ · meaning]
- 26:10/q08/p1 It means to come, with an implication of ease. — Al-Burūsawī explains ītiyān as coming easily. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 26:10/q09 What mission was Moses given by the command to go?  [ٱئْتِ ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · interpretation]
- 26:10/q09/p1 Go to Pharaoh and his people, summon them to fear God and affirm divine oneness, and address their oppression of Israel. — Yaḥyā ibn Sallām, al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī connect the ensuing question to a summons to fear God or his punishment for unbelief. Ibn ʿAṭāʾ, reported by al-Sulamī, says Moses was sent to oneness after seeing God's uniqueness, greatness, knowledge and power; Moses feared denial. Al-Rāzī reports the Muʿtazilite account that at the outset of prophecy Moses summoned to monotheism, would later receive legal commands, and expected miracles if challenged. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the mission included rescuing Israel, comparing 20:47; Ibn Kathīr says God selected and commissioned him. Iṣlāḥī describes his appointment in Ṭuwā; Kur'an Yolu notes the difficulty of confronting Pharaoh as a man from an enslaved people. [14 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; SULAMI(ابن عطاء); THALABI; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL(المعتزلة); QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]

## 26:10/q10 Who are القوم الظالمين?  [ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · referent]
turns on: The historical referent in the next verse versus al-Qāshānī's inward reading of Pharaoh and his people.
- 26:10/q10/p1 Pharaoh's people, characterized as disbelievers or polytheists. — The next verse names Pharaoh's people, as al-Ṭabarī and al-Wāḥidī observe. Muqātil and Ibn ʿAbbās, reported by al-Wāḥidī, call them polytheists; Ibn ʿAbbās, reported by Ibn Abī Ḥātim, calls them disbelievers. Yaḥyā, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Ṭabrisī, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī and others identify the historical people with Pharaoh's nation. Al-Kashshāf says both labels share the same referent. [11 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 26:10/q10/p2 The tyrannical psychic powers serving the Pharaoh-like commanding soul. — Al-Qāshānī gives an inward reading: the soul claims lordship and takes its own perfection in place of God's, which he calls the worst injustice. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 26:10/q11 Why are these people called wrongdoers?  [ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · interpretation]
turns on: The outward crimes of Pharaoh's people versus al-Qāshānī's inward moral allegory.
- 26:10/q11/p1 They wronged themselves by disbelief and sin and wronged Israel by enslavement and torment. — Al-Thaʿlabī, the authorities reported by al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, al-Kashshāf, al-Rāzī, al-Nasafī, al-Burūsawī and al-Ālūsī give both forms of wrongdoing. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, al-Burūsawī and al-Ālūsī specify killing the Israelites' sons; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī names shirk and abuse as reasons for Moses' mission. Al-Wāḥidī's concise account singles out disbelief against themselves. Al-Ṭabrisī describes severe punishment inflicted on Israel. [12 notes: THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أهل المعاني); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 26:10/q11/p2 The commanding soul claims lordship and makes its own perfection a substitute for God's. — Al-Qāshānī calls this the gravest injustice in his inward interpretation of the oppressive powers. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 26:10/q12 How does القوم الظالمين relate to the next naming of Pharaoh's people?  [ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · grammar]
- 26:10/q12/p1 The later 'people' specifies or apposes the first, naming the same group. — Al-Ṭabarī says the second identifies the first; al-Wāḥidī notes that the next verse supplies their name. Al-Jishumī makes the second قوم an accusative appositive or explanatory accusative after ائت. Al-Kashshāf calls the two labels appositional restatements and says leading with 'wrongdoers' marks their injustice. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the sequence highlights shirk and the oppression of Israel as the reason for the mission. [5 notes: TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 26:10/q13 What does the following question about fearing God add to the command?  [ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · links]
- 26:10/q13/p1 It exposes their lack of fear and summons them to piety while warning of punishment. — Yaḥyā says Moses was to call them to fear God; al-Ṭabarī specifies God's punishment for disbelief. Al-Qurṭubī says the question implies that they did not fear him. Iṣlāḥī describes Pharaoh's unrestrained injustice and reads the warning style as conveying anger, yearning, rebuke and imminent punishment; he compares the other prophets' warnings at 26:125, 143, 162 and 178. [6 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 26:10/q14 Why is Moses' story told here?  [* · links]
turns on: The sources give compatible emphases: consolation, warning, corroboration, and the deniers' response to signs.
- 26:10/q14/p1 To console and strengthen Muhammad and the believers through an earlier messenger's struggle and eventual success. — Al-Ṭabrisī says earlier messengers' stories urge perseverance and trust in victory. Abū Ḥayyān links consolation to Quraysh's rejection, comparing their other gods with Pharaoh's self-deification and Moses' followers with Muhammad's believers. Al-Biqāʿī sees prophetic trials exemplifying the preceding close of al-Furqān. Al-Ālūsī stresses that rejection despite clear signs was not unprecedented. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī ties recurring story endings to the sūra's initial rejection at 26:8. Iṣlāḥī includes the companions; Kur'an Yolu argues that Moses' harder mission and success encouraged them. [12 notes: TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(بعض); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 26:10/q14/p2 To warn the deniers through Pharaoh's fate and God's eventual punishment. — Al-Burūsawī makes Muhammad's retelling a warning; al-Biqāʿī points to God's long merciful delay and the power to seize the ungrateful, saying nearby Jews could tell the audience the story. Al-Ālūsī reports an interpretation focused on reminding the Prophet's people of Pharaoh's punishment. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the eventual drowning at 26:63–68 is the admonition's main point, with the mission and rejection explaining it; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says Muhammad's people follow the same path. Iṣlāḥī says opponents are instructed by the episodes. [10 notes: BURSEVI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(شيخ الإسلام); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 26:10/q14/p3 To corroborate revelation with publicly known history and repeated examples. — Al-Biqāʿī says Jews nearby could confirm the account; their acknowledgment supports the Qur'an's origin from the Lord of the worlds against charges of soothsaying or poetry despite prevalent unbelief. Ibn ʿĀshūr says several punishment histories, rather than one, make accidental explanations less plausible by repetition and corroboration; they also recall earlier messengers' arguments. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī notes the repeated correspondence between story endings and the opening rejection. [6 notes: BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 26:10/q14/p4 To show that attachment to desire can make people resist even great signs until they see their fate. — Iṣlāḥī draws this lesson from the opponents' response. Ibn ʿĀshūr likewise relates Pharaoh's defiance despite Moses' supernatural signs to the sūra's reply to demands for signs. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 26:10/q15 Why is Moses placed before Abraham in this sequence?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the priority is explained by the special prophetic and communal parallel or by this sūra's response to demands for signs.
- 26:10/q15/p1 Moses and Abraham offer especially close parallels to Muhammad, and Moses' experience especially parallels his community. — Al-Biqāʿī cites their closeness to Muhammad and shared migration and journey toward the Holy Land. Moses comes first because of his unmatched scripture and signs, his people's guidance and preservation, later prophets and conquests, and Israel's proximity to the Arabs in Medina. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 26:10/q15/p2 Pharaoh's defiance despite Moses' signs directly answers demands for supernatural signs. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says this purpose overrides the usual chronological order and compares 10:2. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 26:10/q16 How does the Moses account connect with the end of al-Furqan?  [* · links]
- 26:10/q16/p1 It exemplifies God's power to seize ungrateful rejecters of messengers and consoles Muhammad through earlier trials. — Al-Biqāʿī connects the narrative with the preceding sūra's ending and the history of prophetic opposition. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 26:10/q17 How does this opening fit the larger Moses narrative and its fuller accounts elsewhere?  [* · links]
- 26:10/q17/p1 It begins the commission and leads through Pharaoh's opposition to the drowning and Israel's flight; other passages supply fuller details of the call. — Iṣlāḥī traces this section from the command to confront Pharaoh through the drowning of Pharaoh and his army. Study Quran includes the sorcerers and Israel's flight and points to the fuller fire and commission account at 20:9–48 and 28:29–35. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī refers to Sūrat Ṭā Hā for the commission. Al-Ālūsī says 26:10 does not begin God's call itself: 20:12–23 gives its earlier part, and retellings vary with context. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the lead-up explains the final sea punishment at 26:63–68. [6 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN; TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 26:10/q18 How does this Moses narrative compare with the one in al-Araf?  [* · links]
- 26:10/q18/p1 Al-Shuʿara gives a detailed, forceful sequence from the commission to the drowning within al-Aʿraf's broader Israelite history. — Sāmarrāʾī says al-Aʿrāf is concise and opens with Moses' short message to Pharaoh at 7:104–105, while al-Shuʿarāʾ begins earlier with God's commission, Moses' fear and request for Aaron, and reassurance at 26:11–15; the latter proceeds event by event through the drowning with stronger confrontation. [3 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR]

## 26:10/q19 What pattern among surahs opening with ṭā does this passage illustrate?  [* · links]
- 26:10/q19/p1 Every such surah places a detailed Moses account near its beginning. — Sāmarrāʾī contrasts this with other surahs that begin with disconnected letters. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR]
