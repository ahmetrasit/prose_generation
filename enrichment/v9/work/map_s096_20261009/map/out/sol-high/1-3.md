# 1:3: 43 questions

## 1:3/q01 Where does al-Raḥmān come from?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ · meaning]
turns on: Arabic mercy derivation versus a Hebrew loan.
- 1:3/q01/p1 It is Arabic and derived from mercy. — Al-Wāḥidī prefers Arabic patterns laḥfān, nadmān and ghaḍbān; al-Layth derives both names from mercy. Ibn ʿĀshūr says sister languages and Yemenite rakhim/rakhma cannot prove borrowing; Jarīr's line is inconclusive. Jishumī affirms the root. [7 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(الليث); JISHUMI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT+; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو العباس)-; IBNASHUR(المبرد)-; IBNASHUR-FULL(المبرد)-]
- 1:3/q01/p2 It is a Hebrew loan adapted into Arabic. — Abū al-ʿAbbās says its wide circulation contrasts with distinctively Arabic al-Raḥīm; al-Mubarrad proposes original khāʾ and cites Jarīr. Ibn ʿĀshūr rejects the claim for lack of proof. [6 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو العباس); IBNASHUR(المبرد,جرير); IBNASHUR-FULL(المبرد); WAHIDI-BASIT-; IBNASHUR-; IBNASHUR-FULL-]

## 1:3/q02 Was al-Raḥmān known as a divine name before Islam?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ · sciences]
turns on: The evidential force of pre-Islamic verse and Meccan objections.
- 1:3/q02/p1 It was unfamiliar or contested among most pre-Islamic Arabs. — Abū al-ʿAbbās says Quraysh knew only the Yamāma claimant and a few poets learned it from scriptural people. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 25:60, 13:30, Meccan al-Mulk's repetition and the later Musaylima poem, yet allows disuse or denial rather than complete ignorance. [11 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو العباس); JISHUMI; IBNASHUR(بعض شعراء بني حنيفة,جمهور الأئمة); IBNASHUR-FULL(جمهور الأئمة); WAHIDI-BASIT-]
- 1:3/q02/p2 Pre-Islamic Arabic poetry shows the name was known. — Al-Wāḥidī records the objection from poetry; he answers that 25:60 may question the attribute or mock it. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the question need not prove foreign origin. [3 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNASHUR; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو العباس)-; IBNASHUR(جمهور الأئمة)-]

## 1:3/q03 What does al-Raḥmān signify?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ · meaning]
turns on: Whether it marks intrinsic abundant mercy, creation, or a special scope.
- 1:3/q03/p1 It names abundant intrinsic mercy reaching creation. — Muqātil calls it merciful; Abū ʿUbayda and Majāz call it possession of mercy. Jishumī and Ibn ʿĀshūr link its form to intensity; Ibn ʿĀshūr says its effects are universal and steady. Elmalılı associates it with essential willing of good and creation from nonexistence. [26 notes: MUQATIL; MAJAZ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيدة); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; IBNASHUR(أبو عبيدة وابن جني والزجاج والزمخشري,الزجاج,صاحب الكشاف); ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; STUDYQURAN]
- 1:3/q03/p2 It designates God's creation or foundational gifts that creatures cannot give. — Jishumī reports both creation against provision and foundational blessings against general mercy; Rāzī points to formation from semen (40:64). Study Quran invokes the Breath and immutable archetypes; Elmalılı describes creating rational free agents. [12 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL; STUDYQURAN; ELMALILI]
- 1:3/q03/p3 It denotes a special name and action. — Sulamī transmits a view making both name and action specific, while Ibn ʿĀshūr reports the name's exclusive or proper-name status. [3 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم); IBNASHUR(جمهور الأئمة); IBNASHUR-FULL(جمهور الأئمة)]

## 1:3/q04 How is al-Raḥmān formed grammatically?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether faʿlān here is an enduring adjective despite a transitive source verb.
- 1:3/q04/p1 It is an enduring quality adjective whose pattern can convey fullness. — Al-Zamakhsharī and many investigators support this. Ibn ʿĀshūr explains that a transitive verb may shift to habitual intransitive sense even if raḥuma is unattested. Al-Zajjāj's ghaḍbān illustrates fullness; Ibn ʿĀshūr qualifies the larger-form rule with diminutive yāʾ and ḥadhir/ḥādhir exceptions. [16 notes: IBNASHUR(الزجاج,صاحب الكشاف,صاحب الكشاف وكثير من المحققين); IBNASHUR-FULL(صاحب الكشاف); SAMARRAI-ASRAR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 1:3/q04/p2 It cannot be an enduring quality adjective because its verb is transitive. — Ibn Mālik compares it to rabb and malik; Ibn ʿĀshūr answers by semantic conversion. [2 notes: IBNASHUR(ابن مالك); IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن مالك); IBNASHUR-; IBNASHUR-FULL(صاحب الكشاف)-]

## 1:3/q05 Does indefinite raḥmān take full case inflection?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ · grammar]
turns on: The diptote criteria for faʿlān and whether its feminine is faʿlānah.
- 1:3/q05/p1 It is diptote. — Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Raḍī and Ibn Mālik choose this treatment; Ibn ʿĀshūr locates the dispute in the Kāfiya's criteria. [3 notes: IBNASHUR(الزمخشري والرضى وابن مالك); IBNASHUR-FULL(الزمخشري)+]
- 1:3/q05/p2 It fully declines. — Banū Asad give every faʿlān adjective a faʿlānah feminine and therefore decline it. [2 notes: IBNASHUR(بنو أسد); IBNASHUR-FULL(الزمخشري)]

## 1:3/q06 Who may bear the name al-Raḥmān?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ · referent]
- 1:3/q06/p1 Only God properly bears al-Raḥmān. — Al-Ḥasan, Jishumī, Rāzī, Biqāʿī, Quṭb and Ibn ʿĀshūr contrast it with raḥīm, applied to the Messenger in 9:128. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls Musaylima's post-Islamic title an act of disbelief, not normal earlier usage. [14 notes: IBNABIHATIM(الحسن); JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR(بعض شعراء بني حنيفة,جمهور الأئمة); IBNASHUR-FULL(الجمهور,جمهور الأئمة); STUDYQURAN]

## 1:3/q07 Is imāla transmitted in al-Raḥmān?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ · readings]
- 1:3/q07/p1 Qutayba from al-Kisāʾī transmits imāla in al-Raḥmān. — Ibn al-Jazarī adds this unlisted transmission alongside imāla in al-ʿālamīn; the same note also records lengthening elsewhere by Kurram from Nāfiʿ and al-Ahwāzī from Warsh. [1 notes: NASHR(قتيبة عن الكسائي، كردم عن نافع، ورش)]

## 1:3/q08 What does al-Raḥīm signify?  [ٱلرَّحِيمِ · meaning]
turns on: Whether it stresses enduring quality, enacted mercy, or recipients.
- 1:3/q08/p1 It denotes compassionate mercy manifest in benefaction, often reaching particular recipients. — Muqātil calls it compassionate through mercy. Majāz and Abū ʿUbayda call it merciful or possessing mercy. Elmalılı makes it the active manifestation of mercy; Ibn ʿĀshūr stresses numerous objects and notes 9:128 applies it to the Prophet. Ṭabāṭabāʾī gives it lasting believer-directed mercy. [16 notes: MUQATIL; MAJAZ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيدة); ELMALILI(eslâf-ı müfessirîn); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]
- 1:3/q08/p2 It marks a general name and action. — Sulamī reports this paired with a specific raḥmān. [1 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم)]

## 1:3/q09 What grammatical form is al-Raḥīm?  [ٱلرَّحِيمِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether faʿīl is intensive and transitive, or an enduring adjective.
- 1:3/q09/p1 It is an intensive form retaining the verb's transitive force. — Sībawayh says so; Ibn ʿĀshūr explicitly prefers it, while agreeing both analyses express intensity. [4 notes: IBNASHUR(سيبويه); IBNASHUR-FULL(سيبويه)+; IBNASHUR+]
- 1:3/q09/p2 It is an enduring quality adjective. — Al-Zamakhsharī and the majority reject faʿīl as an intensive pattern and compare marīḍ and saqīm; Ibn ʿĀshūr prefers Sībawayh. [2 notes: IBNASHUR(صاحب الكشاف والجمهور); IBNASHUR-FULL(سيبويه); IBNASHUR-]

## 1:3/q10 Where should recitation pause around al-Raḥīm?  [ٱلرَّحِيمِ · readings]
turns on: Whether to stop at this verse end or join onward, and how to begin attached phrases.
- 1:3/q10/p1 Joining onward is the majority practice. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya says most grammatically join raḥīm to al-ḥamdu and join its initial alif; the vowel may resolve adjacent consonants. Ibn al-Jazarī dislikes beginning with syntactically dependent rabb al-ʿālamīn and comparable phrases except at verse ends. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(جمهور الناس); NASHR; ABUHAYYAN]
- 1:3/q10/p2 A pause after al-Raḥīm is transmitted. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya cites Umm Salama's report and some Kūfans cutting the initial alif of al-ḥamdu. Ibn al-Jazarī calls stopping at such meaningful verse endings good by Sunna. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(أم سلمة); NASHR]

## 1:3/q11 How do the two mercy names differ in scope?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether their scopes coincide or divide by recipients, time, gifts, or essence and effect.
- 1:3/q11/p1 They are equivalent or near-synonymous expressions of mercy. — Majāz cites nadmān and nadīm, with poetry by Burj, al-Nuʿmān, Barīq and Ḥassān. Al-Wāḥidī and Jishumī report synonymy; Quṭrub and al-Zajjāj make the pair verbal emphasis. Ibn ʿĀshūr objects that context calls for added meaning. [10 notes: MUQATIL; MAJAZ; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BURHAN(بعضهم); IBNASHUR(قطرب); IBNASHUR-; IBNASHUR-FULL(قطرب)-]
- 1:3/q11/p2 Al-Raḥmān reaches all creation, while al-Raḥīm especially reaches believers or the saved. — Al-Ḍaḥḥāk and al-Ṣādiq give the all-creation/believer contrast; Jishumī makes the latter forgiveness. Al-Ḥarālī's nearer reading distinguishes general benefits from felicity-leading mercy. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says creative effusion is universal, while guidance and enduring salvation concern believers. Study Quran pictures sun and individual ray and cites 33:43. [11 notes: IBNABIHATIM(الضحاك); JISHUMI; BIQAI-FULL(الحرالي); TABATABAI(الصادق); STUDYQURAN]
- 1:3/q11/p3 Al-Raḥmān denotes essential mercy; al-Raḥīm denotes its enacted, recipient-directed mercy. — Elmalılı makes the former a stable essential will and the latter active beneficence, noting some reverse them. Ibn ʿĀshūr puts intrinsic character before its many objects. Study Quran calls these universal essential and particular attributive mercy. [8 notes: ELMALILI(Bunu aks edenler); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 1:3/q11/p4 Their difference is uncertain; together they embrace every mode of mercy. — Quṭb declines to rank their scopes, while Muqātil and Khālid b. Ṣafwān say one is more tender than the other without fixing which. Jishumī says both intensify divine blessing. [5 notes: QUTB-ZILAL; MUQATIL; IBNABIHATIM(خالد بن صفوان); JISHUMI]
- 1:3/q11/p5 Al-Raḥmān concerns worldly mercy and al-Raḥīm the afterlife. — Al-Wāḥidī reports this with the greater intensity of the first. Elmalılı reports earlier exegetes' afterlife interpretation, while Rāzī sees worldly mercies followed by unbounded reward and possible pardon after death. [4 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; ELMALILI(eslâf-ı müfessirîn); RAZI-FULL]
- 1:3/q11/p6 Al-Raḥmān creates or gives unproducible foundational gifts; al-Raḥīm provides or rewards acquired deeds. — Jishumī contrasts creation with provision and uncreatable first blessings with merciful possession. Rāzī contrasts divine creation with gifts servants can produce, then formation from semen with perfected Garden for imperfect obedience. Study Quran contrasts bringing existence and sustaining nourishment. [5 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 1:3/q11/p7 Al-Raḥmān gives blessings; al-Raḥīm protects from sin or harm. — Sulamī reports blessing versus protection from sin. Al-Ḥarālī reports created benefits versus harm prevention as one of two readings. [2 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم); BIQAI-FULL(الحرالي)]
- 1:3/q11/p8 Al-Raḥmān denotes enacted mercy and al-Raḥīm essential mercy. — Elmalılı records interpreters who reverse his allocation without naming them. [1 notes: ELMALILI(Bunu aks edenler)]
- 1:3/q11/p9 Al-Raḥmān grants general existence regardless of request; al-Raḥīm responds to free striving. — Elmalılı distinguishes existence and life provision for every rational creature from wanted gifts under one divine will, related to choice and final recompense. [13 notes: ELMALILI(eslâf-ı müfessirîn)]

## 1:3/q12 Why are both mercy names joined?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether pairing reinforces one meaning or adds a distinct aspect and reassurance.
- 1:3/q12/p1 The pairing intensifies and completes the description of mercy. — Majāz's Arabic synonym pairs and poetry defend doubled expression; Jishumī says it intensifies mercy and traces every blessing to God. Quṭb and Study Quran say together they cover mercy's range. [9 notes: MAJAZ; JISHUMI; QUTB-ZILAL; STUDYQURAN; IBNASHUR(قطرب); IBNASHUR-; IBNASHUR-FULL(قطرب)-]
- 1:3/q12/p2 The two forms unite continually renewed and permanently established mercy. — Sāmarrāʾī says faʿlān suggests fresh fullness and faʿīl stable quality; either alone could suggest impermanence or mere stasis. Al-Tafsīr al-Qayyim compares anger and regret; examples include ghaḍbān, ʿaṭshān, ṭawīl, ḍaʿīf and 20:86. [5 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 1:3/q12/p3 The pairing identifies the formerly less familiar name with the God Arabs knew. — Jishumī says Allah was familiar but al-Raḥmān less so. Another report contrasts Torah's al-Raḥmān, Gospel's al-Raḥīm and Qur'an's Allah; Abū al-ʿAbbās contrasts shared Hebrew-Arabic al-Raḥmān with Arabic al-Raḥīm. [4 notes: JISHUMI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو العباس)]
- 1:3/q12/p4 The second adds a broad recipient-directed meaning to the exclusive intrinsic first. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says 9:128 calls the Messenger raḥīm; repetition without new meaning would be rhetorically weak. [10 notes: IBNASHUR(قطرب); IBNASHUR-FULL(الجمهور,قطرب)]
- 1:3/q12/p5 The second name makes small needs approachable after the exclusive majestic first. — Rāzī says humans might hesitate to ask the great Lord for a sandal strap or salt; a report has God invite Moses to request salt and fodder. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 1:3/q13 Why does al-Raḥmān precede al-Raḥīm?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether form strength, divine exclusivity, intrinsic priority, or immediacy determines order.
- 1:3/q13/p1 Its stronger, more exclusive, name-like form comes first. — Jishumī says stronger intensity and exclusivity make it more definite; Biqāʿī orders existence, general mercy, then worship-specific mercy. Ibn ʿĀshūr records most scholars' stronger faʿlān view, while noting the expected rhetorical increase from weaker to stronger. Al-Suyūṭī gives raʾūf then raḥīm as another instance of a less emphatic term following a stronger one. [7 notes: JISHUMI; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR(أبو عبيدة وابن جني والزجاج والزمخشري); IBNASHUR-FULL(الجمهور); ITQAN]
- 1:3/q13/p2 An intrinsic attribute comes before its outward effects among recipients. — Ibn ʿĀshūr makes essential character prior to the many objects of its mercy; 9:128 shows that the latter is also applied to the Messenger. [3 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 1:3/q13/p3 Immediate renewed mercy is mentioned first because people desire what comes soon. — Sāmarrāʾī cites 75:20 and the contrast between faʿlān's renewed fullness and faʿīl's stability. [2 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT]

## 1:3/q14 How are the two names read and parsed in this verse?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · readings]
turns on: Case endings and whether the names continue the preceding construction or detach as praise.
- 1:3/q14/p1 Both are accusative as detached praise. — Abū al-ʿĀliya, Ibn al-Sumayfaʿ and ʿĪsā b. ʿUmar are named by Ibn al-Jawzī and Abū Ḥayyān; al-Ālūsī also names Zayd. Al-Naḥḥās allows an implied praising verb. [8 notes: NAHHAS; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو العاليه وابن السميفع وعيسى بن عمر); ABUHAYYAN(أبو العالية وابن السميفع وعيسى بن عمرو); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ITQAN]
- 1:3/q14/p2 Both are nominative as detached praise or an implied subject. — Abū Razīn al-ʿUqaylī, al-Rabīʿ b. Khuthaym and Abū ʿImrān al-Jawnī are named by Ibn al-Jawzī, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī. [8 notes: NAHHAS; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو رزين العقيلي والربيع بن خيثم وأبو عمران الجوني); ABUHAYYAN(أبو رزين العقيلي والربيع بن خيثم وأبو عمران الجوني); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ITQAN]
- 1:3/q14/p3 Both are genitive, attached as adjectives or apposition. — The majority connect the names to preceding divine epithets; Abū Ḥayyān allows adjectival, appositional and explanatory appositional parses. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says joining the alif of al-ḥamdu may resolve adjacent consonants. [7 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(جمهور الناس); ABUHAYYAN(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ITQAN]
- 1:3/q14/p4 Mixed case endings are grammatically possible. — Al-Naḥḥās allows one nominative and one accusative, or first genitive then second nominative or accusative. [1 notes: NAHHAS]
- 1:3/q14/p5 An opened final mīm joined to an alif was heard, but not established as a transmitted reading. — Al-Kisāʾī cites an Arab's pronunciation; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says he knows no reading transmitter for it. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الكسائي)]

## 1:3/q15 What does mercy mean when attributed to God?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · theology]
turns on: Whether it names essential willing, acts of benefit, both, or humanlike feeling.
- 1:3/q15/p1 It means God's willing good and bringing benefit, without human emotional change. — Al-Wāḥidī calls willing good an essential attribute. Elmalılı prefers both essential will and beneficent acts and distinguishes Turkish rahmet from merhamet. Ibn ʿĀshūr distinguishes affect from effective outcome, invokes 42:11 and 7:156, and says common use makes the divine sense clear, not ambiguous under 3:7; al-Ghazālī says mercy unable to produce its effect is incomplete. [25 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; ELMALILI(müfessirîn); IBNASHUR(أبو حامد الغزالي); IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو حامد الغزالي); ELMALILI+]
- 1:3/q15/p2 It means the effect of mercy: rescue, gentleness and benefaction. — Exegetes reported by Elmalılı take the lexical transfer as an action attribute. Ibn ʿĀshūr says scripture directs ordinary language toward gentle action and aid rather than creaturely passion. Rāzī joins rescue from affliction and delivery of benefits. [7 notes: ELMALILI(müfessirîn); IBNASHUR; RAZI]
- 1:3/q15/p3 The names are truly divine but their ultimate reality exceeds human knowledge. — Sulamī invokes 20:110 against claims to comprehend God's essence; Elmalılı says divine predication is literally true, though not identical to human feeling. Ibn ʿĀshūr says scriptural language expresses exalted meaning as ordinary language permits. [6 notes: SULAMI; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR]
- 1:3/q15/p4 Divine mercy is the same tender emotion as human compassion. — Elmalılı and Ibn ʿĀshūr describe this human sense in order to rule it out for God, citing divine transcendence. [8 notes: ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ELMALILI-; IBNASHUR-]
- 1:3/q15/p5 It means prior willing of good, an essential attribute. — Al-Wāḥidī and some exegetes in Elmalılı's account distinguish divine will from human tenderness; Elmalılı says willing is choice, not emotional affect. [6 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; ELMALILI(müfessirîn)]

## 1:3/q16 What instances display the mercy named here?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · interpretation]
turns on: Which observed benefits, rescue, and apparent harms demonstrate its reach.
- 1:3/q16/p1 Mercy also guides ordinary need, communal welfare, and free striving. — Ibn ʿĀshūr points to accessible earth, purification, good character, and zakāt benefiting society. Elmalılı describes life's provision, choice, a just balance and a hoped-for eternal good; 20:5 depicts encompassing sovereignty and 10:26 promises more to those who do good. [21 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 1:3/q16/p2 Creation, health, provision, insight, rescue, and even beneficial trials display mercy. — Rāzī cites medical discoveries, Galen's optic-nerve dream and vein cure, a raven feeding a bound man, a scorpion saving a youth, and gnats feeding a nestling. He argues schooling or amputation may hurt yet help, as al-Khiḍr's boat, boy and wall hide wisdom (18:79); worship disciplines the soul and Hell directs the wicked (17:7; 51:50). He says good generally exceeds evil and every atom testifies to mercy. [18 notes: RAZI(جالينوس); RAZI-FULL(إبراهيم بن أدهم,ذو النون)]
- 1:3/q16/p3 Mercy remains greater than even a loving mother or the Prophet's compassion. — Rāzī recounts the dying youth reconciled with his mother, asks how God could burn a long-believing servant, and contrasts the Prophet's finite compassion at the broken tooth, debt funeral and slander with God's limitless mercy. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL(Prophet)]

## 1:3/q17 How can divine mercy coexist with evil, punishment, and human choice?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · theology]
turns on: Whether the names fit created disbelief and Hell, and how God and humans share agency.
- 1:3/q17/p1 Trials and punishment may serve a wise mercy judged by their ultimate effect. — Rāzī distinguishes appearance from reality: slight harm can enable greater good, religious duties purify, and al-Khiḍr's acts seemed objectionable to Moses. Elmalılı says harm follows nonexistence, divided wills and false partnership; mercy orders a society otherwise ruined and keeps recompense just. [21 notes: RAZI-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 1:3/q17/p2 God creates human capacities while people choose their striving under His continuing rule. — Elmalılı rejects both absolute determinism and autonomous creation. His examples are sudden paralysis, former Pharaohs' helplessness, and the limited, borrowed character of human authority; necessity witnesses divine power and choice divine will. [23 notes: ELMALILI(eslâf-ı müfessirîn); ELMALILI+]
- 1:3/q17/p3 The Qadariyya question how mercy fits eternal Hell, created unbelief, and blocked faith. — Rāzī reports their objections without resolving them in that note. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(القدرية)]
- 1:3/q17/p4 The Jabriyya say God must create faith, the greatest mercy. — Rāzī reports their argument that if humans created faith, they would deserve these divine mercy names. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(الجبرية)]
- 1:3/q17/p5 Humans have absolute future control after God delegates creation's course. — Elmalılı raises this idea only to reject it as confusing temporary agency with independent ownership and as the root of injustice. [0 notes: ELMALILI-]

## 1:3/q18 What inward distinctions are drawn between the two names?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · inward]
turns on: How the two names address saints, creatures, disclosure, governance, or modes of nurture.
- 1:3/q18/p1 Al-Raḥmān nurtures through intermediaries, sometimes with painful medicine; al-Raḥīm nurtures directly through pure divine words. — Al-Ālūsī interprets the names as unpacking general lordship: the latter pure favor belongs to complete felicity. [5 notes: ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 1:3/q18/p2 Al-Raḥmān addresses saints' secrets and prophets' spirits; al-Raḥīm sustains creatures in this life. — Sulamī includes good and wicked among the creatures sustained. [1 notes: SULAMI]
- 1:3/q18/p3 Al-Raḥmān gives divine disclosure; al-Raḥīm governs. — Sulamī transmits this unnamed inward distinction. [1 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم)]
- 1:3/q18/p4 Al-Raḥmān reveals lights; al-Raḥīm preserves entrusted secrets. — Sulamī transmits this distinct account. [1 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم)]
- 1:3/q18/p5 Al-Raḥmān provides outer and inner sustenance. — Jaʿfar lists food, drink, health, intellect, knowledge, understanding, senses and aspiration. [1 notes: SULAMI(جعفر)]

## 1:3/q19 Why do the mercy names follow 'Lord of the worlds'?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · links]
turns on: How lordship, mercy, fear, praise, and worship cohere in the sequence.
- 1:3/q19/p1 Mercy softens the awe of lordship and gives hope that supports obedience. — Al-Qurṭubī cites 15:49-50 and 40:3 and a Muslim report that full knowledge of punishment or mercy would overwhelm hope or despair. Abū Ḥayyān says God's control makes mercy apt, and rejects alternatives 'constant one' or 'companion' for rabb; Sāmarrāʾī says a real nurturer should be merciful, citing 9:128. [9 notes: QURTUBI(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; RAZI-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 1:3/q19/p2 Mercy explains and completes lordly nurture, giving reason to continue praise. — Biqāʿī says lordship lacks full wellbeing without mercy and the names detail praise twice over. Ibn ʿĀshūr says weak creatures need gentle completion; benefits and duties can be arduous, so the name assures easing and public good. Al-Ālūsī says immense mercy follows universal nurture. [14 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 1:3/q19/p3 The order of lordship, mercy, and judgment is the best rational or rhetorical sequence. — Rāzī calls it unsurpassable. Al-Taftāzānī maps first creation to rabb, preservation to mercy, second creation to judgment, and afterlife preservation to subsequent worship. Bursevī maps creation, nurture, concealment, forgiveness and judgment. Study Quran puts mercy between sovereignty over space and mastery of time; Sāmarrāʾī spans eternity through afterlife. [9 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(مسعود التفتازاني); BURSEVI; STUDYQURAN; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT(التفسير القيم); SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 1:3/q19/p4 The words describe God's praiseworthy perfections and prepare the servant to worship. — Burhān traces successive exclusive praise, universal lordship, mercy and judgment into seeking help. Al-Ālūsī lists inherent perfection, past benefit, future kindness and feared power; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the names legislative mercy; Sāmarrāʾī ties the guidance plea to lordship, mercy and reliance. [6 notes: BURHAN; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; RAZI]
- 1:3/q19/p5 The mercy names are God's self-praise after the creation description. — Qatāda calls this verse God praising Himself; Durr al-Manthūr connects it with Lord of the worlds and the coming judgment. [2 notes: DURR(قتادة); DURR-FULL(قتادة)]

## 1:3/q20 Why do the mercy names recur after the basmala?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the basmala belongs to the sūra, or each occurrence has a distinct rhetorical task.
- 1:3/q20/p1 The basmala and this verse serve distinct functions, so recurrence is purposeful. — ʿAlī b. ʿĪsā and al-Rummānī say the first ties servitude to blessings and the second praise to blessings. Al-Ālūsī says the basmala explains beginning in God's name while this verse explains His right to praise. Al-Bayḍāwī calls it causal explanation; Abū Ḥayyān says, if the basmala is in the sūra, attributes gain reinforcement. [13 notes: JISHUMI(علي بن عيسى); TABRISI(علي بن عيسى الرماني); BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL(بعض ساداتنا); BIQAI(الغزالي); BIQAI-FULL(أبو حامد الغزالي); IBNASHUR-FULL(البيضاوي); IBNASHUR-FULL-]
- 1:3/q20/p2 The recurrence deliberately emphasizes the importance and breadth of mercy. — Jishumī lists emphasis as one explanation; al-Rāzī says Qur'anic repetition is common and mercy deserves special stress before judgment (40:3). Study Quran says the names intensify mercy, while Biqāʿī invokes al-Ghazālī's principle that no Qur'anic repetition is redundant. [9 notes: JISHUMI; ABUHAYYAN; ALUSI(الإمام الرازي); ALUSI-FULL(الإمام الرازي); IBNASHUR-FULL(فخر الدين الرازي); STUDYQURAN; BIQAI(الغزالي); BIQAI-FULL(أبو حامد الغزالي); ALUSI-; IBNASHUR-FULL-]
- 1:3/q20/p3 The apparent repetition indicates the basmala is outside the Fātiḥa. — Al-Ṭabarī says adjacent complete verses would repeat without real separation and cites Jarīr and 18:1-2 for fronted semantic order; Makkī and al-Nasafī say al-ḥamdu does not truly separate them. Ibn ʿĀshūr rejects appeals to mere emphasis and al-Bayḍāwī's praise explanation. [13 notes: TAB; JISHUMI; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN(مكي); ALUSI(بعض ساداتنا); IBNASHUR-FULL(البيضاوي,فخر الدين الرازي); ABUHAYYAN-; ALUSI-; ALUSI-FULL(بعض ساداتنا)-]
- 1:3/q20/p4 The two names were already explained under the basmala. — Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr and Kur'an Yolu refer readers to earlier commentary; Abū Ḥayyān calls them praise epithets following the unique name. [4 notes: TAB; IBNKATHIR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ABUHAYYAN]
- 1:3/q20/p5 There is no repeated basmala in recitations that omit it; the names introduce a title. — Corpus Coranicum notes rites without a basmala, so this verse emphatically introduces the mercy title. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 1:3/q20/p6 The names' first occurrence seeks aid; their second assigns praise. — Jishumī reports this distinction alongside the outside-sūra and emphasis explanations. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 1:3/q21 How does the verse's order relate to nearby praise and sovereignty?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · links]
turns on: Whether to retain the recited order or move mercy beside praise and kingship beside lordship.
- 1:3/q21/p1 The recited order builds from lordship through mercy to judgment and worship. — Abū Ḥayyān rejects Makkī's reordered analysis, arguing that lordship and mercy precede dominion and worship. Rāzī calls the order rationally optimal; Biqāʿī says mercy completes lordship; al-Ālūsī says descriptions explain prior praise and prepare prayer. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI; ALUSI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN+]
- 1:3/q21/p2 Mercy belongs semantically beside praise and kingship beside lordship. — An exegetical group cited by al-Ṭabarī and Makkī in Abū Ḥayyān place mālik next to rabb and mercy next to al-ḥamdu; al-Ṭabarī says Arabic permits such fronting and cites Jarīr and 18:1-2. [6 notes: TAB(جماعة من أهل التأويل); ABUHAYYAN(مكي); ABUHAYYAN-]

## 1:3/q22 What response does God give when this verse is recited in prayer?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · reports]
- 1:3/q22/p1 God calls the recitation an act of extolling or magnifying Him. — Abū Hurayra's prophetic and non-prophetic versions, Jābir's divine saying, and reports through Ibn Abī Ḥātim, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī, al-Rāzī, al-Biqāʿī, al-Durr, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, Abū Dāwūd and Study Quran vary in whether the basmala glorifies, whether Mālik glorifies or entrusts, and how the rest is divided. Baghawī grades his version sound; al-Biqāʿī cites Muslim and the four Sunan. The central answer is praise for al-ḥamdu, extolling for the mercy names, and a granted petition. [23 notes: MUQATIL(أبو هريرة عن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); TAB(أبو هريرة,رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNABIHATIM(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); THALABI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); BAGHAWI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); RAZI-FULL(أبو هريرة عن النبي,الله تعالى); NASAFI(أبو هريرة عن النبي); IBNKATHIR(الله تعالى); BIQAI-FULL(أبو هريرة,الحرالي); DURR(الله); DURR-FULL(الله تعالى); IBNASHUR-FULL(النبيء صلى الله عليه وسلم); TABATABAI(النبي); ABUDAWUD(الله تعالى); QUTB-ZILAL(رسول الله); STUDYQURAN(God)]

## 1:3/q23 What do these names imply about God's relation to creatures?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · interpretation]
- 1:3/q23/p1 God's bond with creatures is comprehensive mercy and care, eliciting praise, trust and affection. — Quṭb contrasts Greek and Babel portrayals of capricious gods and calls mercy a foundational worldview principle. Rāzī speaks of complete generosity before and after death; Sāmarrāʾī reads the names as total mercy in both worlds; al-Bayḍāwī says invocation seeks assistance and mercy for righteous action. [7 notes: QUTB-ZILAL; RAZI-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT(الرازي); IBNASHUR-FULL(البيضاوي)]

## 1:3/q24 What devotional uses of these words are reported?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · reports]
turns on: Hope in frequent recitation versus supplication for rain.
- 1:3/q24/p1 Their frequent recitation gives hope of divine rescue. — Rāzī compares Mary's rescue from slander through one mercy (19:21) with worshippers reciting the names 34 times daily. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 1:3/q24/p2 They were used in a drought prayer for sustaining rain. — A report from ʿĀʾisha names God Lord of the worlds, Merciful and Master of Judgment; Abū Dāwūd calls it rare with a good chain and says it supports the Medinan no-alif reading. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(عائشة)]

## 1:3/q25 What commentary do the divine names require?  [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · interpretation]
- 1:3/q25/p1 They call for explanation of meaning, majesty, divine attributes, and placement. — Ibn Abī Jamra says a commentary must explain the two names, all divine names and attributes, and why these occur here. [1 notes: ITQAN(ابن أبي جمرة)]

## 1:3/q26 Is the basmala counted as a Fātiḥa verse?  [* · sciences]
turns on: How recitation, verse division, and repetition bear on the first verse.
- 1:3/q26/p1 Yes, it is counted as verse one. — Umm Salama and a separate attribution to ʿĀʾisha count the basmala first, the mercy names third, and Mālik fourth. Ibn ʿĀshūr assigns this division to Mecca and Kufa; al-Ḥasan separately counts both debated segments. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL(أم سلمة عن النبي); DURR(رسول الله); DURR-FULL(أم سلمة); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 1:3/q26/p2 No, it is outside the Fātiḥa. — Al-Ṭabarī and al-Nasafī infer this from close repetition. Ibn ʿĀshūr gives Medina's count, which instead separates 'those You favored,' and notes the opening revelation report's omission of the formula (96:1). [7 notes: TAB; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 1:3/q27 What does the Fātiḥa's prayer dialogue teach about worship?  [* · interpretation]
- 1:3/q27/p1 The opening is God's share of praise; worship and aid mediate; petitions are the servant's granted share. — Al-Harālī says God teaches creatures praise they could not formulate and accepts it. Rāzī says the two halves join knowledge of lordship with servitude to fulfill the covenant, citing 17:7, 51:56, 76:2 and 2:40. The reports give variant distributions of basmala and Mālik. [12 notes: RAZI-FULL(الله تعالى); BIQAI-FULL(أبو هريرة,الحرالي); MUQATIL(أبو هريرة عن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); TAB(أبو هريرة,رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); STUDYQURAN(God)]

## 1:3/q28 Where and when was the Fātiḥa revealed?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Meccan majority and evidence versus the Medinan attribution and twice-revealed explanation.
- 1:3/q28/p1 It was revealed early in Mecca. — Most authorities, al-Wāḥidī, Quṭb and Study Quran say so. Abū Maysara tells of Waraqa counseling the fleeing Prophet to hear a caller, who commanded testimony and recitation; ʿAlī calls it a treasure beneath the Throne; Ibn ʿAbbās reports Quraysh's insult. Al-Wāḥidī cites Meccan 15:87 and the implausibility of years of prayer without it. [8 notes: WAHIDI-ASBAB(أبو ميسرة,ابن عباس,الأكثرون,علي بن أبي طالب); QUTB-ZILAL; STUDYQURAN; WAHIDI-ASBAB(الواحدي)+]
- 1:3/q28/p2 It was revealed in Medina. — Mujāhid alone is cited; al-Ḥusayn b. al-Faḍl calls this an isolated lapse, and Study Quran reports the majority's prayer-based objection. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(Mujāhid); WAHIDI-ASBAB(الواحدي,مجاهد)-; STUDYQURAN-]
- 1:3/q28/p3 It was revealed twice, in Mecca and Medina. — Bursevī records this as one explanation of the title 'oft repeated.' [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 1:3/q29 Why is the Fātiḥa called the Opening or Mother of the Book?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether the titles refer to placement, prayer, or a synopsis of the Qur'an.
- 1:3/q29/p1 Mother names its gathering of the Qur'an's major purposes. — Rāzī lists theology, resurrection, prophecy, decree; praise, service and requests; lordship and servitude; theology, duties and purification. Bursevī names divinity, prophecy and decree. Ibn ʿĀshūr lists praise, commands and prohibitions, promise and warning, and also theoretical and practical wisdom. Study Quran connects the title to 3:7 and the celestial archetype. [29 notes: RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI; BIQAI-FULL(الحرالي); ITQAN(البيضاوي,الزمخشري); IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN(ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib)]
- 1:3/q29/p2 Opening names its place at the start of codex, recitation, teaching, prayer, or speech. — Rāzī adds first descent as a proposal; Bursevī adds worldly aims and Paradise's gates. Ibn ʿĀshūr debates whether the title is descriptive annexation or true annexation to the rest of the Book; Study Quran includes opening the heart to faith. [7 notes: RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 1:3/q29/p3 Other titles name its praise, prayer, cure, sufficiency, thanks and supplication. — Rāzī explains Sūrat al-Ḥamd by its first praise word. Biqāʿī links many titles to watchfulness (murāqaba); Bursevī lists prayer, healing, cure, foundation, sufficient, complete, praise, supplication, thanks, invocation, and treasure beneath the Throne. Study Quran links cure to body and soul and foundation to the Qur'an. [7 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; STUDYQURAN]

## 1:3/q30 What is meant by the worlds whose Lord is named before this verse?  [* · referent]
turns on: Whether worlds include every existent, only rational beings, eras, or enumerated realms.
- 1:3/q30/p1 All creatures and existents apart from God. — Qatāda says ʿālamūn has no singular of the same form; al-Qurṭubī prefers the broadest view, citing Pharaoh's question and the heavens and earth in 26:23-24. Al-Zajjāj and al-Khalīl derive the word from a sign pointing to the Creator. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الزجاج,قتادة); QURTUBI-FULL+]
- 1:3/q30/p2 Rational creatures, especially humans and jinn. — Ibn ʿAbbās cites 25:1 for humans and jinn; al-Farrāʾ and Abū ʿUbayda include angels and devils because this plural designates rational beings; other reports include provisioned or spiritual beings. [4 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس,الربيع بن أنس عن أبي العالية,الفراء وأبو عبيدة,زيد بن أسلم)]
- 1:3/q30/p3 People of each age. — Al-Ḥusayn b. al-Faḍl cites 26:165 as evidence for an era's people. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الحسين بن الفضل)]
- 1:3/q30/p4 There are 18,000 worlds. — Wahb b. Munabbih counts this world as one. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(وهب بن منبه)]
- 1:3/q30/p5 There are 40,000 worlds. — Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī counts east-to-west as one. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(أبو سعيد الخدري)]
- 1:3/q30/p6 There are 80,000 worlds, half land and half sea. — Muqātil divides the number equally. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(مقاتل)]
- 1:3/q30/p7 Each of earth's four corners has 1,500 worlds. — Abū al-ʿĀliya, through al-Rabīʿ b. Anas, also names humans and jinn worlds. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الربيع بن أنس عن أبي العالية)]

## 1:3/q31 What is the Fātiḥa's larger structure and purpose?  [* · links]
turns on: How its praise, mercy, judgment, worship and guidance organize the sūra and its relation to the Qur'an.
- 1:3/q31/p1 It proceeds from divine praise and attributes to worship, then the servant's need for guidance. — Rāzī maps refuge, basmala, praise, lordship, mercy, judgment, worship and avoidance of error; Quṭb and Study Quran group God's nature, relation, and human states. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it a literary preface preparing hearers to cast off obstinacy; Biqāʿī and al-Ḥarālī call it the Qur'an's condensed universals and watchfulness. [24 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(أبو الفضل محمد المشدالي المغربي,الحرالي); ITQAN; QUTB-ZILAL; STUDYQURAN; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 1:3/q31/p2 Its diverse verses make purposeful, inimitable guidance rather than needless length. — Al-Ṭabarī answers critics who claim judgment and worship already contain its meaning: the Qur'an gathers earlier scriptures' admonition and praise, adds commands, warnings, stories, arguments and parables, and its unmatched short-sūra composition certifies prophethood. The Fātiḥa moves listeners to gratitude, reliance on God and fear of sin. [7 notes: TAB; TABATABAI(بعض النصارى); TAB(أهل الإلحاد الطاعنون في القرآن)-]
- 1:3/q31/p3 Its arrangement is inimitable and obligatory in the recited order. — Biqāʿī cites al-Iṣfahānī on inimitable order and says rearranging or inserting foreign matter in prayer invalidates the recitation. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 1:3/q31/p4 Al-Baqara answers its final guidance request. — Biqāʿī says the next sūra presents the Book as guidance, models pious and astray groups, and stresses resurrection and inward faith; its cow story, hardened hearts and titles Radiant and Hump fit those themes. [7 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 1:3/q32 How does the sūra encompass scripture and its themes?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: How its brief expressions imply the Qur'an's whole range and earlier revelation.
- 1:3/q32/p1 It includes the Qur'an's central theology, duties, wisdom, and outcomes. — Al-Zamakhsharī divides contents into praise, worshipful command, promise and warning; al-Bayḍāwī gives theoretical wisdom, practical rulings and destinies. Ibn ʿĀshūr says explicit and implied aims prompt fuller study; al-Ḥarālī maps seven universals or 'letters' to Fātiḥa phrases and contrasts its compactness with the dispersed Qur'an. [18 notes: BIQAI-FULL(الحرالي); ITQAN(البيضاوي,الزمخشري); IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 1:3/q32/p2 Its compression can be traced into the basmala and even its opening bāʾ and point. — Study Quran transmits a saying from ʿAlī and reads the point as the Pen's first ink on the Preserved Tablet (68:1; 96:4; 85:22). Al-Ḥarālī calls basmala to Fātiḥa as Fātiḥa to Qur'an. [3 notes: STUDYQURAN(ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib); BIQAI-FULL(الحرالي)]
- 1:3/q32/p3 The report calling it two-thirds counts modes of indication. — Al-Zarkashī and Nāṣir al-Dīn b. al-Mīlaq say inclusion and implication give two of three kinds, without direct correspondence. [1 notes: ITQAN(الزركشي وناصر الدين بن الميلق)]
- 1:3/q32/p4 The report calling it two-thirds counts kinds of rights. — Nāṣir al-Dīn b. al-Mīlaq counts God's right, servants' right on God and interpersonal rights; the sūra explicitly treats the first two. [1 notes: ITQAN(ناصر الدين بن الميلق)]

## 1:3/q33 How do divine power names relate to these mercy names?  [* · theology]
- 1:3/q33/p1 Qādir, Qadīr, Muqtadir and related names express divine power; kingship specifies it. — Rāzī cites 6:65, 75:3, 75:40, 67:1, 18:45, 54:55, 77:23, 3:26, 25:26, 57:2, 22:74 and 51:58. He distinguishes sovereign malik from owner mālik as the higher rank, though the note's cross-reference to 1:3 is loose. [7 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 1:3/q34 How does the mercy verse correspond to the seven acts of prayer?  [* · inward]
turns on: The alignment of seven recited verses with bodily movement and spiritual ascent.
- 1:3/q34/p1 It corresponds especially to rising from bowing, when mercy raises the humbled servant. — Rāzī maps seven verses to standing, bowing, rising, two prostrations, rising between them and sitting. He cites the Prophet on God's merciful look during praise on rising, then maps the acts to seven stages of human formation (23:12-14; 53:42). [3 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 1:3/q34/p2 The whole prayer joins bodily movement with ascent of the soul. — Rāzī describes Muhammad's bodily and spiritual ascents, ranks of spirits and angels, seventy veils of light, bodily purification, choice of companions, raised hands, takbīr, opening glorification, blessed name, exalted majesty and final acknowledgment of God's incomparability. These prepare the reciter to rise toward the source of mercy. [11 notes: RAZI-FULL(النبي)]

## 1:3/q35 What moral transformation does reciting the seven verses seek?  [* · inward]
turns on: How individual phrases remove vices or express the servant's spiritual path.
- 1:3/q35/p1 Each verse counters a vice and the guidance petition seeks purified perception. — Rāzī maps praise to desire, lordship to greed, judgment to anger, worship and aid to pride and vanity, guidance to caprice, favored path to unbelief and doubt, and final phrase to innovation. He also pictures the blessed as mirrors of sanctity. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 1:3/q35/p2 Four divine attributes draw the self's four faculties toward God. — Bursevī relays the Najmiyya interpretation without detailing the faculties in this note. [1 notes: BURSEVI]
- 1:3/q35/p3 Five divine names map to human angelic, devilish, predatory, animal and bodily dimensions. — Al-Ālūsī cites 13:28, 23:97, 25:26, 5:4 and 55:31 as illustrations. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 1:3/q36 Must the Fātiḥa be recited in prayer?  [* · law]
turns on: Whether the sūra's special place in prayer makes its recitation a pillar, and possible substitutes.
- 1:3/q36/p1 Its recitation is required and prayer without it is deficient or invalid. — Rāzī invokes the Prophet's and caliphs' practice, worldwide practice (4:115), 'no prayer without the Opening,' obligatory 73:20, safer course, 24:63, and its irreplaceable lordship-servitude synopsis (20:14; 15:87). He infers a pillar from the divine report naming the Fātiḥa prayer. Quṭb cites both Ṣaḥīḥs; Ibn ʿĀshūr notes a sound deficient-prayer report and says each rakʿa recalls Qur'anic aims. [16 notes: RAZI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL(رسول الله [ ص ]); IBNASHUR-FULL(النبيء صلى الله عليه وسلم); STUDYQURAN; RAZI-FULL+]
- 1:3/q36/p2 An alternative praise formula can be used in later cycles. — Study Quran reports Shiite law allowing either the Fātiḥa or another praise formula in the third and fourth rakʿas. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 1:3/q37 What merits and uses are reported for the Fātiḥa?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Its superlative, healing, repeated and devotional status.
- 1:3/q37/p1 It is the greatest incomparable sūra and seven oft-repeated. — Abū Hurayra's report says nothing like it was given in Torah, Gospel, Psalms or Qur'an (15:87). Study Quran repeats its unrivalled status; al-Suyūṭī reconciles al-Baqara's 'greatest' report by restricting it to other sūras. Biqāʿī connects the Prophet's mission to perfect noble character with ʿĀʾisha's report that his character was the Qur'an. [6 notes: WAHIDI-ASBAB(أبو هريرة); STUDYQURAN(Prophet Muhammad); ITQAN; BIQAI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,عائشة)]
- 1:3/q37/p2 Seven oft-repeated refers to seven verses, prayer repetition, intercession, or two revelations. — Bursevī adds reported reward equivalent to the Qur'an or closure of seven Hell-gates; Study Quran links the title to 15:87. [4 notes: BURSEVI; STUDYQURAN; QUTB-ZILAL]
- 1:3/q37/p3 It heals bodily or spiritual affliction and is recited at life events. — Ibn ʿĀshūr cites Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī's Bukhārī healing recitation over a sting; Study Quran explains the Cure title and mentions funerals and other events. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو سعيد الخدري); STUDYQURAN]
- 1:3/q37/p4 Its revelation was said to frighten Satan. — Study Quran records the attribution to Mujāhid and alternative attribution to the Prophet. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(Mujāhid)]
- 1:3/q37/p5 Its legal contents are said to be extraordinarily deep. — Ibn al-ʿArabī cites a teacher's thousand commands, prohibitions, rulings and reports, and Ibn ʿUmar's eight years teaching it. [1 notes: ITQAN(ابن العربي)]

## 1:3/q38 How does the Qur'an's order relate to its descent?  [* · sciences]
- 1:3/q38/p1 Recitation order can differ from revelation order. — Biqāʿī calls 2:281 the last revealed while its place differs from descent order, citing 75:17; al-Ḥarālī cites 33:50 and 33:52. He distinguishes gradual transition between rulings from downward descent and says the Qur'an descended to the lowest heaven before reaching the Prophet over his mission. [4 notes: BIQAI-FULL(الحرالي)]

## 1:3/q39 Does this sequence establish a rational duty of gratitude before revelation?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether God's praiseworthiness in creation and mercy itself proves a pre-revelation obligation.
- 1:3/q39/p1 It does establish gratitude by reason. — Al-Ālūsī reports the argument that creation, mercy and future judgment establish God's praiseworthiness, but objects that the cited proof is a revealed verse. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); ALUSI-FULL-]
- 1:3/q39/p2 It cannot prove a pre-revelation rational duty by citing revelation. — Al-Ālūsī says the premise comes from scripture, so the argument only establishes a scriptural obligation. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL+]

## 1:3/q40 What qualities justify the praise around this verse?  [* · interpretation]
- 1:3/q40/p1 God creates, owns, blesses and deserves worship; mercy is both worldly and religious. — Jishumī says al-Raḥmān gives blessings of worldly life and religion, al-Raḥīm has expansive mercy, and all praise belongs to the creator-owner. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 1:3/q41 What phonetic pairing appears in the surrounding words?  [* · rhetoric]
- 1:3/q41/p1 Adjacent sounds include mīm with nūn, with dāl and bāʾ in another quoted ending. — Al-Rummānī notes these near consonants as part of his phonetic discussion. [1 notes: RUMMANI]

## 1:3/q42 How may the preceding 'Lord of the worlds' be parsed?  [* · grammar]
turns on: Nominative independent clause versus accusative detached praise.
- 1:3/q42/p1 It is nominative in an independent 'He is Lord' clause. — Al-Qurṭubī reports this option. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]
- 1:3/q42/p2 It is accusative as praise. — Al-Qurṭubī reports this alternative. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]

## 1:3/q43 What does the Junayd story teach about reciting the preceding praise?  [* · reports]
- 1:3/q43/p1 One should complete the Qur'anic wording 'Lord of the worlds'. — Al-Qurṭubī recounts Junayd correcting a man who said only al-ḥamdu lillāh; a creature leaves no trace beside the Eternal. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]
