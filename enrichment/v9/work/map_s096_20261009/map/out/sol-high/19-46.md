# 19:46: 19 questions

## 19:46/q01 Who speaks to Abraham, and what are the gods he rejects?  [ءَالِهَتِى يَٰٓإِبْرَٰهِيمُ · referent]
- 19:46/q01/p1 Abraham's father, called Azar and also identified as Tarikh, speaks about his own idols. — Muqatil and al-Tabrisi name Azar; Ibn Atiyya identifies him as Tarikh and reports that he carved and sold idols and urged worship of them. Al-Biqai and Ibn Ashur say 'my gods' asserts his personal reverence or authority. Al-Tabatabai says the Quran calls the man his father Azar. [8 notes: MUQATIL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 19:46/q02 What does the father's question about turning from his gods mean?  [أَرَاغِبٌ عَنْ ءَالِهَتِى · interpretation]
- 19:46/q02/p1 He rebukes Abraham for deliberately rejecting and abandoning worship of his idols. — Yahya, al-Tabari, al-Tha'labi, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, al-Tabrisi, al-Qurtubi and Ibn Ashur explicitly connect turning away with refusal to worship. Al-Wahidi says raghaba 'an means deliberately leaving; al-Jishumi contrasts desire for a beneficial thing with turning away from a harmful one and notes raghba can also mean abundant giving. Ibn Atiyya says desire may be directed toward or away from a thing. Abu Hayyan calls the tone rebuking and denying. Al-Razi says a genuine inquiry would be futile after repeated warnings; the amazement is baseless beside Abraham's reasoned astonishment at idol worship. Ibn Ashur says the possessive form honors the speaker himself. [19 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 19:46/q03 How is 'arāghib anta' parsed?  [أَرَاغِبٌ أَنتَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the interrogative licenses an indefinite participial subject, or fronting a predicate better explains the emphasis; the first parse avoids separating rāghib from 'an ālihatī.
- 19:46/q03/p1 Rāghib is an interrogative licensed subject, and anta is its agent filling the predicate's place. — Al-Nahhas says the indefinite subject is licensed by the preceding interrogative. Ibn Atiyya calls this Sibawayh's better parse. Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and Abu al-Baqa and Ibn Malik as reported by al-Alusi say this preserves order and keeps rāghib next to its complement 'an ālihatī. Al-Damamini fears ambiguity with a delayed noun in analogous constructions; al-Shumni answers that the ambiguity is between two marked readings, not confusion of agent and object. [8 notes: NAHHAS; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(أبو البقاء وابن مالك,الشمني); ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان)+; IBNATIYYA-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR+; ALUSI-FULL(البدر الدماميني)-]
- 19:46/q03/p2 Rāghib is a fronted predicate, and anta is the delayed subject. — Al-Zamakhshari and al-Nasafi say fronting puts the rejection foremost and conveys astonishment; al-Biqai and al-Burhan find added denial and focus. Al-Kashf, reported by al-Alusi, replies to the separation objection that the intervening subject is related to the understood prepositional complement. Al-Damamini hesitates over the rival participial-subject pattern. Ibn Atiyya admits this as possible while preferring the other parse. [12 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(البدر الدماميني,الزمخشري,الكشف); ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; SAMIN-DURR-; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان)-]

## 19:46/q04 What force does the fronted question carry?  [أَرَاغِبٌ أَنتَ عَنْ ءَالِهَتِى · rhetoric]
- 19:46/q04/p1 It voices astonished, indignant denial that Abraham would leave the idols, placing that act foremost. — Al-Zamakhshari, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan's report, al-Biqai, al-Burhan, al-Bursevi and al-Alusi's report explain the fronting as emphasis on the abandonment. Al-Burhan says 'a-anta rāghib' would lack the same added disapproval. Al-Razi contrasts the father's baseless astonishment with Abraham's rational objection to idol worship; al-Bursevi says the father treats rejection as though irrational. [13 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري,الكشف)]

## 19:46/q05 Why does the father address him as 'Ibrahim'?  [يَٰٓإِبْرَٰهِيمُ · rhetoric]
- 19:46/q05/p1 Using his name instead of an affectionate kinship address marks harshness and rupture. — Al-Zamakhshari contrasts it with Abraham's tactful 'O father'; al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan and al-Bayḍawi note the withheld 'my son.' Al-Biqai sees a break in the relationship. Ibn Ashur counts Abraham's four gentle 'O father' appeals and compares Noah's single address to his son in a shorter setting (31:13; 11:42). Al-Tabrisi reports al-Zajjaj, al-Khalil and Sibawayh on yā abat and yā umat, with the tāʾ replacing possessive yāʾ, like yā 'amma. [7 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(الزجاج والخليل وسيبويه); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 19:46/q06 How is Ibrahim's name read here?  [يَٰٓإِبْرَٰهِيمُ · readings]
turns on: Transmission routes for the proper name and whether alif occurs here or only in al-Baqara.
- 19:46/q06/p1 The usual form has long ī: Ibrāhīm. — The reading source calls this the majority form. Ibn al-Jazari counts 33 occurrences, including 15 in al-Baqara and this vocative in Maryam. Some Ibn Dhakwan routes, including al-Nuqqash from al-Akhfash and al-Mutawwi'i from al-Suri, use the yāʾ form; another transmission limits alif to al-Baqara and has yāʾ elsewhere. [4 notes: NASHR(ابن ذكوان,المغاربة، بعض المشارقة عن ابن الأخرم عن الأخفش); QIRAAT-ER]
- 19:46/q06/p2 A canonical variant reads Ibrāhām, with long ā in the third syllable. — Hisham uses alif at all 33 occurrences. For Ibn Dhakwan, al-Ramli from al-Suri and most other Iraqi al-Akhfash routes use alif; the common Maghribi and one eastern transmission restrict it to al-Baqara, as al-Mahdawi records. The reading source attributes the variation to transmission of the name. [5 notes: NASHR(ابن ذكوان,المغاربة، بعض المشارقة عن ابن الأخرم عن الأخفش,هشام); QIRAAT-ER]

## 19:46/q07 What must Abraham stop doing?  [لَّمْ تَنتَهِ · interpretation]
turns on: The object of 'stop' is unspoken; the sources differ on whether it is Abraham's rebuke and invitation or his abandonment of idol worship.
- 19:46/q07/p1 He must stop criticizing or insulting the idols and calling his father away from them. — Yahya, al-Tabari, al-Wahidi, al-Wajiz, Ibn al-Jawzi, Ibn Kathir and al-Alusi tie the warning to Abraham's denunciation; al-Alusi also names his invitation to the message. Al-Jishumi says intihāʾ is refraining from a forbidden act, rooted in reaching a limit. Abu Hayyan says the object of 'stop' is omitted and may be speaking to the father in this manner and inviting him. [10 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; IBNKATHIR-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 19:46/q07/p2 He must stop turning away from the idols themselves. — Abu Hayyan includes abandonment of the idols as another possible omitted object. Al-Alusi reports a few who make this the command's object, but rejects it as unsound. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 19:46/q08 How does the oath and condition structure the stoning threat?  [لَئِن لَّمْ تَنتَهِ لَأَرْجُمَنَّكَ · grammar]
- 19:46/q08/p1 The first lām introduces a condition with an implied oath; the lām of 'I will stone you' marks the oath's answer. — Al-Burhan explains that oath and condition can govern one another, with the answer of whichever comes first expressed and the other's answer omitted or subsumed. Abu Hayyan says the threat answers an omitted oath. [3 notes: BURHAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 19:46/q09 What does the threat 'I will stone you' mean?  [لَأَرْجُمَنَّكَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether rajm keeps its root sense of throwing stones, is extended to verbal throwing or expulsion, or names another threat; the following order to leave is used on both sides.
- 19:46/q09/p1 He threatens verbal abuse, cursing, or slander. — Al-Farra, Ibn Qutayba, al-Tabari and many transmitters make rajm verbal. Ibn Qutayba explains abuse as verbal throwing, like qadhf; al-Zajjaj compares the charge in 24:4. Al-Razi reports Mujahid's generalization that Qur'anic rajm is verbal blame; al-Burhan and Ibn Faris say this verse is an exception to rajm's usual killing sense. Al-Tha'labi, al-Wahidi and al-Baghawi transmit al-Dahhak, Muqatil and al-Kalbi; al-Tabari transmits al-Suddi, Ibn Jurayj and al-Dahhak. Al-Zajjaj favors this reported interpretation, although he permits literal killing. Al-Zamakhshari links it to rajīm, one expelled by cursing. Al-Wahidi says throwing or stoning may euphemistically mean cursing. The Study Quran calls this most commentators' view and compares 26:116. The Durr report oddly says Abraham threatens his father, while retaining the verbal gloss. - 19:46/q09/p2 He threatens to pelt Abraham with stones, without specifying death. — Al-Hasan is reported by al-Mawardi, al-Wahidi, Ibn Atiyya, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Qurtubi and al-Alusi; some say the pelting would drive Abraham away. Al-Razi says the root's basic sense is stone throwing and prefers the literal reading: 'leave me' tells Abraham to flee. Al-Jishumi, al-Tabrisi, Ibn al-Jawzi and al-Bursevi identify stones as the root sense. Ibn al-Jawzi adds a hadith about not piling stones on a grave and lists conjecture and defense of one's people among other senses; al-Wahidi cites 11:91. Abu Muslim takes stoning's figurative extension to expulsion and distance. [26 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; MAWARDI-FULL(الحسن); WAHIDI-BASIT(الحسن); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(عكرمة); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن البصري); TABRISI(الحسن والجبائي); IBNJAWZI-NUZHA; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الحسن); RAZI-FULL(أبو مسلم); QURTUBI-FULL(الحسن); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحسن، السدي، الضحاك، ابن جريج); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس، السدي، ابن جريج، الضحاك); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الحسن); STUDYQURAN(most commentators); RAZI-FULL+]
- 19:46/q09/p3 He threatens to kill Abraham by stoning. — Yahya expressly says killing with stones. Al-Zajjaj acknowledges it as possible while preferring the reported verbal sense. Al-Ikrima, al-Hasan and others transmit killing by stones; al-Tabatabai calls stoning a degrading death reserved for an outcast. Ibn Atiyya says 'leave me' must then be a separate order, because the dead cannot depart. [15 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; ZAJJAJ(التفسير); KASHSHAF-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL(عكرمة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن البصري); TABRISI(الحسن والجبائي); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحسن، السدي، الضحاك، ابن جريج); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; TABATABAI]

- 19:46/q09/p6 He threatens to expose Abraham's affair, possibly to people who would stone him. — Al-Tha'labi and al-Qurtubi report publicizing his situation; al-Razi says the father could expose him to the people so that they stone and kill him. [3 notes: THALABI; QURTUBI-FULL; RAZI-FULL]
- 19:46/q09/p7 He threatens expulsion or driving Abraham off with stones. — Al-Hasan's reports in al-Mawardi and Ibn al-Jawzi specify pelting until Abraham goes away; al-Zamakhshari and al-Nasafi offer stone throwing to drive him away. Abu Muslim argues that rajm extends from stoning to expulsion, supported by the next command to leave. ## 19:46/q10 What does 'leave me' require?  [وَٱهْجُرْنِى · interpretation]
turns on: Whether hajar calls specifically for stopping speech or for physical separation from the father and his household.
- 19:46/q10/p1 Abraham must leave or keep away from his father, cutting their company and conversation. — Ibn Abbas and al-Kalbi gloss hajar as keeping away or leaving; Abu Zayd distinguishes moving far from a fasting use of the root. Al-Damghani, al-Itqan and Ibn Ashur connect it to withdrawal and no speech. Al-Razi prefers physical separation from father, house and country and compares the Messenger's and believers' migration. Ibn Ashur says ordering the son away casts the action as expulsion and humiliation; stoning is deferred but exclusion immediate. Al-Tabatabai recounts Azar's eventual expulsion of Abraham. Al-Bursevi includes keeping away for a long time while safe and ceasing speech. [11 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو زيد,ابن عباس); DAMGHANI; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; ITQAN; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; RAZI-FULL+]
- 19:46/q10/p2 Abraham must cease speaking to his father. — Al-Razi reports ending speech as a distinct reading. Al-Kalbi as reported by al-Tha'labi says abandon him for a long time and do not speak; al-Damghani and Ibn Ashur also include cutting conversation in hajar. [5 notes: THALABI(الكلبي); DAMGHANI; RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 19:46/q11 How does 'and leave me' join the preceding threat?  [لَأَرْجُمَنَّكَ وَٱهْجُرْنِى · grammar]
turns on: Whether coordination requires two imperative clauses or can directly connect the threat to the command.
- 19:46/q11/p1 It coordinates with an omitted imperative such as 'beware of me': beware me and leave. — Al-Zamakhshari supplies the warning implied by the threat to align two imperatives; al-Razi says 'beware me and leave lest I stone you.' Al-Nasafi and al-Alusi report the same analysis. Ibn Atiyya says a separate order is especially necessary if rajm means killing, because Abraham could not depart after death. [6 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري، author، سيبويه); ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري)]
- 19:46/q11/p2 It coordinates directly with the threat, joining a declarative clause and an imperative. — Abu Hayyan says Sibawayh permits coordination of clauses of different kinds, so no deletion is needed. Al-Alusi reports this view and says its ordering is more affecting. Ibn Ashur makes the threatened stoning deferred but the exclusion from company immediate. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(سيبويه); IBNASHUR-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري، author، سيبويه)+]

## 19:46/q12 What does 'maliyyan' mean in the order to leave?  [مَلِيًّۭا · meaning]
turns on: Temporal malā or malawān and poetic usage versus a circumstantial sense of safety or capacity; the threat's context favors safety for al-Tabari, while the unhamzated reading favors duration for al-Wahidi.
- 19:46/q12/p1 For a long time, an age, or an extended period of estrangement. — Mujahid, al-Hasan, Ibn Jubayr, Ibn Ishaq, Ibn Abbas through Maymun and many others give temporal glosses from 'a while' to 'an age.' Al-Kalbi adds no speaking; Muqatil allows the span of Azar's life. Al-Farra and al-Kisa'i list dialect forms such as malwah and malāwah. Ibn Qutayba cites tamallaytu habībaka and the dual malawān, night and day; al-Mawardi, al-Durr, al-Samin and al-Alusi cite Muhalhil's mourning verse; Abu Hayyan cites poetry about an extended youth. Al-Layth calls it a long span, Abu Ali explains an ample duration and its diminutive morphology; al-Wahidi cites 3:178 for related extension. Sibawayh's phrase 'walked with him miliyyan' also means staying or traveling long. Al-Wahidi prefers duration because a word meaning capable would have hamzah, a form no reader transmitted. Al-Tabari argues against long estrangement because the immediate threat favors leaving safely. - 19:46/q12/p2 Leave safely, soundly and unharmed, before the father's punishment reaches Abraham. — Ibn Abbas, Qatada, al-Dahhak, Ata, Atiyya, Abu Malik and others give this gloss; al-Tha'labi adds Ibn Abbas's 'without blame or reproach.' ## 19:46/q13 What is the grammatical function of 'maliyyan'?  [مَلِيًّۭا · grammar]
turns on: Whether the accusative directly measures time, describes Abraham's state, or qualifies an omitted abandonment or time noun.
- 19:46/q13/p1 It is an adverbial accusative of duration. — Al-Tabrisi, al-Nasafi and the majority reported by Abu Hayyan take it adverbially; al-Qurtubi relays al-Kisa'i's dialect forms with this parse. Al-Samin reports a temporal accusative and al-Alusi says the form is used adverbially. Al-Wahidi reports Abu Ali's explanation of an adjective used as a noun, with a lost radical in its diminutive. [7 notes: TABRISI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو علي الفارسي); QURTUBI-FULL(الكسائي); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور، الحسن، مجاهد، ابن جبير); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]
- 19:46/q13/p2 It is a circumstantial accusative describing Abraham as safe, sound, or able. — Ibn Atiyya, al-Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi connect this parse with the safety and capacity meanings; Ibn Atiyya says self-sufficient and untroubled. Al-Wahidi's reading objection challenges a capacity derivation. [8 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس,الحسن); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس، ابن عطية); SAMIN-DURR(ابن عباس,ابن عطية,الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-BASIT-]
- 19:46/q13/p3 It modifies an omitted verbal noun: a long abandonment. — Al-Samin reports a third parse as an adjective of an omitted cognate object. Ibn Ashur derives it from a disused source and says there is no known simple verb or verbal noun in use. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 19:46/q13/p4 It modifies an omitted noun for a period of time. — Ibn Ashur says al-malā often denotes a period and compares omission of the head noun to 'a ship with planks' at 54:13; al-Alusi notes Ibn Abbas's unspecified 'long' permits a source or adverbial analysis. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس)]

## 19:46/q14 How do pronouns and demonstratives take circumstantial descriptions in the grammar cited with this verse?  [أَنتَ · grammar]
turns on: The referent's prior identification and whether the accusative describes a current state or confirms a known quality.
- 19:46/q14/p1 With a demonstrative, a following noun is predicate and an accusative descriptor is a circumstance drawing attention to a known person's state. — Sibawayh illustrates with 'hādhā 'Abd Allāh munṭaliqan'; the point is the known person's departing, not discovering Abdullah's identity. Dhāka marks distance and other demonstratives behave similarly. A present referent can instead be identified, 'hādhā 'Abd Allāh fa-'rifhu.' [4 notes: SIBAWAYH]
- 19:46/q14/p2 With a pronoun, the accusative state clarifies or confirms an already known referent, especially in boasting or threat; it cannot merely add an unrelated action. — Sibawayh uses ma'rūfan and bayyinan, and Ibn Dara's line illustrates the former. A pronoun presumes an identifiable referent, so munṭaliqan or a new name is unsuitable as mere identifying information; a person behind a wall may identify himself, while an ordinary noun such as 'your brother' allows the construction. Boasting and threatening recall known generosity or courage. [4 notes: SIBAWAYH(ابن دارة)]

## 19:46/q15 How does the father's reply relate to Abraham's preceding counsel?  [* · interpretation]
- 19:46/q15/p1 He answers Abraham's gentle, reasoned invitation with stubborn disbelief, insult, and threat. — Al-Zamakhshari contrasts Abraham's proofs and tact with harsh address; al-Qushayri says Abraham had offered a gracious promise of good and retained hope. Al-Razi contrasts monotheistic proof with the father's imitation and threat. Al-Biqai and Ibn Ashur contrast refined manners with ignorance and hardened disbelief. Al-Alusi says 'he said' answers an implied question; Ibn Ashur says the explicit speech clause separates the dialogue, as at 2:30. Al-Tabatabai summarizes rejection of Abraham's religion, warning, stoning and prolonged separation. [8 notes: QUSHAYRI; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 19:46/q16 What does the exchange show about argument and coercion?  [* · interpretation]
- 19:46/q16/p1 Threatening instead of answering Abraham's proof displays the helplessness of falsehood when it abandons evidence. — Al-Jishumi draws this lesson from the father's turn to threats; al-Razi contrasts Abraham's monotheistic argument with the father's imitation, insult and threat. [2 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL]

## 19:46/q17 How does this exchange connect to the Prophet's experience?  [* · links]
- 19:46/q17/p1 Abraham's harsh treatment consoles Muhammad amid abuse from Quraysh and Abu Lahab. — Al-Zamakhshari and Abu Hayyan mention similar treatment by Quraysh; al-Razi says God recounts the contrast for consolation; al-Biqai also names the Prophet's uncle Abu Lahab. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); BIQAI-FULL]

## 19:46/q18 How did Abraham answer the father's threat?  [* · inward]
- 19:46/q18/p1 He answered with a peaceful greeting and settled insight while still hoping for his father. — Abu Bakr ibn Tahir, in al-Sulami, likens Abraham's reply to the response to ignorant people at 25:63 after ignorance, invitation to idols and threat. Al-Qushayri says Abraham retained hope after his gracious promise was met with threat. [2 notes: SULAMI(أبو بكر بن طاهر); QUSHAYRI]

## 19:46/q19 Do ornamental rhetorical devices establish the Quran's inimitability?  [* · rhetoric]
- 19:46/q19/p1 No; learned and deliberately practiced devices alone cannot prove inimitability. — Al-Baqillani says the devices of badi' are too numerous for an exhaustive catalogue and gives selected examples. He argues that once identified they can be learned and composed like poetry; Abu Tammam's heavily ornamented lāmiyya shows deliberate saturation with crafted devices. [3 notes: BAQILLANI; BAQILLANI+]
