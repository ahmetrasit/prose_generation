# 79:24: 24 questions

## 79:24/q01 What does the initial فاء convey?  [فَقَالَ · rhetoric]
- 79:24/q01/p1 Pharaoh spoke as soon as the people arrived, eager to make the proclamation. — Ibn ʿĀshūr reads the فاء as marking this immediate succession. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 79:24/q02 How does 'he said' relate to the preceding call or striving?  [فَقَالَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the statement explicates the call, coordinates with the earlier action, or requires an implied speech verb.
- 79:24/q02/p1 It restates the preceding call, with the repeated conjunction emphasizing the apposition. — Ibn ʿĀshūr compares the repeated conjunction in 6:99. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 79:24/q02/p2 It is coordinated with 'he strove,' so the proclamation was ordered throughout the kingdom. — Ibn ʿĀshūr offers this alternative, extending the speech beyond the crowd by the palace. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 79:24/q02/p3 An implied 'he says' introduces the quoted claim. — Al-Ālūsī says this second parsing requires an additional figurative use. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 79:24/q03 How was Pharaoh's claim delivered and to whom?  [فَقَالَ · reports]
turns on: Whether Pharaoh spoke to the gathered people himself or had the claim announced for him.
- 79:24/q03/p1 He addressed the assembled people himself. — Muqātil in al-Wāḥidī places him on his throne; Ibn ʿAṭiyya has him gather the kingdom; al-Ālūsī and Elmalılı describe an assembled audience and a ruler's speech. Al-Rāzī allows a personal address at the magicians' gathering, linked to 26:53. Kuran Yolu says he assembled magicians and men. Ibn ʿĀshūr notes that followers and soldiers could have carried out the gathering and call by his command, as al-Manṣūr is said to have built Baghdad. Ibn ʿĀshūr also says the object of 'called' is omitted, and explains that calling literally summons by raised voice or can mean merely raising the voice; he cites al-Ḥarīrī's thirtieth Maqāma and the accusative after vocative particles. [10 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 79:24/q03/p2 He had an announcer deliver the claim, or ordered its proclamation beyond the gathered crowd. — Al-Rāzī gives an announcer as an alternative to Pharaoh speaking personally; Ibn ʿĀshūr's coordinated construction makes it an order throughout the kingdom. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 79:24/q04 Did he call before or after gathering the people?  [فَقَالَ · links]
turns on: Whether the sequence of calling and gathering is read in the order narrated or reversed.
- 79:24/q04/p1 He proclaimed the claim after they gathered. — Ibn ʿĀshūr reads the فاء as immediate proclamation on their arrival; al-Rāzī places the claim at the magicians' gathering. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; RAZI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 79:24/q04/p2 He called first and then gathered the people. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya attributes this reading of the sequence to Ibn ʿAbbās. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس)]

## 79:24/q05 How long or elaborate was the proclamation?  [فَقَالَ أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the three-word quotation represents the whole utterance or a compressed extract of the speech in 43:51–55.
- 79:24/q05/p1 It was a terse three-word utterance, expressing the tyrant's alarm rather than a formal address. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ connects its brevity with the sūra's decisive pace. [1 notes: BINTSHATI]
- 79:24/q05/p2 It belonged to a fuller speech boasting of Egypt's power and belittling Moses. — Iṣlāḥī draws the expanded speech from 43:51–55: Pharaoh boasts of Egypt's kingdom and rivers, disparages Moses, asks why he has no gold ornaments or angelic escort, and fools his disobedient people before their drowning. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 79:24/q06 What force does 'I am your lord' have against Moses' call?  [أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ · rhetoric]
- 79:24/q06/p1 Its restrictive form counters Moses' summons. — Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies the restrictive wording as a response to Moses. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 79:24/q07 What lordship did Pharaoh claim by 'your highest lord'?  [رَبُّكُمُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ · interpretation]
turns on: What Pharaoh counted as a 'lord': supreme deity, other gods or idols, human authorities, benefactor, proximate deity, inherited divine status, or apportioned divine nature.
- 79:24/q07/p1 He claimed supreme lordship, with no lord above him. — Muqātil, al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Nasafī, Bursawī and Elmalılı give the no-higher-lord sense. Elmalılı adds that Pharaoh demanded worship, and reads his tower inquiry in 28:38 as tacit admission of a god whom he refused to rank above himself. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the wording and 28:38 and 26:29 support actual divinity rather than mere kingship. [13 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; THALABI; ELMALILI+]
- 79:24/q07/p2 He ranked himself above the gods or idols his people acknowledged. — ʿAṭāʾ says he made little idols for the people, told them to worship them, and claimed lordship of idols and worshipers; al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī and al-Qurṭubī transmit this interpretation. Al-Kāshifī calls the idols likenesses of Pharaoh; al-Nasafī notes that the people did worship idols. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī regards this as the apparent sense among polytheists. Ibn ʿĀshūr offers the idea that Pharaoh was regarded as lord of many gods, making 'highest' explanatory. Al-Burhān reconciles 7:127 by saying the gods were his property and he first worshiped them in the people's religion before demanding the top rank. Al-Thaʿlabī reports an unnamed explanation that calls the idols lords and makes Pharaoh lord of both the idols and the people. [11 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(عطاء); BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(unattributed view); QURTUBI-FULL(عطاء); BURSEVI(الكاشفي); NASAFI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BURHAN; THALABI(قيل); TABATABAI+]
- 79:24/q07/p3 He claimed supremacy over other human lords, leaders, kings, and officials. — Ibn al-Jawzī and al-Qurṭubī report a hierarchy of chiefs over lower people; al-Bayḍāwī speaks of everyone managing people's affairs, and Bursawī of kings and officials. Al-Biqāʿī portrays humans as ranked lords with Pharaoh highest. Al-Ālūsī describes a speech elevating him above administrators. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports a political reading like Pharaoh's boast of Egypt's kingdom in 43:51, but rejects it as conflicting with the apparent wording and 28:38 and 26:29. [7 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(unattributed view); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); TABATABAI(قيل); TABATABAI-]
- 79:24/q07/p4 He meant that he alone nurtured, benefited, commanded and forbade his people, while denying a Creator and resurrection. — Al-Rāzī and Bursawī portray Pharaoh as a materialist. For al-Rāzī, ربكم refers to nurturer and benefactor, since Pharaoh denied a deity and messenger. Both deny that he claimed to have created the cosmos: that would be self-evidently false, and a person insane enough to doubt a Creator could not have been sent a prophet. [2 notes: BURSEVI+; RAZI-FULL+]
- 79:24/q07/p5 He claimed to be the deity closest to people, administering their provisions, affairs and honor, unlike other gods. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī offers this possible meaning and cites 7:127. [1 notes: TABATABAI]
- 79:24/q07/p6 He claimed the status of Amun-Ra's son and successor. — Ibn ʿĀshūr offers this alternative, explaining that Egyptian priests called the king Amun-Ra's son and held the sun to be that god's manifestation. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 79:24/q07/p7 He assumed divinity was a nature distributed among beings and that its highest possessor could best obtain what he wanted. — Al-Biqāʿī identifies this as Pharaoh's premise behind ranking lords. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 79:24/q08 Is 'highest' an absolute title or a comparison?  [ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the superlative expresses an unrestricted title or a rank over other beings.
- 79:24/q08/p1 It is an absolute, unrestricted superlative, not 'higher than another.' — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ compares the form with superlatives in 92:15, 92:17 and 92:20. [1 notes: BINTSHATI+]
- 79:24/q08/p2 It ranks Pharaoh above other lords, gods, or rulers. — Elmalılı explicitly says 'highest among lords'; al-Biqāʿī ranks humans as lords; Bursawī offers rank over subordinate kings; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reads superiority over acknowledged gods. Ibn ʿĀshūr says lordship over many gods could make 'highest' an explanatory epithet. [6 notes: ELMALILI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL; THALABI(قيل); BINTSHATI-]

## 79:24/q09 Did Pharaoh claim to create the cosmos?  [رَبُّكُمُ · theology]
- 79:24/q09/p1 No; a literal creator claim would be self-evidently false and irrational. — Al-Rāzī and Bursawī say Pharaoh could not have meant that he created the heavens, earth, mountains, plants and animals; someone doubting the existence of a Creator would be insane, and God would not send a messenger to an insane person. [3 notes: BURSEVI; RAZI-FULL+]

## 79:24/q10 How did belief in other gods coexist with Pharaoh's claim?  [رَبُّكُمُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether other gods were subordinate objects of worship, an implicitly admitted deity, or excluded by Pharaoh's materialism.
- 79:24/q10/p1 Other gods or idols existed in the people's religion, but Pharaoh claimed authority or the highest rank over them. — Al-Nasafī notes their idol worship. Al-Burhān says they were Pharaoh's property and he worshiped them before demanding the highest rank, reconciling 7:127. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reads acknowledged polytheist gods; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the king was held to be lord of many gods. [5 notes: NASAFI-FULL; BURHAN; TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL; THALABI(قيل)]
- 79:24/q10/p2 Pharaoh's tower inquiry tacitly conceded a god, while he still denied that god stood above him. — Elmalılı connects this to Pharaoh's order to Hāmān in 28:38. [1 notes: ELMALILI]
- 79:24/q10/p3 He denied any Creator while claiming practical control of his people. — Al-Rāzī and Bursawī describe him as a materialist denying Creator and resurrection. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI]

## 79:24/q11 How did this claim follow Pharaoh's earlier claim in 28:38?  [أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ · links]
- 79:24/q11/p1 He first said he knew no god for his people besides himself, then later claimed to be their highest lord. — Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid link both claims to God's seizure of Pharaoh. Khaythama, ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr, Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid as transmitted by Ibn Kathīr, and Ibn Jarīr's report through Mujāhid and others specify forty years between them. Al-Rāzī calls the former first and latter final claim. Elmalılı also recalls Pharaoh's threat in 26:29; the Study Quran reports the sequence. Abū Bakr transmits the forty-year interval from Ibn ʿAbbās through Abū Ḥuṣayn and Abū al-Ḍuḥā; al-Shaʿbī and ʿĀmir also identify the earlier and later boasts. [15 notes: MUJAHID(ابن عباس,مجاهد); ABDURRAZZAQ(خيثمة); IBNABIHATIM(عبد الله بن عمرو); RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس ومجاهد); ELMALILI(İbn Cerîr); STUDYQURAN(R, Ṭ, Ṭs); TAB-FULL(أبو بكر,ابن عباس,الشعبي,عامر,مجاهد)]

## 79:24/q12 What did Moses offer Pharaoh before the boast, and how did Pharaoh respond?  [فَقَالَ أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ · reports]
turns on: The reports differ on the promised duration and details of Pharaoh's response.
- 79:24/q12/p1 Moses offered lasting youth, kingship and Paradise for faith; Pharaoh refused, with Haman dissuading him in one report. — Muqātil says Moses offered continued kingship, youth without aging and Paradise if Pharaoh confessed God as Lord and himself as servant; Pharaoh retorted that a man is worshiped as lord only if he has a lord. Al-Suddī adds restored pleasures, secure rule and Hāmān's dissuasion before the proclamation. [2 notes: MUQATIL; IBNABIHATIM(السدي)]
- 79:24/q12/p2 Moses offered four hundred years of joy followed by Paradise, but Haman dissuaded Pharaoh from exchanging worship for servitude. — Al-Biqāʿī transmits this through Ḥamza al-Kirmānī; its specified four hundred years distinguishes the report. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL(حمزة الكرماني)]

## 79:24/q13 Why did the boast seem incongruous after Moses' sign?  [أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ · interpretation]
- 79:24/q13/p1 The transformed staff had exposed Pharaoh's helplessness, making the claim sound deranged or uncomprehending. — Al-Qāḍī as cited by al-Rāzī and an unnamed view in Bursawī both draw this inference from his humiliation before the staff. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL(القاضي); BURSEVI(بعضهم)]

## 79:24/q14 What does the comparison with Moses' victory over magic show?  [ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ · links]
- 79:24/q14/p1 Moses was the higher one after the magicians' defeat, because defeating their magic defeated Pharaoh. — Bursawī connects the claim with Moses' assurance in 20:68. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 79:24/q15 What do the reports say Iblis made of Pharaoh's boast?  [أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ · reports]
turns on: The reports differ between Iblis's reflection on his own pride and a direct encounter with Pharaoh.
- 79:24/q15/p1 Iblis wondered what would befall Pharaoh for claiming lordship when his own pride had already brought disaster. — Al-Biqāʿī transmits Satan's comparison with his offense against Adam; al-Qushayrī as reported by Bursawī says Iblīs had only claimed good for himself and suffered calamity. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL(إبليس); BURSEVI(الإمام القشيري)]
- 79:24/q15/p2 Iblis confronted Pharaoh in an Egyptian bath and repeated the boast after Pharaoh failed to recognize his supposed creator. — Al-Thaʿlabī's account, reported by al-Qurṭubī, has Iblis appear as a man and challenge Pharaoh's failure to know one he claimed to have made. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الثعلبي)]

## 79:24/q16 What does the verse show about the danger of royal deification?  [أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the note states a general continuity or identifies a later dynasty and endpoint.
- 79:24/q16/p1 Pharaoh's fabrication persisted among Egypt's kings and followers. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it extreme fabrication; Abū Ḥayyān reports this assessment from him. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية)]
- 79:24/q16/p2 Egyptian royal deification later appeared under Ismaili rulers, from al-Muʿizz until Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn removed it. — Abū Ḥayyān supplies this specific historical attribution. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 79:24/q17 What inward impulse does Pharaoh's claim reveal?  [أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ · inward]
turns on: Whether the inward lesson centers on a universal impulse, its cure, or Pharaoh's spiritual expulsion.
- 79:24/q17/p1 An impulse toward lordship and mastery exists in every soul, though only some openly voice it. — Al-Ghazālī in al-Biqāʿī says the soul recoils from servitude; a gnostic says all souls harbor Pharaoh's claim and resentment at servants or subordinates reveals it. Al-Biqāʿī cites the Prophet's never reproaching a servant. Kuran Yolu's Ghazālī calls it a distorted desire for perfection, elevation and freedom, revealed by some and restrained by others; its authors cite ambition for rank and self-assertion. [7 notes: BIQAI-FULL(الإمام الغزالي,بعض العارفين); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Gazzâlî)]
- 79:24/q17/p2 True perfection and freedom come through knowing and obeying God; rival claims to selfhood bring decline. — Ghazālī as reported by Kuran Yolu adds turning to God, following His will and adopting divine character; he calls other self-claims misery. [2 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Gazzâlî)]
- 79:24/q17/p3 Pharaoh's satanic schemes increased his veil, expelled him from the holy presence and led to subjugation and Fire. — Al-Qāshānī says the claim contested divine majesty, connecting its outcome to 79:25 and to anyone contesting God's grandeur and pride. [2 notes: QASHANI]

## 79:24/q18 Whom does al-Biqāʿī associate with Pharaoh's error?  [أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ · theology]
- 79:24/q18/p1 He associates Ibn ʿArabī, Ibn al-Fāriḍ, their followers and al-Ḥallāj with denial of the sovereign God. — Al-Biqāʿī curses the first group and places al-Ḥallāj after Pharaoh in that error. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 79:24/q19 What does Pharaoh's boast show about divine power and the gravity of his sin?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether the note stresses culpability and punishment or specifically refutes an asserted divine power.
- 79:24/q19/p1 It is a grave claim to divinity for which Pharaoh was seized and punished. — Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid say God seized him for both divinity claims. Al-Qāshānī says contesting divine majesty brought subjugation and Fire (79:25). Asad calls claimed divine status Pharaoh's cardinal sin and transgression of right, cross-referencing 28:38 and 79:17. Elmalılı calls this the condensed depth of his tyranny. Al-Biqāʿī treats Pharaoh's clear signs, especially self-evident productive inference, as proofs, citing 28:40–42, 10:83 and 40:43. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Shaʿbī and ʿĀmir connect Pharaoh's punishment with both his earlier claim in 28:38 and his later claim here. [9 notes: MUJAHID(ابن عباس,مجاهد); QASHANI; ASAD-NOTES; ELMALILI; BIQAI-FULL; TAB-FULL(الشعبي,عامر)]
- 79:24/q19/p2 Pharaoh's claimed power to harm without being harmed is false; only God, his creator and all creatures' creator, has that attribute. — Al-Ṭabrisī explicitly rejects the supposed immunity and assigns it to God. [1 notes: TABRISI]

## 79:24/q20 Do sinful words such as Pharaoh's affect God's Essence?  [* · theology]
- 79:24/q20/p1 No; attributes appearing in originated things cannot affect the divine Essence. — Wāsiṭī answers a question about God's creation and manifestation of sins and words unfit for lordship; he says the Essence transcends even verbal description. [1 notes: SULAMI(الواسطي)]

## 79:24/q21 Could Pharaoh receive divine mercy despite his claim?  [* · theology]
- 79:24/q21/p1 God invited even Pharaoh to repent, and mercy remained a possibility feared by Gabriel at his drowning. — Ibn ʿAbbās says no one may make people despair of repentance and that it begins with God's enabling turn toward the servant. Gabriel reports stuffing Pharaoh's mouth at drowning after his divinity claim, fearing he would receive mercy. [2 notes: DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,جبريل)]

## 79:24/q22 How does the claim serve the sūra's larger presentation?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the connection is to gentle speech, the sūra's final-fate theme, or Pharaoh's preceding conduct.
- 79:24/q22/p1 It condenses Pharaoh's deepest tyranny in a telling that explains the gentle speech addressed to him. — Elmalılı links the proclamation to the preceding divine instruction on gentle speech. [1 notes: ELMALILI]
- 79:24/q22/p2 It belongs to the sūra's decisive demonstration of resurrection, gathering and humanity's final fate. — Al-Biqāʿī says the preceding sūras fully expounded the subject and the oaths mark a climax. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says the brief utterance matches the sūra's decisive pace. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; BINTSHATI]
- 79:24/q22/p3 It follows Pharaoh's broken promises when calamities were lifted and Moses' influence among the Copts rose. — Iṣlāḥī explains the setting with 43:51–55 and Pharaoh's repeated reversals. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 79:24/q23 Whom do the cited poetic lines address?  [* · referent]
turns on: Whether the poem's apparent address or its indirect target determines the referent.
- 79:24/q23/p1 Their apparent address to the Prophet indirectly points to his family; their critics are the Umayyads. — Ibn Qutayba says the lines cannot reproach praise of the Prophet himself: no Muslim would do so, and no one equals or surpasses him. [1 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL+; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL-]
- 79:24/q23/p2 They address the Prophet himself. — This is the reading Ibn Qutayba rejects because Muslims would not reproach his praise and none can equal or exceed him. [0 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL-]

## 79:24/q24 What do the poetic lines imply about restraint in praise?  [* · interpretation]
- 79:24/q24/p1 Restraint in praising the Prophet gives no basis for reproach. — Ibn Qutayba argues that poets exaggerate praise even for ordinary men without blame; none equals or surpasses the Prophet. [1 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL]
