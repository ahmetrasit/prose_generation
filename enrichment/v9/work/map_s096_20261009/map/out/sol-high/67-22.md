# 67:22: 19 questions

## 67:22/q01 How does the opening question advance the preceding argument?  [أَفَمَن · rhetoric]
- 67:22/q01/p1 It forces recognition that the upright walker is better guided and that the two states are unequal. — Bursevi and Alusi say the interrogative hamza leads the conjunction in wording though the logical order is reversed. Ibn Ashur makes the fa a consequence of the preceding signs and calls for acknowledgment. Tabataba'i says the obstinate are distanced from direct address. Ibn Ashur rejects an evenhanded reading like 34:24 because this context argues and proves; Biqai says the deniers' physical route knowledge contrasts with their failure on the spiritual road. [6 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL+]

## 67:22/q02 What does mukibban or akabba mean, and how does its form work?  [مُكِبًّا · meaning]
turns on: Whether the hamza is a regular reflexive or passive marker, marks entering a state, or also occurs in transitive usage.
- 67:22/q02/p1 Akabba means to become bent or prone, entering a state; mukibb is a person bent or fallen face-down. — Kashshaf denies that Form IV is a regular passive and cites infaḍḍa, alʾama and qashʿa/aqshaʿ; inkabba and inqashaʿa are the regular reflexives. Baydawi and Alusi agree. Abu Hayyan and the Shaykh say the hamza marks entering a state; Ibn Ashur calls mukibb the active participle. Razi reports Kashshaf. Samin reports Zamakhshari and the Shaykh, but criticizes the Shaykh's attack as borrowed and quotes a poet. Abu Hayyan disputes Zamakhshari's categorical denial of responsive afʿala using Sibawayh and cites Ibn Maʿzuz's critique. Tabataba'i reports Zamakhshari. Bursevi glosses it as fallen face-down. [15 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL(صاحب الكشاف); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري,الشيخ); ALUSI-FULL(ابن سيده، الجوهري، ابن الحاجب); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(الزمخشري في الكشاف); BURSEVI; BAYDAWI-FULL+; KASHSHAF-FULL+]
- 67:22/q02/p2 Akabba is the intransitive or reflexive counterpart of transitive kabba, an exceptional reversal of the usual Form IV pattern. — Ibn Qutayba distinguishes akabba with alif from kabba without it. Tabari cites al-Aʿsha's fallen camel digging at its sides. Wahidi gives 'he cast him down, so he fell'; Ibn Atiyya cites the Prophet's 'harvest of tongues' for transitive kabba and compares qashaʿa/aqshaʿa. Jishumi explicitly calls this unusual and compares cloud dispersal; Razi reports Wahidi's account. Tabataba'i also gives the transitive 'cast down' gloss. Kashshaf and Baydawi reject treating it as a regular passive. [11 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL(النبي); RAZI-FULL(الواحدي); TABATABAI; KASHSHAF-FULL-; BAYDAWI-FULL-]
- 67:22/q02/p3 Akabbahu can itself be transitive, like kabbahu. — Alusi reports Ibn al-Aʿrabi's lexical evidence and says this usage removes the apparent irregularity. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(ابن الأعرابي)]

## 67:22/q03 What is the grammatical role of mukibban?  [مُكِبًّا · grammar]
- 67:22/q03/p1 It is a circumstantial accusative describing the walker. — Samin says it modifies the subject of yamshi; Abu Hayyan derives it from intransitive akabba. Alusi likewise places it in the relative clause; Bursevi adds that the walker stumbles repeatedly on rough ground with impaired strength. [4 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; BURSEVI]

## 67:22/q04 Whom do the two walkers represent?  [مُكِبًّا سَوِيًّا · referent]
turns on: Whether the images are a general religious parable, a report about named people, a blind/sighted illustration, or a further application.
- 67:22/q04/p1 They represent the unbeliever or polytheist and the believer or monotheist generally. — Ibn Abbas, Mujahid and Dahhak supply the general parable in Tabari, Jishumi, Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan. Muqatil adds blindness of heart versus Muhammad's pure heart, also reporting a specific identification. Wahidi, Baghawi and Qurtubi connect disbelief's blindness with Islam's visible road. Baydawi, Biqai and Alusi describe two religious ways; Ibn Ashur explicitly assigns the first to idolaters and the second to believers, citing divided tribal gods, Joseph 12:39, and 6:153. Tabataba'i says the believer seeks truth with insight; Kuranyolu adds Satan's false paths; Study Quran notes immediate desires. Ibn Ashur says an unassigned reading weakens the warning and could reverse its target. [38 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,الضحاك,مجاهد); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس في رواية الكلبي ومقاتل ومجاهد والضحاك); JISHUMI(ابن عباس، مجاهد، الضحاك); BAGHAWI-FULL; ZAJJAJ; IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس ومجاهد والضحاك); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(المفسرون); RAZI-FULL(آخرون,بعضهم); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس، ومجاهد، والضحاك); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(Q, Ṭs); IBNASHUR-FULL+]
- 67:22/q04/p2 The pair is Abu Jahl and Muhammad. — Muqatil, al-Kalbi, Ibn Abbas and others are reported by Wahidi, Ibn Atiyya, Razi, Qurtubi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan and Ibn Ashur; Kashshaf's Kalbi report names the Messenger. Muqatil contrasts Abu Jahl's blindness of heart and Muhammad's pure heart. [9 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); KASHSHAF-FULL(الكلبي); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس وابن الكلبي); RAZI-FULL(مقاتل); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس والكلبي); NASAFI-FULL(الكلبي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 67:22/q04/p3 The pair is Abu Jahl and Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib. — Wahidi gives Ata from Ibn Abbas; Ibn Atiyya attributes it to occasion-of-revelation transmitters; Razi, Abu Hayyan and Alusi also cite this identification. Kashshaf and Qurtubi report Hamza as an alternate upright person. [8 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(عطاء عن ابن عباس); IBNATIYYA-FULL(رواة الأسباب); RAZI-FULL(عطاء عن ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL(الكلبي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 67:22/q04/p4 The pair is Abu Jahl and Ammar ibn Yasir. — Mawardi, Wahidi, Razi and Qurtubi attribute this to Ikrima. [4 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(عكرمة); WAHIDI-BASIT(عكرمة); RAZI-FULL(عكرمة); QURTUBI-FULL(عكرمة)]
- 67:22/q04/p5 The upright walker is Abu Bakr, opposite Abu Jahl. — Qurtubi reports this alternative without naming a transmitter; Ibn Ashur lists it among reported identifications. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 67:22/q04/p6 The contrast can represent a blind person and a sighted person. — Ibn Qutayba, Kashshaf, Razi, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Bursevi and Alusi use blindness and sight as the physical model. Kashshaf says it represents unbeliever and believer; Alusi says the image can still illustrate the broader comparison. [7 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس); BAYDAWI-FULL(قيل); BURSEVI(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
- 67:22/q04/p7 The contrast is the self bent toward sensual desire versus one following Islam or the path of unity. — Tustari says innate desire deprives the first of divine guidance and makes the second a follower of Islamic laws and prophetic models. Qashani points to the lower realm and natural attractions versus a path of unity whose full measure cannot be attained. [4 notes: TUSTARI; QASHANI]
- 67:22/q04/p8 By application, the verse distinguishes opponents of Ali's authority from his followers. — Tabataba'i reports traditions of this application and says it is not the direct interpretation. [1 notes: TABATABAI(روايات)]

## 67:22/q05 What physical scene explains the face-down walker and his opposite?  [يَمْشِى مُكِبًّا عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِۦٓ يَمْشِى سَوِيًّا · interpretation]
turns on: The commentators supply different physical pictures and degrees of metaphor for the same contrast.
- 67:22/q05/p1 A stumbling traveler on rough or crooked ground is contrasted with one walking steadily on a level, straight road. — Tabari, Mawardi and Qurtubi say the first cannot see ahead or sideways. Kashshaf and Razi mention repeated falls on uneven ground; Razi separately mentions confused wandering. Baydawi and Nasafi describe safe steady walking on an even road. Biqai adds desire, ignorance, repeated hardship, and the upright traveler's raised head and prepared broad road. Tabataba'i says the first misses rises, dips, hazards and destination. Alusi and Bursevi repeat the uneven route. Kuranyolu asks which reaches the goal safely. [18 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 67:22/q05/p2 A blind person gropes, unable to see the road, while a sighted person can follow it. — Ibn Qutayba names blindness, as Ibn Jawzi also reports; Tha'labi says heart and eyes are blind; Jishumi says the first cannot distinguish truth from falsehood. Kashshaf, Razi, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Bursevi and Alusi give this as an alternative model. Bursevi cites a Persian couplet contrasting certainty and blind guesswork. Asad likens immediate worldly fixation to an earthworm. [12 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; THALABI; JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس); BAYDAWI-FULL(قيل); BURSEVI(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(قيل); ASAD-NOTES; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة)]
- 67:22/q05/p3 The first traveler bends close to the ground to search tracks on winding side routes; the second confidently uses a clear main road. — Ibn Ashur says the face-down posture represents the polytheist's uncertain search among tribal gods and for beneficial power, whereas one Lord gives the believer confidence. He calls the description three layered representational metaphors. [5 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 67:22/q05/p4 The fallen person is immersed in error, ignorance or lower desire rather than literally navigating ground. — Mujahid and Ibn Abbas give error versus guidance; Wahidi's Kalbi likens following one's own head to a beast. Jishumi adds inability to tell truth from falsehood; Biqai says desire prevents seeing signs or hearing counsel. Elmalili likens low fixation to crawling animals; Asad to an earthworm. Islahi says enslavement to desire misses the goal. [10 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,مجاهد); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); JISHUMI; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 67:22/q06 Does the walking describe worldly life, Resurrection, or both?  [يَمْشِى مُكِبًّا عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِۦٓ يَمْشِى سَوِيًّا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether walking is a figurative account of present faith, a literal future event, or a present state made visible in the future.
- 67:22/q06/p1 It is a parable for guidance and error in worldly life. — Ibn Atiyya says the first three identifications are figurative and contrasts them with Qatada's literal afterlife view. Abu Hayyan attributes the worldly metaphor to Ibn Abbas, Mujahid and Dahhak. Ibn Ashur argues it depicts the truth of the two creeds here. [7 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(القاضي أبو محمد); RAZI-FULL(آخرون); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس، ومجاهد، والضحاك); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 67:22/q06/p2 The unbeliever literally walks or is gathered face-down at Resurrection, while the believer goes upright. — Qatada repeatedly says worldly disobedience leads to being raised on the face and obedience to upright resurrection. The Prophet answers that the Creator of foot-walking can enable face-walking. Wahidi cites 17:97, Study Quran 25:34, and Ibn Kathir 37:22–26. Baydawi and Bursevi give the destinations as Fire and Paradise; Alusi says this reading is no parable. [21 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB-FULL(آخرون,قتادة); ZAJJAJ(التفسير); WAHIDI-BASIT(قتادة); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(قتادة); BAGHAWI-FULL(قتادة); KASHSHAF-FULL(قتادة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(قتادة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(قتادة); RAZI-FULL(قتادة); QURTUBI-FULL(قتادة); BAYDAWI-FULL(قيل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قتادة); BURSEVI(قتادة); ALUSI-FULL(قتادة); STUDYQURAN]
- 67:22/q06/p3 The worldly spiritual condition becomes a corresponding visible state in the next life. — Ibn Atiyya says disbelievers undergo both states. Ibn Kathir applies the contrast in both lives and cites 37:22–26; Biqai and Bursevi say hidden states are disclosed at Resurrection. Elmalili says the animal-like crawl continues in Hell. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(القاضي أبو محمد); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI]

## 67:22/q07 What report explains how someone can walk on his face?  [يَمْشِى مُكِبًّا عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِۦٓ · reports]
- 67:22/q07/p1 The Prophet says the One who made people walk on their feet can make them walk on their faces. — The reports vary between 'people' and 'the unbeliever' and between making him walk in the next world and raising him there. Abd al-Razzaq, Tabari, Zajjaj, Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, Ibn Kathir, Bursevi and Alusi relay it; Ibn Kathir names Anas as transmitter. Qatada uses it in support of the literal Resurrection reading. [10 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); TAB-FULL(النبي,قتادة); ZAJJAJ(رسول الله); IBNATIYYA-FULL(النبي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(النبي); IBNKATHIR-FULL(أنس بن مالك); BURSEVI(النبي); ALUSI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 67:22/q08 What does ahda mean in this comparison?  [أَهْدَىٰٓ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the form compares two degrees of guidance or names guidance where the other party has none.
- 67:22/q08/p1 It is a comparative of guidance: the upright traveler is more rightly guided. — Ibn Atiyya calls it the comparative of huda; Abu Hayyan says every listener would answer for the upright walker. Nasafi and Burhan give the implied answer. Biqai and Bursevi say a level route can be found by chance, so real guidance requires discernment. Jishumi says the upright walker knows the way. Damghani links just upright walking with guidance. [8 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; JISHUMI; DAMGHANI]
- 67:22/q08/p2 It means 'guided' in superlative form without a real comparison, since the first walker has no guidance. — Ibn Ashur says ahda refers to knowing the road, cites a similar noncomparative reading of 12:33, and allows mockery or light irony. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 67:22/q09 How are the two man clauses and ahda joined?  [أَفَمَن أَهْدَىٰٓ أَمَّن · grammar]
turns on: Whether a single predicate serves both alternatives or one or both descriptions require ellipsis.
- 67:22/q09/p1 The first man is subject and ahda its predicate; the second man is coordinated and needs no separate predicate. — Nahhas, Samin, Alusi and Ibn Ashur give this syntax; Samin uses 'Is Zayd standing, or Amr?' to show that am asks between two things under one predicate. Ibn Ashur says both man forms are relative pronouns. [5 notes: NAHHAS; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 67:22/q09/p2 The second man has an omitted predicate supplied from ahda. — Samin reports Abu al-Baqa's analysis; Samin argues against its need, and Nasafi also states that ahda licenses an omitted predicate. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء); NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR-]
- 67:22/q09/p3 Each half of the paired comparison omits a counterpart supplied by the other. — Burhan says the full comparative has four elements; each description supplies what the other leaves implicit, and the answer favors the upright walker. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 67:22/q10 How is amman read or written here?  [أَمَّن · readings]
turns on: One note concerns Talha's pronunciation; Burhan explains the joined written form.
- 67:22/q10/p1 Talha reads am man with an ungeminated mim. — Ibn Atiyya reports Talha's reading. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(طلحة)]
- 67:22/q10/p2 The written amman is joined because the two walkers form one comparison without separate subcategories. — Burhan compares joined am man in 27:61. He explains that an ma is separated when 'what' contains real subcategories with different rulings and says mimman is always joined because conceptual distinctions do not split actual entities. [3 notes: BURHAN]

## 67:22/q11 What does sawiyyan describe?  [سَوِيًّا · meaning]
turns on: Whether sawiyy is explained as bodily uprightness or the moral guidance that posture signifies.
- 67:22/q11/p1 It means fully upright, even or sound in walking. — Ibn Ashur calls it an active-participle form and cites 19:43; Tabari, Nasafi and Biqai contrast posture and level movement. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; TAB-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 67:22/q11/p2 It can mean just or rightly guided. — Damghani gives 'just or upright' and cites 20:135; Ibn Jawzi's Nuzha reports the exegetical gloss 'guided'. [2 notes: DAMGHANI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أهل التفسير)]

## 67:22/q12 What is the straight path in the verse?  [صِرَٰطٍۢ مُّسْتَقِيمٍۢ · interpretation]
turns on: The expression can denote the road within the image or the religious way represented by that road.
- 67:22/q12/p1 It is a clear, uncrooked and passable road in the travel image. — Tabari says it lacks crookedness; Mawardi says the traveler does not stray. Jishumi, Nasafi, Biqai and Alusi stress clarity, evenness and safety. [6 notes: TAB-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 67:22/q12/p2 It signifies the truth or Islam as the believer's religious course. — Mujahid calls it upright truth; Wahidi reports Ibn Abbas, Kalbi, Muqatil, Mujahid and Dahhak naming Islam. Muqatil, Tustari, Wahidi and Qurtubi mention faith, law or prophetic models. Biqai links it to accepting God and original nature free of desire, anger and self-interest. Elmalili specifies tawhid, ethics and sacred law. [9 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); MUQATIL; TUSTARI; MAWARDI-FULL(مجاهد); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس في رواية الكلبي ومقاتل ومجاهد والضحاك); QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 67:22/q12/p3 In an inward reading it is the perfectly straight path of unity. — Qashani says its entire nature and measure cannot be reached or estimated. [2 notes: QASHANI]

## 67:22/q13 What variant pronunciations of sirat are noted?  [صِرَٰطٍۢ · readings]
turns on: Pronunciation of the initial consonant; the notes say the meaning does not change.
- 67:22/q13/p1 The canonical ishmam gives sad a slight zay sound. — Qiraat-ER says the intermediate pronunciation preserves a trace of the word's non-Arabic origin, between sin and sad. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER]
- 67:22/q13/p2 Qalun from Nafi reads it with sin. — Qiraat-ER says this is closer to its stated Latin strāta source and makes no semantic difference. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER(Qālūn ʿan Nāfiʿ)]

## 67:22/q14 Why is no path named for the face-down walker?  [عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِۦٓ عَلَىٰ صِرَٰطٍۢ مُّسْتَقِيمٍۢ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the silence disparages the first route as no path or economizes the paired contrast.
- 67:22/q14/p1 His crooked route scarcely deserves to be called a path. — Baydawi, Biqai, Bursevi's cited 'author of correspondences,' and Alusi give this reason; Biqai stresses that repeated stumbling does not deter him. [4 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(صاحب المناسبات); ALUSI-FULL]
- 67:22/q14/p2 The second road's straightness supplies the first road's unstated crookedness by contrast. — Ibn Ashur says the contrast is clear without stating the first road's quality. Biqai calls the paired omissions a rhetorical figure that displays the criminal's worst and the Muslim's best feature; Burhan says each half supplies the other's missing element. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURHAN]

## 67:22/q15 What broader lesson is drawn from the two ways?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the reader draws chiefly a spiritual choice or also a concrete ethic for physical roads.
- 67:22/q15/p1 Insight, obedience and monotheism guide a person toward the true goal; desire and ignorance make one stray. — Wahidi's Kalbi describes animal-like headlong following of one's own head. Biqai says the upright person accepts God as Lord and Islam as religion while the other ignores signs and counsel; Ibn Ashur uses tribal idols and 12:39 to expose uncertainty among many gods. Tabataba'i says disbelievers knowingly defy what they must know and do. Islahi says desire prevents reaching the goal, Kuranyolu adds ungrateful Satan-following, and Study Quran mentions petty immediate concerns. Elmalili urges belief in the Creator's oneness and laws, following proofs and walking directly toward Him. [13 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(Q, Ṭs); ELMALILI]
- 67:22/q15/p2 The road image also yields practical duties to keep public roads broad, clean, safe and fair to others. — Elmalili names removal of obstacles and filth, refraining from encroachment, and cites God's making earth a carpet with spacious routes in 71:19–20. He links this physical duty with moral rectitude. [3 notes: ELMALILI]

## 67:22/q16 What report about hearts is applied to this verse?  [يَمْشِى مُكِبًّا عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِۦٓ · reports]
- 67:22/q16/p1 A four-hearts report assigns the inverted heart to the polytheist and recites the face-down verse. — Tabataba'i cites Abu Ja'far in al-Kafi: a bright lamp-like heart belongs to the grateful and patient believer, a sealed heart to the hypocrite, an inverted heart to the polytheist, and a fourth combines faith and hypocrisy. The mixed group, at 'al-Ta'if', is ruined if dying in hypocrisy and saved if dying in faith; Tabataba'i interprets al-Ta'if as demonic visitation and cites 7:201. He reports al-Burhan's chain via Ibn Babawayh, al-Fudayl and Sa'd al-Khaffaf and its 'luminous' variant. [6 notes: TABATABAI(أبو جعفر,ابن بابويه عن الفضيل عن سعد الخفاف عن أبي جعفر)]

## 67:22/q17 How does the comparison connect with nearby verses and other scriptural images?  [* · links]
turns on: The cited connections range from the immediate argument to other verses using related images.
- 67:22/q17/p1 It follows God's signs and blessings as proof against deniers and calls them back to grateful, upright response. — Qushayri says hearing, sight and hearts are special gifts left unthanked and that these verses give deniers unanswered arguments. Biqai says the command to speak recalls divine protection, benefits, power and intellect; Ibn Ashur says the opening fa follows preceding signs and says 12:108 likewise presents Islam's path followed with insight against polytheism. [5 notes: QUSHAYRI; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 67:22/q17/p2 Other passages echo its guidance, path and face-down imagery. — Tabari mentions 4:51 with the comparison and 6:39 with straightness; Wahidi cites 27:90 with face-down casting and 17:97 for afterlife gathering. Ibn Kathir cites 37:22–26 and Study Quran 25:34 for gathering toward Hell. Ibn Ashur cites 6:153 for one straight way versus divided ways and 12:39 for one God against many. [9 notes: TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(قتادة); IBNKATHIR-FULL; STUDYQURAN; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 67:22/q18 How does another Qur'anic image of bewilderment illuminate this walking?  [* · links]
- 67:22/q18/p1 The Qur'anic 'ameh image means heedless bewilderment and blindness of insight, comparable to walking face-down. — Bint al-Shati says the root occurs in seven Qur'anic verses in the imperfect and denotes transgression and bewilderment. Al-Khalil and Ya'qub cite a landmarkless area and camels disappearing beyond known tracks; Ibn al-Athir compares spiritual bewilderment with literal blindness. Ibn Qutayba says ya'mahun is running headlong without sight and directly compares this verse. [4 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن الأثير,ابن قتيبة,الخليل ويعقوب)]

## 67:22/q19 How is the following verse's 'little thanks' construed?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether 'little' denotes a small ineffective amount or serves as an idiom for none.
- 67:22/q19/p1 Its syntax says the addressees give little thanks. — Abu Hayyan says qalilan qualifies an omitted verbal noun, ma is extra, and tashkurun either begins a new clause or is an anticipated circumstantial clause. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 67:22/q19/p2 The little thanks may be some ineffective thanks or idiomatically none at all. — Abu Hayyan reports Ibn Atiyya's two explanations. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية)]
