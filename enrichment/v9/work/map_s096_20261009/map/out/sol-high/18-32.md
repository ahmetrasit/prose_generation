# 18:32: 31 questions

## 18:32/q01 What does mathal mean here?  [مَّثَلًۭا · meaning]
- 18:32/q01/p1 A likeness or illustrative example. — Muqātil calls it a description directed to Meccans; Baghawī says it asks hearers to recall the two men's story. [2 notes: MUQATIL; BAGHAWI-FULL]

## 18:32/q02 What does the command to strike a parable ask the Prophet to do?  [وَٱضْرِبْ مَّثَلًۭا · interpretation]
- 18:32/q02/p1 Recount the illustrative story of two men to the audience. — Abū Ḥayyān glosses the imperative as recounting the story; Baghawī says it asks the audience to recall it. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL]

## 18:32/q03 How is rajulayn construed after mathalan?  [مَّثَلًۭا رَّجُلَيْنِ · grammar]
- 18:32/q03/p1 It is accusative through an understood 'likeness of two men' or 'like two men' construction. — Zajjāj calls it the object implied by striking the parable; Naḥḥās supplies 'the likeness of two men'; Thaʿlabī supplies 'like two men'; Wāḥidī calls the phrase the object of ḍaraba. Samīn reports Abū al-Baqāʾ's omitted mathal mithla rajulayni and notes that ḍaraba can take two objects, comparing 2:26. [5 notes: ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; SAMIN-DURR]

## 18:32/q04 Who are the two men?  [رَّجُلَيْنِ · referent]
turns on: Whether the account identifies particular historical brothers, the two companions of 37:51, a non-sibling pair, or representative parties; the word 'companion' and the apparent wording are cited for the non-sibling reading.
- 18:32/q04/p1 Two Israelite brothers, one believing and the other unbelieving. — Muqātil calls them Yamlīkha and Firtas; other reports give believer Tamlikha, Yemlikha, Yahudha or Judah and unbeliever Fartus, Fatrus, Qatrus, Qartush, Qurtus, Qatras, Bratus or Quṭfayr. Some make them sons of an Israelite king. Reports vary in the names while retaining the brother pair. Bursawī argues that 'companion' can describe a brother and cites their walking through the garden. [30 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; DAMGHANI; JISHUMI(ابن عباس,مقاتل); KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(ابن عباس); IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(بعض المفسرين,مقاتل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس,مقاتل); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس,مقاتل); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ITQAN; BURSEVI(الجلالين); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس,مقاتل); IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن عباس); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(tefsirlerde bir görüş); STUDYQURAN; BURSEVI(أبو حيان)-]
- 18:32/q04/p2 Two Meccan brothers of Banū Makhzūm: the believer Abū Salama and the unbeliever al-Aswad ibn ʿAbd al-Asad. — Al-Kalbī and later reports identify Abū Salama as Umm Salama's husband; Qurtubī reports four thousand dinars inherited by each; Kuran Yolu suggests their gardens were in Ṭāʾif. [10 notes: THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); KASHSHAF-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الكلبي); BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(tefsirlerde bir görüş); STUDYQURAN]
- 18:32/q04/p3 Representatives of the arrogant wealthy unbelievers and the poor believers, including the parties of ʿUyayna and Salman. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya names a Qurashī or Tamīmī notable against Bilāl, ʿAmmār, Ṣuhayb and peers; Ibn ʿAbbās compares ʿUyayna's companions with Salman's. The comparison can address all believers and disbelievers. [6 notes: THALABI; JISHUMI(ابن عباس); IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(İbn Âşûr)]
- 18:32/q04/p4 The Prophet and the Meccan polytheists. — Thaʿlabī, Jishumī and Qurtubī transmit this identification. [3 notes: THALABI; JISHUMI; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 18:32/q04/p5 A wealthy garden owner and his poor neighbor, without a sibling relationship. — ʿAlī ibn Ibrāhīm says this better fits the apparent wording; Abū Ḥayyān infers non-siblings from 'companion,' while Bursawī disputes that inference. [2 notes: BURSEVI(أبو حيان); TABRISI(علي بن إبراهيم)+; BURSEVI-]
- 18:32/q04/p6 The companion and speaker of 37:51 and his debating companion. — Rāzī, Nasafī, Qurtubī, Itqān and Study Quran connect the pair to the companion mentioned in al-Ṣāffāt; the identification can accompany the brother account. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ITQAN; STUDYQURAN]

## 18:32/q05 Did the two men's story happen or serve as an imagined example?  [رَّجُلَيْنِ · reports]
turns on: The narrative's concrete garden details and unprefixed dialogue versus the freedom of a parable to describe a conceivable case.
- 18:32/q05/p1 It recounts a real event. — Ālūsī favors historicity though he allows a hypothetical pair. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites unprefixed dialogue and the man's later defeat in 18:37 and 18:43; Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites concrete garden details, though he regards transmitted identifications as unreliable. [4 notes: TABATABAI(آخرون); ALUSI-FULL+; IBNASHUR-FULL+; TABATABAI+]
- 18:32/q05/p2 It is an imagined, possible case used as a vivid parable. — Commentators cited by Ibn ʿAṭiyya compare the mode to the garden parable in 2:265; Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports this view. Ālūsī allows the possibility but favors a real event. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن عطية); TABATABAI(بعض المفسرين); ALUSI-FULL]

## 18:32/q06 What happened with the men's inherited wealth and their confrontation?  [رَّجُلَيْنِ · reports]
turns on: Reports differ over whether the men were brothers, partners or blacksmiths, and over episodes after the inheritance; four thousand each and eight thousand total can describe the same division.
- 18:32/q06/p1 The believer gave his share for God and heavenly equivalents while the other bought worldly property; the poor believer was later rejected. — Muqātil reports four thousand dinars each, charity to poor, orphans and needy versus houses, livestock and gardens. ʿAṭāʾ reports eight thousand shared, with four gifts of a thousand matching land, house, wife and servants; later versions add heavenly gardens, maidens, immortal youths, rebuke, denial of resurrection and expulsion. Ibn Jawzī's Abū Ṣāliḥ report says help was conditioned on joining the brother's religion; Qurtubī also reports freeing slaves, clothing, feeding and building mosques. The story is connected to 37:50–57. [28 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI(عطاء الخراساني); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(ابن عباس); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الكلبي,مقاتل); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن عباس); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(tefsirlerde bir görüş); STUDYQURAN]
- 18:32/q06/p2 They were partners who owned eight thousand dinars. — ʿAṭāʾ calls the pair partners rather than brothers; the note does not detail how they divided the sum. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(عطاء)]
- 18:32/q06/p3 One report makes them blacksmiths, or heirs who divided eight thousand dinars, before the charitable spending and refusal. — Ālūsī preserves both occupational and inheritance versions and describes gifts for heavenly gardens, a home, spouses and immortal youths. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 18:32/q06/p4 In a further account they fished to test their claims, invoking an idol and God respectively, and the idolater caught many fish. — Qurtubī says the believer caught none; an angel protested, Gabriel showed each man's eventual Paradise or Hell home, and both judged worldly fortune irrelevant. God then took the believer and destroyed the unbeliever; 37:51–52 echoes the later sight of the companion in Hell. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]

## 18:32/q07 Which man received the two gardens?  [لِأَحَدِهِمَا جَنَّتَيْنِ · referent]
- 18:32/q07/p1 The wealthy unbelieving man. — Muqātil identifies the recipient; the reported spending contrast has the unbeliever purchase the gardens while his believing counterpart gives away his share. Ibn Kathīr describes God giving the wealthy man productive vineyards. [3 notes: MUQATIL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 18:32/q08 What does jannah mean for these gardens?  [جَنَّتَيْنِ · meaning]
- 18:32/q08/p1 An orchard, named from trees that shade and conceal what lies beneath. — Māwardī calls it an orchard; Rāzī derives the word from covering and the trees' shade. [2 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; RAZI-FULL]

## 18:32/q09 Were the two gardens separate orchards or one combined estate?  [جَنَّتَيْنِ · referent]
turns on: Whether the later singular garden denotes a combined property or one of the two; one report distinguishes sides within a shared wall.
- 18:32/q09/p1 Two grape orchards or distinguishable sides, perhaps within one estate. — Bayḍāwī calls them two grape orchards; Biqāʿī suggests different terrain, such as plain and mountain, would protect against a single heat or cold calamity. Al-Suddī says one walled orchard had a river dividing sides, each named for its bordering wall. [3 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(السدي رضي الله عنه)]
- 18:32/q09/p2 The dual describes one combined garden. — Al-Farrāʾ's reported argument cites singular 'he entered his garden' in 18:35. [1 notes: BURHAN(الفراء)]

## 18:32/q10 Why was the prosperous man given two gardens?  [جَنَّتَيْنِ · rhetoric]
- 18:32/q10/p1 The pair signifies a full measure of divine favor. — Iṣlāḥī compares the two gardens promised in 55:46. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 18:32/q11 What does 'gardens of grapes' denote?  [جَنَّتَيْنِ أَعْنَٰبٍۢ · meaning]
- 18:32/q11/p1 Vineyards or gardens of grapevines, through fruit naming its tree or an understood word for vines. — Bursawī and Ālūsī offer metonymy or ellipsis; Ālūsī cites al-Rāghib that the grape word can denote both fruit and vine. Ṭabāṭabāʾī likewise says fruit can name its tree. [4 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; BAYDAWI-FULL]

## 18:32/q12 What value do grapes add to this estate?  [أَعْنَٰبٍۢ · interpretation]
- 18:32/q12/p1 Grapevines provide fruit, food, raisins and vinegar and suit varying climates; with palms they signal prosperity and divine bounty. — Biqāʿī says vines thrive in cold yet tolerate heat; Study Quran cites 6:99, 6:141, 23:19 and 36:34 for prosperity and beneficence. Iṣlāḥī calls grapes an element of the ideal Arab orchard. [3 notes: BIQAI-FULL; STUDYQURAN; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 18:32/q13 What does ḥaffafnāhumā mean?  [وَحَفَفْنَٰهُمَا · meaning]
- 18:32/q13/p1 We surrounded or encircled both gardens on their sides. — Majāz cites al-Ṭirimmāḥ's maḥfūfa; Zajjāj cites people surrounding Zayd; Wāḥidī, Ibn Jawzī, Rāzī and Qurtubī compare angels surrounding the Throne in 39:75. Jishumī, Baghawī and Rāzī explain ḥifāf as sides; Ṭabrisī cites Ṭarafa and Rāzī a couch line; Abū Ḥayyān cites a ridge's flanks and related verbal forms; Samīn cites al-Nābigha. [15 notes: MAJAZ; ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(الشاعر); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 18:32/q14 How does the bāʾ work with ḥaffafnāhumā?  [وَحَفَفْنَٰهُمَا بِنَخْلٍۢ · grammar]
- 18:32/q14/p1 It introduces what surrounds the first object, making the verb take a second object. — Rāzī quotes al-Zamakhsharī on the one-object verb plus bāʾ; Bayḍāwī, Nasafī and Ālūsī give the same construction. Samīn distinguishes surrounding a thing and causing it to be surrounded by another. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL(صاحب الكشاف); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]

## 18:32/q15 Where were the palms, and why set them there?  [بِنَخْلٍۢ · interpretation]
- 18:32/q15/p1 The palms ringed the grape gardens as a useful, beautiful border. — Zajjāj and Thaʿlabī place palms around both gardens. Zamakhsharī, Rāzī, Nasafī and Abū Ḥayyān say landowners favor fruit trees around vineyards. Māwardī notes fruit above crops below; Biqāʿī says palms tolerate heat and cold, offer dates and perhaps shield vines; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the fruit-tree ring enhances the garden; Iṣlāḥī adds beauty and shelter from hot winds. [22 notes: ZAJJAJ; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL(صاحب الكشاف); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 18:32/q16 Where were the crops in relation to the gardens?  [بَيْنَهُمَا زَرْعًۭا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether 'between them' denotes the gap separating the orchards or the space beneath and among their trees.
- 18:32/q16/p1 They occupied the space between the two gardens, joining the cultivated areas. — Wāḥidī, Zamakhsharī, Rāzī, Bayḍāwī and Nasafī describe an uninterrupted tract; Baghawī's alternative says no uncultivated patch remained. Biqāʿī says intervening grain also diversifies against blight. [14 notes: THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 18:32/q16/p2 The crops grew among or under the trees inside the cultivated gardens. — Māwardī describes crops below the fruit trees; Jishumī reports crops inside the vineyard; Baghawī places crops around the vines with palms in their middle; Qurtubī says crops were among grapevines. [4 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]

## 18:32/q17 What does the combined planting convey?  [جَنَّتَيْنِ أَعْنَٰبٍۢ بِنَخْلٍۢ زَرْعًۭا · interpretation]
- 18:32/q17/p1 A complete, carefully arranged and productive estate supplying fruit and staple crops. — Zajjāj says cultivation and production were complete and continuous; Māwardī calls fruit above and crops below especially beautiful and useful. Zamakhsharī adds abundant river water; Rāzī notes successive benefits at different times; Bayḍāwī and Nasafī stress continuous cultivation; Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it an unsurpassed estate irrigated by a river; Iṣlāḥī describes an ideal orchard with stream and tributaries. [12 notes: ZAJJAJ; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 18:32/q18 Where were the gardens located?  [جَنَّتَيْنِ · reports]
turns on: The reports offer different sites, while the verse itself supplies no place name.
- 18:32/q18/p1 At Lake Tinnīs. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya's report says one brother sold his share for obedience, was mocked, and the lake flooded overnight; Ibrāhīm ibn al-Qāsim says God divided the site in one night. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عطية); ALUSI-FULL(إبراهيم بن القاسم الكاتب)]
- 18:32/q18/p2 Possibly near Ṭāʾif, where Meccans had orchards. — Ibn ʿĀshūr speculates this setting; Kuran Yolu associates the Meccan Makhzūm account with likely gardens there. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(tefsirlerde bir görüş)]
- 18:32/q18/p3 The location is left unspecified because identifying it adds no significant benefit. — Ālūsī makes this point about the narrative's wording. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 18:32/q19 What does jaʿalnā say about the gardens, and how does its clause function?  [جَعَلْنَا جَنَّتَيْنِ · grammar]
- 18:32/q19/p1 It describes God's provision of the two gardens; the clause explains the parable or qualifies the two men. — Bayḍāwī and Abū Ḥayyān give the explanatory or descriptive attachment; Samīn reports the explanatory reading and an accusative adjective analysis. Ibn ʿĀshūr says God determined the means for the gardens and compares 2:266. [4 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 18:32/q20 Why does a later sentence use singular 'he had fruit' after two men?  [رَّجُلَيْنِ · grammar]
- 18:32/q20/p1 Only one of the two men had fruit; mentioning both in the setup permits the later singular. — Akhfash explains the shift to wa-kāna lahu thamar this way. [1 notes: AKHFASH]

## 18:32/q21 What grammatical number does later kullatā have?  [جَنَّتَيْنِ · grammar]
turns on: Singular ātat and case behavior before an overt noun versus derivation as a dual; the stem's alif and tāʾ receive separate analyses.
- 18:32/q21/p1 It is singular in form while referring to the pair of gardens. — Basrans cite singular ātat and the unchanged alif before an overt noun; Qurtubī adds Jarīr's singular predicate and the analogy of naḥnu. Sibawayh analyzes its alif as feminine and tāʾ as replacing wāw; Abū Ḥayyān repeats this Basran account. [7 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(أهل البصرة,سيبويه); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(البصريون); QURTUBI-FULL+]
- 18:32/q21/p2 It is dual in form and meaning. — Al-Farrāʾ derives it from kull by easing lām and adding a dual alif; Baghdadians agree on dual form. Basrans object that an oblique dual before an overt noun would take yāʾ, treating a contrary poetic form as necessity. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(البغداديون); QURTUBI-FULL(أهل البصرة)-]
- 18:32/q21/p3 Its alif is additional and the word follows the faʿīl pattern. — Al-Jarmī differs from Sibawayh's feminine-alif, replacement-tāʾ analysis. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(سيبويه); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجرمي)]

## 18:32/q22 How does this parable connect with its surrounding passage?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes trace different contextual links: immediate social dispute, reported questions, sūrah theme, al-Ṣāffāt's companion, and other garden passages.
- 18:32/q22/p1 It answers rich unbelievers who disdain poor believers and boast of worldly wealth or supporters. — Rāzī, Qurtubī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ibn Kathīr, Biqāʿī and Study Quran relate it to the command to remain with poor believers and the opponents' demand for their expulsion; Biqāʿī cites 18:28–29. Ālūsī says it contrasts obedience in poverty with disobedience amid favor after earlier afterlife outcomes. Kuran Yolu likewise sees wealth and poverty as tests. [12 notes: RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 18:32/q22/p2 The parable answers questions Meccan polytheists asked after consulting Jews. — Zajjāj and Abū Isḥāq report questions about the Cave Companions, the Spirit and the two men, making the answer an example for the Prophet and both believing and unbelieving hearers. [2 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق)]
- 18:32/q22/p3 It forms part of a broader sūrah warning about worldly beauty, faith and gratitude. — Asad links it to 18:7–8 and calls earthly beauty a divine test. Study Quran describes it as the sūrah's long parable alongside three major narratives. Biqāʿī ties the story sequence to an admonition of Israelites after the Moses–Khiḍr episode; Iṣlāḥī contrasts the ungrateful owner with grateful Dhū al-Qarnayn. [5 notes: ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN; BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 18:32/q22/p4 It illustrates the same brothers' worldly condition that 37:51–61 presents in the Hereafter. — Qurtubī expressly distinguishes the two settings and cites the al-Ṣāffāt dialogue; other accounts link the believer's spending, rejection or later sight of his companion to 37:50–57. [8 notes: THALABI(عطاء الخراساني); JISHUMI; QURTUBI-FULL; ITQAN; IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن عباس); STUDYQURAN]
- 18:32/q22/p5 Its gardens recall other Qurʾānic garden scenes and Meccan demands. — Ibn ʿĀshūr compares 2:266; Study Quran compares 2:264–66, 68:17–32 and 34:15–16 and says the vineyards resemble the demanded palm-and-grape garden with streams in 17:90–91. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]

## 18:32/q23 What does the parable teach about wealth, faith and the Hereafter?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Qurtubī disputes an exclusively renunciatory reading on contextual grounds; other notes emphasize gratitude, obedience and the reversal of worldly fortune.
- 18:32/q23/p1 Wealth is a temporary test; gratitude and obedience, not riches or social rank, determine honor before God. — Jishumī contrasts the poor believer honored by God with the rich unbeliever; Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān warn fortunes can reverse. Qushayrī says the ungrateful owner loses his garden and regrets neglecting gratitude. Biqāʿī says reliance on anything besides God ends in loss; Ālūsī consoles the poor and warns the wealthy. Iṣlāḥī says opponents misread success as proof of their beliefs and deny or presume advantage in the Hereafter; Kuran Yolu calls both wealth and poverty tests. [20 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; QUSHAYRI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 18:32/q23/p2 It urges this community to renounce worldly life and desire the Hereafter. — Māwardī's possibility is reported by Qurtubī, who objects that the context points elsewhere. [0 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الماوردي)-]

## 18:32/q24 How is the parable read inwardly?  [* · inward]
turns on: The inward readings use different symbolic schemes: two spiritual trajectories or the struggle of self and heart.
- 18:32/q24/p1 Two servants receive divine gentleness; one advances from good beginnings through character and pure states to self-annihilation in unity. — Qushayrī describes the successful servant's struggle and completion through spiritual stations. [2 notes: QUSHAYRI]
- 18:32/q24/p2 The other servant neglects his beginning and falls back into habit, heedlessness and estrangement. — Qushayrī describes abandonment of the path followed by separation, expulsion and humiliation. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]
- 18:32/q24/p3 The two men signify an unbelieving self and believing heart, and garden features signify desires and human faculties. — Al-Nīsābūrī maps the gardens to desire and the world, grapes to lusts, palms to leadership, crops to animal pleasures and the river to faculties and senses; later fruit and boasting signify cravings and blameworthy traits, while overturned hands signify regret for a wasted life. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(النيسابوري)]

## 18:32/q25 What does wāw contribute when it coordinates items or clauses?  [وَٱضْرِبْ وَحَفَفْنَٰهُمَا وَجَعَلْنَا · grammar]
turns on: The grammatical force of each coordinating particle: bare joining, succession, alternative or exclusion.
- 18:32/q25/p1 It joins elements under a shared relation without itself specifying order, delay or alternative. — Sībawayh's passage example allows either order or simultaneous action; Jurjānī contrasts fa, thumma and aw. He says coordinated clauses also require a meaningful connection, as with Zayd and ʿAmr, while unrelated clauses become absurd. [4 notes: SIBAWAYH; JURJANI-DALAIL]
- 18:32/q25/p2 Unlike wāw, fa sets the first event as the starting point. — Sībawayh illustrates passing Zayd then ʿAmr. [1 notes: SIBAWAYH]
- 18:32/q25/p3 Unlike wāw, thumma marks two passages with the first as starting point. — Sībawayh says both remain coordinated under the preposition. [1 notes: SIBAWAYH]
- 18:32/q25/p4 Unlike wāw, aw offers alternatives, affirming one rather than both. — Sībawayh explains how to deny both or specify which one is denied. [1 notes: SIBAWAYH]
- 18:32/q25/p5 Unlike wāw, lā affirms the first coordinated noun and excludes the second. — Sībawayh says it clarifies which passage was meant. [1 notes: SIBAWAYH]

## 18:32/q26 How do the parts of a compound simile relate to one another?  [مَّثَلًۭا · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether a comparison treats its images as defining conditions, subordinate descriptions, or parallel components.
- 18:32/q26/p1 A valid comparison must preserve the defining features of the whole compared image. — Jurjānī says a generic red rose fails when the intended flower has white around its red center; his praise-and-pearl example compares the whole wasted act, not hanging alone, with wāw meaning 'with' because no parallel predicates exist. [3 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR]
- 18:32/q26/p2 One image in a compound simile can be subordinate to another, as a descriptive or circumstantial clause. — Jurjānī uses gray hair in youth, a circumstantial wāw with Mars and Jupiter, falling stars as a description of night, and day calling at night's sides. [3 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR]
- 18:32/q26/p3 Some compound comparisons coordinate their components as equals on both sides. — Jurjānī treats Imruʾ al-Qays's paired dates as an exception, with explicit coordination in 'fresh and dry'; a plural subject can encompass distinct equal items. [2 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR]

## 18:32/q27 How are competing poetic depictions assessed?  [مَّثَلًۭا · rhetoric]
turns on: The cited poetic evidence compares natural eloquence, crafted imagery, fuller effects and reversal of an expectation.
- 18:32/q27/p1 Al-Nābigha's opening powerfully depicts distress and sleepless night. — Khaṭṭābī reports praise that no poet began better; he explains the relieved chest through a shepherd settling camels, joining natural ease, eloquence and sweetness. [2 notes: KHATTABI]
- 18:32/q27/p2 Imruʾ al-Qays surpasses al-Nābigha in crafted simile and inventive meaning. — Khaṭṭābī points to embodied night, darkness like colliding sea waves, stars tied motionless, and the reversal from longing for dawn to despair that any time can cure the pain; a report has al-Walīd stamp his foot and al-Shaʿbī recognize the excellence. [5 notes: KHATTABI(الشعبي); KHATTABI+]
- 18:32/q27/p3 Poems on one subject can be ranked by achievement; al-Aʿshā's wine verse surpasses al-Akhṭal's. — Khaṭṭābī says a comparison decides whether one leads, falls short or equals another. Hishām reports al-Shaʿbī's judgment and al-Akhṭal's concession. Khaṭṭābī prefers the image of aroma curing congestion over one merely reaching a person with a cold. [4 notes: KHATTABI(هشام ابن أدهم المازني); KHATTABI+]
- 18:32/q27/p4 A striking poetic contest may build an idea up before reversing or demolishing it. — Khaṭṭābī states the device; Ḥassān's verse for Jabala begins by seeming to disparage the wine cup he desired to renounce. [2 notes: KHATTABI(حسان بن ثابت)]

## 18:32/q28 Is Qurʾānic inimitability a matter of knowing its composition or being able to produce it?  [مَّثَلًۭا · theology]
turns on: Whether knowing compositional rules suffices to reproduce the achievement; Bāqillānī argues that skill and capacity can fail despite knowledge.
- 18:32/q28/p1 It rests on an inability to produce Qurʾānic composition, even when rules are known. — Bāqillānī compares known meters, eloquence, weaving and archery with unequal execution; an exceptional verse or inability to copy an existing line shows knowledge and capacity can diverge. [4 notes: BAQILLANI]
- 18:32/q28/p2 It rests on the Messenger's privileged knowledge of composition. — Bāqillānī attributes this to opponents and objects that it cannot distinguish divine from prophetic authorship. [0 notes: BAQILLANI-]

## 18:32/q29 What do reports say about multiple permitted recitations?  [* · readings]
- 18:32/q29/p1 The Prophet affirmed differing recitations and permission expanded to seven aḥruf for the community's ease. — Ṭabarī reports Gabriel's successive commands at Adhāt Banī Ghifār, Ubayy's encounter with different Sūrat al-Naḥl recitations, the Prophet's affirmation and prayer against Ubayy's misgivings, and requests for forgiveness with a final intercession reserved for Judgment Day. Abū Bakra's version has Gabriel ask for one and Michael for more until six or seven, provided mercy and punishment are not exchanged. Ibn Kathīr reports seven forms from seven Paradise gates as healing and sufficient and grades one Ubayy chain authentic. [11 notes: TAB-FULL(أبي,أبي بن كعب,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبي بن كعب)]

## 18:32/q30 What subjects does the Qurʾān encompass?  [* · sciences]
- 18:32/q30/p1 It includes narratives, the Prophet's career, creation and afterlife, divine names, faith, law, sins and transmitted prophetic reports. — Suyūṭī lists the two men and their gardens among many narratives; then the Prophet's call, migration, battles and miracles; human creation, death, resurrection, Paradise and Hell; God's and the Prophet's names, branches of faith, Islamic law, major and minor sins, and confirmation of prophetic reports. [7 notes: ITQAN]

## 18:32/q31 What is the singular form of rajulayn?  [رَّجُلَيْنِ · meaning]
- 18:32/q31/p1 Rajul, the singular of rijāl ('men'). — Ibn al-Jawzī gives the lexical relation. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-NUZHA]
