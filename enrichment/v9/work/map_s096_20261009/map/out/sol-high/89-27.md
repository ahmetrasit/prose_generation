# 89:27: 24 questions

## 89:27/q01 How is the vocative read?  [يَٰٓأَيَّتُهَا · readings]
turns on: Whether the feminine marker is retained before a feminine addressee.
- 89:27/q01/p1 The common reading has feminine yā ayyatuhā. — Abū Ḥayyān calls this the majority reading; al-Samīn calls it the common reading. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ defends its agreement with feminine singular, dual, and plural vocatives. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN(الجمهور وزيد بن علي); SAMIN-DURR; BINTSHATI(زيد بن علي); BINTSHATI+]
- 89:27/q01/p2 Zayd ibn ʿAlī reads yā ayyuhā without feminine tāʾ. — Al-Zajjāj says either form may address a woman. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn defend nonagreement by analogy with dual and plural vocatives. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ rejects that analogy because feminine dual and plural retain tāʾ; al-Ālūsī calls the grammatical debate irrelevant to the soul's concern. [11 notes: ZAJJAJ; ABUHAYYAN(الجمهور وزيد بن علي); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI(زيد بن علي,صاحب البديع); ALUSI-FULL(زيد); BINTSHATI(أبو حيان,زيد بن علي); BINTSHATI-]

## 89:27/q02 How does the vocative construction work?  [يَٰٓأَيَّتُهَا ٱلنَّفْسُ ٱلْمُطْمَئِنَّةُ · grammar]
turns on: Which noun the adjective modifies and whether feminine agreement is required.
- 89:27/q02/p1 Al-nafs describes ayyu, and al-muṭmaʾinna describes al-nafs. — Al-Naḥḥās says al-muṭmaʾinna may instead modify ayyu in the accusative after the clause is complete. Al-Zajjāj cites Sībawayh's kullatuhunna usage for gendered forms. [2 notes: NAHHAS; ZAJJAJ(سيبويه)]

## 89:27/q03 Who utters the address?  [يَٰٓأَيَّتُهَا · referent]
turns on: Whether the divine address is direct or delivered through angels.
- 89:27/q03/p1 God addresses the believer directly, honoring the soul. — Al-Kashshāf, Rāzī, al-Nasafī, and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ compare God's speech to Moses; Ibn ʿĀshūr allows direct speech in a fresh passage. Quṭb describes an intimate call from the highest assembly. [12 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI(بعض المفسرين); IBNASHUR-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; STUDYQURAN(Āl, Q, R, Sy)]
- 89:27/q03/p2 An angel speaks God's address to the soul. — Abū Ḥayyān takes this as apparent; the Abū Bakr reports explicitly mention an angel. Al-Qurṭubī says angels address God's friends. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ prefers leaving the speaker unrestricted to include both possibilities. [14 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI(بعض المفسرين); QURTUBI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(Āl, Q, R, Sy); ABUHAYYAN+; BINTSHATI+]

## 89:27/q04 What does nafs denote here?  [ٱلنَّفْسُ · meaning]
turns on: Whether it denotes the whole person, self, spirit, or a kind of substance.
- 89:27/q04/p1 Nafs is the person or continuing self, the 'I' of experience. — Rāzī says awareness of self persists through bodily change and when one is unaware of the body; Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 39:56 and 6:151 for the whole person. Elmalılı describes unified awareness, its perceiving and perceived aspects, and God's grounding of that unity in 8:24. [13 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; ALUSI(قيل); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 89:27/q04/p2 Nafs is the spirit that animates the natural or bodily soul. — Tustarī distinguishes life-giving spirit from natural soul. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 12:53; Elmalılı cites 17:85 and 39:42, treating death and sleep as withdrawal, not annihilation. Qushayrī mentions spirit settled toward the still soul. [8 notes: TUSTARI; QUSHAYRI; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 89:27/q04/p3 The soul is immaterial, distinct from the body. — Some cited by Rāzī infer this from knowing the self without noticing the body; Rāzī refers to an objection in Lubāb al-Ishārāt. On this account, coming and returning mean assuming and relinquishing bodily governance. [4 notes: RAZI(قوم); RAZI-FULL]
- 89:27/q04/p4 The soul is a subtle, luminous celestial body. — Others cited by Rāzī say its attachment animates the body and its departure leaves it dead; on this view coming and return are literal. [4 notes: RAZI(آخرون); RAZI-FULL]

## 89:27/q05 How is muṭmaʾinna derived and what is its basic sense?  [ٱلْمُطْمَئِنَّةُ · meaning]
turns on: The proposed root order and the basic sense of composure.
- 89:27/q05/p1 It is an active participle meaning calm, settled, firm, and untroubled. — Rāzī defines iṭmiʾnān as settledness and firmness; Ibn ʿĀshūr describes the participle's calmness. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ calls it a heart verb, surveying eight uses: hearts reassured in 3:126, 8:10, 13:28, and 2:260, and safety in 16:112 and 4:103; bodily calm depends on inner calm. [16 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; IBNASHUR; BINTSHATI(المفسرون); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 89:27/q05/p2 Its root has hamza first and mīm second. — Ibn ʿĀshūr prefers Abū ʿAmr's analysis of the radical order. [1 notes: IBNASHUR(أبو عمرو)+]
- 89:27/q05/p3 It derives by metathesis from ṭaʾmana. — Ibn ʿĀshūr reports Sībawayh's derivation, yielding muṭaʾminna and iṭmiʾnān, citing 2:260 and 4:103. [1 notes: IBNASHUR(سيبويه)]

## 89:27/q07 What does the adjective contribute to the address?  [ٱلْمُطْمَئِنَّةُ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether it praises and assures known addressees or identifies a restricted group.
- 89:27/q07/p1 It praises and reassures those addressed. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says souls would not know they qualified before admission, so the adjective explains the good news. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says predicating calm of the soul stresses its inner essence. [3 notes: IBNASHUR; BINTSHATI]
- 89:27/q07/p2 It identifies the tranquil souls as a particular group. — Ibn ʿĀshūr allows that God may inspire souls to recognize their tranquility, enabling a restrictive adjective. [1 notes: IBNASHUR]

## 89:27/q08 When is the soul addressed?  [يَٰٓأَيَّتُهَا ٱلنَّفْسُ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the address comes at death, resurrection, after judgment, entry to Paradise, or throughout life.
- 89:27/q08/p1 At death, as the believer's soul leaves the body. — Ibn Zayd says this; the Abū Bakr report places the angelic saying at death. Al-Ḥasan describes mutual divine and human satisfaction then. Ibn ʿĀshūr reports Zayd b. Ḥārithah and Abū Ṣāliḥ; Elmalılı says return then means the spirit leaving the body. - 89:27/q08/p2 At resurrection, when souls return to their bodies. — Ibn ʿAbbās and others understand a literal return; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the body is figuratively its lord or owner and connects the scene with 89:21. ## 89:27/q09 To whom does the address apply, and which occasions are reported?  [ٱلنَّفْسُ · referent]
turns on: Whether a reported individual is exclusive or an instance of a general address.
- 89:27/q09/p1 It applies generally to every sincere, obedient believer. — ʿIkrima and al-Farrāʾ report a general application; al-Qurṭubī prefers it. ## 89:27/q14 How does the soul's tranquility relate to other states of the self?  [ٱلنَّفْسُ ٱلْمُطْمَئِنَّةُ · inward]
turns on: How many states there are and how they are ordered.
- 89:27/q14/p1 The Qurʾān names the evil-commanding, self-reproaching, and tranquil states. — Rāzī cites 91:7, 5:116, 32:17, 12:53, 75:2. The Study Quran says desire rules the first, struggles in the second, and is controlled in the third. Elmalılı contrasts earlier worldly and belatedly reproachful souls with timely understanding. [4 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; ELMALILI; STUDYQURAN]
- 89:27/q14/p2 Sufi writers enumerate seven states of the soul. — Elmalılı lists commanding, self-reproaching, tranquil, content, accepted, inspired, and purified and relates self-knowledge to knowledge of God. Al-Ālūsī mentions three stations in this passage: tranquil, content, approved. [4 notes: ELMALILI; ALUSI(السادة الصوفية)]
- 89:27/q14/p3 Guidance can move the tranquil soul into self-reproach. — Junayd says God clothes it in guidance until it becomes a self-reproaching soul. [1 notes: SULAMI(الجنيد)]

## 89:27/q15 How does this address connect with the preceding passage?  [* · links]
turns on: How the positive address relates to prior warning and descriptions of worldly life.
- 89:27/q15/p1 It contrasts the believing soul's good end with worldly regret and punishment. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān say the discourse turns to believers. Al-Qurṭubī contrasts trust with wealth-based accusations against God; al-Biqāʿī sees an answer to the fate of the restless miser. Ibn ʿĀshūr says promise after warning encourages good and deters evil. Qutb stresses intimacy amid terror; Iṣlāḥī contrasts gratitude and patience with pride and despair. [25 notes: IBNATIYYA; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; BIQAI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN(Ubayy ibn Kaʿb)]
- 89:27/q15/p2 Its calm recalls the oath's worship times and anticipates the positive ending. — Neuwirth says those times embody trust and calm nearness; Elmalılı likens the righteous life to the ten nights before Eid and entry into Paradise to the festival dawn. [2 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Neuwirth); ELMALILI]

## 89:27/q16 How does the wording present the address?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether an omitted divine saying or a direct standalone call explains its abruptness.
- 89:27/q16/p1 An omitted 'God says' introduces a future, effective divine address. — Al-Ālūsī says the omission intensifies contrast with 89:24. Al-Qaffāl treats the imperative as declarative: the tranquil soul returns. Elmalılı says the majority infer an omitted divine saying and regard the commands as creative rather than legislative. [6 notes: RAZI(القفال); RAZI-FULL(القفال); ALUSI; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR]
- 89:27/q16/p2 The abrupt direct call honors and reassures the believer now. — Elmalılı allows a direct legislative call at the sūra's close; Kur'an Yolu says its warmth brings future happiness into present life. Ibn ʿĀshūr considers a fresh passage. [4 notes: ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 89:27/q17 Does this passage belong to the original revelation sequence?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether the closing address was revealed together with the preceding verses or added later.
- 89:27/q17/p1 Its placement suggests one sequence with the preceding warnings. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the context puts the speech in the afterlife; if the named occasions imply a Medinan verse, it would be separate and placed here by instruction or thematic affinity. He notes the agreed Meccan classification except a report from al-Dānī. [3 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 89:27/q17/p2 Verses 27–30 are a later addition to the sūra. — Corpus Coranicum says the positive afterlife ending expands an earlier threat-only unit, pairs Garden with 89:23's Hell, and uses direct speech to supersede negative human statements; 23–24 and 27–30 enact the oath's duality. [4 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM]
