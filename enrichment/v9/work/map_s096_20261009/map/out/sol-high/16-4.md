# 16:4: 18 questions

## 16:4/q01 What does God's creating the human from a drop encompass?  [خَلَقَ نُّطْفَةٍۢ · interpretation]
- 16:4/q01/p1 It compresses the stages from a lowly, inert fluid to a formed, nourished, mature human with spirit, sensation, intellect, and speech. — Tabari traces development through successive stages in three darknesses, the breathed spirit, feeding and growth; Zajjaj and Abu Ishaq say other Qur'anic passages detail the stages; Razi cites 23:12–13. Qurtubi notes fluid, development, birth and growth. Baydawi, Nasafi, Bursevi and Alusi describe the fluid as insentient, motionless or unable to keep shape or position. Elmalili attributes sensation, will, speech, eloquence and strength to divine power and the breathed spirit. Ibn Ashur calls attention to the low beginning and intellectual culmination; Study Quran cites 18:37, 22:5, 23:12–14, 35:11, 40:67, 53:45–46, 75:37, 76:2 and 80:19 for human weakness. The cited Imam distinguishes the body's development from the soul's. [14 notes: TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN(IK)]

## 16:4/q02 Who is the human meant by al-insān?  [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · referent]
turns on: Whether the singular is a generic species term, a nameable occasion, or a characterization of the disbelievers addressed.
- 16:4/q02/p1 The human species generally, especially its ordinary descendants created through reproduction. — Tabari says the singular denotes humanity; Ibn Atiyya calls it the species, and Qurtubi, Alusi and Ibn Ashur likewise take it generically; Ibn Ashur calls the definite article mental reference to the known species. Baghawi prefers the general scope. Bursevi excludes the first parents: Adam came from soil and Eve from his left rib. Biqai distinguishes Adam's unqualified water from descendants' ejaculated fluid. Tabatabai excludes the exceptional creations of Adam and Jesus. Ibn Atiyya says the broad address can name intellect and speech as blessings while still warning unbelievers. [10 notes: TAB-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; BAGHAWI-FULL+]
- 16:4/q02/p2 Ubayy ibn Khalaf al-Jumahi, whose challenge about a decayed bone exemplified the words. — Muqatil names Ubayy and says the Prophet killed him at Uhud. Al-Kalbi, Wahidi's commentators, Ibn Abbas and al-Hasan as reported by Jishumi, and other commentators associate the verse with his bone challenge; Ibn Jawzi's Nuzha identifies the human in Nahl, Maryam 19:67 and Ya Sin 36:77 as him. Some sources report this as an occasion, while Alusi says the familiar revelation report properly belongs to the end of Ya Sin. [16 notes: MUQATIL; MAWARDI-FULL(الكلبي); WAHIDI-ASBAB; WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(ابن عباس، والحسن); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(قيل); IBNJAWZI-NUZHA; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(المفسرون); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL(أبي بن خلف); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قوم); BURSEVI(المهدوي); ALUSI-FULL(الواحدي); STUDYQURAN(W)]
- 16:4/q02/p3 The disbelievers or idolaters who argue against God. — Al-Hasan calls him the polytheist; another report from al-Hasan through Ibn Salam speaks of unbelievers debating God's oneness and law. Islahi identifies the unnamed human with the idolaters under discussion and says withholding their name expresses aversion. [3 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(الحسن); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن البصري عن ابن سلام); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 16:4/q03 What does nuṭfah mean?  [نُّطْفَةٍۢ · meaning]
turns on: The word's range of Arabic usage and its intended sense in the creation statement.
- 16:4/q03/p1 A drop of water or clear, flowing liquid. — Samin connects it to naṭafa, to drip, as with water from a person's head; he cites laylah naṭūf for a rainy night and nāṭif for a flowing liquid. Alusi calls clear water its original sense. [4 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]
- 16:4/q03/p2 A man's seminal fluid. — Samin says this may be a euphemistic use; al-Qamus, cited by Bursevi, defines it directly as semen. Alusi says the clear-water term is used for male semen; Bursevi says common scholars deem semen impure, and Elmalili calls it lowly and weak. [5 notes: SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI(القاموس); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 16:4/q03/p3 A pearl, as an additional Arabic use. — Samin cites ṣabī munṭif for a boy with a pearl in his ear; he does not make this the verse's intended fluid. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 16:4/q04 How does min nuṭfah work grammatically?  [مِن نُّطْفَةٍۢ · grammar]
- 16:4/q04/p1 It depends on khalaqa, with min marking the beginning of the process. — Samin gives the attachment and the sense of the preposition. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 16:4/q05 How does fa-idhā present the jump from drop to disputant?  [فَإِذَا · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the abrupt expression primarily compresses time, uses the outcome as a designation, signals forgetfulness, or dramatizes a surprising moral reversal.
- 16:4/q05/p1 It presents the eventual adult state suddenly, while the intermediate stages are understood or encompassed by creation. — Abu Hayyan says the formative stages are left unstated; Samin reports ellipsis as one account and prefers taking khalaqa to cover creation and nurturing until the two qualities appear. Alusi says the sudden 'then' makes rational maturity the culmination and requires no missing stage. Samin also notes the clause's connection to its predecessor. [5 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 16:4/q05/p2 It uses the eventual state as a present designation. — Samin reports an analogy with 'I press wine' for grapes in 12:36. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]
- 16:4/q05/p3 The suddenness stresses how quickly a person forgets his origin. — Samin reports this explanation, and Bursevi says the fa signals rapid forgetting. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI]
- 16:4/q05/p4 It marks the surprising reversal from a humble origin to a gifted yet ungrateful opponent of God. — Ibn Ashur calls idhā a transferred metaphor for an outcome contrary to expectation: one should acknowledge the Creator and resurrection but disputes both; he cites 36:77–78. He says replacing it with fa-huwa or fa-kāna would lose the joint sense of admiration at eloquence and astonishment at disobedience. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 16:4/q06 What does khaṣīm mean and what form is it?  [خَصِيمٌۭ · meaning]
- 16:4/q06/p1 A strong or habitual disputant, one who argues with opponents. — Mawardi gives the basic sense; Abu Muslim stresses strength. Ibn Jawzi, Abu Hayyan, Samin, Bursevi, Alusi, Ibn Ashur and Tabatabai note argumentativeness or intensiveness. Wahidi's lexicographers, repeated by Razi and Alusi, explain faʿīl as an active participant like mufāʿil, comparing nasīb, ʿashīr, akīl, sharīb and Q 4:105; Qurtubi compares nasīb with munāsib. Wahidi and Razi report another derivation from khaṣama/yakhṣimu, supported by Hamza's reading at 36:49. Samin also allows the sense mukhāṣim with analogies khalīṭ and jalīs. Jishumi discusses related khaṣm as a verbal noun used for one or many and either sex, despite possible dual/plural forms and the noun/plural ambiguity of khiṣām. [16 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(أهل اللغة); JISHUMI(أبو مسلم); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(الواحدي); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الواحدي); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 16:4/q07 What does mubīn describe?  [مُّبِينٌۭ · meaning]
turns on: Whether mubīn highlights the visibility of contentiousness or the human's capacity to make thought and argument clear.
- 16:4/q07/p1 The human's contention is plain, evident, or openly displayed. — Wahidi calls the person plainly contentious; Ibn Jawzi says manifest in dispute; Qurtubi allows that a false case is made evident. Abu Hayyan and Bursevi give plain contentiousness as an alternative. Ibn Ashur parses mubīn as a second predicate of huwa rather than a mere adjective of khaṣīm. [6 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 16:4/q07/p2 The human clearly expresses an argument or what is within him through speech. — Tabari mentions articulated speech and argument with the tongue; Mawardi describes expression of the inner thought. Wahidi allows articulation even when false; Qurtubi gives speech as an alternative. Abu Hayyan and Bursevi mention making contention or proofs clear. Alusi says the argument displayed is taught to the human. Ibn Ashur says speech articulates intentions and truth or falsehood by many proofs, including sophistry, and makes mubīn a second predicate of huwa. [8 notes: TAB-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 16:4/q08 What is the force of the drop-to-disputant contrast?  [نُّطْفَةٍۢ خَصِيمٌۭ مُّبِينٌۭ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the passage's dominant context is proof and benefaction or the denunciation of unbelievers; some readings deliberately include both.
- 16:4/q08/p1 It displays God's power and favor in raising inert, weak fluid to articulate intellect and effective argument. — Mawardi and Wahidi's semanticists state the power reading; Ibn Atiyya names the two extremes and, in a broad reading, counts reason and speech as gifts. Ibn Jawzi says humans acquire strength for argument. Razi prefers advocacy with other humans because it fits the proof of a wise creator; Abu Hayyan reports some call the qualities praise, enabling discernment of truth and falsehood. Biqai names speech, understanding and analogical reasoning among human powers. Alusi prefers ability to reason in this blessing context. Elmalili speaks of self-defense; Zamakhshari as reported by Asad emphasizes clear, courageous argument; Asad says Razi supports this reading. Tabatabai admits the blessing reading. Baghawi's general reading includes divine power. [21 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(أهل المعاني); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قوم); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI(الإمام); ASAD-NOTES(Razi,Zamakhshari); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî); ALUSI-FULL+; RAZI-FULL+]
- 16:4/q08/p2 It rebukes a creature who forgets his low origin and uses God's gifts to deny Him, His unity, or resurrection and oppose His messengers. — Tabari and Yahya connect the forgotten origin with 36:77–78 and decayed bones. Al-Hasan, reported by Mawardi, calls it gross ingratitude; Wahidi's semanticists contrast disputation of divinity with the gift. Baghawi calls false argument and denial of evident blessings ugly. Ibn Jawzi argues the original creator can restore life. Razi reports this insolence reading but says it fits the creator-proof context less well. Qurtubi hears wonder at disputing God's power. Ibn Kathir says people are created to serve God and cites 25:54–55 and 36:77,79. Abu Hayyan prefers the rebuke because the surrounding and comparable passages, including 36:77 and 43:58, depict human blame. Alusi reports some favor it among unbelievers' offenses and Al-Kashf says it fits 'exalted above their associations'; Alusi himself argues the Ya Sin resurrection context differs. Ibn Ashur specifies defense of partners and rejection of monotheistic callers. Tabatabai prefers rebuke in context. Islahi adds hostility toward God and denial of resurrection tied to 50:3 and 16:27. Elmalili includes association of partners and logical or philosophical opposition. Study Quran's IK, Z and R stress denial of power, blessings and resurrection; IK cites 51:56 for the purpose of service. Kuranyolu says uniquely human freedom to defy God is itself a divine sign. [35 notes: TAB-FULL; YAHYA-SALLAM; MAWARDI-FULL(الحسن); WAHIDI-BASIT(أهل المعاني); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن البصري عن ابن سلام); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الكشف,بعضهم); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES(Zamakhshari); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(IK,IK, Z,R); ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; TABATABAI+; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 16:4/q08/p3 Both capacities are present: eloquent reason is a divine gift that can also become a means of defiance. — Asad explicitly calls the two readings complementary: reason can produce high achievement or error, with 36:77–78 illustrating misuse. Al-Kashf finds both suitable but says the audacity reading incorporates the blessing reading. Ibn Ashur says the sudden expression combines admiration at the rise to rational speech with shock at disobedience. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الكشف); IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES+]

## 16:4/q09 What does this transformation prove about God's action?  [خَلَقَ نُّطْفَةٍۢ خَصِيمٌۭ · theology]
- 16:4/q09/p1 Human formation and the rise of intellect require a wise, freely acting creator and show His power, oneness, and ability to restore life. — Razi's conditional argument says homogeneous sperm under necessary natural force would yield a sphere; if heterogeneous, its shifting parts could not reliably place brain, heart and other organs. He rejects joined simple spheres and uniform celestial influence; some physicians instead describe sperm as differentiated through four digestions in stomach, liver, veins and organs, with desire's heat liquefying their material. Razi contrasts the capable chick with the initially less perceptive human infant, whose later knowledge requires governance. Alusi's cited Imam repeats the body, celestial and soul arguments; Alusi says celestial efficacy is refuted elsewhere. Biqai says the transformation shows complete divine power and choice and that original creation implies resurrection; humans' sensory and rational powers reveal a Lord superior to the noblest body below. Elmalili stresses power over a weak drop; Ibn Ashur calls human creation a familiar yet wondrous proof of unique divinity. Tabatabai argues the unity of creation and serviceable blessings requires one governor. Kuranyolu pairs the heavens as great world with the human as small world and notes human agency itself. Abu Hayyan reports Razi's ranking of humans after celestial spheres but rejects the claim that spheres outrank humans. [21 notes: RAZI-FULL(الأطباء); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو عبد الله الرازي); BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî)]

## 16:4/q10 What occasion of revelation is reported for this verse?  [* · reports]
turns on: Whether the bone-challenge report establishes this verse's occasion or the occasion of the parallel in Yā Sīn.
- 16:4/q10/p1 Ubayy ibn Khalaf brought or crumbled a decayed bone, challenged the Prophet to say whether God could revive it, and this verse was revealed about that challenge. — Muqatil adds that Ubayy crushed and scattered the bone in the wind and was later killed by the Prophet at Uhud. Al-Kalbi, Wahidi, Ibn Abbas and al-Hasan through Jishumi, Baghawi, Ibn Atiyya, Ibn Jawzi, Qurtubi, Baydawi, al-Mahdawi through Bursevi, Alusi's report from Wahidi and Study Quran repeat the attribution with minor wording differences. Several connect the same challenge to 36:77–78. [16 notes: MUQATIL; MAWARDI-FULL(الكلبي); WAHIDI-ASBAB; WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(ابن عباس، والحسن); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(قيل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(المفسرون); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL(أبي بن خلف); BURSEVI(المهدوي); ALUSI-FULL(الواحدي); STUDYQURAN(W)]
- 16:4/q10/p2 The well-known report assigns revelation for Ubayy's bone challenge to the ending of Yā Sīn, not this verse. — Alusi distinguishes the familiar revelation account from this verse, while accepting that Wahidi reports the challenge and its parallel. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 16:4/q11 What does a prophetic report add about the child of Adam?  [* · reports]
- 16:4/q11/p1 After God makes and forms a person from a drop, he struts, amasses and withholds wealth, then offers charity only when death is imminent. — Ibn Kathir transmits the address to Adam's child from Busr ibn Jihash from the Prophet; its late charity is too late. [1 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(بسر بن جحاش عن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 16:4/q12 How does this verse relate to the Ya Sin passage about resurrection?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether parallel wording establishes the same interpretive emphasis despite differing local contexts.
- 16:4/q12/p1 Ya Sin 36:77–79 echoes or explicates the drop, disputation, forgotten origin, and challenge to revive bones. — Yahya, Tabari and Qurtubi invoke 36:77–78 for the human's forgotten creation and resurrection denial. Wahidi says 36:77 concerns the same incident; Ibn Jawzi's Nuzha compares Nahl, Maryam 19:67 and Ya Sin. Ibn Kathir also uses 36:79 for the Creator's capacity to revive. Ibn Ashur says Ya Sin makes denial of unity and resurrection explicit. Study Quran notes the wording echoed at 36:77. [12 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-ASBAB; WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); IBNJAWZI-NUZHA; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI(المهدوي); IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 16:4/q12/p2 The Ya Sin parallel does not decide which sense dominates this verse, because its immediate context concerns resurrection denial. — Alusi rejects using 36:78 as decisive for the audacity reading here; he contrasts Ya Sin's resurrection setting with this passage's blessing context. He also says the famous occasion report belongs to Ya Sin's end. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)-]

## 16:4/q13 How does human creation fit the surrounding argument in al-Nahl?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the local emphasis is the sequence of proofs and blessings or the warning about human resistance; the notes also allow both functions.
- 16:4/q13/p1 It follows cosmic creation as a second, more familiar proof of God's oneness, wisdom, and power, before the passage turns to other created benefits. — Razi moves from celestial bodies to the human, which he calls the noblest body after the heavens, and reads body and soul as paired signs. Abu Hayyan contrasts an external cosmic proof with proof from one's own creation. Burhan describes a sequence of heavens, humans, livestock and plants, with the diverse plants prompting reflection on a powerful free agent. Biqai calls human creation especially strong evidence. Ibn Ashur says the proof resumes divine uniqueness after visible cosmic creation. Kuranyolu calls heavens the great world and humans the small world, and says verses 1–18 show existence, unity, creative power and blessings after rejecting partners. Tabatabai links creation to a single Lord who governs beneficial things. [8 notes: RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 16:4/q13/p2 It advances the opening warning against association and denial by showing how the intended servant turns against God. — Qurtubi says the proof of oneness turns to human excess. Islahi says verses 1–9 warn Quraysh of approaching punishment, reject partners, affirm accountability for purposeful creation and direct people to God's path; the unnamed idolater here anticipates the challenge over God's associates at 16:27. Abu Hayyan says humans should obey rather than quarrel. [4 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 16:4/q14 How can the next verse's livestock be connected syntactically with this one?  [خَلَقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether al-anʿām is coordinated with al-insān under the earlier verb or starts the next sentence.
- 16:4/q14/p1 Al-anʿām can be coordinated with al-insān under khalaqa, making livestock's formation from a drop a parallel proof. — Ibn Ashur says khalaqahā then opens a new sentence of benefaction; he prefers this parsing because it balances reflection on creation and an allusion to ingratitude. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL+]
- 16:4/q14/p2 Al-anʿām begins the next sentence, directly stating the livestock's creation as benefaction. — Ibn Ashur reports this second parsing; reflection on creation follows by implication through emphasis, and he says neither parsing asserts exclusivity. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 16:4/q15 How does the creation sequence here compare with al-Zumar?  [* · links]
- 16:4/q15/p1 Al-Nahl moves from human creation to livestock made for people; al-Zumar first mentions Adam and Eve, then descendants and provisions. — Samarrai makes this order comparison and cites 16:3–5. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 16:4/q16 What inward meaning is drawn from the human's dispute?  [* · inward]
- 16:4/q16/p1 God grants knowledge and power from lifeless semen, yet a human wrongly claims independent existence and a share in being and acts. — Bursevi reports this from al-Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya; it frames the human as the Creator's adversary. [1 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]

## 16:4/q17 What is said about the purity of the fluid associated with human origin?  [نُّطْفَةٍۢ · law]
turns on: The general ruling on semen versus a reported special quality of the Prophet's bodily discharges.
- 16:4/q17/p1 The common scholarly view treats semen as impure. — Bursevi cites this view and a Persian couplet challenging pride in that origin. [1 notes: BURSEVI]
- 16:4/q17/p2 The Prophet's bodily discharges are pure as a special quality, and semen has a lighter ruling than those discharges. — Bursevi relays this from Insan al-ʿUyun. [1 notes: BURSEVI(إنسان العيون)]

## 16:4/q18 Who is the speaker of khalaqa?  [خَلَقَ · interpretation]
- 16:4/q18/p1 God speaks of His own creation of the human: 'I created.' — Ibn Abbas glosses the verb in the first person as 'I created.' [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس)]
