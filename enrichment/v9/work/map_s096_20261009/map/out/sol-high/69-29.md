# 69:29: 14 questions

## 69:29/q01 What does هَلَكَ mean here?  [هَلَكَ · meaning]
- 69:29/q01/p1 It has vanished or disappeared; its perishing means that it no longer accompanies or benefits him. — Al-Damghani explicitly gives 'be lost/disappear'; al-Burhan glosses it as vanishing. Ibn Ashur calls the perishing metaphorical because the former authority no longer benefits him on this day. Islahi links the sense of disappearance to the following preposition. Al-Khattabi's reported objection is that halak properly applies to concrete things rather than abstractions. [4 notes: DAMGHANI; BURHAN; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KHATTABI-]

## 69:29/q02 Why say that his authority has perished?  [هَلَكَ سُلْطَٰنِيَهْ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether figurative halak can be applied to an abstraction and what force it adds.
- 69:29/q02/p1 Perishing is a forceful metaphor for a final, irreversible loss of authority. — Al-Khattabi answers the objection to applying halak to an abstraction: Arabic allows figurative usage, as in the idiom of a man 'walking' through abundant offspring and the sheep not 'walking' on the wolf; metaphor may be stronger than literal speech, as with stripping the night of day (36:37) and proclaiming a command (15:94). Destruction is stronger than mere departure, which might be reversed. Ibn Ashur says the authority no longer benefits or accompanies him. The reported objection says halak is for concrete things, not authority or knowledge. [4 notes: KHATTABI; IBNASHUR-FULL; KHATTABI-]

## 69:29/q03 What does عَنِّى add to هَلَكَ?  [هَلَكَ عَنِّى · grammar]
- 69:29/q03/p1 It gives the verb the additional sense that the authority went away and is absent from him. — Ibn Ashur says halaka is construed with an because it implies absence; Islahi likewise takes the preposition to make the verb mean going away or disappearing. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 69:29/q04 What is the lost سُلْطَٰنِيَهْ?  [سُلْطَٰنِيَهْ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether sulṭān is the force of an argument, a ruler's dominion, anyone's control of self, or a wider worldly standing; several authors test whether the reading applies to every condemned person or only rulers.
- 69:29/q04/p1 His proof or argument has failed and cannot defend him. — Ibn Abbas says every proof is gone and useless, specifically the argument formerly raised against Muhammad; Asad applies it to arguments against resurrection and judgment. Muqatil says bodily limbs testify to his association of partners with God, undoing his defense; Study Quran cites 24:24, 36:65, 41:20, and 67:10-11 for bodily testimony and confession. Al-Biqai includes specious arguments used by his tongue and helpers to dominate callers to God. Al-Damghani says sulṭān means proof also in passages concerning Moses (6:81, 27:21). Al-Raghib, quoted by Bursevi, says an argument is called sulṭān because its force reaches hearts, especially learned believers. Abu Hayyan and Bursevi favor this scope because the left-hand record and damnation apply to all the wretched, not merely kings; Al-Alusi favors it while conceding that subsequent words may fit kingship. Al-Wahidi notes that recognition comes too late for regret to help. [40 notes: MUQATIL; TUSTARI; TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,الضحاك,عكرمة,مجاهد); KHATTABI(قيل); THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL(مجاهد وعكرمة والسدي والضحاك); WAHIDI-BASIT(الأكثرون,عطاء عن ابن عباس,مقاتل); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; DAMGHANI; BAGHAWI-FULL(أكثر المفسرين); KASHSHAF-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNATIYYA-FULL(عكرمة ومجاهد); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(مجاهد، عكرمة، الضحاك، السدي); RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس,مقاتل); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس، مجاهد، عكرمة، السدي، الضحاك); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL(ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس، مجاهد، الضحاك، عكرمة، السدي); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,عكرمة,مجاهد); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(ابن عباس,الراغب); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس ومجاهد والضحاك وعكرمة والسدي); ELMALILI; STUDYQURAN(Q, R, Ṭ); ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; ALUSI-FULL+; ASAD-NOTES(Ibn Abbas, Ikrimah, Mujahid, Ad-Dahhak)+; BURSEVI+]
- 69:29/q04/p2 His worldly kingship, political command, or power over other people has disappeared. — Ibn Zayd says rulers previously obeyed by companions lose their rule and strength; al-Rabi says he commanded followers and felt invulnerable. Al-Kashshaf, al-Nasafi, al-Razi, Bursevi, and al-Alusi describe the result as poverty and humiliation. Al-Razi adds that kingship had enabled his dispute with the truthful and now only its penalty remains. Al-Biqai says wealth had gained him kingship but cannot preserve it, and its supporters depart. Elmalili pictures unjust rulers and wealthy people who relied on dominion and punished others while imagining themselves immune. Islahi says the authority had made him arrogant and heedless of judgment. Ibn Kathir says neither worldly standing nor wealth protects him and he has no helper. Al-Raghib's lexical range includes coercive mastery, power, and its possessor. Abu Hayyan objects that this cannot describe all the damned; Qatada likewise says they were not all village rulers. Bursevi and Al-Alusi prefer the argument reading for its wider scope. [29 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن زيد); THALABI(ابن زيد); MAWARDI-FULL(الربيع بن أنس); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن زيد,الربيع); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن زيد); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن زيد); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن زيد); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن زيد); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(الراغب); ALUSI-FULL(قيل); ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN; IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن زيد)+; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; BURSEVI-; ALUSI-FULL(قتادة)-]
- 69:29/q04/p3 It is his ordinary control over himself, his body, faculties, religion, or livelihood, now lost. — Qatada says God gave everyone power over their own bodies and commanded obedience while forbidding disobedience; the speaker used bodily control for sin, so this reading does not require him to have been a ruler. Al-Hasan extends a person's dominion to self, religion, and livelihood; al-Wahidi concludes that he now has no control over himself. Bursevi says he can no longer employ powers and limbs in worship after resurrection. Study Quran describes the lost control as inability to govern one's destiny. [9 notes: TAB-FULL(قتادة); MAWARDI-FULL(قتادة); WAHIDI-BASIT(الحسن); DURR-FULL(قتادة); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(قتادة); STUDYQURAN]
- 69:29/q04/p4 It is each person's worldly standing and condition, including numbers and resources, rather than a king's office alone. — Ibn Atiyya explicitly prefers the authority belonging to every person in his worldly condition, numbers, and resources; he cites the Prophet's rule against leading a man in his domain or taking his seat without permission to illustrate ordinary standing. Ibn Kathir says worldly standing cannot help him. Kuranyolu includes property, status, and power among what disappears. Elmalili includes the possessions and government in which he trusted; Study Quran calls it authority or warrant over one's destiny. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNKATHIR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ELMALILI; STUDYQURAN; IBNATIYYA-FULL+]

## 69:29/q05 What is the final هْ in سُلْطَٰنِيَهْ?  [سُلْطَٰنِيَهْ · grammar]
- 69:29/q05/p1 It is hāʾ al-sakt, a pause consonant appended to the underlying form ending in -iya. — Al-Nahhas treats it like the ending of hisabiyah (69:26). Al-Burhan says pause hāʾ preserves the vowel of a built ending and is normally deleted in connected speech; the codex spelling supports stopping on it. Al-Itqan says it is appended at verse endings such as maliyeh, sultaniyeh, and ma hiya. Elmalili says the underlying form ends in -iya and the hāʾ after fatḥa is read with sukūn at pause; he compares kitabiyah, hisabiyah, and maliyah. [5 notes: NAHHAS; BURHAN; ITQAN; ELMALILI]

## 69:29/q06 How is the final هْ read in pause and connection?  [سُلْطَٰنِيَهْ · readings]
turns on: Whether the pause hāʾ remains pronounced during true connection, is omitted there, or is treated as a pause with only a brief interval; reader attributions differ across the listed forms.
- 69:29/q06/p1 Retain the hāʾ both at pause and in connected recitation. — Ibn Mujahid attributes retention in the listed forms to Ibn Kathir, Nafi, Asim, Abu Amr, and Ibn Amir; al-Kisai retains it here though omitting it in lam yatasannah and iqtadih. Al-Jishumi, Ibn al-Jazari, Abu Hayyan, and al-Baydawi describe retention by other or most readers, with pause retention agreed. Abu Hayyan rejects al-Zuhrawi's claim that connected retention is erroneous: the transmitted reading is mass-transmitted and accepted. Al-Burhan says connected retention can reflect an intended pause. [9 notes: IBNMUJAHID(ابن كثير ونافع وعاصم وأبو عمرو وابن عامر,الكسائي); JISHUMI(حمزة ويعقوب); BAYDAWI-FULL(حمزة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); BURHAN(حمزة); NASHR(حمزة، ويعقوب); ABUHAYYAN-FULL+]
- 69:29/q06/p2 Omit the hāʾ in connected recitation but retain it when stopping. — Ibn Mujahid attributes the connected omission in the listed forms to Hamza and says all keep it at pause; al-Kisai omits only in two other forms, thus retaining it here. Al-Jishumi and Ibn al-Jazari include Yaqub with Hamza for maliyah, sultaniyah, and ma hiyah. Ibn Ashur names Hamza and Khalaf. Abu Hayyan also names Ibn Abi Ishaq and al-A'mash for connected omission and says Hamza selects particular forms. Al-Burhan reports Hamza's three-word omission while distinguishing kitabiyah and hisabiyah. Qiraat-ER likewise records omission in connection and retention at pause. [9 notes: IBNMUJAHID(حمزة); JISHUMI(حمزة ويعقوب); BAYDAWI-FULL(حمزة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURHAN(حمزة); NASHR(حمزة، ويعقوب); IBNASHUR-FULL(حمزة وخلف); QIRAAT-ER(Cs)]
- 69:29/q06/p3 Ibn Muhaysin deletes the hāʾ and makes the yāʾ silent. — Abu Hayyan distinguishes Ibn Muhaysin's form from the omission only in connected speech ascribed to Ibn Abi Ishaq and al-A'mash. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 69:29/q06/p4 Stop on the hāʾ, or make an intentional light pause, when using the written pause ending. — Al-Burhan says true connection would require deletion by Arabic grammar whereas stopping honors grammar and codex spelling; he interprets reported connection as reciting with pausal intention and a brief interval. Al-Sakhawi prefers stopping because the hāʾ was added for pause. Ibn al-Jazari follows al-Dani in making a light pause under tahqiq while notionally connecting. This recommendation differs from a claim that transmitted connected retention is invalid, which Abu Hayyan rejects. [5 notes: BURHAN; NASHR(أبو الحسن السخاوي)+]

## 69:29/q07 How is the junction of مَالِيَهْ and هَلَكَ recited?  [هَلَكَ · readings]
turns on: Whether the first hāʾ remains a separate pause consonant, merges with the verb's hāʾ under true connection, or is deleted.
- 69:29/q07/p1 Keep the two hāʾ sounds distinct with iẓhār and a light pause. — Makki reports reading with izhar and calls it correct even though he argues that naql in kitabiyah would imply assimilation here. Al-Sakhawi prefers izhar if connected in intent, because the pause hāʾ and following initial hāʾ remain separate. Ibn al-Jazari follows al-Dani: under tahqiq make a light pause on the first hāʾ while notionally connecting. Abu Shama says izhar needs a slight pause and otherwise becomes an unrecognized pause. Al-Alusi calls Abu Amr's reported assimilation weak because the pause hāʾ must be distinct. [4 notes: NASHR(أبو شامة); NASHR(أبو الحسن السخاوي,مكي)+]
- 69:29/q07/p2 Assimilate the pause hāʾ into the initial hāʾ of هَلَكَ in true connection. — Makki argues that naql in kitabiyah treats its hāʾ as original and would require assimilation in maliyah halaka, although he himself reads izhar. Abu Shama says true connection must assimilate or vocalize the sound; Ibn al-Jazari prefers assimilation with ilqa because the hāʾ is then treated as original. Qiraat-ER describes a geminated consonant. Al-Alusi reports assimilation from Abu Amr but calls it weak; he says an attribution to Warsh is corrected by the report of naql in kitabiyah, not assimilation here. [4 notes: NASHR(أبو شامة,مكي); QIRAAT-ER(Assim); NASHR+; ALUSI-FULL(أبو عمرو,ورش)-]
- 69:29/q07/p3 Delete the pause hāʾ from مَالِيَهْ and connect directly to هَلَكَ. — Qiraat-ER describes direct connection without pause, so maliyah flows syntactically into the following verb rather than standing as an independent exclamation. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER(Cs)]

## 69:29/q08 What effect do the hāʾ endings have in this judgment scene?  [سُلْطَٰنِيَهْ · rhetoric]
- 69:29/q08/p1 Their sound evokes exhausted people's sighs, hardship, and pain during the long judgment ordeal. — Al-Samarrai links the endings in the resurrection scene, including 69:19 and 69:26, to psychological exhaustion and the long severe wait for intercession, citing also 70:4, 74:9, and 76:10 across the two notes. [2 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR-MUH; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 69:29/q09 Whom is the speaker said to represent?  [* · referent]
turns on: Whether named occasion reports identify a specific man or the lament is read as representing a broader class; arguments about all condemned persons bear on the scope.
- 69:29/q09/p1 A report identifies him with Abu Jahl ibn Hisham. — Al-Mawardi transmits this identification without further grounds. [1 notes: MAWARDI-FULL]
- 69:29/q09/p2 The verse was revealed concerning al-Aswad ibn Abd al-Asad. — Al-Mawardi attributes this to al-Dahhak; al-Kashshaf and al-Alusi attribute it to Ibn Abbas. [3 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(الضحاك); KASHSHAF-FULL(ابن عباس); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 69:29/q09/p3 It portrays wealthy Quraysh leaders and other rulers who relied on worldly power. — Bursevi mentions leaders such as al-Walid, whose worldly standing is exposed; Elmalili describes rulers who oppressed others and trusted that their own wealth and rule would protect them. [2 notes: BURSEVI; ELMALILI]
- 69:29/q09/p4 It applies to the condemned generally, including people who never ruled a town. — Qatada argues that not every person entering Hell was a village ruler, since all had control of their bodies; Abu Hayyan says Ibn Abbas's proof reading covers all the damned, while Bursevi says the left-hand record belongs to all the wretched, and al-Alusi weighs the same issue. [5 notes: TAB-FULL(قتادة); DURR-FULL(قتادة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(قتادة)]

## 69:29/q10 What is reported about ʿAḍud al-Dawla and this verse?  [* · reports]
- 69:29/q10/p1 After boasting of his power and mastery of fate in verse, the ruler went mad and could utter or repeat only this lament. — Al-Kashshaf calls him Fannā Khusraw or al-ʿAḍud and says his poem boasted of power; Abu Hayyan calls him ʿAbd al-Dawla ibn Nuwayh, styled King of Kings, and says he failed and went mad. Al-Alusi transmits al-Thaʿalibi's detail that he recited this verse at death. Elmalili says the poem celebrated wine, singers, and a drinking gathering, closed by naming him ʿAḍud al-Dawla, king of kings and vanquisher of fate; soon afterward he went mad and died repeating 69:28-29. Elmalili leaves the state after death to God and points to the reversal of a self-styled conqueror of fate crushed by fate. The accounts vary in the rendering of his name and in whether he uttered the fragment, this verse, or the two-verse lament. [7 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الثعالبي); ELMALILI(Müfessirler)]

## 69:29/q11 What does the loss of authority teach or express in this lament?  [* · interpretation]
- 69:29/q11/p1 Worldly means on which he relied have failed; he is helpless and his regret can no longer help him. — Al-Wahidi says he understands only when regret is useless; understanding while worldly power remained could have helped. Ibn Kathir says neither wealth nor status shields him from punishment and no helper rescues him. Al-Biqai says wealth had secured authority but neither now helps. Al-Tabatabai says he worked for wealth and power as keys to happiness, safety, and desire while turning from God and truth; on the Day wealth and children are useless. Kuranyolu says only good or bad belief and deeds remain when wealth, sovereignty, and status go. Study Quran says he regrets failing to prepare for the Hereafter and cannot control his fate. Islahi says the authority had made him arrogant and heedless of judgment. Elmalili points to powerful oppressors who denied that punishment could reach them. [9 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ELMALILI]

## 69:29/q12 How does the neighboring expression mā shape this lament?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether mā is taken as interrogative or negative.
- 69:29/q12/p1 As a question, it expresses yearning and longing. — Islahi says mā can be interrogative and prefers this reading for the emotional force it gives the speaker's lament in the preceding line. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR+]
- 69:29/q12/p2 As a negation, it denies that the preceding wealth helped. — Islahi acknowledges the negative construal of mā as the alternative to the question. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 69:29/q13 How does the verse fit the surrounding judgment scene?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes supply different links to the preceding wealth lament and to the paired portraits of the blessed and wretched.
- 69:29/q13/p1 The loss of wealth leads to the loss of the authority gained through it; neither remains useful. — Al-Biqai says wealth had been a means to power but cannot preserve that power now; supporters are farther away. Al-Tabatabai says the speaker had trusted wealth and power as worldly means of security, but wealth and children cannot help on this Day. Ibn Kathir says neither wealth nor standing wards off punishment; Elmalili's story repeats both 69:28 and 69:29. [6 notes: BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 69:29/q13/p2 The wretched person's grief mirrors the blessed person's earlier joy before their different food and treatment are described. — Al-Razi observes that the blessed were first shown in joy, good living, eating, and drinking; the wretched are likewise introduced through grief before restraint and food. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 69:29/q14 How does this example of failed authority relate to the wider account of disbelief?  [* · interpretation]
- 69:29/q14/p1 Disbelief is broader than explicit denial; helpless wealth, followers, authority, gods, and intercessors show why one instance can be called denial. — Al-Samarrai compares 3:10 on wealth and children with 14:21, 11:101, 53:26, and 8:54 on other failed sources of help. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
