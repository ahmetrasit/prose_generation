# 84:14: 19 questions

## 84:14/q01 Who is the person called 'he'?  [إِنَّهُۥ · referent]
- 84:14/q01/p1 The disbeliever who receives his record behind his back; Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies this group as polytheists. — Al-Ṭabarī identifies him by the record handed behind his back; al-Zajjāj calls him the disbeliever; Ibn ʿĀshūr contrasts these polytheists with right-hand recipients, the believers (84:7–15). Qāshānī portrays him as veiled from the giver of his blessings; Study Quran associates him with the heedless disbelievers (83:31). [9 notes: TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; QASHANI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN; BURSEVI]

## 84:14/q02 What does the pronoun in 'indeed he' convey?  [إِنَّهُۥ · rhetoric]
- 84:14/q02/p1 It can belittle the person it denotes. — Al-Zarkashī compares pronouns used of Satan as an evident enemy, of Satan and his tribe seeing humans unseen, and of this person who assumes he will not return. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 84:14/q03 What does ẓanna mean here?  [ظَنَّ · meaning]
turns on: Whether ẓann retains its ordinary sense of supposition or denotes certainty.
- 84:14/q03/p1 He reckoned or supposed that he would not return. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya says ẓanna keeps its ordinary meaning. Ibn al-Jawzī distinguishes ẓann, in which one of two possibilities predominates, from doubt, in which they are equal; he glosses this occurrence as ḥasiba and cites 41:22. He also notes tazannī from tazannun for conjecture, ẓanūn for scant goodness, maẓinna for an accustomed place, and ẓinna for accusation. Damghānī gives reckoning or supposing as a sense, and Fatḥ al-Raḥmān explicitly denies that it means certainty. [6 notes: DAMGHANI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أهل التفسير); IBNATIYYA-FULL; BURSEVI(فتح الرحمن); BURSEVI(الفناري)-]
- 84:14/q03/p2 It means certainty. — Bursevī reports al-Fanārī's interpretation from his Tafsīr al-Fātiḥa. Fatḥ al-Raḥmān instead takes it in the ordinary sense of supposition. [1 notes: BURSEVI(الفناري); BURSEVI(فتح الرحمن)-]

## 84:14/q04 How does 'an' and its clause function after ẓanna?  [أَن ظَنَّ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the clause occupies both objects of ẓanna or only one.
- 84:14/q04/p1 Lightened an and its clause fill both object positions of ẓanna. — Al-Naḥḥās assigns the clause to the two objects. Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls an the lightened form of inna and says what follows supplies both objects. Al-Samīn compares 75:3 and reports the grammatical dispute. [3 notes: NAHHAS; IBNATIYYA-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]
- 84:14/q04/p2 The clause fills one object position. — Al-Samīn reports this as the alternative grammatical analysis of lightened an, alongside the two-object analysis, comparing 75:3. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 84:14/q05 How is 'an lan' written in this verse?  [أَن لَّن · readings]
- 84:14/q05/p1 The two words are written separately. — Ibn al-Jazarī says an lan is separate wherever it occurs, including 84:14; he names 18:48 and 75:3 as the joined exceptions. [1 notes: NASHR]

## 84:14/q06 What force does 'lan' add to the person's expectation?  [لَّن · rhetoric]
- 84:14/q06/p1 Its emphatic negation expresses the denier's firm certainty that he will not return to life. — Ibn ʿĀshūr explicitly attributes the force of certainty to lan. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 84:14/q07 What did he believe would not happen when he would not 'yaḥūr'?  [لَّن يَحُورَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the denied return is specified as resurrection before God, a reversal of worldly fortune, or a supposition about God's knowledge.
- 84:14/q07/p1 He believed he would not return to God or rise after death for judgment and recompense. — Muqātil, Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatāda, Mujāhid, Sufyān's lexical gloss, and later exegetes give return, resurrection, or both. Al-Ṭabarī adds that he therefore expected neither reward nor punishment; al-Qurṭubī spells out accounting, reward, and punishment. Al-Ṭabrisī locates the belief in earthly life. Qāshānī says he believed only time governs life and death. Al-Rāghib, reported by al-Samīn and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ, specifies return to life or resurrection; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ calls this a context-fixed Islamic sense. Al-Farrāʾ and al-Ṭabrisī locate the return with God; Ibn ʿĀshūr cites other Quranic return and restoration passages (10:23; 86:8). [38 notes: MUQATIL; FARRA; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,قتادة,مجاهد); ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS(ابن عباس,مجاهد); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس ومقاتل); DAMGHANI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI(مقاتل); QURTUBI-FULL; QASHANI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الراغب); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس، قتادة); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,قتادة,مجاهد); ITQAN; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الراغب,الفراء,القرطبي); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 84:14/q07/p2 He assumed his happy condition would never reverse into grief, death, punishment, or accountability. — Elmalılı reads ḥawr as reversal from completion to loss and enumerates the feared outcomes that the man discounts. [1 notes: ELMALILI]
- 84:14/q07/p3 The supposition can also include that God does not know much of what people do. — Damghānī gives this as an alternative formulation of what the person reckons. [1 notes: DAMGHANI]

## 84:14/q08 What does the verb yaḥūr mean and what usage supports it?  [يَحُورَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether Labīd's poetic use describes a return to ash or becoming ash; the verse itself is explained through return.
- 84:14/q08/p1 It means to return, turn back, or retrace one's steps; the root also describes repeated movement or return to a former state. — Al-Ṭabarī, al-Naḥḥās, al-Wāḥidī, al-Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn, and Ibn ʿĀshūr give the return sense. Al-Wāḥidī adds maḥār, the destination. Labīd's meteor returns to ashes after shining; Abū ʿUbayda also cites al-Munakhkhal's wa-lā taḥūrī. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Ibn ʿAbbās cite a Bedouin woman's ḥūrī to her daughter. Al-Qurṭubī compares ḥawārī bread restored to whiteness; Bursevī compares the fuller restoring cloth. Al-Rāghib describes back-and-forth movement in a thing or thought; al-Samīn cites water circulating in a pool, perplexity in an affair, the rotating pulley axle, and returning speech. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ distinguishes h-w-r from h-y-r, notes this form occurs only here, and relates dialogue in 18:34, 18:37 and taḥāwur in 58:1, with derivatives for returning speech, an axle, helper, echo, and round eyes. Ibn ʿĀshūr says return to a former place extends metaphorically to a former condition and Quranic restoration (10:23; 86:8). Elmalılı emphasizes decline from improvement. [35 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(ابن زيد,سفيان); ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيدة,لبيد); IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الراغب); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس، قتادة); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,لبيد); ITQAN; BURSEVI(ابن عباس); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن عباس,الزمخشري)]
- 84:14/q08/p2 In Labīd's line, yaḥūr may mean 'become' rather than literally 'return'. — Al-Samīn reports grammarians taking the verb like ṣāra, supported by Labīd's image of a bright meteor becoming ash. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ prefers al-Ṭūsī's 'become' for that poem, while distinguishing the Quranic afterlife sense. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الطوسي)+]

## 84:14/q09 Is yaḥūr Arabic or described as Abyssinian?  [يَحُورَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the word is treated as Arabic on poetic and spoken evidence, Abyssinian by transmitted gloss, or a shared form.
- 84:14/q09/p1 It is an Arabic root meaning return, attested in speech and poetry; the languages may share the form. — Al-Wāḥidī, al-Qurṭubī, and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ adduce Labīd and the Bedouin woman's ḥūrī. Al-Qurṭubī allows that Arabic and Abyssinian share the derived word. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ distinguishes Arabic h-w-r from h-y-r, and Ibn ʿAbbās's Labīd citation is presented as Arabic evidence. [10 notes: TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيدة,لبيد); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس,عكرمة وداود بن أبي هند); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,لبيد); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن عباس)]
- 84:14/q09/p2 It is also identified as an Abyssinian word or gloss for 'return'. — ʿIkrima, Dāwūd ibn Abī Hind, and one Ibn ʿAbbās report identify the Abyssinian usage; an ʿIkrima example means 'go back to your family.' Al-Qurṭubī allows a shared derivation. Al-Suyūṭī notes the word in Ibn ʿAbbās's answers to Nāfiʿ ibn al-Azraq; his wider survey assembled over a hundred foreign-origin Quranic terms, combining 27 versified by Ibn al-Subkī, 24 added by Ibn Ḥajar, and sixty-odd of his own, including Salsabīl, Ṭā Hā, Sijjīl, and Kāfūr. [9 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(عكرمة وداود بن أبي هند); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,عكرمة); ITQAN(داود بن هند); BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن عباس)]

## 84:14/q10 What does the supplication against ḥawr after kawr mean?  [يَحُورَ · meaning]
turns on: Which sort of reversal the prayer's ḥawr specifies: religious relapse, general decline, or vacillation.
- 84:14/q10/p1 Seeking refuge from returning to disbelief after faith. — Al-Ṭabarī explains the prayer attributed to the Prophet this way; al-Naḥḥās reports the same explanation as one opinion. Al-Ṭabarī states no grading for the report. [3 notes: TAB-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); NAHHAS(قيل)]
- 84:14/q10/p2 Seeking refuge from decline after increase or from a good state to a worse one. — Al-Naḥḥās reports the prophetic supplication and this alternative explanation. Ibn ʿAṭiyya explains ḥawr as retracing one's steps. Bursevī says return from a good state; Elmalılı cites the supplication as a reversal from improvement to loss. [6 notes: NAHHAS(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,قيل); IBNATIYYA-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI]
- 84:14/q10/p3 Seeking refuge from vacillation after firm progress. — Al-Samīn gives this reading of the saying and associates the root with back-and-forth motion. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 84:14/q11 Can yaḥūr function grammatically like ṣāra in Labīd's verse?  [يَحُورَ · grammar]
turns on: The parsing of ramādan in the cited poem.
- 84:14/q11/p1 Yes; it can govern a nominative subject and an accusative predicate. — Al-Samīn reports grammarians using Labīd's line as evidence, with ramādan as predicate. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]
- 84:14/q11/p2 The poetic ramādan can instead be a circumstantial accusative. — Al-Samīn reports the competing parse, which leaves yaḥūr with its return sense. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 84:14/q12 How does this verse explain the punishment described around it?  [* · links]
- 84:14/q12/p1 The person's denial of return explains his fate in the blaze and his hard reckoning. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the assertion explains why the person given his book behind his back burns in Hell; inna carries the explanatory force otherwise expressed by a fāʾ. Al-Biqāʿī links complacent pleasure in sins to hard reckoning and a sorrowful return. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]

## 84:14/q13 How does the next verse answer his expectation?  [* · links]
- 84:14/q13/p1 Balā affirms that he will return, and God's sight or prior knowledge exposes and threatens him. — Al-Farrāʾ, al-Baghawī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Ṭabrisī, and Bursevī read balā as correcting the negated belief. Al-Baghawī and al-Ṭabrisī say God watched him from creation; al-Ṭabrisī reports al-Zajjāj saying God knew him before creation and knew his return. Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls the response a threat. Al-Zarkashī reports that many readers and linguists prefer stopping at balā in ten such occurrences because it answers the prior statement independently, though some begin there. [7 notes: FARRA; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(الزجاج); BURSEVI; BURHAN+]

## 84:14/q14 How is disbelief in return connected to pleasure and sin?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes describe both the effect of denying return on conduct and the effect of sin on belief.
- 84:14/q14/p1 Expecting no return or recompense lets a person pursue worldly pleasure, sin, and heedlessness. — Al-Ṭabarī says he neither hoped for reward nor feared punishment; al-Ṭabrisī says he violated prohibitions; Qāshānī says blessings veiled their giver and he trusted time; Bursevī explains his joy by denial of resurrection. Al-Biqāʿī says he enjoys wrongdoing without grieving, Study Quran calls the joy worldly heedlessness, and Iṣlāḥī says he expects no share in the Hereafter. Jishumī condemns restricting aspirations to this life as ending in regret. [8 notes: TAB-FULL; JISHUMI; TABRISI; QASHANI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 84:14/q14/p2 Immersion in sin turns a person away from the afterlife and makes resurrection seem unlikely. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī explicitly states this causal direction while interpreting return to the Lord for accounting and recompense. [1 notes: TABATABAI]

## 84:14/q15 How does this verse fit the sūra and its predecessor?  [* · links]
- 84:14/q15/p1 It belongs to the division of humanity by its return to God and continues the prior sūra's contrast of reward and punishment. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says 84:7–15 details the return announced in 84:6: believers receive records in their right hands, polytheists behind their backs. Al-Biqāʿī says al-Inshiqāq continues al-Muṭaffifīn's ending, where allies receive reward and enemies punishment after denying resurrection and judgment (83:1). [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]

## 84:14/q16 Why does the verse end with yaḥūr rather than yaḥūrā?  [يَحُورَ · rhetoric]
- 84:14/q16/p1 The longer ending would change the meaning; the verse keeps the meaningful form despite the surrounding sound pattern. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī says Quranic endings put meaning first while allowing matching sounds where suitable. He cites departures from recurring rhyme in 20:78 and 21:66, then identifies 84:14 as a case where yaḥūrā would alter meaning. [5 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR-MUH; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 84:14/q17 How were the seven reading schools chosen, and do they exhaust transmitted readings?  [* · readings]
- 84:14/q17/p1 They are a later practical selection of trusted regional readers, while other valid transmitted readings remain. — Al-Suyūṭī relays Makkī: early lists had many readers and regional traditions; around 200 AH named schools prevailed, and Ibn Mujāhid later substituted al-Kisāʾī for Yaʿqūb. Ibn Jubayr had earlier chosen five corresponding to five confirmed ʿUthmānic codices; two alleged further codices to Yemen and Bahrain were unconfirmed. The seven were selected as memorization became harder, using trustworthiness, long practice, and broad transmission. Yaʿqūb, Abū Jaʿfar, Shayba, and others still circulated. Criteria are sound heard transmission, Arabic, and codex agreement; Nāfiʿ and ʿĀṣim have the strongest chains, Abū ʿAmr and al-Kisāʾī the most eloquence. Makkī rejects equating the seven readers with the hadith's seven aḥruf because that would exclude other sound readings. [6 notes: ITQAN(مكي)]

## 84:14/q18 How does al-Ālūsī distinguish divine speech from its wording and meaning?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether speech names the unitary attribute, inward wording and meaning, or meaning alone; the sequence objection concerns the existence of wording.
- 84:14/q18/p1 God's attribute of speaking is one, with relations to many uttered books and words, and is itself neither external nor mental letters. — Al-Ālūsī distinguishes the attribute from what is spoken. The Imam argues that eternal speech has always been command, prohibition, and report, which describe spoken contents; the one attribute has multiple relations. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الإمام)]
- 84:14/q18/p2 Inner speech joins inward wording and meaning; mentally existent wording can retain order with all parts present together. — Al-Ālūsī answers a sequence objection by distinguishing external from mental wording and says al-Mawāqif can be read consistently. He says real wording may mean inward wording, as in a memorizer, and defines inner speech among the people of truth as inward wording with meaning. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 84:14/q18/p3 Some Ashʿarīs are understood as treating inner speech as pure meaning without wording. — Al-Ālūsī says al-Mawāqif appears to read them this way because they call the verbal Quran God's speech only metaphorically; his own definition includes inward wording. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 84:14/q19 What does the abrogation objection concern in discussions of Quranic speech?  [* · theology]
- 84:14/q19/p1 It concerns spoken content rather than the act of uttering, since a ruling may be abrogated while its recitation remains. — Al-Ālūsī reports this as the fifth objection. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(المعترض)]
