# 111:1: 28 questions

## 111:1/q01 What does tabbat mean?  [تَبَّتْ · meaning]
turns on: Whether tab chiefly denotes destruction, unsuccessful effort, straying, or deprivation of good.
- 111:1/q01/p1 Ruin or destruction through loss — Al-Zajjaj and many exegetes gloss loss or ruin; Zamakhshari cites an aged, ruined woman, Razi cites the failed plot in 40:37 and a Bedouin's 'I am ruined' heard by the Prophet, and Ibn Ashur takes the curse as a return of Abu Lahab's own words. Biqai surveys tab, tabab and tibab as loss, deficiency and cutting off: Ibn Maktum, Ibn Durayd and al-Qazzaz distinguish their forms. His extended reversed-root batt survey gives complete cutting, irrevocable acts, old age, weak animals, settled matters, oaths, fasting intention, provision and severed bonds; he also notes unusual senses such as dates, clothing and the chest. Ibn Kathir says Abu Lahab's effort was wasted; Razi connects abandonment of faith, speech and acts with ruin. - 111:1/q01/p2 Frustration or failure of an aim — Ibn Abbas says Abu Lahab's previously trusted tribal standing let him repel visitors, but his public hostility made them doubt him; Razi adds his hand on visitors' shoulders. Ata says he expected to overpower Muhammad, so his imagined upper hand failed. Elmalili describes powerlessness and frustration in every effort. - 111:1/q01/p3 Being emptied of all good — Yaman ibn Ri'ab, Ibn Waththab and later transmitters say the hands became empty of every good; Abu Hayyan and Alusi treat this as close to loss and destruction. - 111:1/q01/p6 Destruction rather than ordinary material loss — Bint al-Shati says tab is chiefly destruction, whereas khusr extends to commercial and spiritual loss; she notes tabbat's unique Quranic form and 40:37 as the other root occurrence. Ibn al-Athir supplies an implied verb for tabban laka and Ibn al-Sikkit groups it with grave curses. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ]

## 111:1/q02 Is the first clause a curse, a statement, or a prediction?  [تَبَّتْ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether past tense is imprecation, report, or certain future prediction.
- 111:1/q02/p1 An imprecation or curse — Farra, Tabari's grammarian, Zamakhshari, Razi, Sibawayh via Burhan, Ibn Kathir, Itqan and Qutb read the first past verb as an imprecation. Ibn Ashur says it replies to Abu Lahab's own insult with fitting requital; Burhan answers Ibn al-Tarawah that it tells the audience Abu Lahab deserves such prayer. Qutb says the curse is issued and fulfilled within the short verse. [40 notes: FARRA(عبد الله); TAB(عبد الله,نحوي); TUSTARI; THALABI; MAWARDI(ابن مسعود); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن مسعود); WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء); BAGHAWI(الفراء); KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(قيل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الفراء); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI(الفراء); QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; ABUHAYYAN; SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURHAN(سيبويه); ITQAN(أبو شامة); BURSEVI; ALUSI(الفراء); ALUSI-FULL(الفراء); ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; CORPUSCORANICUM(Fischer); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; ISLAHI-TADABBUR-]
- 111:1/q02/p2 A factual statement or certain future prediction — Nahhas and Tabrisi allow a declarative reading; Biqai treats the past as already decreed and inescapable, while Islahi calls it a future prediction expressed in certain past tense near Badr. Elmalili says subsequent ruin supports declarative rendering. Islahi rejects reading the phrase as tit-for-tat condemnation. [19 notes: NAHHAS; TABRISI(قيل); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ELMALILI+; ISLAHI-TADABBUR+]
- 111:1/q02/p3 A declarative form that both condemns and effects divine ruin — Elmalili says the informative form can perform a curse and divine ruin, carrying blame; Tabataba'i gives a curse on self and deeds or God's decreed ruin. [2 notes: ELMALILI; TABATABAI]

## 111:1/q03 Why is tabbat feminine while tabba is masculine?  [تَبَّتْ وَتَبَّ · grammar]
- 111:1/q03/p1 Tabbat agrees with the feminine dual hands; tabba refers to Abu Lahab — Al-Akhfash compares daraba and darabat for the feminine ta and masculine past; Jishumi says tabbat follows the feminine dual hands and tabba the masculine Abu Lahab. Nahhas reports that the still ta can be explained as a particle or a vowel dropped. [3 notes: AKHFASH; NAHHAS; JISHUMI]

## 111:1/q04 Why open with ruin and repeat the verb?  [تَبَّتْ وَتَبَّ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the opening is invective, emphatic repetition, or a part-to-whole progression.
- 111:1/q04/p1 Repetition intensifies the curse — Majaz cites 'I saw', 'awla laka' and three plus seven as ten to show Quranic repetition emphasizes. Corpus Coranicum reports Paret's comparison with the double qutila of 74:19-20; Qutb says the opening issues and completes the curse at once. [6 notes: MAJAZ; MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; CORPUSCORANICUM(Fischer); QUTB-ZILAL]
- 111:1/q04/p2 It begins a rebuke like satirical invective — Ibn Ashur likens the destruction opening to 83:1 and Arabic satire: Abd al-Rahman ibn al-Hakam and Abu Tammam begin hostile poems with ruin; Abu Bakr ibn al-Khazin adapts the technique for praise. Burhan and Itqan list al-Mutaffifin, al-Humaza and al-Masad as curse openings. [13 notes: BURHAN(سيبويه); ITQAN(أبو شامة); IBNASHUR(أبو بكر بن الخازن,أبو تمام,عبد الرحمن بن الحكم); IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو بكر بن الخازن,أبو تمام,عبد الرحمن بن الحكم)]
- 111:1/q04/p3 Repetition distinguishes hands or work from the whole self — Ibn Ashur says the joined clause covers the whole man after his harmful hands, a semantic difference sufficient for waw; Biqai calls the completion total and irreversible. [7 notes: BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 111:1/q05 What do Abu Lahab's two hands refer to?  [يَدَآ · referent]
turns on: Whether hand denotes the person, his acts, resources, paired fortunes, or literal instruments of harm.
- 111:1/q05/p1 The whole person, by naming a part for the whole — Tabari and Mawardi take 'hands' for their owner; Thalabi, Baghawi, Ibn Jawzi, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Razi and Abu Hayyan call this Arabic part-for-whole usage and cite 22:10 or 2:195. Zamakhshari and Razi cite what hands send ahead; Samin says the wording moves from part to whole. Alusi rejects a proposed restriction that the whole must literally cease with the part and cites established usage. Corpus Coranicum prefers Fischer's synecdoche because the dual fits an abstract 'merits' poorly. [40 notes: TAB; NAHHAS; THALABI; MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(قيل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI(الفراء,محيي السنة); ALUSI-FULL(الفراء); CORPUSCORANICUM; CORPUSCORANICUM+]
- 111:1/q05/p2 His deeds or efforts — Tabari takes hands as deeds; Mawardi and Damghani say most action is performed by hands, citing 48:10, 36:35 and 22:10. Muqatil via Wahidi says abandoning faith ruined his hands. Ibn Kathir, Tabataba'i and Alusi see failed efforts against the Prophet; Razi and Baydawi distinguish worthless work from loss of self. Ibn Abbas and Mujahid respectively specify failure in monotheism and good works. [26 notes: TAB; MAWARDI(ابن عباس,مجاهد); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس,مجاهد); WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); DAMGHANI; TABRISI(مقاتل); RAZI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 111:1/q05/p3 His literal hands as instruments of harm — Nahhas, Zamakhshari, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Bursevi and Ibn Ashur cite a raised stone that God prevented; Tariq's Dhu al-Majaz report says he bloodied Muhammad's legs. Razi cites pushing visitors, blowing on empty hands and a speaking kid goat whose hands he tore; Durr and Alusi recount the hand gesture after the boycott. Ibn Ashur cites al-Nabigha's curse on harmful hands and an Asadi's blessing of helpful hands. [43 notes: NAHHAS; KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI(طارق المحاربي,محمد بن إسحق); RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس,طارق المحاربي,محمد بن إسحاق); QURTUBI(طارق بن عبد الله المحاربي,قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(طارق بن عبد الله المحاربي,قيل); BAYDAWI(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI(ابن عباس,طارق المحاربي); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس,طارق المحاربى); IBNASHUR(أعرابي من بني أسد,النابغة,طارق المحاربي); IBNASHUR-FULL(النابغة,طارق المحاربي); DURR(ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 111:1/q05/p4 His wealth and possessions — Thalabi, Mawardi and Baghawi cite the Arabic expression 'little in hand' for little wealth; Abu Muslim contrasts wealth with the self using 39:15. [12 notes: THALABI; MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; BAGHAWI(قيل); BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI(أبو مسلم); RAZI-FULL(أبو مسلم); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 111:1/q05/p5 His power and means of achieving aims — Razi suggests religion and worldly/future fortune, capacities to gain benefit and repel harm, or weapon and shield. - 111:1/q05/p9 His favors to Muhammad and Quraysh — Al-Maturidi in Alusi reads the two hands as favors Abu Lahab claimed with Muhammad and Quraysh; enmity nullified the first, and Quraysh's defeat the second. [2 notes: ALUSI(التأويلات); ALUSI-FULL(الماتريدى)]

- 111:1/q05/p12 His merits — Rubin, reported by Corpus Coranicum, infers lost merits from a report that Abd al-Uzza vowed to tend al-Uzza's shrine; the same author objects that a dual concrete noun poorly fits abstract merit. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Rubin); CORPUSCORANICUM-]

## 111:1/q06 Who is Abu Lahab in the verse?  [أَبِى لَهَبٍۢ · referent]
turns on: Whether Abu Lahab is the historical uncle or an archetypal figure; accounts also vary his personal name.
- 111:1/q06/p1 The Prophet's paternal uncle, Abd al-Uzza son of Abd al-Muttalib — Muqatil, Tabari, Baghawi, Qurtubi, Ibn Kathir, Ibn Ashur and others identify Abd al-Uzza ibn Abd al-Muttalib, Muhammad's paternal uncle. Ibn Kathir and Ibn Ashur also call him Abu Utba. Reports portray persistent hostility despite kinship, including the Dhu al-Majaz market pursuit. Durr-Full reports him among the Prophet's uncles and in Hell. [30 notes: MUQATIL; TAB(سفيان); THALABI; BAGHAWI; BAGHAWI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN; MAJAZ; DURR-FULL(أبو سعيد الخدري)]
- 111:1/q06/p2 The uncle, but with an alternate personal name — Thalabi reports Kuthayba, Mawardi-Full Abd Hashim, and Tabataba'i Abd Manaf among variants; these differ from the widely given Abd al-Uzza. [3 notes: THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; TABATABAI(بعضهم)]
- 111:1/q06/p3 A figurative or archetypal person destined for flame — Horovitz proposes Father of Flame as a figurative kunya coined by the sura; Corpus Coranicum suggests a generic damned archetype like 92:15 and says uncle identification may reflect later reception. It notes verse 3's fire retrospectively defines the initially enigmatic name. [6 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Horovitz)]

## 111:1/q07 What does Lahab mean and why was he called that?  [لَهَبٍۢ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the epithet arose from his appearance or resources, or from the sura's fire image.
- 111:1/q07/p1 Flame or heat, evoking handsome red or radiant cheeks — Bursevi defines lahab as flame or heat. Muqatil says his red cheeks seemed lit; Hasan, Ibn Kathir, Qurtubi, Ibn Ashur and others give beauty or radiant face. Asad calls the pre-Islamic nickname nonpejorative; Kuran Yolu adds cheeks reddening with anger. Abu al-Duha and Muqatil are cited for the same appearance account. [30 notes: MUQATIL; IBNABIHATIM(الحسن); MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو الضحى); BAGHAWI(مقاتل); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF(غير مسمى); KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(مقاتل); RAZI; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; DURR(الحسن); DURR-FULL(الحسن); BURSEVI; ALUSI; ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; IBNASHUR; TABATABAI(بعضهم); ASAD-NOTES(Baghawi; Muqatil; Zamakhshari; Razi); STUDYQURAN; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 111:1/q07/p2 A trait-based kunya marks association rather than an actual son — Zamakhshari compares Abu al-Sharr and Abu al-Khayr and reports the Prophet's calling Abu al-Muhallab Abu Safra for a yellow face. Ibn Ashur says ab can denote association with a thing and cites Taftazani on names as omens and a poem about Abu al-Mahasin; Bursevi says no son Lahab is required. [9 notes: KASHSHAF(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); KASHSHAF-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR(التفتزاني,الشاعر); CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 111:1/q07/p3 The sura coined or reshaped it to mark a person bound for Hell — Horovitz doubts it was an actual earlier name; Corpus Coranicum sees the later fire image as supplying its meaning, while Tabataba'i and Elmalili stress the final association with Hell. [6 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Horovitz); TABATABAI; ELMALILI]
- 111:1/q07/p4 His wealth occasioned the epithet — Bursevi alone mentions wealth as another possible source of the epithet. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 111:1/q08 Why does the verse use his kunya?  [أَبِى لَهَبٍۢ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether public recognition, avoidance of an idolatrous name, honor, humiliation, or fire imagery motivates the kunya.
- 111:1/q08/p1 It avoids the idolatrous name Abd al-Uzza — Ibn Qutayba asks how the Prophet could say a name making someone servant of an idol. Thalabi, Mawardi, Zamakhshari, Razi, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Biqai, Itqan and others say Abd al-Uzza was avoided. Mawardi-Full also reports Abd Hashim. Qurtubi calls the idolatrous attribution unfit for Scripture; Itqan says Abu Lahab is the Quran's only kunya. [33 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; THALABI; MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(قيل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURHAN; ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI(Keşşâf); IBNASHUR; TABATABAI]
- 111:1/q08/p2 It was his best-known designation — Ibn Qutayba says identifying labels need not describe traits literally and cites people known by kunyas; Mawardi, Zamakhshari, Baydawi, Qurtubi, Tabrisi, Biqai, Alusi and Ibn Ashur say this name was commoner than his given name. Zamakhshari says familiarity makes the hostile identification clear and lasting and compares uninflected Ali ibn Abu Talib and two names distinguished by case; Alusi says it publicizes the curse. Alusi rejects al-Sayyid's claim that fame alone creates the hellish allusion. [39 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; MAWARDI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; KASHSHAF(غير مسمى); KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة); RAZI; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURHAN; ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI(Keşşâf); IBNASHUR; THALABI; ALUSI(السيد)-; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 111:1/q08/p3 It alludes to his fiery abode — Zamakhshari, Razi, Qurtubi, Burhan, Nasafi, Alusi, Ibn Ashur, Elmalili and Tabataba'i connect the name with the Fire. Alusi favors an allusion moving from 'one attached to flame' to hellishness and cites al-Miftah that literal and implied senses can coexist; he rejects al-Sayyid's need for prior fame and cites a couplet. Corpus Coranicum notes verse 3 retrospectively brings out the fire imagery. [43 notes: KASHSHAF; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI; QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); BURHAN; ITQAN(ابن مالك,قيل); BURSEVI; ALUSI(السيد,العلامة الثاني); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI(Keşşâf); IBNASHUR(أبو هريرة,التفتزاني,الشاعر); CORPUSCORANICUM; BIQAI; ALUSI+; TABATABAI+]
- 111:1/q08/p4 It mocks his bright cheeks and pride — Zamakhshari proposes irony directed at his admired cheeks; Razi and Alusi add pride, while Qurtubi says God chose a less flattering fire-name instead of Abu al-Nur or Abu al-Diya. He says God's decisive decree wills human acts as voluntary, so choice persists; Alusi reports the conditional threat formulation. [4 notes: TABATABAI(مجمع البيان); ALUSI-FULL]
- 111:1/q16/p3 He need only affirm the message generally, not every prediction — Alusi reports an answer distinguishing required general belief in the Prophet from assent to every Quranic prediction about oneself. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 111:1/q16/p4 Hell does not itself prove lifelong unbelief — Alusi reports that Hell may punish a sinful believer, so a threat of fire alone need not prove perpetual disbelief; he says this does not solve the case of unbelievers addressed in al-Kafirun. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 111:1/q16/p5 Another kalam answer was offered then rejected without its argument here — Alusi names al-Ka'bi, Abu al-Husayn al-Basri and Qadi Abd al-Jabbar as offering another answer rejected by the Imam, but gives no details and refers readers elsewhere. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الكعبى وأبو الحسين البصرى والقاضى عبد الجبار)]

## 111:1/q17 How does this sura connect with neighboring suras?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the preceding victory, contrast in obedience, or wider sura sequence controls the link.
- 111:1/q17/p1 After al-Nasr's mass conversion comes the loss of one who stayed outside faith — Razi sets al-Masad after al-Nasr's victory and mass entry into faith: the obedient gain victory and reward, the disobedient lose. Abu Hayyan and Alusi contrast converted Meccans with Abu Lahab who stayed outside. Biqai says al-Nasr's al-Tawwab raises the question whether this enemy changed and recalls former weakness and 3:12. [10 notes: RAZI; ABUHAYYAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI(قيل); ALUSI-FULL]
- 111:1/q17/p2 The sequence contrasts obedient victory with disobedient loss — Razi uses 51:56, al-Kafirun and al-Nasr to contrast obedience and idolatry with worldly and afterlife outcomes. Alusi reports the Imam's arrangement but rejects that particular formulation. [4 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; ALUSI(الإمام); ALUSI-FULL(الامام)]
- 111:1/q17/p3 The sura points forward to monotheism and refuge — Elmalili reads the conclusion as leading to Ikhlas, sincere monotheism and seeking refuge to avoid ruin. Biqai-Full links adjacent al-Ikhlas with victory and divine sovereignty. [12 notes: ELMALILI; BIQAI(أبو جعفر ابن الزبير,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Imām Farāhī,Imām Ḥamīd Uddin Farāhī)]
- 111:1/q17/p4 Promise or victory is followed by threat — Alusi offers promise followed by warning and reports an account linking al-Kafirun's separation to promised victory then hostile-uncle ruin. Farahi says al-Lahab's place among al-Nasr, al-Ikhlas and the refuge suras must serve a purpose. [5 notes: ALUSI(الإمام,قيل); ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Imām Farāhī,Imām Ḥamīd Uddin Farāhī)]
- 111:1/q17/p5 The grouping completes the mission and separates allies from opponents — Ibn al-Zubayr, through Biqai, reads al-Kafirun as separating allies and enemies and al-Nasr as signs the Prophet's mission is complete; Islahi maps a conquest sermon to God's oneness, fulfilled promise and enemies' defeat. [9 notes: BIQAI(أبو جعفر ابن الزبير,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); BIQAI-FULL(أبو جعفر بن الزبير); ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Imām Farāhī)]

## 111:1/q18 What does the verse teach about kinship, standing, and conduct?  [* · interpretation]
- 111:1/q18/p1 Opposing the Prophet leads to ruin — Sulami's Abu Bakr ibn Tahir calls not knowing Muhammad's prophetic worth manifest loss; Qushayri says wealth cannot help an opponent. Qutb says God fights on the Prophet's behalf. Biqai says the closest unbeliever's ruin displays divine sovereignty. [12 notes: SULAMI(أبو بكر بن طاهر); QUSHAYRI; QUTB-ZILAL; BIQAI; ALUSI(الإمام); ELMALILI; TABATABAI; IBNASHUR]
- 111:1/q18/p2 Kinship cannot save without faith and deeds — Ibn Atiyya notes the uncle was destined for misery despite kinship; Biqai and Ibn al-Zubayr say faith and following deeds, not blood, save from Hell. Islahi compares Noah's son, Abraham's father and Lot's wife; Elmalili adds that status, wealth and intercession cannot rescue persistent unbelief. [15 notes: IBNATIYYA; IBNATIYYA-FULL; BIQAI(أبو جعفر ابن الزبير); BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; RAZI]
- 111:1/q18/p3 God judges hearts and deeds rather than appearance or wealth — Biqai cites a Prophetic saying that God judges hearts and deeds, not appearance or wealth. Asad and Kuran Yolu say Abu Lahab's arrogance resisted equality of rich and weak before God; Razi describes unequal worldly fortunes as a test. [4 notes: BIQAI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); BIQAI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ASAD-NOTES]
- 111:1/q18/p4 God defends those who keep silent under abuse — Razi takes 25:63 and the Abu Bakr story, where an angel replied until he answered and Satan came, as evidence that God aids one who does not trade insults. Qutb and Biqai read direct divine defense of the Messenger. [8 notes: RAZI(أبو بكر); RAZI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; BIQAI]
- 111:1/q18/p5 The Prophet made no religious concession for his uncle — Razi says confronting the uncle exposed self-interested opposition and proves no religious concession even for kin; Islahi says allegiance, not lineage, makes closeness, and publicly naming him reveals truth's self-interested enemies. [8 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 111:1/q19 Why does this sura speak directly without saying 'say'?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Why direct divine speech is used in place of a command to the Prophet.
- 111:1/q19/p1 God answers because the insult targeted Muhammad — Razi distinguishes al-Kafirun, where the unbelievers attacked God and Muhammad was told to reply, from this attack on Muhammad that God answered Himself. Biqai says direct speech strengthens vindication and warning. [5 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL]
- 111:1/q19/p2 God spares the Prophet from cursing his uncle directly — Razi says the Prophet was spared directly cursing his uncle out of kinship respect, unlike the command qul in al-Kafirun; Biqai calls the direct divine form more decorous toward kin. [4 notes: RAZI; RAZI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL]
- 111:1/q19/p3 The Prophet stays silent while God defends him — Razi invokes the command in 25:63 to answer ignorance peacefully and the angel's defense of silent Abu Bakr; Biqai says absence of qul gives more immediate divine defense. [6 notes: RAZI(أبو بكر); RAZI-FULL]

## 111:1/q20 What are the sura's names, place, order, and verse count?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether Safa fixes an early Meccan date or the prediction points closer to Badr.
- 111:1/q20/p1 It is Meccan and has five verses — Mawardi, Baghawi, Zamakhshari, Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Ibn Kathir, Alusi and others call it Meccan; Ibn Atiyya and Ibn Ashur say this is consensual. Most give five verses. Islahi disputes the dating inference from the Safa report. [22 notes: MAWARDI; BAGHAWI; KASHSHAF; IBNATIYYA; QURTUBI; BAYDAWI; NASAFI; ABUHAYYAN; IBNKATHIR; DURR(ابن الزبير وعائشة,ابن عباس); DURR-FULL(ابن الزبير، عائشة,ابن عباس); ALUSI; ALUSI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(many); IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR-]
- 111:1/q20/p2 Its names include Tabbat, al-Masad, Abu Lahab, and al-Lahab — Ibn Ashur says most codices and al-Tirmidhi call it Tabbat, some al-Masad, many exegetes Abu Lahab, and Abu Hayyan al-Lahab; Ibn al-Arabi's longer phrase is merely a heading. Itqan and Study Quran also give al-Masad, while Asad describes a final-word naming claim. [10 notes: ITQAN; IBNASHUR(أبو حيان,ابن العربي); IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 111:1/q20/p3 It was sixth in revelation order, with an early date — Ibn Ashur calls it sixth after al-Fatiha and before al-Takwir, and reports a fourth-year date; Asad also calls it early. To reconcile the later 26:214 with Safa he posits an earlier similar warning, drawing on Abu Usama's wording. [9 notes: IBNASHUR(أبو أسامة عن ابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن عباس,سعيد بن جبير); ASAD-NOTES]
- 111:1/q20/p4 It was revealed near Badr after conclusive proclamation — Islahi says a prediction after conclusively conveying truth and shortly before Abu Lahab's death suits Badr better than early Mecca; he rejects using the Safa episode to date revelation. [4 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 111:1/q21 What collateral reports accompany the sura?  [* · reports]
turns on: Reports of accompanying events differ.
- 111:1/q21/p1 His wife and household joined his campaign — Ibn Hisham and Suyuti say Umm Jamil laid thorns; Qutb names her Arwa, daughter of Harb and Abu Sufyan's sister, and says the couple waged a campaign. Kashshaf-Full gives thorn scattering and an alternative gossip sense of carrying firewood. [10 notes: IBNHISHAM(ابن إسحاق); QURTUBI; QURTUBI-FULL; SUYUTI-LUBAB(يزيد بن زيد); QUTB-ZILAL; KASHSHAF-FULL]
- 111:1/q21/p2 Umm Jamil brought a stone but could not see the Prophet — Asma bint Abi Bakr and other reports say Umm Jamil arrived with a stone and taunting rhyme after revelation, saw Abu Bakr but not the Prophet, and was screened by the Qur'an or a veil linked to 17:45-46. Tabataba'i reports a version through Musa ibn Ja'far and comparable Sunni chains. [6 notes: IBNABIHATIM(أسماء بنت أبي بكر); QURTUBI(الحميدي وغيره); QURTUBI-FULL(الحميدي وغيره); IBNKATHIR-FULL(أسماء بنت أبي بكر); TABATABAI(موسى بن جعفر عليه السلام)]
- 111:1/q21/p3 The opponents abused the name Mudhammam — Qurtubi reports the Prophet saying Quraysh cursed 'Mudhammam' while he was Muhammad, so God diverted the abuse; another report says Umm Jamil stumbled, used that name and was answered by Umm Hakim. [3 notes: QURTUBI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); QURTUBI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(أم حكيم بنت عبد المطلب)]
- 111:1/q21/p4 The Prophet defended Abu Lahab's daughter Durra — Durr-Full says the daughter Durra was insulted as an enemy's daughter, she defended her father, and the Prophet forbade harming a Muslim for a disbeliever and asserted his kinship intercession. [3 notes: DURR-FULL(أبو سعيد الخدري,ابن عمر، أبو هريرة، عمار بن ياسر,جعفر بن محمد عن أبيه)]

## 111:1/q22 What inward reading is given to the ruin?  [* · inward]
turns on: Different inward emphases on evil disposition and recognition of the Prophet.
- 111:1/q22/p1 His hands cause evil acts and his self is fit for fire — Qashani treats the hands as causes of evil deeds and the ruined self as disposed to fire by essence and quality; his kunya signals permanent attachment. Tustari separates worldly wealth from the soul's destruction. [4 notes: QASHANI; TUSTARI; BIQAI-FULL]
- 111:1/q22/p2 Failure to know the Prophet's worth is the deepest loss — Sulami's Abu Bakr ibn Tahir says failure to perceive Muhammad's nearness, prophethood and love causes estrangement. Qushayri laments anyone who misses his worth, and says wealth cannot protect an opponent. [3 notes: SULAMI(أبو بكر بن طاهر); QUSHAYRI]
- 111:1/q22/p3 The basmala heals sinners and strips away the knowers' states — Qushayri says the basmala repairs sinners' deeds and hope, but strips knowers of states and grants union after annihilation. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]

## 111:1/q23 Can one Quranic passage be superior to this sura?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether merit belongs equally to all divine speech, depends on subject and effect, or is purpose-relative.
- 111:1/q23/p1 Speech about God is superior to speech about Abu Lahab — Izz al-Din says discourse about God excels discourse about others, so al-Ikhlas excels the sura about Abu Lahab; Burhan says Ghazali and Ibn al-Arabi agree. Zarkashi favors the abundance and wonder of divine unity and attributes; Itqan cites examples such as 2:255 and 112:1. [4 notes: BURHAN(عز الدين); ITQAN(عز الدين بن عبد السلام,قيل)]
- 111:1/q23/p2 Each is most eloquent for its own purpose — Al-Khuyi says no wording better serves a curse than Tabbat and none better states unity than al-Ikhlas, so eloquence must be judged against each passage's subject and no absolute ranking follows. [3 notes: BURHAN(القاضي شمس الدين الخويي); ITQAN(الخويي)]
- 111:1/q23/p3 No passage is inherently better since all is God's speech — Burhan reports al-Ashari, Qadi Abu Bakr and Ibn Hibban denying intrinsic preference among God's speech; Malik disliked repeatedly selecting one sura, and Yahya ibn Yahya says ranking passages is wrong because it implies defect elsewhere. [2 notes: BURHAN(الشيخ أبو الحسن الأشعري,مالك)]
- 111:1/q23/p4 Merit depends on reward, spiritual effect, or practical benefit — Itqan reports that some locate merit in reward, awe, reflection and receptivity, while al-Halimi also looks at practical usefulness of commands, prohibitions, promise and warning. [2 notes: ITQAN(الحليمي,بعضهم)]

## 111:1/q24 Is the phrase poetic because it matches a meter?  [تَبَّتْ يَدَآ أَبِى لَهَبٍۢ وَتَبَّ · rhetoric]
- 111:1/q24/p1 No; accidental meter does not make Quranic prose poetry — Al-Baqillani says accidental match with a meter is insufficient: poetry requires intentionally balanced, metrical parts. Al-Jahiz notes ordinary requests can scan by chance. Burhan lists Quranic phrases including this opening as metrical coincidences. [3 notes: BURHAN; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الباقلاني,الجاحظ)]

## 111:1/q25 What other formal observations are made about this wording?  [* · rhetoric]
- 111:1/q25/p1 It has distinctive lexical, recitational, and literary afterlife features — Bint al-Shati notes tabbat occurs uniquely here and the root otherwise at 40:37. Nashr reports Muhammad ibn Badhan rebuking a reciter who stopped after 'hands of Abu Lahab' to demonstrate madd lengths. Elmalili cites Fuzuli's later Arabic curse on scribes echoing the opening. [3 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ; NASHR(محمد بن بضحان); ELMALILI(Fuzûlî)]

## 111:1/q26 What interpretive details of the following verses explain the opening?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the surrounding verses explain the opening through wealth, punishment, wife, or a reciter's fate.
- 111:1/q26/p1 His fiery punishment and his wife's conduct complete the threatened ruin — Kashshaf-Full says future sa-yasla threatens certain punishment even if delayed; it reports Umm Jamil's thorn scattering or gossip and portrays both spouses burning. Qutb says their joint campaign explains God's intervention; the wife's wood and neck image humiliates a wealthy household. [7 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL]
- 111:1/q26/p2 A report promises distance from Abu Lahab to a reciter of the sura — Kashshaf-Full and Baydawi-Full transmit a Prophetic hope that a reciter of the sura will not share Abu Lahab's abode. [2 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); BAYDAWI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 111:1/q26/p3 His money and what he earned cannot help him — Kashshaf-Full discusses ma aghna as question or negation and ma kasab as acquisition or earnings. It reports children through Ibn Abbas, evil opposition through Dahhak, imagined useful deeds through Qatada, and wealth/children as attempted ransom; alternative pairs include capital/profit, livestock/offspring and inherited/earned property. [7 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(ابن عباس,الضحاك,قتادة)]
- 111:1/q26/p4 The remainder elaborates the wife and rope imagery or recitational variants — Kashshaf-Full reports accusative hammalata as an insult preferred by Zamakhshari, other nominative, accusative and tanwin readings, and the rope of masad as twisted fiber or other material. It also gives her wood bundle or future Zaqqum/Dari' firewood and a chain of fire; poetry illustrates the rope's tightness. [3 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL+]

## 111:1/q27 What objections about figurative, ambiguous, or repeated Quranic wording cite this verse?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether such figures are defects or ordinary intelligible Arabic usage.
- 111:1/q27/p1 Critics challenge figurative and ambiguous expressions, repetition, and apparent form — Ibn Qutayba reports critics objecting to ambiguous guidance, implied omissions, added or reversed words, metaphor, inversion, kinaya here and in 25:28, repetition, and form differing from intended meaning. [6 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL(الطاعنون)]
- 111:1/q27/p2 Ibn Qutayba says he answers those objections and defends known identifying names — Ibn Qutayba says he has answered those objections and others and kept unusual vocabulary in another book; he defends identifying labels against literalist objections and explains Arabs' auspicious or ugly names as omens. [3 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL]

## 111:1/q28 What numerical signs and other sura-wide features are proposed?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Which numerological or basmala-wide frame is inferred.
- 111:1/q28/p1 Word and pronoun counts forecast years and historical events — Biqai reads fifteen words as pointing to Abu Lahab's death in the fifteenth prophetic year and relates added basmala and pronouns to conquests and years 6, 8, 9 and 11 AH; twenty-three words suggest the Farewell Pilgrimage and completed religion at 5:3. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 111:1/q28/p2 The basmala's names frame guidance and divine majesty — Biqai-Full reads the basmala's divine names as majesty, guidance and communication to friend and foe, with special guidance for the favored. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
