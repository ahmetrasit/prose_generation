# 20:43: 17 questions

## 20:43/q01 Why is the command to go to Pharaoh repeated?  [ٱذْهَبَآ إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the earlier order is compared with the singular commission at 20:24 or the joint wording at 20:42, and whether its earlier scope, destination, or mode of travel was left implicit.
- 20:43/q01/p1 The repetition emphasizes or renews the command. — Al-Wāḥidī and al-Ṭabrisī call it emphasis; Ibn al-Jawzī connects it to the earlier command at 20:24. Al-Qurṭubī reports that the first address honored Moses alone and the second stresses that both brothers must go. Al-Biqāʿī says the danger makes the order hard for ordinary human nature to accept. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the renewal matches the joint address and warning against slackening. [6 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 20:43/q01/p2 The earlier command addressed Moses alone, whereas this one includes Aaron. — Al-Ṭabrisī says the dual commission makes Aaron a prophet and partner. Al-Qurṭubī adds that Moses was honored by the initial singular address. Al-Bayḍāwī says this makes the wording nonredundant. Al-Ālūsī confirms that the imperative here addresses both brothers and identifies the clear contrast with the singular command at 20:24, while reporting a comparison with 20:42; the Study Quran likewise notes the echo of 20:24. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the dual command restates the earlier order to Moses and his absent brother, and prefers a subsequent meeting of the two brothers. [8 notes: TABRISI(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); STUDYQURAN; IBNASHUR-FULL+]
- 20:43/q01/p3 The earlier command omitted or left open the destination, which is explicitly Pharaoh here. — Abū Ḥayyān says the second command supplies the earlier omitted object and specifies delivery of the message. Al-Samīn says both journeys target Pharaoh: his name is omitted earlier and stated here, while 'with My signs' is stated earlier and omitted here. Al-Nasafī calls the earlier command unqualified and this one qualified, citing 20:24. Al-Ālūsī reports the contrast with 20:42. [4 notes: NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 20:43/q01/p4 The earlier command concerned the people generally, whereas this one singles out Pharaoh. — Al-Qaffāl, as cited by al-Rāzī, speaks of Israelites and Pharaoh's people generally. Al-Qurṭubī reports all people; Abū Ḥayyān calls this interpretation more distant. Al-Samīn finds it unlikely because both journeys target Pharaoh. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL(القفال); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); SAMIN-DURR-]
- 20:43/q01/p5 The dual command clarifies that both brothers must go together, rather than either going alone. — Al-Qaffāl says the earlier wording could permit separate journeys. Al-Qurṭubī reports that one brother alone would not suffice; al-Ālūsī records the same distinction from 20:42. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL(القفال); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 20:43/q01/p6 The renewed order resumes the main mission after the preceding exchange and prepares what follows. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says it returns to the main purpose after the dialogue, explaining God's preparation and selection of Moses at 20:41 for the great task alongside Aaron. Bursevī says the repeated command sets up what follows. [2 notes: BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 20:43/q02 How can both brothers be addressed when Aaron was absent from Moses' initial encounter?  [ٱذْهَبَآ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the dual speech is representative, occurs after a later meeting, or directly addresses Aaron already present; Ibn ʿĀshūr appeals to the narrative sequence and their joint fear at 20:45.
- 20:43/q02/p1 The command was given after Moses and Aaron met. — Al-Qaffāl suggests Moses paused after the answer to his request at 20:36 until meeting Aaron. Bursevī allows that the brothers had already met. Ibn ʿĀshūr prefers Moses telling Aaron the restated command when they met, after which both voiced the fear in 20:45. Al-Iṣlāḥī places this after Moses reached Egypt because Aaron was absent from the valley of Ṭuwā; al-Ālūsī reports a version after Moses returned to Egypt. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL(القفال); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNASHUR-FULL+]
- 20:43/q02/p2 God addressed Moses as leader of a mission that included Aaron, using a dual address by attribution or predominance. — Al-Qaffāl says the dual speech can be hypothetical and cites collective attribution in 2:72 and 63:8. Bursevī calls this grammatical predominance. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL(القفال); BURSEVI]
- 20:43/q02/p3 Aaron was already with Moses when the dual command was spoken. — Ibn ʿĀshūr reports a direct shift to both brothers after Aaron joined Moses by revelation in Goshen; their shared fear at 20:45 follows without a narrated journey between Horeb and Egypt. He prefers a later meeting account instead. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL-]

## 20:43/q03 How did Aaron come to meet Moses for their joint mission?  [ٱذْهَبَآ · reports]
turns on: The accounts differ about what prompted Aaron, where he met Moses, and whether their meeting preceded the dual command.
- 20:43/q03/p1 God instructed or revealed to Aaron in Egypt to meet Moses. — Al-Ṭabrisī reports that Aaron was in Egypt and, when Moses was told to come, God instructed Aaron to meet him; they conferred and went to Pharaoh. Al-Bayḍāwī offers a divine instruction. Al-Ālūsī reports revelation to Aaron in Egypt. Ibn ʿĀshūr's alternative has Aaron joining Moses by revelation in Goshen. [4 notes: TABRISI(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 20:43/q03/p2 Aaron heard that Moses was approaching and went to meet him. — Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī report this possibility without specifying divine revelation. [2 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 20:43/q03/p3 God inspired Aaron to meet Moses. — Al-Ālūsī lists inspiration alongside, but separately from, revelation in Egypt. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 20:43/q03/p4 Aaron went to Sinai to meet Moses. — Al-Ālūsī gives this as another possible location for their meeting. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 20:43/q03/p5 Moses returned to Egypt and Aaron came out to meet him before the joint command. — Al-Ālūsī reports this sequence; al-Iṣlāḥī infers the command was after Moses reached Egypt because Aaron was absent in Ṭuwā. Ibn ʿĀshūr prefers a meeting after Moses received and relayed the command, with their joint fear following. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; IBNASHUR-FULL+]

## 20:43/q04 What destination and task does the command give Moses and Aaron?  [ٱذْهَبَآ إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ · interpretation]
- 20:43/q04/p1 They are to go to Pharaoh with God's message and restrain his transgression. — Abū Ḥayyān identifies Pharaoh as the specified destination and says they bring the message. Ibn ʿĀshūr says Moses' task, whether inferred from his selection and signs or stated in omitted wording, is to convey God's command turning Pharaoh from worship of other than God; Moses accepts and seeks help despite fear. He says the reason clause makes restraining Pharaoh's transgression the mission's purpose. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 20:43/q05 Who is Pharaoh here, and how is his title used?  [فِرْعَوْنَ · referent]
- 20:43/q05/p1 Pharaoh is al-Walīd ibn Muṣʿab; his foreign title also names tyrants by extension. — Bursevī says the foreign name generated a verb for acting like Pharaoh and that tyrants can be called pharaohs and devils. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 20:43/q06 What does Pharaoh's transgression mean?  [طَغَىٰ · meaning]
turns on: The notes give disbelief, arrogant disobedience and tyranny, or the specific claim to divinity and lordship as the content of ṭughyān; some sources combine these descriptions.
- 20:43/q06/p1 He exceeded the bounds of obedience and servitude through rebellion, arrogance, and tyranny. — Al-Wāḥidī says he rose up arrogantly; al-Ṭabrisī says he exceeded the limit in tyranny. Ibn Kathīr adds arrogant defiance of God and disobedience. Bursevī defines ṭughyān as exceeding the limit in disobedience and says Pharaoh exceeded servitude. Muqātil says Pharaoh disobeyed God for four hundred years. [5 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; TABRISI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 20:43/q06/p2 He transgressed by claiming divine lordship. — Al-Nasafī names the claim to lordship; Abū Ḥayyān adds corruption and claims of divinity and lordship apart from God; Bursevī says Pharaoh exceeded servitude by claiming divinity. [3 notes: NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 20:43/q06/p3 He disbelieved. — Yaḥyā ibn Sallām gives disbelief as the sense of Pharaoh's transgression. [1 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM]
- 20:43/q06/p4 In an inward reading, his transgression is intoxication from divine overpowering, an attribute of the commanding soul. — Al-Ālūsī reports this explanation and contrasts the spirit's intoxication from divine gentleness, which produces ecstatic utterances and claims of selfhood. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 20:43/q07 Why is Pharaoh's transgression stated as the reason for the command?  [إِنَّهُۥ طَغَىٰ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the clause is read for its immediate causal force, its emphatic and striking repetition, or as recalling an earlier explanation.
- 20:43/q07/p1 It explains why the brothers must confront and restrain Pharaoh. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says the causal clause signals the mission and the need to turn Pharaoh from worship of other than God; Moses therefore accepts and seeks assistance rather than objecting from fear. He also says their mission is to restrain the transgression. Abū Ḥayyān says Pharaoh's corruption and claim to lordship explain why he is named as the destination. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 20:43/q07/p2 Its repetition emphasizes the reason and exposes Pharaoh's folly and that of his followers. — Al-Biqāʿī says the repeated reason heightens wonder at Pharaoh's lack of sense and at those who follow him. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 20:43/q07/p3 The nature of his transgression has already been explained earlier in the sūra. — Al-Wāḥidī says the explanation has preceded; Ibn al-Jawzī locates it at 20:24. [2 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNJAWZI-ZAD]

## 20:43/q08 What do the spelling and root of طَغَىٰ indicate?  [طَغَىٰ · grammar]
- 20:43/q08/p1 The final sound is written with an alif-shaped yāʾ, pointing to the root طغي; a wāw root form is also possible. — Ibn ʿĀshūr compares the yāʾ root with رضي and the possible wāw form طغو with يدعو. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 20:43/q09 Who is the mission ultimately meant to reach?  [إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ · inward]
turns on: Whether Pharaoh is taken as the direct addressee whose claim must be answered or the outward target through whom believers are rescued.
- 20:43/q09/p1 Its inward beneficiaries are the magicians and other prepared believers among Pharaoh's people. — Ibn ʿAṭāʾ, reported by al-Sulamī, says Moses is outwardly sent to Pharaoh but truly to the magicians, since prophets are sent to rescue God's believing friends among enemies rather than to enemies who lack standing with Him. Bursevī also transmits Ibn ʿAṭāʾ's point. Al-ʿArāʾis, quoted by Bursevī, adds Ḥabīb al-Najjār, Pharaoh's relative and wife, and the magicians as examples of prepared believers drawn out from unbelievers. [3 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء); BURSEVI(ابن عطاء,العرائس)]
- 20:43/q09/p2 Pharaoh himself is confronted so that his false claim is exposed and his argument cut off. — Al-ʿArāʾis, cited by Bursevī, says God sent both brothers to Pharaoh to expose his claim and warn any claimant without divine proof. [1 notes: BURSEVI(العرائس)]

## 20:43/q10 What does sending prophets to enemies teach?  [* · theology]
turns on: The cited explanations emphasize different beneficiaries and lessons of a mission among enemies; Ibn ʿAṭāʾ distinguishes its outward destination from its inward purpose.
- 20:43/q10/p1 It exposes the enemy's inability to guide anyone and the emptiness of claims without divine proof. — Al-ʿArāʾis, quoted by Bursevī, compares the enemy to a doctor unable to treat others or himself; it also says the confrontation cuts off Pharaoh's argument and warns every claimant lacking proof from God. [2 notes: BURSEVI(العرائس)]
- 20:43/q10/p2 It teaches prophets that election is not produced by worldly causes and calls for gratitude. — Al-ʿArāʾis gives this lesson in Bursevī's report and adds that the mission may draw prepared believers from among unbelievers. [1 notes: BURSEVI(العرائس)]
- 20:43/q10/p3 God sends prophets to rescue His believing friends amid enemies. — Ibn ʿAṭāʾ, cited by al-Sulamī and Bursevī, points especially to the magicians; al-Sulamī's version says enemies themselves lack standing with God to warrant a prophetic mission. [2 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء); BURSEVI(ابن عطاء)]

## 20:43/q11 How should Pharaoh's transgression affect the brothers' manner of preaching?  [إِنَّهُۥ طَغَىٰ · interpretation]
- 20:43/q11/p1 Even a great transgressor should be addressed gently, without harshness or violence. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls gentle speech an essential etiquette of inviting people to faith. Kur'an Yolu says the contrast between Pharaoh's great transgression and the command to speak gently shows the importance of method and tone for successful preaching. [2 notes: TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 20:43/q12 What inward figures do Moses, Aaron, and Pharaoh represent?  [* · inward]
- 20:43/q12/p1 Moses is the heart, Aaron the intellect, and Pharaoh the tyrannical commanding soul. — Al-Qāshānī says heart and intellect confront the soul with God's proofs and remembrance, while God encourages and protects them in that struggle. Al-Ālūsī reports that Pharaoh's transgression reflects intoxication from divine overpowering, an attribute of the commanding soul; he contrasts it with the spirit's intoxication from divine gentleness, which produces ecstatic utterances and claims of selfhood. [3 notes: QASHANI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 20:43/q13 What report describes Moses' arrival at Pharaoh's door?  [إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ · reports]
- 20:43/q13/p1 Moses waited outside Pharaoh's door for a year without finding a messenger. — Al-Qurṭubī labels this an Israelite report and says it consoles later believers who face oppressors. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الإسرائيليات)]

## 20:43/q14 What illustrations of departure accompany the command to go?  [ٱذْهَبَآ · reports]
turns on: The two illustrations are an account of prompt obedience to a dream command and a poetic longing to depart.
- 20:43/q14/p1 A spiritual master obeyed a dream command to migrate from Filibe to Constantinople. — Bursevī says his master dreamed at midmorning, then woke, prayed, washed, departed on foot, and left his family behind. [1 notes: BURSEVI(شيخي وسندي)]
- 20:43/q14/p2 A poetic speaker longs to leave his present station and hear news from the beloved's street. — Bursevī quotes a couplet of Ḥāfiẓ in which companions will ask for such news on the day of departure. [1 notes: BURSEVI(الحافظ)]

## 20:43/q15 Which other passages does the command to Moses and Aaron recall?  [* · links]
- 20:43/q15/p1 It echoes Moses' commission in 20:24 and other accounts of his mission. — The Study Quran links the dual command to 20:24, 79:17, 26:10–11, 27:12, and 28:32. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 20:43/q16 Which first-person yāʾ readings does Ibn Mujāhid record in connection with this passage?  [* · readings]
turns on: The named readers differ over which listed first-person yāʾs take opening or sukūn.
- 20:43/q16/p1 Nāfiʿ opens the listed first-person yāʾs except ولي فيها and أخي اشدد, where he uses sukūn. — Ibn Mujāhid reports Nāfiʿ's pattern and its two exceptions. [1 notes: IBNMUJAHID(نافع)]
- 20:43/q16/p2 Abū ʿAmr uses sukūn on ولي فيها and حشرتني أعمى, opening the other listed first-person yāʾs. — Ibn Mujāhid reports Abū ʿAmr's two exceptions to opening. [1 notes: IBNMUJAHID(أبو عمرو)]
- 20:43/q16/p3 Ibn Kathīr uses sukūn on ولي فيها، ويسر لي أمري، لذكري إن، على عيني إذ, and برأسي إني; he opens the rest. — Ibn Mujāhid reports Ibn Kathīr's five exceptions to opening. [1 notes: IBNMUJAHID(ابن كثير)]
- 20:43/q16/p4 Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī, Ibn ʿĀmir, and ʿĀṣim through Abū Bakr use sukūn on all the listed first-person yāʾs. — Ibn Mujāhid reports these readers together with the same pattern. [1 notes: IBNMUJAHID(حمزة، الكسائي، ابن عامر، عاصم عن أبي بكر)]

## 20:43/q17 How are a mystic's ecstatic utterances judged in comparison with Pharaoh?  [فِرْعَوْنَ طَغَىٰ · inward]
turns on: Whether intoxication excuses an ecstatic utterance or leaves the speaker comparable to Pharaoh.
- 20:43/q17/p1 Some excuse the ecstatic utterance of an intoxicated mystic such as al-Ḥallāj. — Al-Ālūsī reports this judgment from some speakers. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قالوا)]
- 20:43/q17/p2 Those jealous for God see no difference between such a mystic and Pharaoh. — Al-Ālūsī reports their opposing judgment. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قالوا)]
