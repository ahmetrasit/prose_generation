# 11:54: 24 questions

## 11:54/q01 How is 'we say only that' construed?  [إِن نَّقُولُ إِلَّا ٱعْتَرَىٰكَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the words after illā are direct quoted content or require an omitted noun or verb; al-Samin favors the direct construction.
- 11:54/q01/p1 The accusation is quoted as the content or object of 'we say'; the negative and exception restrict their speech to this charge. — Al-Zamakhshari calls the clause the object and illā redundant as a meaning gloss; al-Baydawi likewise calls the exception exhaustive. Abu Hayyan and al-Samin take direct quotation as the simple syntax, like 'I said only: Zayd is standing'; al-Samin says al-Zamakhshari's redundancy is semantic, not the syntactic analysis, and rejects needless ellipses. Al-Nasafi and al-Tabrisi identify in as negative. Ibn Ashur says qawl denotes an actual utterance reflecting their belief. [9 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء)+]
- 11:54/q01/p2 The clause explains an omitted verbal noun or omitted general statement: their sole saying about Hūd is this accusation. — Al-Tabrisi's cited grammarian makes the exception from the verbal noun implied by 'say', comparing 37:59 and 10:45. Bursevi supplies the omitted verbal noun. Al-Alusi reconstructs an omitted general term and specific saying after the restrictive construction, but also reports the no-ellipsis quoted-word analysis. [3 notes: TABRISI(صاحب كتاب كشف الجامع النحوي); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء)-]
- 11:54/q01/p3 The accusation is accusative as the object of an omitted 'mention'. — Abu al-Baqa proposes this alternative; al-Samin rejects the omission because direct quotation already accounts for the wording. [0 notes: SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء)-]

## 11:54/q02 What does iʿtarāka mean?  [ٱعْتَرَىٰكَ · meaning]
- 11:54/q02/p1 It means that something came upon, struck, touched, or afflicted Hūd. — Abu Ubayda derives it from ʿarawtu and cites Abu Khirash's yaʿrūhu; Ibn Qutayba cites ʿaranī/iʿtarānī and al-Nabigha's ʿāriyan, a visitor seeking bounty. Ibn al-Arabi and Ibn al-Jawzi also compare visiting for a favor. Al-Tabari and al-Tabrisi cite poetic usage; al-Jishumi cites taʿrūnī for a seizure at remembering the beloved. Al-Samin identifies form VIII of ʿarā/yaʿrū and says it takes two objects, the second with a preposition. Biqaʿi evokes forceful clinging; al-Alusi evokes approaching a person's side. Ibn Ashur calls the bāʾ in bi-sūʾ accompanying contact, while Bursevi calls it causal. Al-Qurtubi compares 22:36. [23 notes: MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB(النابغة); TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن الأعرابي); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(الشاعر); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة,النابغة); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 11:54/q03 What does 'some of our gods' identify?  [بَعْضُ ءَالِهَتِنَا · referent]
- 11:54/q03/p1 The speakers mean one or some of their idols, which they treat as gods able to act against Hūd. — Al-Qurtubi identifies the gods as idols; Muqatil and others speak of one idol, while some reports say idols or gods in the plural. Al-Elmalılı says the speakers assign individual idols invented names and specialties. Al-Nahhas says the masculine verb follows the grammatical form of baʿḍ; feminine agreement by meaning would also be possible. [4 notes: MUQATIL; QURTUBI-FULL; ELMALILI; NAHHAS]

## 11:54/q04 Why do they say 'some' gods and an indefinite 'evil'?  [بَعْضُ ءَالِهَتِنَا بِسُوٓءٍۢ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the indeterminate wording downplays the accusation or heightens the feared harm and the idols' supposed power.
- 11:54/q04/p1 The understatement minimizes their insolence by attributing an unspecified evil to only some gods. — Al-Alusi reports this explanation of the two indefinite expressions. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
- 11:54/q04/p2 The indefinite evil magnifies the harm and naming only some gods magnifies their power. — Al-Alusi reports that only someone struck by much evil would speak so irrationally. Ibn Ashur says alleging that one idol harmed Hūd threatens that all together could crush him; al-Elmalılı's account of specialized deities explains why one is named. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قيل); IBNASHUR-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 11:54/q05 What 'evil' did the people claim struck Hūd?  [بِسُوٓءٍۢ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether 'evil' is identified specifically as mental derangement or left as unspecified harm.
- 11:54/q05/p1 They alleged madness, derangement, or a damaged mind, making his preaching seem irrational. — Mujahid, Ibn Abbas, and many exegetes specify madness. Muqatil allows mental impairment; Ibn Zayd says his mind became confused; al-Baydawi says they treated his speech as ravings; al-Tabatabai says it therefore seemed unworthy of attention. Al-Farra, as reported by Muqatil, distinguishes vocalized khabal meaning madness from still-bāʾ khabl, a disabling bodily illness. Al-Qurtubi cites Ibn Abbas and others; al-Wahidi calls this the general exegetical view. [34 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); MUQATIL(الفراء); IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(ابن زيد,ابن عباس,مجاهد); ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(مجاهد); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس، الحسن، مجاهد، قتادة); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(ابن عباس ومجاهد); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,مجاهد); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(Kl)]
- 11:54/q05/p2 They allege harm or an evil effect from the idols without specifying madness. — Qatada says the alleged harm explains why Hūd disparaged their gods; Abdullah ibn Kathir calls it evil. Al-Dahhak reports their fear that harm might yet befall him. These descriptions do not specify the nature of the evil. [7 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB-FULL(الضحاك,عبد الله بن كثير,قتادة); IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); DURR-FULL(قتادة)]

## 11:54/q06 Why and when, according to them, did an idol afflict Hūd?  [ٱعْتَرَىٰكَ بَعْضُ ءَالِهَتِنَا بِسُوٓءٍۢ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the alleged injury retaliated against his criticism, preceded and caused it, or remained a threatened future injury.
- 11:54/q06/p1 It punished his insults, criticism, opposition, or urging people away from idol worship; this supposedly explains his conduct. — Mujahid's second report says insult and disparagement made Hūd mad. Al-Zajjaj, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, al-Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, and others supply retaliation for reviling idols. Al-Baydawi says they portrayed the preaching as ravings; Abu Hayyan adds his call to worship God alone. Biqaʿi observes that people ordinarily fear and avoid offending one believed able to harm them. The Study Quran's Kl says the supposed spell followed his curse of an idol. [21 notes: TAB-FULL(مجاهد); ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس، الحسن، مجاهد، قتادة); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(ابن عباس ومجاهد); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(Kl)]
- 11:54/q06/p2 An idol afflicted him before his rejection of its worship, for some other cause; that affliction supposedly generated his opposition. — Ibn Ashur states this temporal reading and calls it incoherent sophistry: their gods would have provoked someone who then opposed them. Qatada's paraphrases put the supposed harm before Hūd's disparagement, without detailing its cause. [5 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB-FULL(قتادة); IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); DURR-FULL(قتادة)]
- 11:54/q06/p3 They fear or warn that the idols may still harm him, and urge him to stop so he will be safe. — Muqatil says they disclaim wishing harm and tell him to avoid the idols; al-Dahhak reports their fear that the gods might afflict him. [2 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(الضحاك)]

## 11:54/q07 What does their accusation reveal about their outlook?  [إِن نَّقُولُ إِلَّا ٱعْتَرَىٰكَ بَعْضُ ءَالِهَتِنَا بِسُوٓءٍۢ · theology]
turns on: The notes draw different, compatible lessons from the charge: belief in idols' agency and contempt for prophetic admonition.
- 11:54/q07/p1 They foolishly suppose stone idols can defend themselves, take revenge, and perhaps reward people. — Al-Kashshaf and Abu Hayyan call this extreme ignorance. Bursevi says their assertion of the idols' ability to harm prompts Hūd's denial of their divinity. [3 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 11:54/q07/p2 Their charge treats repentance and monotheistic preaching as madness, exposing entrenched resistance to guidance. — Al-Kashshaf says their prior answers showed hard hearts, heedlessness, slander, and resistance; he rebukes professing Muslims who likewise call penitents mad, associating that conduct with irreligion and heresy. Al-Tabatabai's account says most of ʿĀd called Hūd foolish and mad and demanded punishment. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 11:54/q08 How does the accusation connect with the preceding exchange?  [إِن نَّقُولُ إِلَّا ٱعْتَرَىٰكَ بَعْضُ ءَالِهَتِنَا بِسُوٓءٍۢ · links]
turns on: Whether the charge chiefly explains their view of the message, motivates Hūd's challenge, or intensifies the prior refusals.
- 11:54/q08/p1 It explains what they think of Hūd's preaching after rejecting his message. — Ibn Ashur calls it an explanatory resumption naturally invited by their rejection. Biqaʿi frames the implied question why Hūd would oppose his cousins and closest companions if his speech were unclear or unjust; the unusual accusation draws attention to his answer. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 11:54/q08/p2 It gives a reason for his ensuing challenge after they said they would not heed him. — Al-Alusi reports this link to 11:53. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
- 11:54/q08/p3 It reinforces and escalates their earlier refusals, ending by depicting him as mad. — Al-Alusi reports reinforcement of their refusal to abandon gods or believe Hūd and himself traces the ascent from contesting evidence to refusing the command, denying credibility, and alleging madness. He notes that absence of a conjunction may mark the sentence as more than mere emphasis, while still confirming the preceding exchange. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]

## 11:54/q09 How is the yāʾ of innī read?  [إِنِّىٓ · readings]
- 11:54/q09/p1 Nāfiʿ and Abū Jaʿfar open or vowel the first-person yāʾ in 'innī ush'hidu'. — Ibn al-Jawzi reports Nāfiʿ; Ibn al-Jazari names Nāfiʿ and Abū Jaʿfar, the two Medinan readers, and lists this among ten loci, including 27:29, 3:36, 5:29, 5:115, 6:14, 7:156, 28:27, and 39:12; his note also mentions 12:59. [3 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(نافع); NASHR(المدنيان,نافع، أبو جعفر)]

## 11:54/q10 Why does Hūd call God to witness?  [أُشْهِدُ ٱللَّهَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the emphasis is his personal renunciation or his completed prophetic testimony; both are reported as purposes.
- 11:54/q10/p1 God's witness solemnly establishes his sincere, longstanding innocence and confession of monotheism. — Al-Qurtubi says Hūd calls God as witness concerning himself. Al-Kashshaf says God's witness establishes monotheism. Biqaʿi says the supreme sovereign attests his innocence before Him. Al-Alusi likens it to an oath; al-Tabatabai says it certifies sincerity. Ibn Ashur analyzes ash'hidu as a performative utterance phrased like a report, akin to contract formulas whose effects are not externally visible; al-Alusi reports both a performative and an actual evidentiary summons, favoring the figurative or performative account. [7 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 11:54/q10/p2 God witnesses that Hūd is a true messenger who conveyed his message despite their denial. — Ibn Ashur relates the summons to their rejection of his signs, resolve to keep worshipping idols, and praise of idol power. Elmalılı says Hūd presents himself as a major divine proof; Kur'an Yolu says he invokes God for his prophethood. [3 notes: ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 11:54/q11 Why does Hūd call the people to witness?  [وَٱشْهَدُوٓا۟ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the human audience functions chiefly as public proof and notice or as the target of a contemptuous challenge; some sources give both.
- 11:54/q11/p1 Their witnessing publicizes and confirms his disavowal, establishes proof against them, and makes the declaration decisive. — Al-Wahidi and al-Tabrisi say even unfit witnesses receive notice and warning so proof stands against them. Al-Qurtubi says the call makes the declaration emphatic and known, not that they qualify as witnesses. Abu Hayyan describes customary confirmation by God and people. Ibn Ashur says witnesses make renunciation certain and irrevocable. Al-Tabatabai says they can observe the idols' silence and their own inability to retaliate. The basic summons is also stated by Muqatil, al-Baghawi, al-Razi, al-Baydawi, Ibn Kathir, and Elmalılı. [15 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT(أهل المعاني); BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 11:54/q11/p2 The command taunts and dismisses the idolaters and their religion. — Al-Kashshaf compares telling an estranged person to witness that he is unloved; al-Nasafi repeats the analogy. Bursevi says the summons is sarcastic because the people are hostile. Biqaʿi says it exposes their incapacity and shows he expects all to see their religion's worthlessness. Al-Alusi compares telling an opponent to hear that one does not fear him. [7 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 11:54/q12 Why does he say 'I call God to witness' but command 'you, witness'?  [أُشْهِدُ ٱللَّهَ وَٱشْهَدُوٓا۟ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the different forms chiefly mark God's status and contempt for the people, or serve the syntax and performative force of the summons.
- 11:54/q12/p1 The shift honors God and denies the idolaters equal standing, while exposing and scorning their religion. — Al-Kashshaf says God's witness establishes monotheism whereas their commanded witness scorns their religion, and explains the imperative in place of ash'hidukum by the estranged-person analogy. Al-Biqaʿi says deference prevents phrasing the summons as if equating them with God. Al-Burhan notes the same shift of form. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL]
- 11:54/q12/p2 The imperative preserves the declaration's performative force or avoids joining unlike sentence forms. — Bursevi glosses it as 'I call you to witness' to avoid coordinating an imperative with a declarative. Ibn Ashur says the imperative prevents the performative from sounding like mere reporting. Al-Alusi reports that some allow coordination across these forms, while others supply 'and I say'; he favors the figurative reading of God's witnessing. Al-Itqan identifies the passage as a shift from past-tense qāla to imperative ish'hadū. [4 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ITQAN]

## 11:54/q13 Which verb governs 'annī barīʾ'?  [أُشْهِدُ ٱللَّهَ وَٱشْهَدُوٓا۟ أَنِّى بَرِىٓءٌۭ · grammar]
turns on: Whether competing government assigns the complement only to the second verb or permits both verbs to share it.
- 11:54/q13/p1 The second verb, ish'hadū, governs the shared clause; the first has an omitted complement. — Abu Ali, reported by al-Wahidi, compares 18:96 and says the preposition before an with ash'hidu is omitted. Al-Samin invokes competing government: using the first verb would require an object pronoun after the second. [2 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو علي); SAMIN-DURR]
- 11:54/q13/p2 Both ash'hidu and ish'hadū govern the same complement. — Bursevi describes a shared complement. Al-Alusi says unlike verbs can share an object when each can govern it. [2 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 11:54/q14 What does Hūd's declaration of being free accomplish?  [أَنِّى بَرِىٓءٌۭ مِّمَّا تُشْرِكُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the note states the content of Hūd's repudiation or highlights its formal effect as an enacted renunciation.
- 11:54/q14/p1 He explicitly repudiates their idols, partners, and idolatry before God and his people. — Muqatil, al-Baghawi, al-Razi, al-Baydawi, Ibn Kathir, Elmalılı, and Asad give the basic disavowal. Al-Qurtubi specifies freedom from worshipping the idols. Al-Alusi says his earlier call to worship God alone implicitly denied their divinity, while this states it openly; the nominal clause asserts longstanding innocence. Biqaʿi says he places the idols beside the people as powerless. The declaration answers their allegation immediately, as Ibn Ashur observes. [15 notes: MUQATIL; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 11:54/q14/p2 His public renunciation is a performative act, though he was already innocent. — Al-Tabatabai says renunciation is suitably enacted by a declaration even when innocence existed from the start. Ibn Ashur treats ash'hidu as a performative expression and says the public renunciation is made certain before witnesses. [3 notes: TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 11:54/q15 What does mā in 'mimmā tushrikūn' mean?  [مِّمَّا تُشْرِكُونَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether mā denotes the partners themselves or the act of partnership; al-Alusi favors the relative sense and rejects al-Tibi's separate analysis.
- 11:54/q15/p1 It is a relative pronoun: the beings or things they make partners with God. — Al-Samin and al-Zamakhshari allow this. Al-Alusi calls it most natural and says 'apart from Him' attaches to tushrikūn, since taking it circumstantially adds no useful restriction; he rejects al-Tibi's different account. Ibn Ashur supplies an elided relative clause. Al-Kashshaf and al-Alusi stress that God neither appointed these partners nor authorized them; Biqaʿi says they are lowly whatever or whoever they are. [8 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(زمخشري); IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 11:54/q15/p2 It is a verbal noun: Hūd disavows their act of associating partners with God. — Al-Samin, al-Zamakhshari as reported by al-Alusi, and Bursevi allow this sense; al-Kashshaf phrases the alternative as their association of gods besides Him. Al-Alusi prefers the relative reading. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(زمخشري)]
- 11:54/q15/p3 Al-Tibi offers a different syntactic analysis of the phrase. — Al-Alusi reports and rejects it as unsound; the note gives no further content of al-Tibi's analysis. [0 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الطيبي)-]

## 11:54/q16 How does the disavowal answer the accusation and lead to the challenge?  [أَنِّى بَرِىٓءٌۭ مِّمَّا تُشْرِكُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: The sources emphasize either Hūd's deliberate challenge or the evidentiary force of the opponents' failure to harm him; the two points can coexist.
- 11:54/q16/p1 Certain of the idols' falsity, Hūd intensifies his criticism and openly challenges them and their worshippers to harm him. — Al-Wahidi and Ibn al-Jawzi say he answers supposed punishment by criticizing more. Ibn Ishaq says he challenges the alleged idols to inflict worse harm. Ibn Atiyya says he defies the numerous people and their idols; Bursevi says the following fa makes the challenge flow from their harm claim and his renunciation, and 'all' answers their 'some', as al-Samarraʾi notes. Bursevi glosses kayd as secretly planning harm for people and just requital when ascribed to God, explains the circumstantial 'you and your gods all', and says Hūd refuses any delay or respite. Al-Iṣlāḥī attributes the swift interruption and challenge to zeal for monotheism. [12 notes: IBNABIHATIM(محمد بن إسحاق); WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; BURSEVI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; SAMARRAI-ASRAR]
- 11:54/q16/p2 Their idols' failure to retaliate proves their impotence and refutes the claim that an idol harmed him. — Al-Tabatabai says the challenge gives a rational proof against their divinity; his remaining unharmed is also a miraculous sign confirming his message. Ibn Ashur says the declaration interrupts Hūd's next statement, continued at 11:57. [4 notes: TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 11:54/q17 What does Hūd's solitary challenge show?  [* · interpretation]
- 11:54/q17/p1 His fearlessness before a powerful hostile people shows faith in God's protection and confirms his message. — Al-Kashshaf calls the confrontation a great sign of trust in divine protection; Abu Hayyan says it shows sincerity and confidence in the promised support. Ibn Atiyya calls his surviving the many powerful people a miracle. Al-Tabatabai says their failure to harm him confirms his message; Biqaʿi says Hūd treats both idols and people as powerless. [5 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 11:54/q18 What inward lesson is drawn from the challenge?  [* · inward]
- 11:54/q18/p1 The heart can withstand the plots of soul, its traits, Satan, desire, and the world through divine support. — Bursevi applies the surrounding challenge to these forces, which continually scheme against the heart. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 11:54/q19 How does this passage echo or differ from other Qurʾanic wording?  [* · links]
turns on: The notes identify different textual connections rather than competing readings.
- 11:54/q19/p1 The repeated first-person yāʾ suits Hūd's personal confrontation, compared with its single occurrence in the Aʿrāf account. — Al-Samarraʾi compares 7:196 with 11:54–57 and notes the repetition in Hūd's speech. His explanation of 'all' replying to the people's 'some' links 11:54 and 11:55. [2 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR]
- 11:54/q19/p2 The idolaters' madness charge against Hūd parallels accusations of madness or possession against Muḥammad. — The Study Quran cites 15:6, 23:70, 34:8, 37:36, 44:14, 68:51, and 81:22; its Kl note also compares 7:66. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN(Kl)]
- 11:54/q19/p3 Calling God 'the One who originated me' elsewhere in the passage fits the threat to Hūd: his creator will protect him. — Al-Samarraʾi connects that wording with the following statement that God can replace the people in 11:57. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 11:54/q20 What background about ʿĀd and Hūd frames this exchange?  [* · reports]
- 11:54/q20/p1 ʿĀd were powerful ancient Arab idolaters; God sent their kinsman Hūd, most rejected him, and a destructive wind ended their civilization while believers survived. — Al-Tabatabai places ʿĀd after Noah in al-Aḥqāf, notes possible first and second ʿĀd, distrusts extant tales, and says the Torah omits them. He describes imposing bodies, towns, gardens, date palms and crops (89:6–11), then monuments, imagined permanence, tyranny and idolatry. Hūd called for truth, monotheism, justice and mercy; few believed, while others called him foolish and mad and demanded punishment. They mistook the approaching cloud for rain; a barren furious wind lasting seven nights and eight days destroyed them while Hūd and believers survived. He calls Hūd the second prophet whose anti-idolatrous struggle the Qurʾan narrates and says God praises him among noble messengers. [9 notes: TABATABAI]

## 11:54/q21 What raven-and-dove report occurs among the transmitted notes?  [* · reports]
- 11:54/q21/p1 A raven sent to inspect the earth settled on a carcass; a dove returned with an olive leaf and received a neck collar and leg bands. — ʿAbd al-Razzaq transmits this account from Qatāda; the note gives no explicit link from the report to the wording of 11:54. [1 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة)]

## 11:54/q22 What principles bear on transmitting the variant reading at innī?  [إِنِّىٓ · readings]
turns on: How to understand the seven aḥruf, and why a named reading depends on transmitted recitation rather than a later guess from the codex.
- 11:54/q22/p1 The seven aḥruf are seven Arab dialects spread through the Qurʾan, not reader-selectable alternatives. — Ibn Ashur reports this from Abu ʿUbayd, Thaʿlab, al-Azhari and, by attribution, Ibn Abbas. He rejects it because the concession had to grant latitude, and the many Qurʾanic tribal dialect words, perhaps fifty according to al-Suyuti citing Abu Bakr al-Wasiti, do not explain the number seven. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو عبيد وثعلب والأزهري,السيوطي); IBNASHUR-FULL-]
- 11:54/q22/p2 The seven aḥruf are permitted pronunciation variants, including imāla, length, shortening, hamza, and easing, while Qurʾanic wording is preserved. — Ibn Ashur prefers this view. He says the community sought conformity with the Prophet's recitation through the final review and Abu Bakr's codex, with the Companions agreeing after the concession's cause ended; he rejects other weak explanations, noting as many as thirty-five proposed answers. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL+]
- 11:54/q22/p3 The seven aḥruf are the seven famous canonical readings. — Ibn Ashur rejects this equation as contrary to consensus. Abu Shama says no evidence limits readings to seven and suggests the number was chosen incidentally, auspiciously, or to imply the hadith's seven; Ibn Ammar criticizes the public confusion. Al-Suyuti says Ibn Jubayr chose five authorities for five codex regions; Ibn al-Arabi says Ibn Mujahid first chose seven, initially Yaʿqub then al-Kisaʾi, while Yaʿqub, Abu Jaʿfar and Shayba also have sound readings. [0 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو شامة,ابن العربي,ابن عمار,السيوطي)-]
- 11:54/q22/p4 A Qurʾanic reading requires sound transmission, not mere compatibility with script and grammar. — Ibn Ashur says differences existed before ʿUthman's standard codex, apart from dialectal pronunciation, and recounts al-Zibriqan's mistaken readings learned from a codex instead of a teacher, including abahu for iyyahu, ghurratin for izzatin and yanihi for yughnihi. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(حماد بن الزبرقان)]

## 11:54/q23 How are the verse and sūra boundaries around this passage established?  [* · sciences]
turns on: The distinction between transmitted boundaries, which must be followed, and proposed formal signs that help explain where they fall.
- 11:54/q23/p1 The boundaries are fixed by transmitted authority, even when length and syntactic completion vary. — Al-Zamakhshari, reported by Ibn Ashur, grounds them in transmission; Ibn Ashur says reported boundaries must be followed, as Ibn al-Arabi and al-Zamakhshari stress. Ibn al-Arabi observes the difficulty caused by varying verse lengths and endings before or after complete meanings. Ibn Ashur says the sequence of sūras may have been a matter of Companion judgment according to al-Baqillani, and reports the Prophetic-era custom of gauging time by verse counts, including a fifty-verse dawn interval. [5 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو بكر ابن العربي,الباقلاني,الزمخشري)]
- 11:54/q23/p2 A verse's extent may follow the end of revelation and be signaled by a recurring sound pattern at its close, subject to exceptions. — Ibn Ashur proposes this indicator. He defines fawāṣil by matching or near-matching final letters, sounds and spoken forms, both vocalized and unvocalized, comparing sajʿ and sustained poetic pattern. Every such close marks an ayah even when its point remains unfinished; without it a completed point rarely marks an end, though Ṣād 38:1 is an exception. Opening sūras show long alif or extended wāw/yā endings; disjoint-letter openings vary in whether they count as verses. A long passage can remain one verse until its thought concludes, as at David's judgment in 38:24. [8 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 11:54/q24 Should recitation pause at the end of this verse despite the continuing syntax?  [* · sciences]
- 11:54/q24/p1 Yes; verse endings merit a pause even where grammar continues into the next verse. — Ibn Ashur cites 11:55 and 40:71–73 as syntactically continuous across boundaries. He says fawāṣil contribute to inimitability and eloquence, and pausing lets their patterned sounds work on listeners; missing such endings spoils poetic rhyme or sajʿ, and explaining meaning belongs to a lecturer rather than overriding a reciter's craft. Some authorities, transmitted via al-Itqan from Abu Amr, call pausing at verse ends Sunnah; al-Bayhaqi's Shuʿab al-Iman prefers it even across connected syntax; Umm Salama's report in Abu Dawud describes the Prophet pausing verse by verse. [8 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(أم سلمة,البيهقي,بعضهم)]
