# 79:41: 11 questions

## 79:41/q01 What does al-maʾwā denote here?  [ٱلْمَأْوَىٰ · meaning]
- 79:41/q01/p1 Paradise is the person's final refuge, dwelling, and place of return. — Al-Ṭabarī specifies residence on Resurrection Day and refers to his discussion of 55:46. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Qurṭubī gloss maʾwā as a settled dwelling; Ibn ʿĀshūr derives it as a place noun from 'return,' the home reached after other affairs. Al-Māwardī calls it one's dwelling; al-Baghawī and Ibn Kathīr call it the final shelter. Al-Nasafī calls it the destination and place of return. Al-Farrāʾ, al-Ṭabrisī, and al-Ālūsī identify its occupant as one who fears God and restrains desire. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Biqāʿī stress that Paradise alone is this final home. [13 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 79:41/q02 How does 'the abode' mean this person's abode?  [ٱلْمَأْوَىٰ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the definite article itself carries the missing possessive sense or a pronoun/possessive construction must be understood.
- 79:41/q02/p1 The definite article substitutes for an omitted possessive pronoun: 'his abode.' — Al-Ṭabrisī reports this from another grammarian; al-Burhān attributes it to the Kufans and applies it to both Hell and Paradise. Al-Itqān includes Kufans, some Basrans, and many later grammarians. Ibn ʿĀshūr describes the Basran–Kufan difference over whether the article replaces the possessive or modifies an implied possessive construction. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ allows this analysis. [6 notes: TABRISI(الزجاج); BURHAN(الكوفيون); ITQAN(الكوفيون); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 79:41/q02/p2 An understood pronoun or possessive construction supplies 'for him' or 'his' alongside the definite article. — Al-Ṭabrisī attributes an understood 'for him' to al-Zajjāj. Al-Zajjāj invokes a poet's 'lower the gaze,' addressed to a man whose own gaze is intended, to support 'his refuge' for Hell and Paradise. Al-Itqān says opponents of article substitution supply the pronoun. Ibn ʿĀshūr records the alternative implied possessive construction; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ permits an omitted pronoun. Al-Naḥḥās says the parsing matches one just given, without further detail in this note. [6 notes: ZAJJAJ(الشاعر); NAHHAS; TABRISI(الزجاج); ITQAN(الكوفيون); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 79:41/q03 Does 'Paradise alone' exclude any prior stay in Hell?  [ٱلْجَنَّةَ ٱلْمَأْوَىٰ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether 'alone' rules out a preceding stay in Hell or describes the permanence of Paradise once entered.
- 79:41/q03/p1 Paradise alone is the person's final home or refuge. — Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Biqāʿī, and al-Ālūsī express exclusivity; al-Ālūsī says the contrast apparently applies to all people of the Fire and Paradise. Bursevī raises the apparent difficulty of a sinful believer first entering Hell. [3 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 79:41/q03/p2 The exclusivity means that whoever enters Paradise never leaves it, even if a sinful believer first enters Hell. — Bursevī offers this resolution because taking Paradise as the person's sole abode without qualification seems too exclusive for a sinful believer who first enters Hell. [1 notes: BURSEVI+]

## 79:41/q04 Why is 'Paradise' singular here when another passage has two gardens?  [ٱلْجَنَّةَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether rhyme licenses a singular sense for the dual despite a description that treats both gardens as distinct.
- 79:41/q04/p1 The dual 'two gardens' elsewhere can mean one garden; its form serves the rhyme. — Al-Itqān reports al-Farrāʾ comparing the dual jannatān with this verse's singular janna; he also likens it to calling two men ashqāhā and attributes the forms to rhyme. [1 notes: ITQAN(الفراء); BURHAN-]
- 79:41/q04/p2 The dual's description as 'both with branches' resists treating it as a single garden. — Al-Burhān suggests this verse's singular Paradise may have prompted al-Farrāʾ's rhyme explanation, but cites the dual 'both with branches' as an objection; the note also mentions 79:40–41 and 20:117. [1 notes: BURHAN+]

## 79:41/q05 What conduct brings the promised Paradise?  [ٱلْجَنَّةَ · interpretation]
turns on: Al-Qāshānī gives an inward account of the fear and restraint that the other notes describe as obedient conduct.
- 79:41/q05/p1 Fearing God and restraining desire, expressed as obedience and avoidance of sin, lead to Paradise. — Al-Farrāʾ names fear of the Lord and self-restraint. Al-Jishumī says the Garden is earned by preferring obedience and avoiding sins, both included in restraining desire; he sets obedience earning Paradise against the determinists' claim. Al-Bāqir, in Qummī's report cited by al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ, says one able to sin who refrains from fear of God and His prohibition receives Paradise. Kuranyolu says expectation of accountability leads one to set aside passing desires and live responsibly, with reward and salvation promised. [6 notes: FARRA; JISHUMI; TABRISI; TABATABAI(محمد الباقر); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 79:41/q05/p2 Fearing the Lord's station raises one to the heart and witnessing divine governance; restraint through fear or awe leads to paradise according to spiritual rank. — Al-Qāshānī connects the ascent to the heart, witness of divine governance, fear or awe, restraint, and differing ranks of paradise. [1 notes: QASHANI]

## 79:41/q06 Is the connection between obedience and Paradise a matter of human response or determinism?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether the differing outcomes are earned through obedience and sin, as al-Jishumī argues.
- 79:41/q06/p1 Paradise is attained through obedience and Hell deserved through sins, against the determinists' claim. — Al-Jishumī reads the opposed outcomes as a distinction between obedience and rebellion; he says preferring obedience and avoiding sin both fall under restraining desire. [2 notes: JISHUMI; JISHUMI+]
- 79:41/q06/p2 The determinists' claim opposes making obedience and sin the grounds of the respective outcomes. — Al-Jishumī identifies and argues against this claim, but the note gives no fuller formulation of it. [0 notes: JISHUMI-]

## 79:41/q07 Whom do the two contrasting outcomes cover?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the two descriptions exhaust individual fates, especially for people of mixed deeds or believers who enter Hell first.
- 79:41/q07/p1 The descriptions divide the people of Hell and Paradise generally by their characteristic vices and virtues. — Al-Rāzī says fear and restraint encompass all virtues, as the Hell group's paired descriptions encompass all vices. Al-Ālūsī says the contrast apparently applies to all people of the Fire and Paradise. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the relative clauses hint that each group's traits cause its deserved abode. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ says transgression and preferring worldly life characterize one exemplary class, fear and restraint the other; Islahi and Kuranyolu likewise describe opposed attitudes and outcomes. [7 notes: RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]
- 79:41/q07/p2 The two classes are exemplary; the fate of weak people and those mixing good and bad remains open to God's forgiveness or intercession. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ identifies the two classes as unbelieving wrongdoers and faithful, pious people and explicitly reserves mixed cases to God. Bursevī's concern about sinful believers initially entering Hell also shows why the contrast need not describe every stage of every person's fate. [3 notes: TABATABAI; BURSEVI; TABATABAI+]

## 79:41/q08 How are fear and restraint ordered and the contrasting groups expressed?  [* · rhetoric]
- 79:41/q08/p1 Fear comes before restraint as its cause; the paired descriptions sum up virtue and vice, and the relative clauses imply why each group receives its abode. — Al-Rāzī pairs fear with the opposite of transgression and restraint with the opposite of preferring worldly life; fear depends on knowledge (35:28) and produces restraint, so cause precedes effect. He says the two descriptions encompass all virtues and vices, referring also to 79:17 and 79:38. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the paired descriptions of remembering and the Garden a collect-then-divide structure; 'the self' has the same generic definite sense as 'the abode.' He says relative clauses indicate why each group deserves its abode. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 79:41/q09 How does the promise of Paradise fit the surrounding passage and surah?  [* · links]
- 79:41/q09/p1 It completes the contrast between the rebellious and the God-fearing after the proof of resurrection, directing listeners toward lasting reward. — Al-Biqāʿī says the surah establishes resurrection and gathering and displays humanity's final fate, with its oaths marking a decisive climax after preceding surahs' exposition. Ibn ʿĀshūr says judgment and the warning and glad tidings follow proof of resurrection; the wise choose lasting reward over passing pleasure. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾ says Hell's people come first because polytheists are being addressed. Islahi contrasts the disobedient fate with glad tidings for those who feared God; Kuranyolu describes opposed attitudes ending in loss or salvation; Study Quran locates the believers/disbelievers contrast in verses 34–41. [6 notes: BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]

## 79:41/q10 About whom were these verses reportedly revealed?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports differ in named figures, including the brother's name, and in their accounts of Badr and Uḥud.
- 79:41/q10/p1 Musʿab ibn ʿUmayr and his brother Abū ʿAzīz are linked to the opposed outcomes. — Al-Kashshāf reports that Musʿab killed his brother at Uḥud and defended the Messenger until struck by arrows; al-Rāzī gives the same setting but says spears pierced him. Ibn ʿAbbās, as reported by al-Ālūsī, calls one brother tyrannical and worldly and Musʿab fearful and restrained. Al-Ālūsī also reports Musʿab's protection of the Prophet and death by arrows, with the Prophet's words of entrusting him to God and recalling his two cloaks and golden sandal strap. His citation of al-Kashshāf calls the brother captured and says their mother was exceptionally wealthy. Al-Māwardī names only Musʿab in his revelation report; these accounts vary in details of capture, killing, and arrows or spears. [6 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس,الكشاف)]
- 79:41/q10/p2 Musʿab and his brother ʿĀmir are the two figures. — Al-Qurṭubī reports al-Ḍaḥḥāk from Ibn ʿAbbās: ʿĀmir was captured at Badr, identified himself as Musʿab's brother, and initially received kind treatment before Musʿab told his captors to bind him. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الضحاك عن ابن عباس)]
- 79:41/q10/p3 Abū Jahl and Musʿab are linked to the two verses. — Al-Ālūsī reports this as another account from Ibn ʿAbbās. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 79:41/q10/p4 The first of the two verses concerns al-Naḍr and his son al-Ḥārith. — Al-Ālūsī records this additional report without assigning the Paradise verse separately. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس)]

## 79:41/q11 What is meant by reports of the Quran's seven letters and Paradise's seven gates?  [ٱلْجَنَّةَ · reports]
turns on: Whether 'seven letters' names variant Arabic wording or distinct meanings, and whether the seven gates tradition is about Quranic themes rather than conflicting recitations.
- 79:41/q11/p1 The seven letters in recitation are Arabic tongues; the seven gates point to Quranic contents that lead practitioners to Paradise. — Al-Ṭabarī cites established reports through ʿUmar, Ibn Masʿūd, Ubayy and others in which the Prophet approved differing recitations and said God commanded seven letters. He argues that their dispute concerned wording, since they accepted the Quran's commands, prohibitions, promises, warnings, prophetic arguments, admonitions, and parables. Semantically opposed variants would make God command, forbid, and permit the same act, contradict Q 4:82, and imply the Prophet gave opposing judgments for one matter. Ibn Kathīr reports al-Ṭabarī's point that Arab dialects exceed seven, so the Quran came in some Arabic tongues; he also transmits reports from Ubayy and Ibn Masʿūd that the Quran descended from seven gates of Paradise, and al-Ṭabarī's gloss of these as commands, prohibitions, encouragement, warning, stories, and parables. [9 notes: TAB-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو جعفر بن جرير الطبري,أبي بن كعب وعبد الله بن مسعود)]
- 79:41/q11/p2 The seven aspects are Quranic themes such as commands, prohibitions, exhortation, warnings, stories, and parables, distinct from the recitation reports. — Ibn Kathīr says advocates of the thematic account do not claim it explains the earlier recitation reports; they mean the Quran came in seven aspects, as transmitted from the Prophet and Companions. He frames the question of evidence for the dialect account over the thematic one. Al-Ṭabarī himself explains the seven gates as Quranic meanings leading practitioners to Paradise. [4 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو جعفر بن جرير الطبري); TAB-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL+]
- 79:41/q11/p3 The seven letters are seven divergent meanings, including conflicting legal or doctrinal meanings. — Al-Ṭabarī identifies this opposing interpretation and rejects it: conflicting meanings would affirm contradiction in the Quran despite Q 4:82, make one act simultaneously commanded, forbidden, and permitted, and attribute opposed judgments on one matter to the Prophet. [0 notes: TAB-FULL(من خالف قولنا في ذلك)-]
