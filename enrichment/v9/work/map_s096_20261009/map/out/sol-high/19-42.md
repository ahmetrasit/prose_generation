# 19:42: 28 questions

## 19:42/q01 How is 'when he said' attached to the preceding description of Abraham?  [إِذْ قَالَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether idh is appositional or governed by kāna or the preceding descriptions, and whether those parses meet the grammatical objections.
- 19:42/q01/p1 It is an appositive of inclusion to Abraham. — Al-Samīn cites an earlier parallel at 19:16 and notes the intervening 'he was truthful, a prophet'; Bursawī and Ibn ʿĀshūr take the time as specifying Abraham, and Ibn ʿĀshūr says this moment merits remembrance. Al-Ālūsī acknowledges objections about an uncoordinated substitute and the usual indeclinability of idh, yet prefers this parse. [5 notes: SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ)-]
- 19:42/q01/p2 It depends on kāna, with the description intervening parenthetically. — Al-Bayḍāwī allows the parenthesis; al-Nasafī reports the attachment. The Shaykh objects that governing a temporal adverb by kāna is disputed; al-Ālūsī notes further grammatical problems. [4 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ)-; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 19:42/q01/p3 It depends on ṣiddīqan nabiyyan or its descriptive sense: Abraham had both qualities when he spoke. — Al-Zamakhsharī, Abū al-Baqāʾ, al-Nasafī and al-Samīn give the reading. The Shaykh objects that a compound cannot jointly govern the adverb, ṣiddīq is already modified unless Kufan grammar is used, and dependence on nabī would imply prophethood began then. [6 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء,الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR+; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ)-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 19:42/q02 Who was the father Abraham addressed?  [لِأَبِيهِ · referent]
turns on: Whether ab denotes a biological father here or a figurative paternal relative; sources appeal to the plain Qur'anic wording, other family passages, and reported identifications.
- 19:42/q02/p1 He was Āzar, Abraham's father. — Muqātil, al-Jishumī, al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, al-Qurṭubī and Ibn ʿĀshūr name Āzar; al-Jishumī calls him an unbeliever and cites other Qur'anic verses against figurative reinterpretation. Al-Ḍamghānī and Ibn al-Jawzī cite this as an instance of ab meaning an actual father, with 6:74 and other family passages. Islāhī rejects the uncle claim and says later Sabāʾī writers promoted it; he says he had refuted the Torah's account. A reported prophetic exhortation to call one's father 'my father' as Abraham did appears to imply biological fatherhood. [14 notes: MUQATIL; DAMGHANI; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أهل التفسير); ALUSI-FULL(أنس؛ أبو نعيم؛ الديلمي); ISLAHI-TADABBUR+]
- 19:42/q02/p2 Āzar was Abraham's paternal uncle, or another paternal figure rather than his biological father. — Islāhī reports the Jewish uncle claim and says exegetes accepted it without much deliberation; al-Ālūsī says many hold the uncle reading figuratively. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the addressee was an uncle, maternal grandfather, or stepfather, referring to an earlier discussion. [4 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Jews,our exegetes); ALUSI-FULL(أنس؛ أبو نعيم؛ الديلمي); TABATABAI; JISHUMI-; ISLAHI-TADABBUR-]

## 19:42/q03 What does ab mean in this address?  [أَبِيهِ يَٰٓأَبَتِ · meaning]
- 19:42/q03/p1 It means father, including a direct or near father in family address. — Al-Ḍamghānī cites this address, Āzar and 80:34; Ibn al-Jawzī cites 4:11, 6:74, 28:23 and 80:34. He contrasts light bāʾ ab with doubled bāʾ abb, 'pasture,' and notes abba, 'prepare to depart,' with forms abā, abābah, abīban and al-Aʿshā's poetic example. Al-Zajjāj notes the dual abawān includes the mother, citing 4:11 and feminine-looking descriptions of males such as rajul rabʿa and ghulām yafaʿa. [7 notes: DAMGHANI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أهل التفسير); ZAJJAJ]

## 19:42/q04 What is the tāʾ in yā abati?  [يَٰٓأَبَتِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether tāʾ replaces possessive yāʾ, intensifies an understood possession, or is a vocative or pause addition.
- 19:42/q04/p1 It replaces the possessive yāʾ of 'my father' in this vocative. — Al-Khalīl says a hāʾ replaces yāʾ, readily omitted in address; Sībawayh and later commentators treat the resulting tāʾ as the substitute. Al-Rāzī, al-Nasafī, Bursawī and al-Ālūsī say substitute and original are normally not combined, although rare alif forms have competing explanations. Ibn ʿĀshūr suggests an old Arabic form; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī restricts the substitution to address. Al-Zajjāj compares yā amma and yā khāla, where a feminine-looking form can address either sex, and says yā ab is possible only in vocative use. [14 notes: TAB-FULL(نحويو البصرة); ZAJJAJ(الخليل); JISHUMI(غير مسمى); IBNATIYYA-FULL(سيبويه); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL(ابن عامر); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 19:42/q04/p2 The tāʾ strengthens the possessive address, with the possessive yāʾ understood. — Al-Jishumī and al-Ṭabrisī say the ending intensifies possession; al-Jishumī says kasra seeks the yāʾ and a stop keeps tāʾ. Al-Farrāʾ, as reported by Ibn ʿAṭiyya, keeps an implicit possessive yāʾ and pauses with tāʾ. [3 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; IBNATIYYA-FULL(سيبويه)]
- 19:42/q04/p3 It is an added vocative or pause marker, rather than a simple replacement of yāʾ. — Basran grammarians call hāʾ an added vocative form, with abah in pause and tāʾ in connection or possession, and permit abbreviated yā ab (12:4). Kufans call hāʾ in abah/ummah a conventional pause marker which becomes tāʾ before possessive yāʾ. An alternative analysis treats tāʾ as an inserted vocative marker like yā Ṭalḥa; al-Jishumī reports fatḥa as representing yā abatāh. [4 notes: TAB-FULL(نحويو البصرة,نحويو الكوفة); JISHUMI(غير مسمى); IBNATIYYA-FULL(هارون)]

## 19:42/q05 How is the tāʾ of yā abati read?  [يَٰٓأَبَتِ · readings]
turns on: The transmitted kasra and fatḥa vocalizations, their proposed derivations, and Hārūn's objection to fatḥa.
- 19:42/q05/p1 It is read with kasra. — Al-Jishumī says all but the named fatḥa readers use kasra; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Ibn ʿĀshūr call this the majority reading. Al-Nasafī reports Ibn ʿĀmir with either vowel. QIRAAT-ER describes kasra as a stabilized vocative ending. [5 notes: JISHUMI(ابن عامر وأبو جعفر); IBNATIYYA-FULL; NASAFI-FULL(ابن عامر); IBNASHUR-FULL; QIRAAT-ER]
- 19:42/q05/p2 It is read with fatḥa. — Al-Jishumī names Ibn ʿĀmir and Abū Jaʿfar throughout the Qur'an; Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī also name al-Aʿraj. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Ibn ʿĀshūr explain it as deleting the alif of yā abatā; al-Jishumī reports yā abatāh, while QIRAAT-ER suggests an accusative-like vocative ending or another vocalization tradition. Hārūn criticized it, but al-Ālūsī calls it valid. Ibn Masʿūd or ʿAbd Allāh's codex has wā abati with wāw for yāʾ. [7 notes: JISHUMI(ابن عامر وأبو جعفر,غير مسمى); IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عامر، الأعرج، أبو جعفر، هارون); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عامر؛ الأعرج؛ أبو جعفر); IBNASHUR-FULL; QIRAAT-ER; IBNATIYYA-FULL(هارون)-]

## 19:42/q06 How is yā abati pronounced when stopping?  [يَٰٓأَبَتِ · readings]
turns on: Whether the ending behaves as a feminine hāʾ in pause or retains the written tāʾ; the readers transmit both.
- 19:42/q06/p1 Pause with hāʾ. — Al-Zajjāj, al-Jishumī and reports of Sībawayh allow hāʾ as the feminine-looking ending. Al-Naḥḥās gives that rationale; Ibn al-Jazarī says Ibn Kathīr, Ibn ʿĀmir, Abū Jaʿfar and Yaʿqūb stop with hāʾ in Yūsuf, Maryam, al-Qaṣaṣ and al-Ṣāffāt. The Kufan report permits hāʾ in yā abatā, though calls it unusual. [8 notes: TAB-FULL(نحويو البصرة,نحويو الكوفة); ZAJJAJ; JISHUMI(الزجاج); IBNATIYYA-FULL(سيبويه); NAHHAS; NASHR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 19:42/q06/p2 Pause with tāʾ. — Ibn Kaysān permits it because the ending attaches to an inseparable element; al-Farrāʾ retains tāʾ, and al-Jishumī says the possessive analysis keeps tāʾ. Ibn al-Jazarī says the other readers stop on written tāʾ. The Kufan report permits this in yā abatā. [7 notes: TAB-FULL(نحويو الكوفة); JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL(سيبويه); NAHHAS; NASHR; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 19:42/q07 Why does Abraham say yā abati and repeat it?  [يَٰٓأَبَتِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the focus is affectionate kinship or the vocative's function in directing attention to the ensuing question.
- 19:42/q07/p1 It appeals tenderly to kinship and softens correction of his father. — Al-Zamakhsharī says each of four counsels begins this way as a plea for closeness; Abū Ḥayyān, al-Biqāʿī, al-Ālūsī, Islāhī and KURANYOLU stress courtesy despite idolatry and threats. Al-Burhān and al-Itqān compare Luqmān's yā bunayya and Aaron's yā ibn umm. Bursawī says gentleness eases acceptance; al-Ālūsī draws a rule of kindness in calling relatives to truth. A prophetic report says God told Abraham to perfect manners even toward unbelievers, promising shelter beneath the Throne and nearness; Abū Hurayra transmits a version. [14 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(الرسول صلى الله عليه وآله وسلم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو هريرة,الزمخشري); BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; QUSHAYRI]
- 19:42/q07/p2 The added vocative summons an already present father to attend to the argument. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says Abraham begins with sensory evidence and anticipates resistance to advice from a son. Al-Burhān and al-Itqān classify a call preceding an interrogative or declarative statement, with this address as an interrogative example. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; BURHAN; ITQAN]

## 19:42/q08 What kind of question is 'why do you worship'?  [لِمَ تَعْبُدُ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the question is described as an indirect literal request for grounds or as a rebuke emphasizing the irrationality of the act.
- 19:42/q08/p1 It asks for a reason and thereby exposes the absence of any sound justification. — Ibn ʿĀshūr prefers the literal question following al-Kashshāf: the father is challenged to supply a reason, and the wording indirectly denies that he can. His grandfather says the question lets the father recognize his inability to justify worship. Al-Zamakhsharī says the opening awakens him to entrenched error; al-Biqāʿī and al-Ālūsī say the indirect approach avoids provoking obstinacy while exposing futility. [6 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL+]
- 19:42/q08/p2 It is a compassionate rebuke of manifestly irrational idol worship. — Al-Wāḥidī describes a rebuke; Ibn ʿĀshūr says revelation enabled Abraham to convey invalidity to his people. Islāhī says handmade idols' powerlessness violates intellect and human nature; the Study Quran notes parallel arguments at 7:194–196. [4 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]

## 19:42/q09 What does taʿbudu mean here?  [تَعْبُدُ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether this use denotes literal cultic worship alone or a broader act of supplication, submission, or obedience.
- 19:42/q09/p1 It means literal worship of idols. — Al-Rāzī says the later prohibition on worshipping Satan at 19:44 uses 'worship' figuratively for obedience, but that does not alter this phrase's literal meaning. Al-Jishumī reports worship as the straightforward sense; Muqātil and other commentators describe the father's idolatry. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; JISHUMI; MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM]
- 19:42/q09/p2 It can describe supplication, submission, or obedience. — Al-Jishumī lists these senses alongside worship, without choosing a single gloss; he later notes worship and following are acts of human agents, using this against opponents' account of created acts. [2 notes: JISHUMI]

## 19:42/q10 How is mā in mā lā yasmaʿ construed?  [مَا · grammar]
turns on: Whether mā introduces a relative clause or is an indefinite noun qualified by the clause.
- 19:42/q10/p1 It is a relative pronoun. — Al-Zamakhsharī allows this in both mā lā yasmaʿ and the following mā lam ya'tika; Abū Ḥayyān says it is likely. Al-Ālūsī reports the option. [3 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 19:42/q10/p2 It is an indefinite noun described by the following clause. — Al-Zamakhsharī, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī report this alternate parse. [3 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 19:42/q11 What does mā refer to as the object of worship?  [مَا · referent]
turns on: Whether the objects are simply inert idols or images connected to planets, intercessors or talismanic beliefs.
- 19:42/q11/p1 It denotes idols, images or statues. — Al-Zajjāj, al-Baghawī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Ṭabrisī, Bursawī and others identify lifeless idols. The Study Quran reports that some commentators take simple idols. Muqātil, Yaḥyā, al-Ṭabarī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr explain the same object through its inability to hear, see or help. [20 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(some commentators)]
- 19:42/q11/p2 It may include celestial bodies and other objects worshipped by his people. — Al-Jishumī reports this broader referent alongside idols; al-Rāzī reports planetary images and planets among possible theories of the father's worship. [2 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL]
- 19:42/q11/p3 The idols may have represented revered people believed to intercede with God. — Al-Rāzī reports this possible rationale; the Study Quran attributes the proposal to him alongside powerful stars. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(some commentators)]
- 19:42/q11/p4 They may have been talismans linked to rare planetary conjunctions. — Al-Rāzī lists this among possible rationales and later answers that benefit was sought from talismans or planets. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 19:42/q12 What does saying the idols neither hear nor see convey?  [لَا يَسْمَعُ وَلَا يُبْصِرُ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the denial marks literal lack of sensory perception in idols or the broader rhetorical usage in which ineffectual perception is treated as absent.
- 19:42/q12/p1 They lack perception: they hear and see nothing, including prayers, praise and worship. — Al-Ṭabarī says they cannot answer a call or see and assist; al-Wāḥidī says they cannot hear prayer or see worship. Al-Rāzī says they cannot distinguish obedience from disobedience; al-Ālūsī allows either specific praise and humility or any perceptible thing. The Study Quran relays al-Rāzī's point; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī contrasts the maker's knowledge with the product's lack of awareness. [14 notes: TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(al-Rāzī)]
- 19:42/q12/p2 Hearing and sight can be denied figuratively where they bring no useful knowledge or effect. — Al-Jurjānī says life without knowledge can be called death, and unbeneficial hearing or sight deafness or blindness; a person so called gains nothing from the faculties. Qualifying 'deaf to what offends' limits the denial to a circumstance. He also distinguishes stronger and milder exclusion by 'the man' versus 'a man' or 'a thing'. [5 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR]

## 19:42/q13 Why are the objects of 'hear' and 'see' unstated?  [لَا يَسْمَعُ وَلَا يُبْصِرُ · rhetoric]
- 19:42/q13/p1 The statement asserts the complete absence of hearing and sight, so no particular object is intended. — Al-Zamakhsharī says the objects are omitted and not intended; Abū Ḥayyān says the point is lack of the faculties without specifying an object. Al-Nasafī says shayʾ can be supplied. Al-Burhān compares 'He gives life and death': omission focuses on divine power or the idol's lack of senses. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURHAN]

## 19:42/q14 What does 'cannot avail you anything' mean?  [وَلَا يُغْنِى عَنكَ شَيْـًۭٔا · meaning]
- 19:42/q14/p1 It cannot bring benefit, meet a need, avert harm or protect from punishment. — Muqātil specifies benefit in the Hereafter; al-Ṭabrisī mentions worldly help. Al-Wāḥidī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Ṭabarī and the Study Quran emphasize warding off harm, including God's punishment; al-Ālūsī glosses benefit. Al-Rāzī says worship gains nothing and avoids no danger, and cites Abraham's breaking of idols at 21:63 as proof they cannot protect even themselves. Al-Biqāʿī adds that even a blind and deaf being might help by speech, whereas idols cannot. Al-Ṭabarī contrasts the Lord who hears, sees, assists and removes harm. [20 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; YAHYA-SALLAM; SULAMI(بعضهم); WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(Ṭabarī)]

## 19:42/q15 What is the grammatical role of shayʾan?  [شَيْـًۭٔا · grammar]
turns on: Whether shayʾan names the action or the thing provided.
- 19:42/q15/p1 It is an accusative verbal noun conveying no benefit at all. — Al-Zamakhsharī supplies a parallel sense for the preceding verbs; al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī report the same possibility. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 19:42/q15/p2 It is the direct object of yughni. — Al-Zamakhsharī compares aghnā ʿannī wajhaka; al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī report the object parse. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 19:42/q16 What is Abraham's argument against worshipping these objects?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the decisive defect is sensory and practical incapacity, the reversal of maker and made, created dependence, or the father's proposed intermediary doctrine.
- 19:42/q16/p1 Their lack of hearing, sight and power to benefit or harm makes worship futile. — Al-Rāzī calls these three disqualifying attributes and notes that an idol cannot distinguish worshippers, hear prayer or yield ritual benefit. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls the question a summary of several arguments; Islāhī says handmade powerless idols violate intellect and human nature. The Study Quran cites 7:194–196. Al-Biqāʿī says a worshipped being should not be inferior to its worshipper. [13 notes: RAZI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN(al-Rāzī); BIQAI-FULL]
- 19:42/q16/p2 A human maker is more capable than the thing made and should not worship it. — Al-Rāzī compares a hearing, seeing human with an idol; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the maker has knowledge and power lacking in the product and compares 37:95. His note on the language of rational beings argues that what lacks awareness cannot merit lordship, comparing 26:70 and 26:74. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 19:42/q16/p3 Worship belongs only to the independent creator and original benefactor, so even capable created beings do not qualify. — Al-Zamakhsharī says even an angel or prophet with hearing, sight and power remains a creature; worship is ultimate veneration owed to the creator, provider, life-giver, death-giver, rewarder and punisher, and worship elsewhere is injustice and disbelief. Al-Rāzī cites 19:36 and 2:28; al-Jishumī lists God's power, knowledge, life and eternality; al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī likewise exclude dependent angels and prophets. Al-Rāzī contrasts God who answers prayer and meets needs (20:46; 27:62). [10 notes: JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 19:42/q16/p4 The criticism still reaches a father who sought help through planets, talismans or intermediaries. — Al-Rāzī reports the objection that a father may have treated idols as creators, planetary images, honored intercessors, or conjunction talismans. He calls belief in carved wood as creator madness, but concedes the sensory argument does not directly refute planetary or intercession doctrines; his reply is that obedience to idols brings no benefit and neglect no harm. The Study Quran reports his star or revered-person explanation. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(some commentators)]

## 19:42/q17 What does the verse imply about divine attributes and human dependence?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether the point is God's necessary capacities or the servant's resulting dependence on Him.
- 19:42/q17/p1 God must hear, see, know and possess power to answer and benefit. — Al-Qushayrī requires perfect hearing, sight and power over benefit and harm. Al-Rāzī says Abraham's rebuke proves these attributes must be known before invocation; an incapable deity would fall under the rebuke. His fuller exposition says an idol unable to distinguish obedience cannot be divine, whereas God hears and answers. He rejects the view that one may invoke God without grasping the premises. [4 notes: QUSHAYRI; RAZI-FULL; RAZI-FULL+; RAZI-FULL(بعض الناس)-]
- 19:42/q17/p2 Human limits require divine aid and lead the servant to humble petition. — Al-Rāzī says one cannot fully guard interests, gain what is absent or preserve what is present; humans are weak in knowledge and action. Even rigorous thinkers can remain mistaken for life, and the few rightly guided show unaided will is insufficient. Certainty about this yields humility and inward then spoken invocation for refuge. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 19:42/q18 What inward lesson is drawn from the powerless object of worship?  [* · inward]
- 19:42/q18/p1 One should not attach the heart to a creature or attribute independent causal power to it. — Al-Qushayrī says no creature originates or creates, so imagining even a sliver of independent efficacy resembles idol worship. Al-Qāshānī takes worship of anything besides God as seeking created things and assigning them causal effect, though they truly provide no benefit. Al-Sulamī counsels against relying on one unable to hear prayer, see one's state or meet needs. [3 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم); QUSHAYRI; QASHANI]

## 19:42/q19 How does this address fit Abraham's story and the sūra?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the connection foregrounds the prophetic narrative, its monotheistic message to later audiences, or contrast with the star episode.
- 19:42/q19/p1 It recounts Abraham's prophetic challenge to his idolatrous father and people. — Al-Ṭabarī introduces Abraham as God's intimate friend, truthful in speech, reports and promises, mentioning 3:41. Al-Nasafī says God commands Muhammad to recite and convey the story (26:69). Ibn Kathīr says the audience worship idols while claiming Abraham's way. The Study Quran lists other confrontations at 6:74–83, 21:51, 26:69, 37:84 and 43:26. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the rebuke came through revelation to convey invalidity to the people. [5 notes: TAB-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 19:42/q19/p2 The Abraham account supports the sūra's teaching of one God and contrasts it with later religious claims. — Asad connects Abraham and Jesus as mortal servants of the one God. Islāhī says the prophets' accounts show Christians that Muhammad's message is continuous with earlier prophets and warn Quraysh that Abraham's and Ishmael's religion differs from their practice. [2 notes: ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 19:42/q19/p3 The direct refutation of idols contrasts with Abraham's gradual testing of the star hypothesis. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī infers from 6:74 and 19:42 that Abraham stayed with the star hypothesis until it set; prior knowledge that it would set would have allowed as immediate a refutation as his argument against idols. [1 notes: TABATABAI]

## 19:42/q20 How does Abraham's counsel develop after this question?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the notes explain the logical sequence of counsel or its ultimately ineffective outcome.
- 19:42/q20/p1 He moves from sensory evidence and uselessness to guidance, Satan's influence and a warning. — Ibn ʿĀshūr's grandfather traces the sequence: the father cannot justify worship of what cannot hear or see, then cannot gain from it; the son next states his limited received knowledge, addresses resistance to a younger adviser, names Satan and warns hypothetically that idols cannot punish desertion. Al-Bayḍāwī says reason requires purposeful action and Abraham invites his father to a straight path on knowledge he received (19:43). [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL]
- 19:42/q20/p2 Repeated compassionate counsel cannot undo a prior decree of exclusion. — Al-Qushayrī says Abraham showed every form of compassion, but his father's continued refusal shows later effort did not overturn the prior decree. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]

## 19:42/q21 What conduct is reported or taught by Abraham's address to his father?  [* · reports]
turns on: The notes convey separate reports and lessons about manners, paternal address, and advice from a younger person.
- 19:42/q21/p1 He was commanded to maintain good manners even toward unbelievers. — Al-Zamakhsharī transmits a prophetic report in which God promises Abraham shelter beneath the Throne and nearness if he perfects manners even with unbelievers; Abū Ḥayyān gives an Abū Hurayra report and says he omits al-Zamakhsharī's extended exhortation. [2 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(الرسول صلى الله عليه وآله وسلم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو هريرة)]
- 19:42/q21/p2 A child should address a father as 'my father' rather than by name. — Al-Ālūsī reports from Anas through Abū Nuʿaym and al-Daylamī a saying raised to the Prophet; he observes its possible implication for biological fatherhood and notes the competing figurative uncle interpretation. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أنس؛ أبو نعيم؛ الديلمي)]
- 19:42/q21/p3 A younger person may advise an elder when guided by revelation. — KURANYOLU draws this lesson from Abraham's respectful address despite his father's idolatry and threats. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 19:42/q22 What background is given for deficient and extended final-weak nouns?  [* · grammar]
turns on: Whether the final weak radical follows a short vowel or an alif; several exceptions require attested usage.
- 19:42/q22/p1 Deficient forms have a final weak radical after a short vowel, identified by pattern comparison or attested usage. — Sībawayh says the final alif replaces yāʾ or wāw and ordinary case endings are hidden. He compares maʿṭā/mushtarā with makhraj/muʿtarak, maslaqā/maslanqā with sound-root parallels, aʿshā/aʿmā/aqnā with afʿal plus fiʿl, and weak verbal nouns hawā, radā, ṣadā, lawā, karā, ghawā, ṭawā with sound-root fiʿl or faʿlān. Group plurals ʿurā and furā are short. Qafā, raḥā, rajā al-biʾr, riḍā and badā require hearing rather than unguarded analogy; he gives ḥalab as badā's parallel. [10 notes: SIBAWAYH]
- 19:42/q22/p2 Extended forms have a final weak radical after alif, shown by regular-root analogues or attestation. — Sībawayh gives istiṣqāʾ, ishtirāʾ, iʿṭāʾ, al-iḥbinṭāʾ, al-islinqāʾ, action noun al-nizāʾ versus al-qināṣ, and ḍamma-initial ʿuwāʾ, duʿāʾ, zuqāʾ. Initial ḍamma seldom yields a deficient verbal noun; samāʾ, rishāʾ, ālāʾ and miqlāʾ require hearing. Afʿila plurals usually indicate an extended singular, as aqbiya/arshiya, with nadā/andiya an exception. Ghirāʾ and ẓamāʾ are attested anomalies. Al-Khalīl compares shortened bukā to ḥuzn. [10 notes: SIBAWAYH(الخليل)]

## 19:42/q23 What distinction between literal and figurative predication is supplied alongside the verse?  [* · rhetoric]
- 19:42/q23/p1 Figurative wording may alter the action meant, or assign a real action to a figurative agent. — Al-Jurjānī treats escape from near-death as 'created anew' because the state is likened to nonexistence. 'Spring made flowers' uses real making attributed to spring rather than God, as do verbs such as weaving, forging, embroidering and painting; 'the report pleased me' likewise makes the report figuratively causal. In the Qur'anic revival of earth at 30:19 he locates metaphor in 'life', with a referent other than literal earth. He says literal or metaphorical status must attach to the content asserted or its attribution, since every assertion is of something. [6 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR]

## 19:42/q24 What kinds of omitted objects do the rhetorical examples distinguish?  [* · rhetoric]
- 19:42/q24/p1 An object may be omitted to assert an act itself, to leave a known object understood, or to conceal it artfully. — Al-Jurjānī says adding 'dinars' changes 'he gives' into an assertion about a particular gift; conventional examples imply lending an ear or lowering an eyelid. Al-Buḥturī omits what observers see and listeners hear to veil praise of al-Muʿtazz's virtues and imply his caliphal right against al-Mustaʿīn; visible and audible virtues torment rivals. Al-Burhān gives a separate parable of disbelievers like cattle hearing a herder's call, where things invoked cannot hear, see or understand and an ellipsis is present. [7 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL; BURHAN]

## 19:42/q25 What linked-clause arrangements are illustrated in the ancillary rhetorical discussion?  [* · rhetoric]
- 19:42/q25/p1 Interwoven paired clauses and division followed by collection make later parts depend on earlier ones. — Al-Jurjānī describes jointly planned compositions without one exhaustive rule: al-Buḥturī pairs love with estrangement and depicts time's attack on the weak; another conditional pairs remembered kinship with tears and conflict with bloodshed. Sulaymān ibn Dāwūd al-Quḍāʿī balances fall and rise, prosperity and hardship. Kathīr's search for ʿAzza becomes vanishing cloud shade, completed as the seeker settles beneath it. Ḥassān divides harm to enemies and benefit to allies, then collects them into inherited disposition, sharpened by saying new traits are worst. An unnamed poet first separates two hopes for permanence and then joins both in tomorrow's change, which brings peace. [11 notes: JURJANI-DALAIL]

## 19:42/q26 What general criteria for transmitted readings are mentioned?  [* · readings]
- 19:42/q26/p1 Readers transmit established regional traditions, while specialists evaluate report strength and linguistic correctness. — Ibn Mujāhid compares qirāʾa criticism with hadith criticism: widespread sound reports, sound uncommon reports, rare dialect, grammatically weak recitation, and obvious or subtle errors must be distinguished. Only specialists in reports and rulings can judge disputes, and persistence in error can discredit a transmitter. Mecca, Medina, Kufa, Basra and Syria followed locally established readers accepted in public and private. He quotes Abū ʿAmr likening his generation to small plants beneath earlier scholars' tall palms. [5 notes: IBNMUJAHID(أبو عمرو بن العلاء)]

## 19:42/q27 What unrelated lexical equivalence is recorded in the notes?  [* · meaning]
- 19:42/q27/p1 ʿAṣī and ʿĀṣī have the same meaning. — Al-Naḥḥās reports al-Kisāʾī's equivalence without tying it to a word in this verse. [1 notes: NAHHAS(الكسائي)]

## 19:42/q28 What illustration of reasoning about ultimate loss is quoted?  [* · reports]
- 19:42/q28/p1 A couplet says the speaker loses nothing if deniers of resurrection are right, while they lose if he is right. — Ibn ʿĀshūr attributes the couplet to ʿAlī and contrasts the speaker with an astrologer and physician. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(علي رضي الله عنه)]
