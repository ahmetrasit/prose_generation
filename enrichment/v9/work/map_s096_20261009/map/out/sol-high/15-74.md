# 15:74: 12 questions

## 15:74/q01 What does the pronoun in 'its upper part' and 'its lower part' refer to?  [عَٰلِيَهَا سَافِلَهَا · referent]
turns on: Whether the pronoun follows the expressly mentioned singular city or can refer to the understood plural towns despite their not being named earlier.
- 15:74/q01/p1 The previously mentioned city or town of Lot's people. — Abu Hayyan and al-Samin point to the city as the expressed antecedent; al-Samin prefers it. Al-Alusi calls it the most natural reference. Ibn Ashur says both feminine pronouns refer to the city, while the later plural 'upon them' resumes the people. Al-Baydawi also allows the city; Ibn Atiyya's report and Bursevi speak of a single town. Al-Biqa'i describes the city's tops going below and notes a difference from the pronoun reference in Hud. [8 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(قيل); BURSEVI; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 15:74/q01/p2 The towns of Lot's people. — Al-Zamakhshari takes the pronoun as referring to the towns, although, as Abu Hayyan notes, the word 'towns' had not previously appeared; al-Samin transmits the same view. Al-Nasafi also names the towns; al-Baydawi and al-Alusi allow the plural reference. Muqatil identifies four cities: Sodom, Damura, Amura, and Sabura. [7 notes: MUQATIL; KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL]

## 15:74/q02 What happened when God made its upper part its lower part?  [فَجَعَلْنَا عَٰلِيَهَا سَافِلَهَا · interpretation]
turns on: The reports describe a literal inversion of the settlement; Farahi explains the overturned houses through an intensifying sand-laden wind.
- 15:74/q02/p1 The settlement was turned upside down, its high part becoming its low part. — Muqatil speaks of four overturned cities. Al-Baydawi explains the high and low parts as an inversion of the city or villages. Ibn Atiyya, al-Nasafi, Ibn Kathir, Bursevi, and al-Biqa'i describe the inversion; Bursevi says the town remained inverted on its people. Al-Burhan observes that this wording makes both the starting part and the resulting part of a transformation explicit. [9 notes: MUQATIL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 15:74/q02/p2 A wind laden with sand and pebbles overturned and buried the houses. — Farahi says the wind first formed clay stones that pelted the people, then grew stronger until the houses were overturned and buried; he relates this account to 29:40, 11:82, and 53:53-54. Islahi introduces Farahi's detailed account as an explanation of this verse's concise punishment statement. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Ḥamīd al-Dīn Farāhī)]

## 15:74/q03 How was the settlement lifted before its overturning?  [فَجَعَلْنَا عَٰلِيَهَا سَافِلَهَا · reports]
- 15:74/q03/p1 Gabriel raised the settlement high and then overturned it. — Ibn Atiyya reports that Gabriel uprooted the city and raised it until heaven's angels heard its roosters and dogs. Al-Nasafi says Gabriel lifted the towns to heaven. Bursevi says he carried the town on his wing close to heaven before turning it upon its people. Ibn Kathir says the towns were lifted into the sky and overturned without naming the agent in this note. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(قيل); NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BURSEVI]

## 15:74/q04 How are the two parts of the city parsed after 'We made'?  [فَجَعَلْنَا عَٰلِيَهَا سَافِلَهَا · grammar]
- 15:74/q04/p1 'Its upper part' is the first object of jaʿalnā and 'its lower part' is the second. — Bursevi gives this parsing. Al-Alusi specifies that the first object is the city's surface and what stands on it, while its lower part is the second object. [2 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 15:74/q05 Why does the wording name the upper part before the lower part?  [عَٰلِيَهَا سَافِلَهَا · rhetoric]
- 15:74/q05/p1 The order heightens the horror by stating the original high part and then its low destination. — Bursevi says this order is more terrifying than reversing the two objects. Al-Burhan says expressions of transformation can make source and destination explicit; he compares the expression 'day a livelihood,' in which maʿāsh can denote a time or the verbal noun livelihood. [2 notes: BURSEVI; BURHAN]

## 15:74/q06 When did the stones fall relative to the overturning?  [وَأَمْطَرْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ حِجَارَةًۭ · reports]
- 15:74/q06/p1 They fell amid the upheaval, before the overturning was complete. — Bursevi explicitly says the rain came during the upheaval and before the final inversion; al-Alusi says it came amid the overturning. In Farahi's account the sand-laden wind first pelted the people with clay stones, then intensified until it overturned and buried the houses. [3 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Ḥamīd al-Dīn Farāhī)]

## 15:74/q07 Whom did the rain of stones strike?  [عَلَيْهِمْ حِجَارَةًۭ · referent]
- 15:74/q07/p1 Lot's people, including inhabitants and people who survived or were away from the towns. — Al-Tabari, al-Nasafi, Ibn Kathir, al-Biqa'i, al-Alusi, and Kur'an Yolu describe stones falling on the people or inhabitants. Muqatil says a stone reached and killed someone outside the four towns. Ibn Atiyya's report says survivors of the overturning were struck. Al-Kawashi, as reported by Bursevi, says stones also reached those away from the afflicted lands. Ibn Ashur identifies 'upon them' as resuming the earlier plural group rather than the singular city. [10 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(قيل); NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(الكواشي); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 15:74/q08 What does sijjīl mean?  [سِجِّيلٍ · meaning]
turns on: The competing material descriptions and proposed derivations: Persian sang-gil, written sijill, sijjin as a punishment record, and an asserted Arabic form whose poetic proof al-Alusi challenges.
- 15:74/q08/p1 Clay or stone-and-clay material, often hardened or baked like bricks, with a commonly given Persian derivation from sang and gil. — Muqatil calls the stones mixed with clay; ʿIkrima calls them clay in a report transmitted from Qatada. Al-Tabari describes baked-clay stones, and Ibn Atiyya gives baked-clay bricks while mentioning a worldly term or Persian origin. Ibn Qutayba reports the Persian sang (stone) and gil (clay) analysis; al-Alusi calls the hardened-clay sense common and the Persian loan usual. Al-Baydawi gives hardened clay. Al-Qamus, cited by Bursevi, gives clodlike stones or a Persian-derived term. Farahi's derivation, transmitted by Islahi, uses sang and gil and cites the Qur'anic explanation as pebbles of sijjil and clay stones (51:33). Kur'an Yolu and al-Samarra'i describe baked-clay stones. [11 notes: MUQATIL; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; TAB-FULL(عكرمة); IBNATIYYA-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 15:74/q08/p2 Clay inscribed with names or a divine mark, connected to sijill, a written record. — Al-Zajjaj derives sijjil from sijill and cites the marked clay stones of 51:33. Al-Zamakhshari also cites 51:33-34. Al-Baydawi reports clay bearing writing. Bursevi records the explanation that the stones bore the people's names. Al-Alusi reports the derivation from sijill, a book, with names or God's decree of punishment written on the clay. [5 notes: ZAJJAJ; KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 15:74/q08/p3 A record of the decree of punishment, equated with sijjīn. — Bursevi reports this explanation with reference to 83:8-9. Al-Azhari, as quoted by Bursevi, calls the sijjin explanation the clearest of those reported. [2 notes: BURSEVI; BURSEVI(الأزهري)+]
- 15:74/q08/p4 An Arabic word, with a variant form sijjīn supported by a line of Tamīm ibn Muqbil. — Abu ʿUbayd and others argue this from the variant and the poetic line. Al-Alusi calls the linguistic argument weak. Al-Asmaʿi declined to explain the line's word because he remembered hearing sakhināʾ ('hot') rather than sajīnan. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أبو عبيد); ALUSI-FULL(الأصمعي)-]
- 15:74/q08/p5 Stones baked in Hell. — Bursevi lists this among reported explanations of the stones. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 15:74/q09 When did the cry and the city's upheaval occur?  [* · reports]
turns on: The notes locate the cry at sunrise as the end of destruction or at dawn/an unexpected time; al-Biqa'i distinguishes dawn uprooting from sunrise's final cry.
- 15:74/q09/p1 The city began to be uprooted at dawn, and the final cry came at sunrise. — Al-Biqa'i reconciles dawn destruction with the cry at sunrise by assigning dawn to the uprooting and sunrise to the concluding cry. Al-Samarra'i also places the cry at sunrise and lists the overturning and stones with it. Al-Razi names cry, overturning, and stones as the three punishments without assigning times. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 15:74/q09/p2 The terrible cry came at dawn or at an unexpected hour. — Kur'an Yolu gives these alternatives while describing the ruined land and the subsequent rain of baked-clay stones. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 15:74/q10 What does the destruction of Lot's people teach its audience?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes draw different lessons from the same fate: rescue of believers, a visible sign, a warning to Quraysh, and the consequence of persistent defiance.
- 15:74/q10/p1 Their destruction and their believing household's rescue show the different outcomes of unbelief and faith. — Al-Jishumi says the surrounding verses show Lot's people destroyed while the believing members of his household were saved. [1 notes: JISHUMI]
- 15:74/q10/p2 The enduring remains are a recognizable sign from which people can learn. — Al-Jishumi says the remains give people a lesson and divine لطف. Ibn Abbas, as reported in al-Durr, compares the destruction's recognizable sign to a seal-ring sent to a household that confirms a genuine message. Islahi says Quraysh passed the settlement, so the historical site gave them evidence. [3 notes: JISHUMI; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 15:74/q10/p3 Those who demand to see angels should be ready for a punishment like Lot's people's. — Islahi applies the historical account to the Quraysh, who passed the settlement. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 15:74/q10/p4 It shows the fate of people who defy God and a prophet's warning and persist in shameless immorality. — Kur'an Yolu links this moral reading to accounts at 7:80,84; 11:69,83; 26:160,173; and 27:54,58. [1 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 15:74/q11 Where do the commentators direct readers for fuller explanation of this punishment?  [* · links]
turns on: The cited fuller treatment is either the Hūd account and its commentaries or Farahi's detailed explanation and lexical study.
- 15:74/q11/p1 To the account or commentary in Sūrat Hūd. — Al-Wahidi and Ibn al-Jawzi refer specifically to 11:82. Al-Razi says he explained the later punishment details in Hūd; al-Baydawi likewise points to his fuller account there. Ibn Kathir refers to his earlier explanation of sijjil in Hūd. The Study Quran points to 11:82-83. Asad directs readers to his note 114 on sūrah 11, as recorded by this note. [7 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN]
- 15:74/q11/p2 To Farahi's detailed account and his al-Fīl commentary on sijjīl. — Islahi introduces Farahi's explanation of the punishment and specifically directs readers to Farahi's al-Fīl commentary for his research on the word sijjīl. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 15:74/q12 How does this account of punishment compare with the account in al-Naml?  [* · links]
- 15:74/q12/p1 The punishment described in al-Hijr is harsher than the rain punishment mentioned in al-Naml. — Al-Samarra'i compares the cry at sunrise, overturning, and baked-clay stones here with the rain punishment in al-Naml. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
