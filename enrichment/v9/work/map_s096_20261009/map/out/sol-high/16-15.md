# 16:15: 23 questions

## 16:15/q01 What does 'cast' mean for the mountains here?  [وَأَلْقَىٰ · meaning]
turns on: Whether casting functions as a general verb of creation, a more specific divine act, or a picture of the features' placement and formation.
- 16:15/q01/p1 He created or made the mountains in the earth. — Damghānī cites 50:7; al-Zajjāj and Abū Ḥayyān connect it with jaʿala and the mountains as pegs (78:7); al-Rāzī cites 50:7, 41:10 and 20:39 to show that casting need not mean throwing downward. Abū Ḥayyān says interpreters explicitly use this sense and rejects Ibn ʿAṭiyyah's consensus claim. Al-Ālūsī reports this as Ibn ʿAṭiyyah's starting gloss. [9 notes: ZAJJAJ; IBNATIYYA-FULL(المتأولون); DAMGHANI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عطية)]
- 16:15/q01/p2 The expression more specifically depicts God bringing mountains into being by His own power, rather than forming them from earth. — Ibn ʿAṭiyyah invokes the report that mountains appeared after the unstable earth and the need to supply a separate verb for rivers; Abū Ḥayyān transmits both arguments. Al-Ālūsī prefers this more specific sense, while Abū Ḥayyān disputes the accompanying claim of consensus. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عطية)+; IBNATIYYA-FULL+; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]
- 16:15/q01/p3 'Casting' pictures features added to an already complete earth, perhaps after its first creation. — Ibn ʿĀshūr suggests mountains arose from seismic disturbances, rivers from heavy rain, and roads and markers later; he also proposes celestial debris for mountains, with the term extended to other features as at 54:25. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 16:15/q01/p4 The verb may evoke several actual modes of mountain formation, including eruptions, earthquakes, or incoming celestial masses. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī contrasts alqā with jaʿalnā across 15:19, 50:7, 31:10, 27:61, 21:31, 77:27 and 13:3, while deferring claims of scientific miracle to specialists. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 16:15/q02 Why are the mountains said to be cast 'in' the earth?  [وَأَلْقَىٰ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ · rhetoric]
- 16:15/q02/p1 The preposition stresses that the mountains are complete, rooted and firm within earth. — Al-Ālūsī contrasts 20:39, 'I cast affection upon you,' and compares mountains to nails embedded in wood. Bursevī pictures unsupported mountains as pebbles cast by a hand, displaying God's power. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL; BURSEVI]

## 16:15/q03 What are the rawāsi?  [رَوَٰسِىَ · meaning]
- 16:15/q03/p1 They are firmly settled mountains. — Ibn Qutaybah, Ibn ʿAṭiyyah and al-Qurṭubī derive the term from rasā, to settle or remain firm; Ibn ʿAṭiyyah cites a poet's fixed peg and al-Qurṭubī a poem using tarsū for steadiness. Al-Ṭabarī and Bursevī give the singular rāsiyah; Bursevī explains its feminine ending, while Ibn ʿĀshūr discusses variant pronunciations, its conventional mountain sense, irregular fawāʿil plural and 13:3. Study Quran cites 78:7's stakes and other mountain passages. [17 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; MAJAZ; ABDURRAZZAQ(الحسن); IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN; DURR-FULL(قتادة)]

## 16:15/q04 What does it mean that mountains keep the earth from swaying?  [رَوَٰسِىَ أَن تَمِيدَ بِكُمْ · interpretation]
turns on: The mechanism assigned to mountains: simple anchoring, boat-like ballast, mass on a sphere, crustal formation, or God's customary use of causes; Rāzī and later critics test the physical assumptions.
- 16:15/q04/p1 Mountains hold the earth steady so its inhabitants can live and travel without shaking or tipping. — Muqātil, al-Ṭabarī, Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Wāḥidī and many others give the direct sense. Al-Zajjāj and Study Quran compare mountains with pegs at 78:7; Biqāʿī says even slight daily tilting would reverse rivers or spill seas. Ṭabāṭabāʾī specifies disruption of livelihoods, and Islahi calls this maintaining balance. Study Quran adds that this stability ends at the Last Day (73:14; 56:4–6; 101:5). [17 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL(الحسن); ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; DURR-FULL(قتادة); BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 16:15/q04/p2 The earth floated and rocked like a boat on water until the weight of mountains anchored it. — Al-Rāzī identifies this as the majority account; Bursevī says the earth was created unstable on water and gives 6,673 mountains excluding hills, likening their support to bones in an animal and citing the boneless soothsayer Saṭīḥ. Al-Ālūsī reports the boat analogy. Rāzī objects that a naturally heavy earth would sink unlike an airy wooden boat; on a theology of directly created motion and rest weights explain nothing; water itself needs an account of its stability; and whole-earth motion would be imperceptible. Al-Ālūsī repeats these objections and notes the response that ship passengers may miss motion whereas local quakes are sensed. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL(الجمهور); ALUSI-FULL; BURSEVI; RAZI-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام)-]
- 16:15/q04/p3 On a spherical earth, mountains' roughness and mass point toward the center and impede rotation or movement. — Al-Rāzī starts from earth's demonstrable spherical shape; al-Bayḍāwī adds that a light simple earth could rotate like the spheres or move from slight causes, while mountains anchor it like pegs. Ibn ʿĀshūr suggests elevations balance roundness against disturbance during motion through space. Al-Ālūsī reports Rāzī and Bayḍāwī; critics object that philosophical earth has straight rather than circular inclination and that mountains are tiny relative to its diameter. A responder invokes their combined weight and dense rock; a careful scholar allows either circular motion or easy rolling of a naturally resting sphere. Al-Ālūsī notes a 187-mountain regional count against a proposed thirty per region; another critic says the solution fails for philosophers and theologians. [9 notes: RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام,البيضاوي,المجيب,بعض المحققين); IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)-]
- 16:15/q04/p4 Mountain uplift and crustal pressure help harden and balance the crust over an unstable interior, making life possible. — Asad allows that the equilibrium is relative and interrupted by earthquakes and volcanoes, citing 78:7, 21:31 and 31:10. Kuranyolu reports his magma-and-gas account, calls it reasonable, and adds that buildings on firm mountain ground tend to fare better than those in valleys and plains; mountains contribute to terrestrial balance. [6 notes: ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Muhammed Esed); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR+]
- 16:15/q04/p5 God first made the earth move for an unknown wisdom, then steadied it through mountains according to His customary use of causes. — Al-Ālūsī reports this reply to the physical objections; al-Qurṭubī says God could have stilled the earth directly yet chose mountains, establishing the use of causes. Bursevī likewise invokes God's customary ordering. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI]

## 16:15/q05 What is reported about the earth and mountains at creation?  [رَوَٰسِىَ أَن تَمِيدَ بِكُمْ · reports]
turns on: The accounts differ in speaker, details of the earth's movement and complaint, and the sequence of the strength exchange.
- 16:15/q05/p1 The new earth swayed; angels feared it could not support inhabitants; by morning mountains steadied it, their origin unknown to the angels. — The account is attributed variously to al-Ḥasan, Qays ibn ʿAbbād or ʿUbādah, and Wahb ibn Munabbih; Qatādah likewise says the earth moved before the mountain stakes. Some tellings omit either the angels' fear or ignorance of the mountains' origin. Kuranyolu summarizes older commentaries as describing an initially flat, uninhabitable earth. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Nasafī relay the account without a named transmitter. [13 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(الحسن); TAB-FULL(الحسن,قيس بن عباد); BAGHAWI-FULL(وهب); IBNATIYYA-FULL(قيس بن عباد); QURTUBI-FULL(وهب بن منبه); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL(قيل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); IBNKATHIR-FULL(الحسن,قيس بن عبادة); DURR-FULL(قتادة); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Eski tefsirler)]
- 16:15/q05/p2 The earth heaved and complained about carrying sinners and corpses; God anchored it with visible and unseen mountains, though it still quivered. — The report is attributed to ʿAlī; versions describe human sin, impurity, and the earth trembling like flesh. [3 notes: TAB-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); QURTUBI-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); IBNKATHIR-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب)]
- 16:15/q05/p3 The Prophet said mountains steadied the earth, followed by a sequence of stronger things ending in secret charity. — Al-Qurṭubī transmits this through Anas; al-Tirmidhī grades it gharīb and says this is its only known marfūʿ route. An unattributed exchange in al-Durr instead compares water, fire, wind, man and woman. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(أبو عيسى,النبي)]
- 16:15/q05/p4 An unattributed strength exchange places water above fire, wind above water, man above wind, and woman above man. — Al-Durr gives this separate chain of comparisons without an attribution. [1 notes: DURR-FULL]

## 16:15/q06 What does tamīda mean?  [تَمِيدَ · meaning]
- 16:15/q06/p1 To move unsteadily, sway, rock, tilt, or even overturn with its inhabitants. — Mujāhid and Abū Mujāhid emphasize tipping or overturning. Ibn Qutaybah illustrates a person swaying in walking; al-Ṭabarī a rolling ship, seasickness and vertigo; al-Zajjāj and Ibn al-Jawzī dizziness; al-Qurṭubī swaying branches and strutting. Jishumī adds a discussion of māʾida, variously connected to feeding or dining. Al-Ālūsī specifies motion of a great thing. Rāzī links the linguistic discussion with 4:176. [22 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); YAHYA-SALLAM(عاصم بن حكيم وابن مجاهد); MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(مجاهد); ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن قتيبة,الزجاج); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; DURR-FULL(مجاهد); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 16:15/q07 How does 'an tamīda bikum' express the reason for placing mountains?  [أَن تَمِيدَ بِكُمْ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the ellipsis is a negative particle after an (Kufans) or an aversion noun before it (Basrans).
- 16:15/q07/p1 It is a purpose clause with an understood negation: so that the earth does not sway with you. — The Kufan analysis supplies omitted lā; al-Ṭabarī compares 4:176, and al-Itqān cites wider omission of lā. Ibn ʿĀshūr prefers this as the apparent analysis, using ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm's line about hospitality to prevent a curse. Al-Zajjāj and Ibn ʿAṭiyyah identify the accusative causal function; al-Ṭabrisī calls it purposive. Al-Nasafī records this alongside the aversion analysis. [16 notes: TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكوفيون والبصريون); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الكوفيون); NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ITQAN; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL(عمرو بن كلثوم); IBNASHUR-FULL(نحاة الكوفة)+]
- 16:15/q07/p2 It means placing mountains out of dislike or fear that the earth should sway, with an omitted noun of aversion. — The Basran analysis supplies a causal noun before an; al-Nasafī notes noun deletion is common. Al-Zajjāj as reported by Ibn al-Jawzī and al-Ṭabrisī allow 'for fear that' wording. [8 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(الكوفيون والبصريون); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(البصريون); NASAFI-FULL; TABRISI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL(نحاة البصرة)]

## 16:15/q08 What does 'rivers' refer to here?  [وَأَنْهَٰرًۭا · referent]
turns on: Whether the illustration uses naturally named rivers or human-benefiting canals.
- 16:15/q08/p1 The rivers God made throughout the earth, including famous named rivers. — Al-Wāḥidī names the Nile, Euphrates, Tigris, Sayḥān and Jayḥān; al-Ṭabrisī adds similar rivers. Bursevī locates the Euphrates at Kufa, Tigris at Baghdad, Jayḥūn at Balkh, Jayḥān at Adana, Sīḥūn in India, Sayḥān at al-Maṣṣīṣa and Nile in Egypt. Mujāhid says every town has rivers. Bursevī calls anhar the plural of nahr, also nahar, a watercourse. [8 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; TABRISI(قيل); DURR-FULL(مجاهد); BURSEVI]
- 16:15/q08/p2 They include canals flowing through the earth for human benefit. — Islahi uses canals in his explanation of God's provision. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 16:15/q09 How is 'and rivers' governed grammatically?  [وَأَنْهَٰرًۭا · grammar]
turns on: Whether alqā can directly govern rivers by meaning 'made,' or whether the change of object requires a supplied verb, and what that verb is.
- 16:15/q09/p1 It coordinates directly with rawāsi under alqā, understood as 'made' or 'created.' — Al-Rāzī cites 50:7, 41:10 and 20:39; Abū Ḥayyān cites 78:6–7 and 41:10; al-Samīn explicitly rejects Ibn ʿAṭiyyah's elided-verb parse. Al-Bayḍāwī, Bursevī and al-Ālūsī also allow the original verb to govern rivers; al-Ālūsī denies consensus for an ellipsis. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 16:15/q09/p2 An omitted verb such as 'He made' or 'created' governs rivers (and sometimes paths). — Al-Ṭabarī compares a rajaz line coordinating an unheard quality with sound; al-Wāḥidī's Abū Isḥāq appeals to 78:7; al-Ṭabrisī cites two poetic examples and says rivers cannot directly coordinate with alqā. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah takes this ellipsis as support for a more specific alqā; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī report it but reject any claim that it is consensus. Al-Qurṭubī allows 'made in' or 'cast into'; Islahi notes recoverable wording about paths and landmarks. [14 notes: TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية,الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; SAMIN-DURR-]
- 16:15/q09/p3 The omitted verb may specifically be 'He split or carved rivers through the earth.' — Abū al-Baqāʾ's proposal is reported by Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn (who calls it apt) and al-Ālūsī; the latter cites a line about feeding straw and cold water to illustrate coordination of clauses. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو البقاء); ALUSI-FULL(أبو البقاء); SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء)+]

## 16:15/q10 What benefits do rivers supply?  [وَأَنْهَٰرًۭا · interpretation]
- 16:15/q10/p1 They supply water, irrigation, provision and transport for settlements and fields. — Al-Ṭabrisī names directing water to gardens; Ibn Kathīr describes rivers crossing regions, deserts and mountains to inhabited lands, with differing sizes, speeds and seasons under God's measure. Ibn ʿĀshūr mentions drinking, field irrigation and boats; Ṭabāṭabāʾī includes people and domestic animals. Al-Ṭabrisī also says the sea was subdued for travel and benefit in the surrounding context. [8 notes: MUQATIL; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 16:15/q11 Why do rivers follow mountains in the verse?  [رَوَٰسِىَ وَأَنْهَٰرًۭا · rhetoric]
- 16:15/q11/p1 Most rivers begin at springs in mountains. — Al-Rāzī attributes this to rational sciences; Abū Ḥayyān transmits him, al-Ālūsī repeats it, and Study Quran cites the pairing with 13:3 and 27:61. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو عبد الله الرازي); ALUSI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(al-Rāzī)]

## 16:15/q12 How do seas avoid overflowing despite river inflow?  [وَأَنْهَٰرًۭا · interpretation]
- 16:15/q12/p1 Seasonal heat evaporates water, which condenses into rain and replenishes rivers, balancing river inflow to the seas. — Biqāʿī reasons that unbalanced inflow would inundate the earth; summer solar heat remains in the ground through winter, warms waters, lifts vapor like steam from a pot, and renewed rain revives land and rivers each year. [4 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 16:15/q13 What are the subul?  [وَسُبُلًۭا · meaning]
- 16:15/q13/p1 Roads, routes and passable ways across land, including passages through mountains. — Al-Ṭabarī gives sabīl as the singular and compares ṭarīq/ṭuruq; Qatādah cites the usage at 20:53. Al-Suddī specifies routes between mountains; Ibn Kathīr cites 21:31 for mountain passes. Ibn ʿĀshūr says sabīl here is an overland route and attaches paths to rivers because travel was the most recently mentioned benefit. Ṭabāṭabāʾī includes both natural openings and tracks constructed by human travel or labor; Bursevī calls them clear ways. [18 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); TAB-FULL(قتادة); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; DURR-FULL(السدي,قتادة); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 16:15/q14 How can roads made by people be attributed to God?  [وَسُبُلًۭا · interpretation]
- 16:15/q14/p1 Human-made paths arise from created materials and their effects, so God can be credited with them. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī compares human-made rivers and markers and cites 37:96; his category of subul includes natural passages and tracks made by travel or work. Ibn ʿĀshūr says such provisions arise through God's inspiration. [3 notes: TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 16:15/q15 How do the routes help people?  [وَسُبُلًۭا لَّعَلَّكُمْ تَهْتَدُونَ · interpretation]
- 16:15/q15/p1 They lead travelers to intended destinations, towns, livelihoods and dwellings without getting lost. — Al-Ṭabarī calls them a mercy for needs and livelihoods; Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Wāḥidī describes routes to every land. Rāzī cites 20:53, Abū Ḥayyān 43:10, and Ibn Kathīr notes passages cut through mountains. Ibn ʿĀshūr includes route and river-mooring distances. [20 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 16:15/q16 What guidance does 'so that you may be guided' mean?  [لَّعَلَّكُمْ تَهْتَدُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the purpose clause concerns travel on the paths alone, reflection on all the preceding signs, or both.
- 16:15/q16/p1 Practical guidance to one's destination while traveling. — Ibn ʿAṭiyyah presents this as a possible sense; al-Ṭabarī, Ibn al-Jawzī and others explain avoidance of being lost. Al-Ālūsī says the purpose most naturally attaches to the routes. [7 notes: TAB-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 16:15/q16/p2 Spiritual guidance to the Creator, God's oneness, truth and religion through these signs. — Ibn ʿAṭiyyah prefers this reflection on created things; al-Ṭabrisī reports guidance to oneness. Abū Ḥayyān includes truth and religion. Al-Ālūsī says this reading extends the purpose to preceding signs; Biqāʿī calls mountains, seas and rivers signs for spiritual routes. [7 notes: TABRISI(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL+]
- 16:15/q16/p3 It bears both the practical sense of finding a way and guidance to a wise Creator. — Al-Ālūsī reports this double sense; Ibn ʿĀshūr applies the parenthesis to routes, moorings and distances as well as monotheism through signs of the sole Creator. Biqāʿī likewise pairs physical and spiritual routes. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قيل); IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]

## 16:15/q17 What is the scope and force of laʿallakum?  [لَّعَلَّكُمْ تَهْتَدُونَ · grammar]
turns on: The notes address both its causal force here and a reported general restriction on its predicate.
- 16:15/q17/p1 Laʿalla can express purpose: 'in order that you may be guided.' — Al-Burhān cites Thaʿlab's causal reading of 20:44 and also mentions 7:158. Al-Ālūsī says the purpose most naturally concerns routes but can extend to all preceding signs; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the guidance clause parenthetical and gives practical and spiritual applications. [3 notes: BURHAN; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 16:15/q17/p2 A past-tense clause cannot serve as a predicate after laʿalla. — Al-Burhān reports this grammatical restriction from al-Rummānī; it concerns the construction of laʿalla generally. [1 notes: BURHAN(الرمانى)]

## 16:15/q18 Does sitting on a mountain count as sitting on earth for an oath?  [فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ رَوَٰسِىَ · law]
- 16:15/q18/p1 Yes: common usage counts a mountain as earth, despite a possible contrary impression from this wording. — Al-Ālūsī prefers this result for an oath not to sit on earth while acknowledging the verse's apparent distinction. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL+]

## 16:15/q19 What broader lesson do the created blessings teach?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes give both devotional and epistemic implications of the signs.
- 16:15/q19/p1 They invite gratitude for God's beneficial creation and recognition that worship is due to Him. — Jishumī lists animals, trees, fruits and other subjected benefits; Rāzī says the passage opens a catalogue of earthly blessings; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls them evidence prompting gratitude. Islahi says deities that created none of these things do not merit worship. [4 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 16:15/q19/p2 The signs require people to reason about creation and undermine uncritical imitation. — Jishumī argues that reflection is a human act, so the call to reflect is meaningful and obligates reasoning. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 16:15/q20 How does this verse connect with other Qurʾānic passages and its context?  [* · links]
turns on: One connection is a close parallel verse; the other is the blessing sequence's fit with the sūra's later ending.
- 16:15/q20/p1 It closely parallels 21:31 and belongs to a wider set of mountain and earth-sign passages. — Study Quran calls 21:31 nearly identical and points also to 21:30; for stabilization it cites 13:3, 15:9, 41:10, 50:7, 77:27, 79:32, 31:10 and 78:7. Ibn Kathīr cites 21:31 for paths cut in mountains. [3 notes: STUDYQURAN; IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 16:15/q20/p2 The surrounding catalogue of blessings suits al-Naḥl's emphasis on God's mercy and its ending raḥīm at 16:18. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī contrasts 14:34's kaffār with 16:18's raḥīm: Ibrāhīm's surrounding verses 14:28–31 describe human wrongdoing, while al-Naḥl 16:5–15 names divine gifts and attributes. He says cadence affects hearing and soul but contextual meaning and word order also matter; he rejects contrived explanations that can be reversed to fit another wording. [5 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR]

## 16:15/q21 What do the adjacent sea expressions mean?  [* · meaning]
- 16:15/q21/p1 Makhr describes a ship cutting water apart, and can also name forceful wind noise or furrowing and watering land. — Al-Ṭabrisī explains a mākhirah ship by water splitting to either side; he also gives the sound of wind, earth furrowed for cultivation, and land watered for improvement. These glosses concern the adjacent sea context. [4 notes: TABRISI]

## 16:15/q22 What does Bursevī say about earth itself?  [فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ · meaning]
- 16:15/q22/p1 Earth is spherical, occupies the world's center, and its name is linked to consuming human bodies. — Bursevī gives these cosmological and etymological details while explaining the ground that bears mountains. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 16:15/q23 How is the implied wording about paths and landmarks explained?  [وَسُبُلًۭا · grammar]
- 16:15/q23/p1 A recoverable verb such as 'made passable' or 'placed' governs paths, with similar ellipsis for landmarks. — Al-Samīn offers both verbs; Islahi says Arabic leaves understood elements unsaid in the paths-and-landmarks construction and supplies them in translation. Abū al-Baqāʾ, reported by Abū Ḥayyān, says landmarks may coordinate with mountains as placed signs. [3 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو البقاء)]
