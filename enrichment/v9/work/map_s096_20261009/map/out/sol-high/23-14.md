# 23:14: 35 questions

## 23:14/q01 What does the opening thumma signal?  [ثُمَّ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether thumma marks elapsed time, elevated rank, or both.
- 23:14/q01/p1 A real interval or remote transition before the drop becomes a clot. — Al-Burhān notes intervening stages; al-Biqāʿī says the white drop may spoil or begin development; Ibn ʿĀshūr stresses the startling change in density and color. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports complete separation between stages. [5 notes: BURHAN; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(قيل)]
- 23:14/q01/p2 A rise in rank or wonder, rather than necessarily a long interval. — Ibn ʿĀshūr and al-Biqāʿī describe the transformation as extraordinary; al-Ālūsī permits rank for thumma. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 23:14/q02 What is the nutfa and how does it become the next stage?  [ٱلنُّطْفَةَ · interpretation]
- 23:14/q02/p1 The reproductive fluid develops in the womb into the red clot. — Muqātil describes fluid becoming blood; al-Ṭabarī says the secure womb holds it to its appointed development (23:13); al-Rāzī treats the change as new qualities and accidents; al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, al-Biqāʿī, Bursevī describe white semen turning red. Ibn Kathīr says both parents' fluids contribute and cites Ibn Masʿūd's report that the drop spreads to hairs and nails before becoming a clot. Elmalılı speaks of implantation or fertilization. [11 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNKATHIR-FULL(عبد الله بن مسعود عن خيثمة); ELMALILI]
- 23:14/q02/p2 The semen can be called dead before God makes it living. — Al-Dāmghānī compares 2:28, 40:11, 3:27 and 30:19; al-Burhān relays Ibn Masʿūd's living-from-dead reading and an account of all animals before life. [4 notes: DAMGHANI; BURHAN(ابن عطية,ابن مسعود)]

## 23:14/q03 What does ʿalaqa mean and why is it called that?  [عَلَقَةًۭ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the term describes blood, attachment, leech-like appearance, or their combination.
- 23:14/q03/p1 A piece of blood, congealed or clotted, that becomes the embryo. — Ibn Qutayba calls it one clot, singular of ʿalaq; al-Māwardī and Ibn ʿAṭiyya call it fresh blood; Jishumī distinguishes congealed from flowing blood. Al-Rāghib as reported by Bursevī and other exegetes use clotted blood; al-Biqāʿī emphasizes its thick intense redness. Al-Ālūsī calls it qualitative motion of the blood. [12 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; MAWARDI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; JISHUMI; BURSEVI(الراغب); BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(عكرمة); ELMALILI(Müfessirler); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 23:14/q03/p2 The name points to attachment or clinging in the womb. — Al-Māwardī says it is the first attachment; Jishumī gives the root's senses of connection, a tether, and marriage obligations, citing a hadith with ʿalāʾiq. Elmalılı and Kur'an Yolu relate it to implantation and uterine attachment; Ibn ʿĀshūr says maternal blood nourishes the attached embryo through uterine veins. [8 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI; ELMALILI(Müfessirler); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 23:14/q03/p3 The embryo at this stage resembles a leech. — Ibn Kathīr describes its elongation and redness; al-Sāmarrāʾī in both works invokes modern observations for the resemblance. [3 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 23:14/q04 What does muḍgha mean and what is its form?  [مُضْغَةًۭ · meaning]
- 23:14/q04/p1 A small, soft morsel of flesh of a chewable size, initially without distinct form. — Ibn Qutayba compares ghurfa for a scoopful; Jishumī gives mudāgh for chewed food and mudāgha for what remains in the mouth. Al-Sāmarrāʾī stresses teeth marks and modern resemblance to chewed flesh; al-Ālūsī treats the clot-to-flesh change as substantial rather than merely qualitative. [16 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; MAWARDI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; JISHUMI; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT(كتب التفسير); SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 23:14/q05 How are the embryonic stages physically transformed?  [فَخَلَقْنَا ٱلْعَلَقَةَ مُضْغَةًۭ فَخَلَقْنَا ٱلْمُضْغَةَ عِظَٰمًۭا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the account is a simple sequence or explains transformations through qualities, substance, and underlying matter.
- 23:14/q05/p1 The clot becomes a morsel, which is formed or hardened into bones. — Muqātil and al-Thaʿlabī give the sequence. Jishumī calls bones strengthened; Ibn Kathīr describes forming head, hands, feet, sinews and veins; al-Biqāʿī describes heat and hardening. Bursevī says the greater part hardens after forty-three days; Ibn ʿĀshūr says bones form inside the morsel. Al-Ālūsī considers small and large bones and changing dispositions of matter; Elmalılı likens soft fetal bones hardening later to an egg shell, and calls them the body's framework. [21 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI; JISHUMI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(البيضاوي,قيل); ELMALILI; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس وقتادة); STUDYQURAN; DURR-FULL(عزير)]
- 23:14/q05/p2 The stages arise from one underlying matter through changing forms and qualities. — Al-Ālūsī accepts continuous underlying matter unless evidence shows otherwise; he describes the clot to morsel as substantial change and reports al-Bayḍāwī's different account of which stages share an underlying reality. [4 notes: ALUSI-FULL(البيضاوي)]

## 23:14/q06 How long do the stages last and when does the angel act?  [ٱلنُّطْفَةَ عَلَقَةًۭ مُضْغَةًۭ ثُمَّ أَنشَأْنَٰهُ · reports]
turns on: The forty-day and four-month reports distinguish stages, recording, and ensoulment; one report also apportions bodily tissues by parental fluid.
- 23:14/q06/p1 The drop, clot, and morsel each last forty days or nights, then the spirit is breathed. — Yaḥyā gives forty nights each; al-Baghawī reports forty days between stages. Ibn Kathīr cites Ibn Masʿūd's Prophetic report: afterward an angel breathes spirit and records provision, lifespan, deeds and destiny, with final destiny possibly differing from apparent deeds; he notes both Ṣaḥīḥ collections transmit it. Ibn ʿĀshūr grades the hadith sound. [5 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(عبد الله بن مسعود عن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNASHUR-FULL(النبي)]
- 23:14/q06/p2 An angel acts after forty nights or four months, recording or breathing as the reports describe. — Ibn Kathīr cites Ḥudhayfa b. Usayd's forty-night recording of sex, destiny, deeds, effects, trials, and provision, then a sealed record; Anas describes the angel reporting each stage and recording if God wills creation. ʿAlī says breathing occurs after four months in three darknesses. The reports vary in emphasis and timing. [4 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(أنس عن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,حذيفة بن أسيد عن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,علي بن أبي طالب); DURR-FULL(علي)]
- 23:14/q06/p3 The man’s fluid yields bones and sinews, and the woman’s fluid flesh and blood. — Ibn Kathīr transmits a Prophetic answer to a Jewish question, with the questioner confirming agreement with earlier prophets; his general account also names both parents' fluids. [2 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(عبد الله عن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 23:14/q07 How does khalaqnā work in the transformation clauses?  [خَلَقْنَا · grammar]
- 23:14/q07/p1 It means made or transformed and takes two objects here. — Al-Nasafī contrasts ordinary single-object khalaqa; al-Samīn says it carries the meaning of jaʿala, while jaʿala can conversely mean create with one object, citing 6:1. [2 notes: NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]
- 23:14/q07/p2 Creation of a changed form entails God replacing qualities and adding parts. — Al-Rāzī describes removal and creation of accidents, plus additional components. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 23:14/q08 Why do thumma and fa alternate through the stages?  [ثُمَّ فَخَلَقْنَا فَكَسَوْنَا ثُمَّ أَنشَأْنَٰهُ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the conjunctions distinguish time, closeness of change, or rank.
- 23:14/q08/p1 Fa marks close succession, while thumma marks a larger interval or more remote transition. — Al-Biqāʿī says stages after the clot ascend without delay; Ibn ʿĀshūr says proximity is relative even when each lasts long. Al-Suyūṭī says intervals depend on the events. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports that thumma marks complete break, fa nearer succession. Al-Ālūsī says more surprising transitions receive thumma. Al-Bayḍāwī and Bursevī connect the conjunctions to distinct kinds of change. [10 notes: BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ITQAN; TABATABAI(قيل); ALUSI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; BURHAN]
- 23:14/q08/p2 The final thumma marks the far higher rank of the living new human. — Al-Bayḍāwī and Bursevī say it marks a great difference between two creations; Ibn ʿĀshūr says life begins with ensoulment and thumma marks rank; al-Ālūsī allows rank in addition to time. [4 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 23:14/q09 How are the two bone nouns read?  [عِظَٰمًۭا ٱلْعِظَٰمَ · readings]
turns on: The transmitted forms, whether singular is generic, and the value of the plural in a body with many bones.
- 23:14/q09/p1 Both are plural: ʿiẓāman and al-ʿiẓām. — Al-Ṭabarī prefers the agreement of the authoritative readers; Abū ʿAlī prefers the plural because other Qurʾānic references to bones are plural (17:49, 79:11, 36:78). A human has many bones; al-Ālūsī says shapes and hardness vary, with one reported count of 248. The rasm can hold forms with or without alif. - 23:14/q09/p2 Both are singular: ʿaẓman and al-ʿaẓm, a generic or collective noun. — Ibn ʿĀmir and Abū Bakr from ʿĀṣim are regularly named; Ḥafṣ and other transmitters differ. - 23:14/q09/p3 The first noun is singular and the second plural. — Ibn Jinnī prefers the progression from preceding singular embryonic nouns to general plurality; he cites poetry including Ṭufayl's singular ʿaẓm. - 23:14/q09/p5 Ibn Masʿūd's wording adds sinew to bone before the flesh covering. — Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn ʿAṭiyya report a reading in which the drop is made bone and sinew, then clothed with flesh. [2 notes: TAB-FULL(عبد الله); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن مسعود)]

## 23:14/q10 What bones or bodily structure are meant?  [عِظَٰمًۭا · referent]
turns on: Whether the singular bone denotes the skeletal substance generally or a particular axial bone.
- 23:14/q10/p1 The developing skeleton and bodily support. — Ibn Kathīr says the morsel is shaped into head, limbs, bone, sinews and veins; al-Biqāʿī describes bones from head to feet; Elmalılı calls bones the framework and pillars; al-Ālūsī stresses their variety. [4 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 23:14/q10/p2 The bone refers specifically to the backbone. — Ibn Kathīr reports Ibn ʿAbbās's identification and a Ṣaḥīḥ hadith that all of Adam's body decays except the tailbone, from which he was created and will be reassembled. [2 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو هريرة عن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,ابن عباس)]

## 23:14/q11 What is meant by clothing bones with flesh?  [فَكَسَوْنَا ٱلْعِظَٰمَ لَحْمًۭا · interpretation]
- 23:14/q11/p1 God grows or places flesh around bones, covering, binding, and strengthening them like clothing. — Al-Dāmghānī includes sinews, veins, flesh and skin (2:259); Ibn Kathīr, al-Biqāʿī, Bursevī and Elmalılı mention fitted cover and support. Al-Bayḍāwī says the flesh is leftover morsel or nourishment grown onto bone; al-Ālūsī says leftover flesh or new flesh from womb blood. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the wording need not imply an interval of bare bone. [16 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; THALABI; DAMGHANI; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 23:14/q11/p2 The clothing expression is a metaphor for flesh covering bone. — Al-Rāzī says flesh is pictured as clothing; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls it a subtle implied metaphor. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 23:14/q12 To whom does the pronoun in anshaʾnāhu refer?  [أَنشَأْنَٰهُ · referent]
turns on: Whether the antecedent is the person already named or the developing physical entity.
- 23:14/q12/p1 It refers to the human being under formation. — Al-Ṭabarī connects it with the human of 15:26; al-Farrāʾ names the human; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says it refers to the person whose bones were clothed, distinct in essence and properties from the material body. [6 notes: TAB-FULL; FARRA; NASAFI-FULL; TABATABAI]
- 23:14/q12/p2 It refers to the bone, drop and lump conceived as one entity, or the thing just mentioned. — Al-Ṭabarī and al-Farrāʾ allow a unified entity of the prior materials; al-Nasafī says it can refer to the immediately mentioned thing. [3 notes: TAB-FULL; FARRA; NASAFI-FULL]

## 23:14/q13 What does anshaʾa mean here?  [أَنشَأْنَٰهُ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the verb simply describes origination or signals a new mode of being.
- 23:14/q13/p1 To bring into being, originate, and nurture a new kind of being. — Al-Dāmghānī cites 21:11, 56:35, 67:23, 6:133 and 56:61; al-Rāghib, reported by al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, joins originating and nurturing. Al-Biqāʿī reports al-Rāzī that the root letters indicate elevation or loftiness. [3 notes: DAMGHANI; TABATABAI(الراغب); BIQAI-FULL(الرازي)]
- 23:14/q13/p2 The shift from khalq to inshāʾ marks the emergence of a new self unlike the prior matter. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says life, knowledge and power were not contained in the earlier physical stages; the pronoun still links the person to that matter. [3 notes: TABATABAI]

## 23:14/q14 How is the new-creation clause read?  [ثُمَّ أَنشَأْنَٰهُ خَلْقًا ءَاخَرَ · readings]
- 23:14/q14/p1 Ibn Masʿūd's reading adds 'for him' to the new-creation clause. — ʿAbd al-Razzāq attributes this wording to Ibn Masʿūd. [1 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(ابن مسعود)]

## 23:14/q15 What is the other creation?  [خَلْقًا ءَاخَرَ · interpretation]
turns on: The phrase may name an event in the womb, birth, later bodily and mental development, or the entire human transformation; some commentators explicitly broaden or reconcile the readings.
- 23:14/q15/p1 The spirit is breathed into the formed fetus, making inert matter a living human. — Al-Ṭabarī prefers this by analogy with Adam's clay and spirit; Ṣāḥib al-Naẓm appeals to the shift in wording. ʿAlī says an angel breathes after four months in three darknesses. Ibn Kathīr says movement, hearing, sight and perception follow and treats later life stages as compatible consequences. Al-Ālūsī notes a rational soul created for or in the body; al-Qāshānī reads God's spirit and divine image inwardly. Many early authorities are named across sources. Al-Ālūsī derides that answer. ## 23:14/q16 Why is this called another creation?  [خَلْقًا ءَاخَرَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether otherness denotes life and faculties, bodily differentiation, or a self distinct from its material body.
- 23:14/q16/p1 Life and faculties make the human radically distinct from the inert preceding matter. — Al-Rāzī says mute matter becomes living and articulate; al-Nasafī adds hearing and sight. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī argues the knowing capable person differs essentially from the body and uses 32:10–11, where the person is taken at death while the body scatters. Ibn ʿĀshūr says thumma marks rank; Islahi sees resurrection power in this transformation. [11 notes: RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 23:14/q16/p2 The human has unusually rich and differentiated bodily and personal traits. — Al-Qushayrī contrasts uniform drop with diverse flesh, bones, hair, nails, nerves, skin, brain and veins, and lists divergent temperaments and traits; al-Ālūsī reports the microcosm verse. [3 notes: QUSHAYRI; ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
- 23:14/q16/p3 The person is a self that uses the body as an instrument. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī likens person and body to writer and pen and reads 32:10–11 as distinguishing them. [2 notes: TABATABAI]

## 23:14/q17 What does the verse imply about the timing or nature of the spirit?  [ثُمَّ أَنشَأْنَٰهُ خَلْقًا ءَاخَرَ · theology]
turns on: Whether the human named by the verse is bodily composite or a distinct immaterial self, and when spirit comes to be.
- 23:14/q17/p1 The human is a composite bodily being, against a spirit-only or indivisible account. — Al-Ṭabrisī, al-Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān say the described stages refute al-Naẓẓām and the philosophers or Maʿmar; al-Ālūsī reports the Imam's argument. The philosophers identify the person with an immaterial rational soul, neither divisible nor inside or outside the body. [6 notes: TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام,الفلاسفة)]
- 23:14/q17/p2 The spirit is created after bodily formation and joins or permeates it. — Al-Ālūsī says temporal thumma supports a later rational soul, the view of most Islamic theologians and Aristotle; for animal spirit this is plain. He reports disagreement whether the rational spirit attaches to or permeates the body; most Muslims say permeates. [4 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعض المسلمين,قيل)]
- 23:14/q17/p3 The person is distinct in essence from the prior matter and uses the body as a tool. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī links this to being taken at death in 32:10–11 while the body disperses. [2 notes: TABATABAI]

## 23:14/q18 Does the new-creation wording affect compensation for a usurped egg that hatches?  [خَلْقًا ءَاخَرَ · law]
turns on: Whether a new physical identity ends the owner's claim to the transformed property.
- 23:14/q18/p1 The taker owes the egg, not the chick, because the chick is another creation. — Abū Ḥanīfa's inference is transmitted by al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, Bursevī and al-Ālūsī. [5 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL(أبو حنيفة); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو حنيفة); BURSEVI(أبو حنيفة); ALUSI-FULL(أبو حنيفة)]
- 23:14/q18/p2 The chick remains compensable as part of the owner's usurped property. — Al-Kashf says distinct creation does not defeat the owner's title under opponents' principles; al-Taqrīb says liability rests on its being part of the property, not on identity of names. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(صاحب التقريب,صاحب الكشف)]

## 23:14/q19 What does tabāraka mean?  [فَتَبَارَكَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether emphasis falls on exaltation, abundance of blessing, the root's permanence, or the verbal form.
- 23:14/q19/p1 God is exalted, holy and worthy of enduring praise. — Yaḥyā compares 23:116; al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī and Ibn al-Jawzī stress God's permanence; Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it a derived form of bāraka. Al-Rāzī links blessing's increase to rising above others; al-Biqāʿī says God is fixed in perfection and free of defect. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī add holiness and divine knowledge and power. - 23:14/q19/p2 The word derives from firmness, settling or permanence. — Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Ṭabrisī and Jishumī derive it from abiding; al-Rāghib compares water settled in a pool. - 23:14/q19/p3 God possesses abundant, lasting good and pours blessing on creation. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī relays al-Rāghib's pool image for settled divine good (7:96) and connects blessing with God's measuring and creation; Ibn ʿĀshūr speaks of immense welfare decreed for people and baraka as increase. Al-Rāzī offers all blessings coming from God. [10 notes: RAZI-FULL(قول); QURTUBI-FULL; TABATABAI(الراغب); IBNASHUR-FULL; ELMALILI]
- 23:14/q19/p4 It is a past verb with a fixed, noninflecting form. — Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī describe its verbal form; al-Ālūsī notes it usually has a nonfeminine subject. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 23:14/q20 Why does the praise follow the creation account, and why name Allah?  [فَتَبَارَكَ ٱللَّهُ · rhetoric]
turns on: The sources give the transition grounds in divine power and beneficence, human honor, humans' inability to praise during formation, and the majestic name.
- 23:14/q20/p1 The extraordinary formation displays God's power, wisdom and generosity and calls forth praise. — Al-Khaṭṭābī mentions subtle wisdom and mercy; al-Māwardī and Ibn Kathīr note favor, precise formation and resurrection power; al-Bayḍāwī, Ibn ʿĀshūr, Islahi and Kur'an Yolu connect praise with wondrous power and gratitude. [13 notes: KHATTABI; MAWARDI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ITQAN; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; TABATABAI]
- 23:14/q20/p2 The praise marks humanity's special honor among created beings. — Al-Qushayrī notes that God does not praise Himself this way after enumerating heavens, Throne and other beings; he links 95:4 but says divine self-praise outranks praise of humanity. Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya and Mathnawī verses cite 17:70 and 95:4; Kur'an Yolu says the closing praise reflects high regard for humans. [8 notes: QUSHAYRI; BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية,المثنوي); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 23:14/q20/p3 God praises Himself on behalf of humans who could not yet praise Him. — Al-Qushayrī and people of spiritual insight reported by Bursevī say humans were not able to thank Him as their stages were being described. [2 notes: QUSHAYRI; BURSEVI(بعضي اهل وجدان)]
- 23:14/q20/p4 The majestic name Allah gathers divine names and makes the audience feel awe. — Al-Biqāʿī says it gathers all beautiful names; al-Ālūsī says the shift to it marks these acts as divine prerogatives and invites glorification. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 23:14/q21 How is aḥsan al-khāliqīn parsed?  [أَحْسَنُ ٱلْخَٰلِقِينَ · grammar]

- 23:14/q21/p1 Its comparative measure, 'in creating or estimating,' is elided. — Al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn and Bursevī say khāliqīn makes the omission understood; al-Samīn compares 22:39. - 23:14/q21/p3 It is predicate of an omitted huwa. — Al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn and Abū al-Baqāʾ list this parse; al-Samīn calls the ellipsis less likely. [4 notes: NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء); ALUSI-FULL(أبو البقاء); SAMIN-DURR-]
- 23:14/q21/p4 Aḥsanu is an adjective of Allah. — Al-Samīn prefers it because derived apposition is uncommon and argues the superlative annexation can be genuinely definite; al-Ālūsī prefers the same. ## 23:14/q22 What does khāliqīn mean and can beings besides God be called creators?  [ٱلْخَٰلِقِينَ · meaning]

- 23:14/q22/p2 The plural includes Jesus, who was allowed to create or shape. — Ibn Jurayj invokes Jesus and 3:49; al-Ṭabrisī distinguishes shaping clay from divine creation, and al-Burhān reports al-Qāḍī Abū Bakr's appeal to that sense. ## 23:14/q23 What does 'best' say about God's creation and human evil acts?  [أَحْسَنُ · theology]
turns on: Whether goodness means flawless construction, moral goodness excluding evil acts, or the Creator's incomparable scope.
- 23:14/q23/p1 God's creating is unbounded, exact and free of defect. — Jishumī says God makes substances, accidents, life, death and agents without tools or limits; al-Ṭabrisī says His exact measure has no disparity; Elmalılı returns every created agency to Him. [5 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; ELMALILI]
- 23:14/q23/p2 Best means precise, well-composed creation; this does not exclude God's creation of unbelief and sin. — Al-Rāzī answers the Muʿtazila that excellence can mean exact composition and that acts are good from God, who is subject to no higher command. Al-Ālūsī likewise says all is good insofar as it comes from God. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 23:14/q23/p3 Because all God's creation is good and wise, He does not create unbelief or sin; humans originate those acts. — The Muʿtazila infer this from the praise and from their claim of deliberate human origination. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL(المعتزلة); ALUSI-FULL(المعتزلة); RAZI-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 23:14/q24 Who first uttered the closing phrase in the reports?  [فَتَبَارَكَ ٱللَّهُ أَحْسَنُ ٱلْخَٰلِقِينَ · reports]
turns on: The named speaker and sequence vary, and the Ibn Abī Sarḥ and Muʿādh reports face chronological or transmission objections.
- 23:14/q24/p1 ʿUmar anticipated or concurred with the revealed ending. — Some reports have him speak as the Prophet paused, others after revelation began or before the matching ending descended; the Prophet is reported to say it was revealed that way. Al-Wāḥidī and al-Rāzī list it among four of ʿUmar's agreements, alongside the Station, veiling and admonition to wives. Al-Itqān treats Companion agreement as an occasion of revelation and reports a Prophetic saying that truth was put on ʿUmar's tongue and heart. - 23:14/q24/p2 The scribe ʿAbd Allāh ibn Abī Sarḥ anticipated a phrase and then claimed prophethood or apostatized. — Accounts vary whether he anticipated the closing praise or 'another creation,' and whether he later converted at the conquest or died an unbeliever. Al-Ṭabrisī says, if sound, coincidence is possible and Ibn Abī Sarḥ was self-deceived; al-Nasafī objects that a Medinan apostasy conflicts with the Meccan sūra. Al-Ālūsī considers a Medinan verse or dictation, noting implications for al-Suyūṭī's Meccan exceptions. [15 notes: THALABI(ابن عباس); IBNATIYYA-FULL(عبد الله بن أبي سرح); TABRISI(عبد الله بن سعد بن أبي سرح); RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(عبد الله بن أبي سرح); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(عبد الله بن أبي سرح); BURHAN; BURSEVI(عبد الله بن أبي سرح); ALUSI-FULL(عبد الله بن سعد بن أبي سرح); NASAFI-FULL-]
- 23:14/q24/p3 Muʿādh ibn Jabal anticipated the ending. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Qurṭubī, al-Nasafī and Abū Ḥayyān report this variant. - 23:14/q24/p4 The accounts may reflect memorable anticipations of an ending cued by its preceding words. — Al-Burhān's tawshīḥ or al-muṭmiʿ has an opening that makes its close foreseeable, like the supports of a sash; al-Ālūsī says strong Qurʾānic composition often reveals the ending. This helps explain coincident speech without treating it as revelation from the speaker. [4 notes: BURHAN(ابن وكيع); ALUSI-FULL; ITQAN]

## 23:14/q25 Does anticipation of the verse's words challenge its inimitability?  [فَتَبَارَكَ ٱللَّهُ أَحْسَنُ ٱلْخَٰلِقِينَ · theology]
turns on: Whether one predicted phrase is enough to rival the larger revealed discourse.
- 23:14/q25/p1 No: a coincident short phrase does not match the Qur'an's miraculous discourse. — Al-Rāzī says the utterance may fall below the amount where miraculous quality appears; Bursevī and al-Ālūsī compare the threshold of a complete shortest sūra. Al-Ṭabrisī says coincidence is possible and does not make the scribe a prophet. Al-Ālūsī says the ending depends on preceding composition. [5 notes: TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; RAZI-FULL(عبدالله بن سعد بن أبي سرح)-]
- 23:14/q25/p2 The scribe's anticipation seemed to him to undermine the Prophet's claim. — In al-Rāzī's version Ibn Abī Sarḥ objects that a human had uttered comparable words; al-Rāzī rejects the inference. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(عبدالله بن سعد بن أبي سرح); RAZI-FULL-]

## 23:14/q26 How is the closing praise used in Prophetic prayer?  [فَتَبَارَكَ ٱللَّهُ أَحْسَنُ ٱلْخَٰلِقِينَ · reports]
turns on: The reports agree on the praise in prostration; associated reports vary on prayer gestures.
- 23:14/q26/p1 The Prophet recited it in prostration while submitting to God and praising the creation of face, hearing and sight. — ʿAlī reports multiple forms in Musnad Aḥmad, Ibn Ḥibbān, Abū Dāwūd, Ibn Mājah, Muslim, al-Nasāʾī and al-Tirmidhī; al-Nasāʾī also transmits Jābir and Muḥammad b. Maslama, the latter in night prayer. Al-Tirmidhī grades the cited versions ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ. [13 notes: MUSNAD-AHMAD(علي بن أبي طالب عن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNHIBBAN(علي بن أبي طالب,علي رضوان الله عليه); ABUDAWUD(علي بن أبي طالب عن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNMAJAH(علي); MUSLIM(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); NASAI(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,رسول الله); TIRMIDHI(علي بن أبي طالب)]
- 23:14/q26/p2 A related prayer report includes hand raising at specified transitions, over which jurists differed. — Al-Tirmidhī reports al-Shāfiʿī and associates following the practice, Aḥmad not endorsing it, and some Kufans confining it to voluntary prayer; Sulaymān al-Hāshimī compares its transmission with al-Zuhrī's well-known chain. [5 notes: TIRMIDHI(سليمان بن داود الهاشمي,علي بن أبي طالب)]

## 23:14/q27 When does an embryo count as an infant for the withdrawal or infanticide discussion?  [ثُمَّ خَلَقْنَا ٱلنُّطْفَةَ عَلَقَةًۭ ثُمَّ أَنشَأْنَٰهُ خَلْقًا ءَاخَرَ · law]
turns on: The sources distinguish the completion of developmental stages from an earlier legally significant formed body.
- 23:14/q27/p1 Withdrawal is not minor infanticide before the embryo completes the seven stages. — ʿAlī's answer is reported by Ibn al-Jawzī and Ibn ʿĀshūr, with ʿUmar approving in the latter; Ibn ʿAbbās is also reported to reject calling withdrawal infanticide before those stages. [3 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(علي); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL(علي)]
- 23:14/q27/p2 A formed embryo has legal effect for the carrying slave woman. — Al-Wāḥidī says that once bones and flesh are formed it is a human child and its mother becomes an umm walad; al-Qurṭubī sends fuller legal discussion of the stages to his commentary on 22:5. [2 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; QURTUBI-FULL]

## 23:14/q28 What does the sequence show about God's power and resurrection?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the stages are used chiefly as proof of resurrection, divine lordship and design, or human honor and duty.
- 23:14/q28/p1 Creation from a tiny beginning through elaborate stages proves divine power, wisdom, mercy and ability to recreate the dead. — Al-Māwardī says resurrection is easier than first making a human from nothing; Jishumī and al-Burhān frame the stages as an argument for the afterlife (75:40). Islahi relates 22:5 and 23:15–16, arguing that this ordered growth cannot be accidental matter and the One who made it can remake it. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says the prior creation account makes resurrection plain and needs less emphasis; for God neither act is harder. [17 notes: KHATTABI; MAWARDI-FULL; JISHUMI; BURHAN; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT(روح المعاني); SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 23:14/q28/p2 Gradual transformation reveals purposeful precision and diverse organs from homogeneous matter. — Bursevī answers the objection that instantaneous creation might show more power: staged formation better displays each organ's distinct shape and role. Al-Qushayrī lists varied structures from one drop; Kur'an Yolu recalls the soil-derived biological supply chain; Islahi says science uncovers hinted details, while al-Sāmarrāʾī says anatomy confirms the stages and some scientists converted. [10 notes: QUSHAYRI; BURSEVI(الأسئلة المقحمة); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 23:14/q28/p3 The creation is a sign of human honor and responsibility. — Al-Qushayrī links 95:4 and sees divine self-praise after human creation as unique; Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya and the Mathnawī cite 17:70 and 95:4. Elmalılı warns humans to seek felicity and the qualities their creation makes possible; Islahi recalls angels bowing to Adam and humans as vicegerents. [10 notes: QUSHAYRI; BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية,المثنوي); ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 23:14/q28/p4 The human account also teaches divine lordship, worship and gratitude. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī links the believers' virtues to God's sole lordship; Kur'an Yolu says these gifts call for gratitude; Islahi says reflection reveals a beneficent, peerless Creator rather than blind matter or a mere first cause. [5 notes: TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 23:14/q29 How does this passage connect to nearby verses and Sūrat al-Ḥajj?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the link emphasized is neighboring afterlife promises, the next death and resurrection verses, or parallel Qurʾānic creation passages.
- 23:14/q29/p1 It develops the argument from creation to death and resurrection, parallel to 22:5. — Al-Wāḥidī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn ʿĀshūr, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī and Study Quran point readers to 22:5–6 for the stages; Islahi says the present passage elaborates that resurrection proof. Al-Rāzī cites 23:15 as support for life-stage interpretation, and al-Sāmarrāʾī contrasts emphasis on death and resurrection in 23:15–16. [16 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; RAZI-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT(روح المعاني); SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 23:14/q29/p2 The account belongs to broader Qurʾānic descriptions of human development and other creatures. — Al-Khaṭṭābī recalls 86:6; Ibn ʿAṭiyya connects seven human stages with Ibn ʿAbbās's sevens and produce in 80:27. Al-Dāmghānī relates 2:259 and other creation verses; the Study Quran introduction recalls 22:5. Uzayr's reported discourse broadens to water, heavens, lands, animals and plants; Wahb speaks of Adam's clay, senses, spirit and four humors. [14 notes: KHATTABI; IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس وعمر); DAMGHANI; STUDYQURAN; DURR-FULL(عزير,وهب بن منبه)]
- 23:14/q29/p3 The passage follows the believers' promised blessings by recalling human origins and God's power over their changes. — Jishumī gives three links: shift from promised afterlife blessings to original human blessings; creation in stages as a resurrection warrant; and worldly changing states set against eternal Firdaws. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī moves from believers' virtues to divine lordship; Islahi says deniers mock the promised afterlife. [6 notes: JISHUMI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 23:14/q29/p4 The perfected creation makes the following announcement of death surprising and therefore more emphatic. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī and Rūḥ al-Maʿānī say a listener might expect such a wondrous human to endure; this explains extra emphasis on mortality, whereas recreation is rationally easier after original creation. [6 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT(روح المعاني); SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 23:14/q29/p5 The wider sūra repeatedly shows wealthy and powerful people opposing prophets. — Study Quran notes a recurring pattern of opposition to prophetic warnings throughout the sūra and compares 7:59–62 and 7:136. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 23:14/q30 Why does the account use past-tense verbs for an ongoing process?  [خَلَقْنَا أَنشَأْنَٰهُ · rhetoric]
- 23:14/q30/p1 The past tense can assert the certainty of the account while each human's creation continues. — Kur'an Yolu therefore translates the verbs in the present and notes that Qurʾānic past forms can also emphasize certain truth. [2 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 23:14/q31 How can the written skeleton support the bone readings?  [عِظَٰمًۭا ٱلْعِظَٰمَ · readings]
- 23:14/q31/p1 The same rasm accommodates singular and plural forms with or without an alif. — Al-Itqān includes this embryology phrase in its list of regular reading variants represented by one spelling, with parallels such as mālik/malik, plural forms with tāʾ, and other omitted or alternative letters. Its comparison with fākihūn illustrates absent alif despite a legitimate reading. [6 notes: ITQAN]

## 23:14/q32 What did Ibn ʿAbbās's argument from seven stages establish?  [* · reports]
- 23:14/q32/p1 He connected the seven human stages with other sevens when identifying the Night of Decree as the twenty-seventh night. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports ʿUmar praising Ibn ʿAbbās's insight; he says the seven human stages refer to this passage and the seven provisions to produce in 80:27. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس وعمر)]

## 23:14/q33 What do related reports say about creation of Adam and the wider world?  [* · reports]
- 23:14/q33/p1 God diversifies bodies, senses and provision through His creative command. — Wahb's account of Adam adds clay, water, senses and a spirit for moral discernment and planning; the four-humor account links bodily balance to health. ʿUzayr describes heavens and earth separated from water, night and day, diverse animals from water (24:45), varied plants from one earth and water, and limbs and nourishment in the womb. [9 notes: DURR-FULL(عزير,وهب بن منبه)]

## 23:14/q34 What inward meaning is drawn from the creation and final praise?  [* · inward]
turns on: The inward readings range from divine names manifested in creation to personal spiritual realization.
- 23:14/q34/p1 The human is prepared for gnosis, love, servanthood and spiritual effacement. — Al-Qushayrī speaks of rare mature states and individual gifts of inspiration; al-Qāshānī interprets breathed divine spirit and image; Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya names the human a repository of gnosis and care. Al-Rāzī's gnostics contrast ʿUmar's good fortune and Ibn Abī Sarḥ's misery under 2:26. [6 notes: QUSHAYRI; QASHANI; BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية); RAZI-FULL(العارفون)]
- 23:14/q34/p2 Creation manifests God's eternal name Creator while human spiritual capacity remains dependent on divine disclosure. — Al-Sulamī reports al-Ḥusayn's four quarters: divinity, traces of lordship, lights of governance and perception, and movement and rest; God formed Adam by power, descendants in stages, and reveals only what creatures can bear. [2 notes: SULAMI(الحسين)]
- 23:14/q34/p3 The created human body and world invite contemplation of God's hidden essence and beautiful art. — Elmalılı quotes a poem on dust turned to beauty and the world as witness to divine existence. [1 notes: ELMALILI]

## 23:14/q35 What linguistic or rhetorical parallels are cited around the verse?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: The notes use several literary, syntactic and lexical parallels to interpret the verse's conclusion and life theme.
- 23:14/q35/p1 The verse's ending is cued by its beginning, an example of tawshīḥ or al-muṭmiʿ. — Al-Burhān explains that the opening anticipates the end, comparing it with a sash; al-Itqān calls the conclusion apt and al-Ālūsī compares verses whose openings reveal their rhymes. [4 notes: BURHAN(ابن وكيع); ITQAN; ALUSI-FULL]
- 23:14/q35/p2 The broader lexicon of death contrasts life's creation with death or inert matter. — Ibn al-Jawzī distinguishes mawt, mawta, mawāt, madness-like usage and the place Muʾta; al-Dāmghānī calls semen dead before enlivening. Al-Burhān reports a living person emerging from dead seed, with an alternative that animals are dead before given life. [6 notes: IBNJAWZI-NUZHA; DAMGHANI; BURHAN(ابن عطية,ابن مسعود)]
- 23:14/q35/p3 Eloquent creation language can establish doctrine and evoke awe, fear or other emotions. — Al-Burhān uses the resurrection argument after the human seed as an example, alongside concise replies to difficult questions and speech that evokes compassion, aversion, courage or fear. It also defines tajrīd as extracting a comparable entity and describes a resumptive waw as beginning an independent clause. [6 notes: BURHAN; ITQAN]
