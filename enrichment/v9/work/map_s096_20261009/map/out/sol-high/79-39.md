# 79:39: 11 questions

## 79:39/q01 What does al-jahim mean?  [ٱلْجَحِيمَ · meaning]
- 79:39/q01/p1 A fiercely blazing fire. — Bint al-Shati says the religious term retains the lexical sense of intense ignition. [1 notes: BINTSHATI]

## 79:39/q02 Whose abode is Hell in this clause?  [ٱلْجَحِيمَ ٱلْمَأْوَىٰ · referent]
- 79:39/q02/p1 The transgressor who prefers worldly life, especially the unbelieving wrongdoer described in the preceding verses. — Muqatil characterizes him as fixed in shirk, without fear of God or judgment, and consuming forbidden things. Al-Kalbi through Wahidi, Farra, and Ibn al-Jawzi through Zajjaj identify the previously described person. Kashshaf, Razi, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Burhan, Bursevi, Islahi, Kur'an Yolu, and Study Quran likewise identify Hell as this person's abode or bad end. Bursevi specifies unbelieving transgressors while warning other readers; Study Quran, reporting Razi, specifies the highest degree of intellectual and practical corruption. Tabatabai identifies this class as unbelieving wrongdoers. [15 notes: MUQATIL; FARRA; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURHAN; BURSEVI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(al-Rāzī); TABATABAI]

## 79:39/q03 What does al-ma'wa denote?  [ٱلْمَأْوَىٰ · meaning]
- 79:39/q03/p1 A place of refuge, return, protection, and settled residence. — Wahidi says it shelters or receives someone; Ibn Atiyya adds settling and remaining; Nasafi calls it the place of return. Bint al-Shati describes sought refuge, protection, and residence. She says Qur'anic ma'wa occurs only for the afterlife, with Paradise or Hell and its other names, marking the permanent final abode (32:19; 53:15; 79:41; 3:151, 162, 197; 8:16; 5:72; 57:15; 29:25; 45:34; 4:97, 121; 10:8; 17:97; 32:20; 9:73, 95; 66:9; 13:18; 24:57; 79:39). She says awā occurs fourteen times with safety or refuge (93:6; 8:72, 74), while āwā can mean granting refuge or hoped-for or imagined refuge (8:26; 18:10, 16, 63; 12:69, 99; 23:50; 33:51; 11:80, 43; 70:13). [7 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNATIYYA-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BINTSHATI]

## 79:39/q04 How is the possessor of al-ma'wa understood grammatically?  [ٱلْمَأْوَىٰ · grammar]
turns on: Whether al- substitutes for possession, an omitted lahu supplies the subject-predicate link, or contextual definiteness alone expresses the known possessor; Abu Hayyan and Samin dispute whether Zamakhshari's wording supplies that link.
- 79:39/q04/p1 The definite article replaces the possessive pronoun: it is his abode. — Zajjaj reports this and adduces the poet's 'lower the gaze,' addressed to a man whose own gaze is meant; the comparison applies to both Hell and Paradise. Nahhas, Nasafi, Samin, Burhan, Biqai, Abu Hayyan, and Alusi attribute the substitution to Kufans. Qurtubi and Baydawi state it as their analysis. Burhan connects it to Hell for one preferring worldly life and Paradise for the God-fearing. Samin cites a poem combining al- and a possessive pronoun against substitution. Alusi says the possessor is obvious and omission suits the verse-ending rhyme. [14 notes: ZAJJAJ(الشاعر); NAHHAS(الكوفيون); NASAFI-FULL(الكوفيين); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الكوفيون); BURHAN(الكوفيون); BIQAI-FULL(الكوفيون); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; BAYDAWI-FULL+; QURTUBI-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR-]
- 79:39/q04/p2 A possessive link is omitted after 'the abode': it is the abode for him. — Nahhas, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Burhan, Biqai, Alusi, and Tabatabai give the Basran construction al-ma'wa lahu; Razi supplies lahu because the referent is clear, comparing 'lower your gaze.' Samin says a subject-predicate link must be supplied, though omission is eased at a verse ending. He answers the Shaykh that Zamakhshari's contextual understanding amounts precisely to this Basran ellipsis. Alusi says the known possessor and rhyme favor omission. [11 notes: NAHHAS(الكوفيون); NASAFI-FULL(سيبويه والبصريين); SAMIN-DURR(الكوفيون); BURHAN(البصريون); BIQAI-FULL(البصريون); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; RAZI-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 79:39/q04/p3 The known possessor is understood from context, with the abode definite in context. — Zamakhshari in Kashshaf and as quoted by Samin glosses it as the transgressor's abode and compares 'lower the gaze'; possession is understood rather than replaced by the article. Alusi attributes the omitted-possessor explanation to him. Abu Hayyan and the Shaykh object that this account supplies no overt subject-predicate link; Samin replies that it is the Basran ellipsis in substance. [3 notes: SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL+; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ)-]

## 79:39/q05 What is the grammatical role of hiya?  [هِىَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether hiya separates the two nominal elements or itself serves as the subject.
- 79:39/q05/p1 Hiya is a separating pronoun. — Kashshaf, Baydawi, and Alusi offer this parsing. Alusi says the resulting clause confines refuge to Hell alone. [3 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 79:39/q05/p2 Hiya is the subject referring to Hell. — Kashshaf, Baydawi, and Alusi offer this parsing. Alusi says the resulting clause confines refuge to Hell alone. [3 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 79:39/q06 What other uses of lam are distinguished in discussion of the article?  [ٱلْمَأْوَىٰ · grammar]
- 79:39/q06/p1 The article's lam is normally light; other lam forms can mark reverence, distance, affirmation, exclusivity, or emphasis. — Burhan says the article's lam is normally light, but in Allah it is emphatic after preceding fatha or damma to convey reverence. He says the lam in demonstratives indicates or reinforces distance, over which grammarians differ. With weakened inna it is called initial or differentiating lam, separating affirmative from negative inna and confirming the predicate (69:12; 90:17; 47:21). Burhan reports the Kufan explanation of lam as 'except' with negative inna and al-Kisa'i's nominative reading of the mountains clause, making the plot intended only to make them disappear. He says emphatic lam may precede a subject, follow inna on a delayed subject or predicate, or attach to a fronted predicate, and distinguishes the emphases of inna and lam. He reports Sa'id's open-hamza reading of 'they surely eat' and explains its lam as licensed with kasra-inna or a fronted predicate; the underlying order in another example is 'they wander in their intoxication.' [8 notes: BURHAN(الكسائي,الكوفيون,سعيد)]

## 79:39/q07 When is choosing worldly life blameworthy in the fate described here?  [* · interpretation]
- 79:39/q07/p1 When worldly life is preferred to the Hereafter and its desires displace fear of God; worldly benefit that preserves the Hereafter is permitted. — Muqatil links the condemned choice with shirk, lack of fear, and consuming forbidden things. Jishumi calls choosing worldly life over the Hereafter blameworthy and the converse praiseworthy. Ibn Ashur limits the blame to preference that costs the afterlife, citing counsel to Qarun (28:77). Tabatabai says transgression entails denial of the afterlife, preference for this world, and pursuit of desire; fear of God restrains worldly attachment and desire. [4 notes: MUQATIL; JISHUMI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 79:39/q08 For whom is Hell a permanent final abode?  [* · theology]
- 79:39/q08/p1 It is permanent for the defining unbelieving transgressors; a lesser sinner may be punished without Hell becoming the ultimate abode. — Razi infers that a sinner lacking the two defining traits is not assigned Hell permanently. Study Quran reports Razi that combined intellectual and practical corruption merits eternal punishment, while lesser cases may be punished without Hell becoming their final refuge. Bursevi says believing sinners can leave Hell, so the passage addresses unbelievers while warning others. Tabatabai leaves weak or mixed-deed people to God's forgiveness or intercession. Islahi says the transgressors have no other place and remain forever; Bint al-Shati's survey of Qur'anic ma'wa presents it as the permanent final abode. [7 notes: RAZI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(al-Rāzī); BURSEVI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BINTSHATI]

## 79:39/q09 How is this clause connected to the earlier 'when' of the Overwhelming Event?  [* · links]
turns on: Which following clause supplies the response to idha, or whether an omitted response is explained by the subsequent division.
- 79:39/q09/p1 This Hell clause answers that earlier conditional. — Ibn al-Jawzi reports al-Zajjaj treating this clause as the response to 'when the Overwhelming Event comes.' [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج)]
- 79:39/q09/p2 The implied response is 'then the matter is as follows,' followed by the Hell clause. — Kashshaf supplies this response after idha before explaining Hell as the transgressor's abode. [1 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL]
- 79:39/q09/p3 The response is the following fa-amma division. — Alusi reports Abu Hayyan preferring this and considers it sound; the syntax is straightforward if the preceding clause is conditional. He cites 2:38. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان)+]
- 79:39/q09/p4 The response is omitted, and the subsequent division explains it. — Alusi presents this alternative but finds proposals deriving the omission from what precedes or follows unconvincing. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 79:39/q10 How does Hell's abode fit the passage's account of final fates?  [* · links]
- 79:39/q10/p1 After introducing resurrection, the passage divides people into the condemned and the saved, presenting Hell's people first and then their contrasting fate. — Razi says the account moves from Resurrection generally to the wretched and blessed, starting with the wretched. Nasafi contrasts the Hell dwellers with those who fear their Lord's standing and restrain desire (79:40-41). Tabatabai identifies transgression and preference for this life versus fear and restraint; he says the Hell group comes first because the address targets polytheists. Kur'an Yolu says the brief verses summarize attitudes leading to immense loss or salvation. Biqai says the sura establishes resurrection and gathering, showing final human fates after preceding suras' exposition, with oaths at a decisive climax. [7 notes: RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 79:39/q11 What inward reading makes Hell their home?  [* · inward]
- 79:39/q11/p1 Transgressors sink from balanced human nature toward animal or predatory ranks and prefer sensory pleasure to real life, so Hell becomes their home. — Qashani grounds this reading in their excess beyond the balance of human nature and preference for sensory pleasure. [1 notes: QASHANI]
