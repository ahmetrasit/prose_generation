# 16:78: 34 questions

## 16:78/q01 How does this verse connect to its surrounding passage?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the connection is made through earlier gifts, the Hour, or what follows.
- 16:78/q01/p1 It continues the proofs of God's oneness through His gifts and power. — Al-Ālūsī prefers coordination with the making of spouses in 16:72; al-Ṭībī instead connects it to God's unlimited power in 16:77. Ṭabāṭabāʾī sees the sequence of favors as proofs of lordship, resurrection, prophecy, and legislation. Study Quran compares the bringing forth of milk (16:66), honey (16:69), and the Hour (16:77). [4 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الطيبي); TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 16:78/q01/p2 First creation from the womb proves the possibility of the Hour and resurrection. — Abū Ḥayyān says the Hour and human ignorance have just been mentioned. Biqāʿī says the Creator who formed people in the inaccessible womb can bring them from earth still more readily. Bayḍāwī points from gradual creation to God's power even to raise creatures all at once. [5 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL]
- 16:78/q01/p3 The next bird verse follows by inference from this act of bringing forth. — Al-Burhān explains why 16:79 begins without a conjunction; Abū Ḥayyān says the bird passage selects sight as an especially striking means of perceiving creation. [2 notes: BURHAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 16:78/q01/p4 The faculties prepare readers to reflect on the subjects in the following verses. — Kur'an Yolu says reflection draws lessons toward good; al-Nasafī calls the next verse another proof of God's power. [2 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; NASAFI-FULL]

## 16:78/q02 What does God's bringing people from their mothers' wombs show?  [وَٱللَّهُ أَخْرَجَكُم مِّنۢ بُطُونِ أُمَّهَٰتِكُمْ · theology]
turns on: Which divine attribute or consequence the act is taken to demonstrate.
- 16:78/q02/p1 It displays divine power to create and resurrect. — Bayḍāwī reasons from gradual creation to God's power to raise all at once; Biqāʿī compares bringing people from the earth with bringing them from wombs, and stresses the inaccessible place where faculties were formed. [3 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 16:78/q02/p2 It reveals the one knowing, powerful, freely acting benefactor. — Biqāʿī argues natural forces are themselves created and act only as God directs; the signs invite acknowledgment of the messengers' teaching. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 16:78/q03 What does أُمَّهَٰت mean, and how is its form explained?  [أُمَّهَٰتِكُمْ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the discussion concerns the added letter alone or a usage distinction between human and animal mothers.
- 16:78/q03/p1 It is the plural of mother, with an added hāʾ or tāʾ; أمات is the shorter form. — Al-Zajjāj, Thaʿlabī, Jishumī, Abū Isḥāq via Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ṭabrisī, Rāzī, Qurṭubī, Nasafī, Bursevī, and Ṭabāṭabāʾī compare أهرقت/إهراق with أرقت/أراق. Al-Ālūsī cites الأمومة and Qusayy ibn Kilāb's أمهتي; he calls singular addition rare and verbal addition rarer. Al-Naḥḥās instead calls the plural of أمّهة, while reporting the augmentative view. Samīn refers readers to his earlier discussion in al-Nisāʾ. [16 notes: ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS; THALABI; JISHUMI; IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبو إسحاق); TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(قصي بن كلاب); TABATABAI; SAMIN-DURR]
- 16:78/q03/p2 The longer form is used of human mothers, the shorter of animal mothers. — Al-Ālūsī reports this marked versus unmarked usage; Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports the same distinction as a saying. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI(قيل)]

## 16:78/q04 How is أُمَّهَٰتِكُمْ read?  [أُمَّهَٰتِكُمْ · readings]

The Kisaʾī/Hamza summaries in Jishumī, Rāzī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, and Qirāʾāt-ER vary in detail. Al-Nashr says this applies in connected reading across four occurrences; Ibn al-Jawzī names 24:61, 39:6, 53:32. [11 notes: THALABI(الأعمش); BAGHAWI-FULL(حمزة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL(الأعمش وابن وثاب وحمزة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(حمزة); NASHR(حمزة والكسائي); ALUSI-FULL; JISHUMI(حمزة والكسائي); RAZI-FULL(حمزة والكسائي); IBNATIYYA-FULL(حمزة والكسائي); QIRAAT-ER(vw)]

Baghawī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Jishumī, Rāzī, Bayḍāwī, and Nasafī report the hamza's kasra less precisely; al-Ālūsī's summary instead assigns kasra to al-Kisāʾī's mīm. Qirāʾāt-ER gives the intermediate vowel form. [13 notes: THALABI(حمزة والكسائي); BAGHAWI-FULL(الكسائي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL(الكسائي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الكسائي); NASHR(حمزة والكسائي); IBNATIYYA-FULL(حمزة والكسائي); JISHUMI(حمزة والكسائي); RAZI-FULL(حمزة والكسائي); BAYDAWI-FULL(الكسائي وحمزة); NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; QIRAAT-ER(vw)]
- 16:78/q04/p3 The initial hamza takes ḍamma and the mīm fatḥa. — Thaʿlabī, Baghawī, Ibn al-Jawzī, and Qurṭubī assign it to the remaining readers; Qurṭubī calls the hamza's ḍamma original. Ibn al-Jawzī says the readings recur at 24:61, 39:6, and 53:32, with no disagreement when beginning recitation. [5 notes: THALABI(الباقون); BAGHAWI-FULL(الباقون); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; QURTUBI-FULL(الباقون)]
- 16:78/q04/p4 The initial hamza is omitted and the doubled mīm takes kasra. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān attribute this to al-Aʿmash; al-Ālūsī also reports it. Abū Ḥātim objects to deletion and says the mīm's kasra depended on the deleted hamza. Abū Ḥātim calls deletion poor but prefers the retained fatḥa to a kasra whose conditioning hamza was dropped. Al-Ālūsī repeats the judgment. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن أبي ليلى وأبو حاتم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن أبي ليلى); ALUSI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو حاتم)+; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حاتم)+]

## 16:78/q06 What does knowing nothing at birth encompass?  [لَا تَعْلَمُونَ شَيْـًۭٔا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether 'anything' is unrestricted, narrowed to a particular unknown, or restricted to acquired knowledge.
- 16:78/q06/p1 Newborn humans begin ignorant of things generally. — Muqātil, Ṭabarī, Wāḥidī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Rāzī, Bayḍāwī, Biqāʿī, Bursevī, and Study Quran state the broad ignorance. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī prefer unrestricted wording, especially under negation. Bursevī includes worldly and afterlife matters and contrasts animal innate skills; Saʿdī's ant couplet makes the contrast. Study Quran pairs 16:70 and 30:54 on the rise and loss of knowledge. Al-Burhān uses it for ignorance preceding understanding. [15 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(المفسرون); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(الشيخ سعدي); STUDYQURAN; BURHAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل)+; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 16:78/q06/p2 It specifically means ignorance of the primordial covenant. — Al-Wāsiṭī invokes the 'Am I not your Lord?' covenant (7:172); Qurṭubī and al-Ālūsī locate it in the fathers' loins. Bursevī says people forget what souls or their atoms knew in the spirit world and covenant. [5 notes: SULAMI(الواسطي); QURTUBI-FULL(قول); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم); BURSEVI]
- 16:78/q06/p3 It means ignorance of one's decreed happiness or misery. — Sulamī's unnamed authority and Qushayrī mention one's unknown lot; Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, and al-Ālūsī transmit the limited interpretation. Qushayrī adds that God may turn an enemy or errant person into a saint, so no one knows their end. [6 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم); QUSHAYRI; QURTUBI-FULL(قول); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 16:78/q06/p4 It means ignorance of one's interests, benefits, or harms. — Qushayrī includes one's welfare; Ṭabrisī says benefits and harms; Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, and al-Ālūsī report the interests interpretation. [5 notes: QUSHAYRI; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL(قول); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 16:78/q06/p5 It means ignorance of the Benefactor's rights. — Al-Nasafī takes the clause circumstantially and ties the unknown rights to later gratitude, worship, and action. [2 notes: NASAFI-FULL]
- 16:78/q06/p6 It denies acquired sensory, imaginative, and intellectual knowledge, while leaving immediate self-awareness outside its scope. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites 16:70 on acquired knowledge and rejects the reading that would deny immediate self-knowledge. [2 notes: TABATABAI(بعضهم); TABATABAI+]

## 16:78/q07 Does a person initially lack awareness of the self?  [لَا تَعْلَمُونَ شَيْـًۭٔا · theology]
turns on: Whether 'nothing' includes presential self-awareness and when the soul's blankness is placed.
- 16:78/q07/p1 Initial absence of knowledge includes even self-knowledge. — Some interlocutors of Ṭabāṭabāʾī draw this implication; al-Ālūsī rejects the alleged proof of invariable self-awareness as dependent on disputed claims that the soul is immaterial and all immaterial things know. Self-identity need not amount to knowing the soul's essence; awareness may require a condition. Al-Ṭūsī points to fainting and illness. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(المحقق الطوسي)+; TABATABAI(بعضهم)-]
- 16:78/q07/p2 The soul has immediate awareness of itself even before acquired learning. — Philosophers reported by al-Ālūsī call this presential knowledge; the Shaykh argues one never loses it even asleep or intoxicated and uses it for an immaterial soul. Bahmanyār says the soul's existence is intellectual and its perception constitutes its existence, echoed in al-Shifāʾ. Ṭabāṭabāʾī excludes this from the verse's denial. [4 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الشيخ,بعضهم,بهمنيار); TABATABAI+; ALUSI-FULL(المحقق الطوسي)-]
- 16:78/q07/p3 The blank state belongs to the soul before attachment to the body. — An unnamed scholar offers this to reconcile blankness with a baby's instinctive breast-seeking. Al-Ālūsī calls it strange because the proposed blank state would lie after bodily attachment yet before the behavior. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعض الأجلة); ALUSI-FULL-]

## 16:78/q08 How can knowledge arise after initial ignorance?  [لَا تَعْلَمُونَ شَيْـًۭٔا وَجَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ وَٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether sensory experience itself yields cognitive basics, an Active Intellect supplies universals, or infant guidance comes by inspiration.
- 16:78/q08/p1 God teaches through senses and reasoning: sensed particulars yield conceptions and necessary judgments, then acquired knowledge. — Ṭabarī says reason distinguishes good and evil and memory preserves learning. Wāḥidī and Ibn ʿAṭiyya call senses a route or ladder. Rāzī divides knowledge into self-evident and acquired, raises the regress if axioms must be learned, and answers that sensory impressions produce conceptions which occasion axioms without prior learning; theoretical conclusions need proof. Bayḍāwī, Bursevī, and al-Ālūsī describe repeated perception, comparison, and reasoning. Elmalılı reads the verse as favoring empiricism and practice. [21 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(المفسرون); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ELMALILI; RAZI-FULL+]
- 16:78/q08/p2 Particular sense impressions prepare the Active Intellect to impart universals. — Al-Ālūsī reports philosophers' four stages of intellect: potential, habitual, actual, and acquired; they hold that the soul itself does not perceive material particulars. He notes the long dispute. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الحكماء)]
- 16:78/q08/p3 Infants can be divinely inspired to suckle without instruction. — Qushayrī points to taking the mother's breast without prior warning, command, or rebuke; Bursevī contrasts humans' lack of the innate skills by which animals seek mother and food. [2 notes: QUSHAYRI; BURSEVI]

## 16:78/q09 What is reported about a newborn's earliest sensations?  [لَا تَعْلَمُونَ شَيْـًۭٔا · reports]
- 16:78/q09/p1 For seven days a newborn is ḥadhiran and perceives neither comfort nor pain. — Wahb's report is transmitted by Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(وهب); ALUSI-FULL(وهب)]

## 16:78/q10 How is لَا تَعْلَمُونَ شَيْـًۭٔا attached grammatically?  [لَا تَعْلَمُونَ شَيْـًۭٔا · grammar]
turns on: Whether the note concerns the ḥāl within the birth clause or where that clause ends.
- 16:78/q10/p1 It is a circumstantial clause describing the people as they are brought out. — Ṭabrisī, Abū Ḥayyān, Samīn, and al-Ālūsī explicitly parse it as ḥāl; al-Nasafī also calls the newborn's lack of knowledge circumstantial. [6 notes: TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 16:78/q10/p2 The birth and ignorance clause ends at شيئا before a new clause on the faculties. — Ṭabarī, Thaʿlabī, Baghawī, and Qurṭubī mark this sentence boundary; Ṭabarī and Baghawī distinguish faculties created before birth from knowledge after it. [4 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]

## 16:78/q11 How is شَيْـًۭٔا parsed?  [شَيْـًۭٔا · grammar]
turns on: Whether شيء functions as the thing known or as a cognate accusative for knowledge itself.
- 16:78/q11/p1 It is the object of تعلمون, meaning 'recognize' or 'know'. — Ṭabrisī and Samīn allow the ordinary object. Al-ʿIzz objects that an ordinary 'know' with a cognate object would require two omitted objects and prior knowledge of the first. Al-Ālūsī replies that شيء can be the first object while only the second is omitted. Al-Burhān says cognitive علم takes meanings as objects. [6 notes: TABRISI; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(العز); BURHAN; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 16:78/q11/p2 It is an accusative cognate object, meaning 'any knowledge'. — Ṭabrisī and Samīn supply an implied لا تعلمون علما. Al-Ālūsī also allows it under negation; al-ʿIzz challenges this if علم retains its ordinary two-object sense. [4 notes: TABRISI; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(العز)-]

## 16:78/q12 How does وَجَعَلَ relate to أَخْرَجَكُم?  [وَجَعَلَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the conjunction joins predicates within one report or starts a new report.
- 16:78/q12/p1 It may coordinate with أخرجكم within the same report about God. — Wāḥidī says waw does not impose time order. Abū Ḥayyān, Samīn, and al-Ālūsī explicitly allow coordination within the predicate; Bursevī reports that the faculties' effects appear after birth though they existed potentially earlier. [5 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; BURSEVI(الإرشاد)]
- 16:78/q12/p2 It may begin an independent clause coordinated with the opening statement. — Ṭabarī, Thaʿlabī, Baghawī, and Qurṭubī end the prior statement at شيئا; Abū Ḥayyān, Samīn, and al-Ālūsī also allow an independent new report. [7 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]

## 16:78/q13 Does the order of bringing out and giving faculties imply when the faculties were created?  [أَخْرَجَكُم وَجَعَلَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the wording refers to creation of the faculties or to their later exercise.
- 16:78/q13/p1 No: the faculties were made before birth, while knowledge or their effective use comes after birth. — Ṭabarī, Thaʿlabī, Baghawī, and Qurṭubī distinguish prenatal faculties from postnatal knowledge or grant. Wāḥidī and Rāzī say waw has no temporal force; Abū ʿUbayda says Arabic may reverse listed events, and Ibn al-Jawzī adduces a line naming lesser assessed dues before greater. Bursevī's al-Irshād treats the powers as potential until their later effects; al-Ālūsī similarly emphasizes activation. [10 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أبو عبيدة); RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI(الإرشاد); ALUSI-FULL]
- 16:78/q13/p2 Hearing and sight may name the acts of listening and seeing, which occur after birth. — Rāzī offers this as another solution to the apparent chronological objection. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 16:78/q14 How many objects does جَعَلَ take here?  [وَجَعَلَ لَكُمُ · grammar]
- 16:78/q14/p1 It takes one object if it means 'create', but two if it means 'render', with لكم the second. — Al-Ālūsī gives the two grammatical construals. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 16:78/q15 What are the faculties given for?  [ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ وَٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the faculties are explained as routes to knowledge generally, as ways to receive divine guidance, or as capable of failing that purpose.
- 16:78/q15/p1 They are instruments of perception and acquisition of knowledge. — Ṭabarī details sound, conversation, sight, recognition, retention, and reflection. Ṭabrisī calls the senses sound routes to percepts and hearts the seat of understanding. Abū Ḥayyān says organ names stand for faculties, with hearing and sight for sensibles and reason for intelligibles. Rāzī, Bayḍāwī, Bursevī, al-Ālūsī, Ṭabāṭabāʾī, and Kur'an Yolu explain the move from particulars to thought; Ṭabāṭabāʾī includes touch, taste, and smell. [19 notes: TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(المفسرون); RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Taberî)]
- 16:78/q15/p2 They receive revelation and moral admonition, perceive God's signs and blessings, and know His greatness. — Ibn ʿAbbās, Qushayrī, Sulamī's Abū ʿUthmān, Qurṭubī, and the commentators reported by Rāzī pair hearing with divine address or commands, sight with divine acts, and hearts with the Maker. Al-Ālūsī also reports hearing Qurʾān and Sunna as transmitted proofs and seeing created wonders; he insists the outward senses retain their ordinary meanings. Study Quran relates the grouping to guidance and cites 22:46 and, for gifts, 10:31, 23:78, 32:9. [14 notes: SULAMI(أبو عثمان المغربي,بعضهم); QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL; RAZI-FULL(المفسرون); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس,بعضهم); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; STUDYQURAN]
- 16:78/q15/p3 They can become impaired or impervious to guidance. — Study Quran cites 2:7, 6:25, 6:110, 7:179, and 18:57. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 16:78/q16 Why is ٱلسَّمْع singular while ٱلْأَبْصَٰر and ٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ are plural?  [ٱلسَّمْعَ ٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ ٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the singular follows the word's grammatical class, its collective force, or differences in the faculties' objects.
- 16:78/q16/p1 سمع is a verbal noun or generic collective and thus remains singular. — Jishumī and Bursevī call it a verbal noun; Jishumī also reports the generic noun view. Ibn al-Jawzī says the singular form refers collectively and points to 2:7; al-Ālūsī gives the verbal-noun explanation. [5 notes: JISHUMI(قيل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 16:78/q16/p2 Hearing has one kind of object, while sight has many. — Al-Ālūsī reports this account of singular versus plural. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 16:78/q16/p3 Sight and hearts vary more greatly than hearing. — Biqāʿī suggests this while acknowledging that only God knows the full extent of the variation. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 16:78/q17 Why is hearing mentioned before sight?  [ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether primacy is assigned to revelation, development, or the number of objects perceived.
- 16:78/q17/p1 Hearing receives revelation and divine speech. — Bursevī adds that some prophets lost sight but not hearing; al-Ālūsī reports receiving revelation as an explanation. [2 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 16:78/q17/p2 Hearing develops before sight in a newborn. — Bursevī says newborn eyes open later; al-Ālūsī reports the developmental explanation. [2 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 16:78/q17/p3 Audible objects are fewer. — Al-Ālūsī reports this as another ordering rationale. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]

## 16:78/q18 Why do hearing and sight precede the heart?  [ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ وَٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ · rhetoric]
- 16:78/q18/p1 Outward perception supplies or precedes inward understanding. — Al-Ālūsī says the outward senses contribute to the heart's cognition; another comparison makes senses servants preceding the master, with fewer objects than the heart's almost innumerable objects, though sight and intellect both have limits. Rāzī and Bursevī likewise trace cognition from perception to judgment. [5 notes: ALUSI-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI]

## 16:78/q19 What does the choice of hearing and sight imply about other senses?  [ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the named senses represent sensory knowledge broadly or bear on a disputed theory of internal senses.
- 16:78/q19/p1 It emphasizes these two while the senses also include touch, taste, and smell. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī lists those other senses as starting points for conceptions; Rāzī says other senses likewise supply particulars. [2 notes: TABATABAI; RAZI-FULL]
- 16:78/q19/p2 Some take the pair as evidence against philosophers' five internal senses. — Al-Ālūsī reports the inference and says it needs fuller discussion. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]

## 16:78/q20 What is implied about speech by mentioning hearing?  [ٱلسَّمْعَ · interpretation]
- 16:78/q20/p1 Speech is inferred from hearing. — A view reported by Qurṭubī reasons that deaf people do not speak and that hearing entails speech. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قيل)]

## 16:78/q21 What do ٱلْأَبْصَٰر designate?  [ٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ · meaning]
- 16:78/q21/p1 They are the eyes or faculty of seeing. — Jishumī contrasts plural eyes with the verbal noun سمع; Bursevī calls أبصار the plural of بصر, the eye's sense. Ṭabarī says they enable seeing and distinguishing persons. [3 notes: JISHUMI; BURSEVI; TAB-FULL]

## 16:78/q22 What are ٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ?  [ٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the designation is explained anatomically as hearts or functionally as intellect and understanding.
- 16:78/q22/p1 They are hearts, including the heart's cognitive and reflective power. — Muqātil and Ṭabarī identify hearts; Ṭabarī assigns knowing, retaining, reflecting, and understanding. Biqāʿī adds subtle heat and bodily regulation; Bursevī and al-Ālūsī call fuʾād the heart's center, as heart is to chest. Kur'an Yolu notes 'heart' can mean intellect as well as feeling and treats Tabari's heart and intellect as interchangeable. Samarrai distinguishes cool calculation from the heart's motive force. [10 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; TABRISI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Taberî); SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 16:78/q22/p2 They are the rational faculty that grasps intelligibles. — Abū Ḥayyān describes reason as the third faculty distinct from the two senses; Nasafī calls it understanding that supports action; Kur'an Yolu quotes Tabari's gloss as intellects. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Taberî)]

## 16:78/q23 How is ٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ formed as a plural?  [ٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ · grammar]
- 16:78/q23/p1 It is the broken plural of فؤاد, comparable to أغربة from غراب. — Al-Zajjāj, Wāḥidī, Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī, Rāzī, Qurṭubī, Nasafī, Samīn, Bursevī, al-Ālūsī, and Ṭabāṭabāʾī state the relation. Nasafī's comparison is recorded as أغربة from غريب. Al-Zajjāj and several followers say there is no larger plural فئدان; Wāḥidī says غراب unlike فؤاد does have غربان. Al-Ālūsī's report of al-Zajjāj, however, says both أفئدة and فئدان occur, alongside أغربة and غربان. [15 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); RAZI-FULL(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج); TABATABAI]

## 16:78/q24 Why is a plural of paucity used for ٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ?  [ٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the form is a conventional plural used for abundance or a rhetorical hint about rare true understanding.
- 16:78/q24/p1 It serves a large number because no other regular abundance plural is attested. — Zamakhsharī, as reported by Samīn and al-Ālūsī, says the few form covers both quantities; Nasafī and Bursevī's Baḥr al-ʿUlūm agree, the latter pointing to the general 'for you.' Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls it a broken plural of paucity without an attested abundance form. Samīn objects to Zamakhsharī's claim that only شسوع is heard, citing أشساع and calling شسوع predominant; al-Baḥr repeats the correction. [7 notes: NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); BURSEVI(بحر العلوم); ALUSI-FULL(البحر,الزمخشري); TABATABAI; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 16:78/q24/p2 The few form hints that true hearts are rare. — Rāzī says most people sink into animal appetites and predatory traits rather than certain knowledge. Samīn and al-Ālūsī transmit the speculation. Abū Ḥayyān calls it foolish or nonsensical and al-Baḥr sharply criticizes it. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الرازي); ALUSI-FULL(التفسير الكبير); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو عبد الله الرازي)-; ALUSI-FULL(البحر)-]

## 16:78/q25 What can the heart or soul perceive?  [ٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ · theology]
turns on: Whether perception of material particulars belongs to the soul and how faculties are divided.
- 16:78/q25/p1 The soul grasps universals and particulars, through senses and without them. — Al-Ālūsī reports this as the Sunni view. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أهل السنة)]
- 16:78/q25/p2 The soul does not perceive material particulars. — Al-Ālūsī reports this philosophers' view and their lengthy debate; on their account the outer senses pass particular impressions through imagination before universals are imparted. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الحكماء)]
- 16:78/q25/p3 The heart perceives only what is outside spatial and quantitative bounds; each object has its own perceptive faculty. — Al-Ālūsī reports this claim and dismisses it. [0 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)-]

## 16:78/q26 What is the purpose of giving these faculties?  [لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether thanks is stated generally, as faith and worship, as proper use of each faculty, or as inward return to the Giver.
- 16:78/q26/p1 People should thank God for the faculties and His successive blessings. — Yaḥyā ibn Sallām, Ṭabarī, Thaʿlabī, Qushayrī, Baghawī, Ṭabrisī, Qurṭubī, Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān, Qatāda, and al-Ālūsī state the purpose. Ṭabarī says the thanks belong to God alone, not idols with no share in the gifts, and cites 2:52. Qurṭubī's other explanation emphasizes seeing signs of workmanship that lead to gratitude; Biqāʿī adds recognizing God's knowledge, power, and care. [13 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL; THALABI; QUSHAYRI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL(قول); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; DURR-FULL(قتادة); BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 16:78/q26/p2 Gratitude includes acknowledging God's oneness, believing, worshipping, and fulfilling His rights. — Muqātil calls the response affirmation of divine oneness; Ibn ʿAṭiyya connects it with belief; Nasafī with action, worship and divine rights; Kur'an Yolu says the elevated human capacity for knowledge and thought calls for thanks. [4 notes: MUQATIL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 16:78/q26/p3 Gratitude is using each faculty for the divine purposes for which it was entrusted. — Bursevī urges hearing God's speech, prophetic reports, and saints' wisdom and seeing signs of God's existence, unity, knowledge, and power; misuse betrays the trust. Al-Ālūsī reports the same purpose account. Sulamī's Abū ʿUthmān distributes commands, wonders, and recognition to hearing, sight, and hearts. Saʿdī's lines urge listening to Qur'an, avoiding falsehood, noticing the Creator's artistry, and overlooking a brother's faults; Ṣāʾib calls the heart a trustee threatened by thieves. [5 notes: SULAMI(أبو عثمان المغربي); BURSEVI(الشيخ السعدي,صائب); ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 16:78/q26/p4 Gratitude means recognizing ongoing gifts and returning to the Giver. — Sulamī says this directly; Bursevī's Najmī interpretation says seek God through gifts and look past them to Him. [2 notes: SULAMI; BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]

## 16:78/q27 From whose perspective is the expectation in لَعَلَّكُمْ?  [لَعَلَّكُمْ · grammar]
- 16:78/q27/p1 The expectation is expressed from the human audience's perspective. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya makes this qualification. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL]

## 16:78/q28 What is complete divine blessing in the inward readings?  [لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ · inward]
turns on: Which spiritual state is taken to complete the blessing.
- 16:78/q28/p1 It is contentment with the courses of divine decree. — An unnamed authority in Sulamī gives this account. [1 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم)]
- 16:78/q28/p2 It is gnosis in this life and vision of God in the next. — Ibn ʿAṭāʾ gives this account in Sulamī. [1 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء)]
- 16:78/q28/p3 It is delight in surrender and relinquishing self-direction. — An unnamed authority in Sulamī gives this account. [1 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم)]
- 16:78/q28/p4 It is a heart without hidden association and a self without ostentation or desire for reputation. — Abū Muḥammad al-Jarīrī gives this account in Sulamī. [1 notes: SULAMI(أبو محمد الجريري)]

## 16:78/q29 How are the faculties understood at deeper inward levels?  [ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ وَٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ · inward]
turns on: Whether the inward reading follows layered faculties or the soul's emergence from nonexistence.
- 16:78/q29/p1 Bodily senses are animal-like, spiritual faculties angel-like, and the inmost faculties perceive through God. — The Najmī interpretations distinguish body, spirit, and secret; a cited sacred report says God becomes the servant's hearing, sight, and tongue. Bursevī says inward gratitude hears only God's speech, sees only His beauty, loves only His essence, and speaks only with Him. [3 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية,النبي)]
- 16:78/q29/p2 The wombs signify nonexistence, and divine teaching restores knowledge of the names and primordial response. — Bursevī calls nonexistence the true mother; before divine teaching people knew nothing. At 'Am I not your Lord?' (7:172), disclosure of lordship and the light of divine hearing gave them a tongue to answer yes. [2 notes: BURSEVI]

## 16:78/q30 How do the faculties relate to the sequence of divine attributes?  [ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ وَٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ · inward]
- 16:78/q30/p1 Human development from hearing through sight to speech mirrors a pattern of perfection. — Bursevī arranges seven divine attributes—life, knowledge, will, power, hearing, sight, speech—and relates the human sequence to them. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 16:78/q31 What report about provision accompanies the image of birth?  [أَخْرَجَكُم مِّنۢ بُطُونِ أُمَّهَٰتِكُمْ · reports]
- 16:78/q31/p1 The Prophet assures people not to despair of provision: every child is born bare, then God provides. — Ḥubba and Sawāʾ ibn Khālid transmit this report. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(حبة وسواء ابنا خالد)]

## 16:78/q32 What legal discussion does prenatal development prompt?  [بُطُونِ أُمَّهَٰتِكُمْ · law]
turns on: Whether the pregnancy came from a lawful marriage or illicit sex.
- 16:78/q32/p1 Marriage to a woman pregnant from lawful marriage is forbidden; pregnancy from illicit sex is disputed. — Bursevī states the distinction. He cites a prophetic prohibition on impregnating another man's occupied field and a report claiming intercourse sharpens a fetus's hearing and sight. [3 notes: BURSEVI(النبي)]

## 16:78/q33 What do the verse and nearby passages say about the course of human knowledge?  [* · links]
- 16:78/q33/p1 Knowledge and strength emerge after ignorance and can diminish again in old age. — Study Quran cites 16:70 and 30:54. Ṭabāṭabāʾī also invokes 16:70 to define the acquired knowledge absent at birth. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN; TABATABAI]

## 16:78/q34 What broader claim about Qur'anic wording accompanies this verse?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the observation concerns verbal choice or inherited spelling conventions.
- 16:78/q34/p1 Small verbal distinctions in the Qur'an are intentional and display artistry and inimitability. — Sāmarrāʾī states this general maxim. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR]
- 16:78/q34/p2 Qur'anic and prosodic spelling follow inherited conventions that cannot be freely analogized. — Sāmarrāʾī cites the Companions' written forms نعمت/نعمة and رحمت/رحمة and says some spellings lack a stated explanation. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
