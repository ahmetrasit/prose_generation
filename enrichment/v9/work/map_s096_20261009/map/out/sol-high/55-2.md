# 55:2: 34 questions

## 55:2/q01 Who is the teacher named by this clause?  [عَلَّمَ · referent]
- 55:2/q01/p1 God, the All-Merciful, is the teacher of the Qur’an. — Muqatil and Qushayri identify Him as the one denied by disbelievers; Qushayri reports that the Real alone acquainted servants with it. Al-Wahidi says God identifies Himself through His works to establish His oneness. Bursevi attributes teaching ultimately to God even where teachers and memorizers mediate, comparing Adam’s names, David’s armor, Jesus’s learning and al-Khidr’s hidden knowledge (2:31; 21:80; 3:48; 18:65). [5 notes: MUQATIL; QUSHAYRI(قيل); WAHIDI-BASIT; BURSEVI]

## 55:2/q02 What does ʿallama mean here?  [عَلَّمَ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the verb denotes instruction and enabling learning or is derived from ʿalāma, a sign.
- 55:2/q02/p1 God instructed in the Qur’an, imparting its wording and meanings. — Ibn Ashur distinguishes doubled ʿallama, instruction, from form IV aʿlama, merely informing; Maʿn ibn Aws’s archery line and 5:110 and 36:69 illustrate teaching a discipline. Al-Alusi includes reliable understanding, layered insights and cosmic events. Elmalılı uses Turkish ta‘lim etti to retain this sense alongside the sign interpretation. [8 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI(Âlûsî); ALUSI-FULL+; ELMALILI+; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 55:2/q02/p2 God made the Qur’an easy to learn, remember and recite. — Al-Zajjaj and transmitters give this gloss; Qurtubi, al-Alusi and Study Quran cite the repeated refrain in 54:17, 22, 32, 40. Al-Imam permits a figurative sense of enabling knowledge; al-Kashifi says God eases Muhammad’s learning so he can teach others. Other definitions take teaching as preparing or awakening the mind to conceive meanings. [14 notes: ZAJJAJ; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); WAHIDI-WAJIZ(قيل); BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI(الزجاج); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL(الزجاج); IBNKATHIR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام,بعضهم); BURSEVI(الكاشفي); ELMALILI; STUDYQURAN(Others)]
- 55:2/q02/p3 God made the Qur’an a sign or miracle for reflection and proof of prophethood. — Al-Zamakhshari reports this alternative; Qurtubi says a sign concerning what people worship. Al-Samin derives the verb from sign, as do al-Alusi, Elmalılı and Kuranyolu; they relate it to the preceding surah’s moon miracle (54:1). Abu Hayyan calls it remote because the clauses enumerate favors, and al-Alusi calls the derivation far-fetched despite its cited parallels. Elmalılı’s translation deliberately keeps the possibility open. [7 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ELMALILI+; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 55:2/q03 Who received the Qur’an taught here?  [عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · referent]
turns on: The unnamed first recipient may be inferred from the next verse, prophetic transmission, angelic descent, or an inward account of spirits.
- 55:2/q03/p1 Humanity or the community is the intended learner. — Al-Samin prefers humanity because the teaching is general and 55:3 names the human; al-Alusi finds this clearest in context. Ibn Atiyya says people learn and memorizers and understanders gain special merit. Al-Wahidi’s alternative says this community memorized it. Al-Wahidi, Qushayri, Ibn ʿAta and Bursevi describe Muhammad passing it to his community; Qushayri compares Adam’s taught names (2:33), prayer’s required Fatiha and worshippers’ intimate address to God. Al-Tabrisi says the teaching supplies needed religious wisdom; Study Quran says human beings were created for knowledge. [17 notes: WAHIDI-WAJIZ(قيل); IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; STUDYQURAN; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); TABRISI(الكلبي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن السائب); QUSHAYRI; BURSEVI(ابن عطاء); ALUSI-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 55:2/q03/p2 Muhammad is the immediate learner, then he conveys or teaches the Qur’an to others. — Al-Kalbi and Ibn al-Saib specify Muhammad and onward teaching; Qurtubi says he conveyed it to everyone. Al-Wahidi and Ibn Ashur tie God’s instruction of Muhammad to denial of a human teacher. Bursevi specifies Gabriel as the outward intermediary, and elsewhere includes divine mysteries (4:113). Al-Sulami and Qushayri compare Muhammad’s instruction with Adam’s names and 4:113. Al-Samin and al-Alusi list Muhammad as a possible omitted object. [16 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم); WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL(الكلبي); TABRISI(الكلبي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن السائب); QURTUBI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; QUSHAYRI]
- 55:2/q03/p3 Gabriel or the angels are learners. — Al-Razi says angels could receive it before human creation, citing the preserved book touched by the purified and its descent from the Lord (56:77–80). Abu Hayyan and al-Samin list Gabriel; al-Alusi adds the nearest angels and notes that this entails the Qur’an being God’s speech. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
- 55:2/q03/p4 Spirits received it directly before embodiment, while bodies or souls later learn through them or intermediaries. — Al-Sulami reports both bodies following spirits and al-Husayn’s distinction between directly instructed spirits and mediately instructed souls. Qushayri says children learn before hearing a person and likens this prior love to a poem. [3 notes: SULAMI(الحسين,بعضهم); QUSHAYRI(قيل)]

## 55:2/q04 How is the object structure of ʿallama parsed?  [عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · grammar]
turns on: Which derivation and argument structure ʿallama has, and which of its two objects is unstated.
- 55:2/q04/p1 The verb takes two objects; Qur’an is the second, and the first, the learner, is omitted. — The Master of Composition compares ordinary ‘Zayd fed food.’ Abu Hayyan identifies the missing object as first; al-Samin calls this stronger, and Ibn Ashur analyzes the doubled form. Al-Alusi and Elmalılı explain that omission leaves the benefit general, allowing Gabriel, Muhammad or humanity as learner. Al-Alusi says the Imam may merely have demanded another object beside Qur’an, so his supposed error is uncertain. [13 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(صاحب النظم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 55:2/q04/p2 An alternative parse describes the second object as omitted. — Al-Razi is attributed this analysis by Abu Hayyan; al-Alusi reports the Imam similarly, though he leaves open that the Imam intended an additional object rather than a true reversal. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام)]
- 55:2/q04/p3 On the sign derivation, the verb needs no omitted learner. — Elmalılı makes this grammatical consequence explicit; al-Samin gives the sign derivation. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ELMALILI]

## 55:2/q05 How do the opening clauses attach to al-Raḥmān?  [عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the clauses are coordinated predicates or one begins an independent construction.
- 55:2/q05/p1 ʿAllama al-Qur’an and the following clauses are successive predicates of al-Raḥmān. — Al-Zamakhshari takes the opening name as subject and the verbs as predicates; Abu Hayyan calls it the apparent analysis. Al-Samin says the clauses appear predicative and prefers this over Abu al-Baqa’s independent-clause proposal. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 55:2/q05/p2 The opening name has an omitted predicate or the teaching begins a fresh thought. — Abu Hayyan acknowledges a parse supplying an omitted predicate; Study Quran says the teaching clause may begin a new thought. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 55:2/q05/p3 ‘Created man’ is a fresh clause, with ‘taught him’ possibly a prospective circumstance with implied qad. — Al-Samin reports Abu al-Baqa’s parse but calls it unobvious, saying al-Zamakhshari gives only the successive-predicate parse. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء); SAMIN-DURR-]

## 55:2/q06 Why are the opening favor clauses unjoined?  [عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · rhetoric]
- 55:2/q06/p1 Each clause stands independently in a close enumeration of blessings and rebukes deniers. — Al-Zamakhshari illustrates the rebuke with a beneficiary helped through poverty, lowliness and scarcity who is then asked which kindness he denies; conjunctions resume when clauses are more naturally paired. Al-Samin stresses the list’s close connection. [5 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 55:2/q07 What does the past tense in ‘taught’ convey?  [عَلَّمَ · rhetoric]
- 55:2/q07/p1 It expresses God’s care and invitation. — Al-Sulami attributes this reading to al-Wasiti. [1 notes: SULAMI(الواسطي)]

## 55:2/q08 Why is Qur’an teaching named before human creation?  [عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · rhetoric]
turns on: The sources explain priority by rank and purpose, celestial chronology, or pre-eternal inward teaching.
- 55:2/q08/p1 It is the supreme religious blessing and is the purpose for which humans were created. — Al-Zamakhshari says the purpose precedes creation in presentation; al-Razi says expression and creation explain the means of teaching. Al-Samin calls teaching the cause of creation despite later timing. Abu Hayyan calls it religion’s foundation and salvation’s path. Al-Samarrai cites 51:56, worship as the purpose of humans and jinn, and says order may follow purpose, merit and context. Al-Baydawi calls Qur’an the foundation of religion and law; Ibn Ashur says religion reforms this life and saves in the next. [13 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL(الكشاف)]
- 55:2/q08/p2 Qur’an teaching belongs to the celestial order and preceded humanity’s creation. — Al-Razi pairs teaching the Qur’an above with teaching expression below, naming the celestial first; his angelic reading invokes 56:77–80. Al-Samarrai says Qur’an, as God’s speech and knowledge, existed before humanity, so it precedes in existence as well as purpose. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 55:2/q08/p3 Pre-eternal preparedness or teaching precedes embodied human creation, while expression follows it. — Ibn ʿAta says teaching was known eternally and appeared at creation; Bursevi’s unnamed authority contrasts the unified Qur’anic origin with differentiated Furqan (25:1) and says expression depends on embodiment. Bursevi explains the stated order from that distinction. [4 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء); BURSEVI(ابن عطاء,بعضهم)]

## 55:2/q09 What is the Qur’an designated here?  [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · referent]
turns on: Whether ‘Qur’an’ names the recited revelation or an inward unified human capacity.
- 55:2/q09/p1 The verbal revelation to Muhammad, recited in worship and inimitable even at a single sura. — Ibn Ashur gives this definition and says the named Qur’an signals Islam and the favors of prior religions (6:92). Al-Zamakhshari calls it the summit and standard of heavenly books, and al-Biqaʿi calls it written and recited, gathering good and distinguishing confusion. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 55:2/q09/p2 In an inward reading, it is complete human preparedness or Qur’anic intellect, containing realities, qualities and rulings. — Al-Qashani contrasts its unified origin with its final differentiation as Furqan (25:1); Bursevi reports a related account of pre-eternal readiness. [2 notes: QASHANI; BURSEVI(بعضهم)]

## 55:2/q10 How is ‘Qur’an’ read?  [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · readings]
- 55:2/q10/p1 Ibn Kathir and others omit the hamza in Qurān, with no change of meaning. — Qiraat-ER says the pronunciation is smoother and reflects Hijazi hamza softening. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER(Ibn Kathīr and others)]

## 55:2/q11 Is the Qur’an created?  [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · theology]
- 55:2/q11/p1 It is uncreated, and the contrast between teaching Qur’an and creating humanity supports this. — Ibn Atiyya counts fifty-four scriptural mentions of Qur’an without calling it created, versus eighteen explicit mentions of human creation; al-Suyuti reports others making the same argument. Al-Alusi says angelic or Muhammadan reception entails the Qur’an is God’s speech. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ITQAN(جماعة); ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]

## 55:2/q12 What benefits does teaching the Qur’an confer?  [عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · interpretation]
- 55:2/q12/p1 It is divine mercy and the greatest gift, guiding belief and conduct toward salvation. — Al-Tabari says it enables obedience, avoidance of anger, reward and escape from punishment; Qatada calls it a tremendous blessing. Al-Nahhas says it distinguishes what pleases and displeases God. Al-Kashshaf, al-Baydawi, Abu Hayyan, al-Biqaʿi, al-Alusi, Ibn Ashur and Kuranyolu rank it above other blessings or books; al-Biqaʿi says it clarifies what to pursue and avoid and leads to the promised abode. Ibn Kathir connects easy memorization and understanding to mercy. Islahi says it reforms mistaken belief and deed for success here and hereafter, giving instruction before punishment; Study Quran calls revelation sustaining mercy. Qushayri stresses mercy brings people to Qur’an and also describes rescue from idolatry and faith. Al-Razi calls it the greatest favor. [24 notes: TAB-FULL(قتادة); NAHHAS; QUSHAYRI(قيل); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN]

## 55:2/q13 What claims about the Qur’an does this opening answer?  [عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · reports]
turns on: Which hostile claim or occasion the opening responds to.
- 55:2/q13/p1 It answers the charge that a human taught Muhammad: the All-Merciful taught him. — Al-Wahidi, al-Tabrisi, Abu Hayyan, Ibn Ashur and Study Quran relate this claim to 16:103. Ibn Ashur also says it rebuts claims of ancient fables, magic, soothsayer speech and poetry. [7 notes: WAHIDI-WAJIZ; TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN(some commentators)]
- 55:2/q13/p2 It answers Meccans who denied the name al-Raḥmān or confused it with Musaylima of Yamama. — Al-Wahidi reports Muqatil’s account: after 17:110, Meccans said they knew only Musaylima as Rahman; 25:60 records their denial. Abu Hayyan reports a refusal to prostrate to al-Rahman, leading to the opening. [2 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(مقاتل)]

## 55:2/q14 How does teaching the Qur’an relate to Adam’s teaching and human speech?  [عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · links]
- 55:2/q14/p1 God’s teaching Muhammad and the community recalls His teaching Adam the names, while speech lets people learn and address Him. — Al-Sulami and Qushayri compare Adam’s names with Muhammad’s Qur’an and Ibn ʿAta says the community is favored over nations (2:31); Qushayri stresses that Adam reported to angels while worshippers recite Fatiha to God. Al-Wasiti traces scriptural inheritance and dignity to divine teaching (35:32; 17:70). Al-Jurjani says speech transmits otherwise private knowledge, distinguishes faith from unbelief, and conveys known qualities through composition and order. Al-Burhan sees 55:1–4 as honoring eloquence and adds 3:138 and 16:89. Islahi says speech equips humans to teach one another and learn revelation. [16 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء,الواسطي,بعضهم); QUSHAYRI; BURSEVI(ابن عطاء); JURJANI-ASRAR; BURHAN; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 55:2/q15 How does this verse connect with the sūra’s setting and neighbors?  [* · links]
- 55:2/q15/p1 The sura turns from al-Qamar’s power and warnings to universal mercy, proof and repeated blessings for humans and jinn. — Al-Biqaʿi says mercy completes sovereignty and the opening name announces blessing; Jaʿfar praises al-Qamar’s concise complete stories and the Qur’an’s miraculous arrangement (11:44; 15:94; 54:17–18), while al-Biqaʿi adds examples of immediately evident eloquence. Al-Samarrai says the address to humans and jinn follows earlier instruction in Qur’an and justice of weighing. Kuranyolu links the opening name to boundless mercy (1:1). Study Quran and others connect easy teaching with al-Qamar’s refrain (54:17, 22, 32, 40). [7 notes: BIQAI-FULL(جعفر بن الزبير); SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(Others)]

## 55:2/q16 What inward account of Qur’an teaching is given?  [عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · inward]
- 55:2/q16/p1 Divine mercy discloses Qur’anic knowing directly within receptive servants, though outward transmission uses intermediaries. — Al-Sulami and Qushayri report spirits taught before bodies and children’s prior receptivity, which Qushayri illustrates with love poetry. Ibn ʿAta says Gabriel mediates outwardly but not inwardly. Al-Alusi reports Sufis on merciful self-disclosure through names and attributes and likens spiritual unfolding to following recitation with God’s explanation (75:18–19). Bursevi says inspiration can be received in Arabic, Persian or Turkish without visible intermediary, while an angel usually mediates invisibly and reception continues until Judgment; inner divine speech takes languages as garments in communication. [10 notes: SULAMI(الحسين,بعضهم); QUSHAYRI(قيل); BURSEVI(ابن عطاء,بعضهم); ALUSI-FULL(ساداتنا الصوفية)]

## 55:2/q17 Can angels recite the Qur’an?  [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · theology]
turns on: Whether reports of Gabriel’s recitation permit a general claim about angelic recitation.
- 55:2/q17/p1 Ibn al-Salah says recitation is a human grace; angels listen eagerly to human recitation. — Al-Alusi reports his claim. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(ابن الصلاح)]
- 55:2/q17/p2 Al-Alusi hesitates to exclude angels, since Gabriel is said to have recited it. — He doubts whether the report is accepted even with Gabriel excepted. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 55:2/q18 What knowledge does the Qur’an contain, and how far can people grasp it?  [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · interpretation]
- 55:2/q18/p1 It encompasses all or vast knowledge, beyond any individual’s complete grasp. — Ali, Ibn Masud, Ibn Abbas and al-Mursi say it contains or clarifies everything; Ibn Abbas would find even a lost camel halter in it. Al-Mursi says only God fully encompasses it, with much inherited by the Prophet and early generations before later weakening; Elmalılı cites 3:7. Abu Hurayra’s raised report says not even an atom, mustard seed or mosquito would be omitted. Al-Burhan says its benefits are inexhaustible, its divine speech exceeds human understanding, and all sciences relate to God’s acts and attributes; Ibn Masud urges deep investigation of it for earlier and later knowledge. Al-Harali says divine gifts give the most complete scholars understanding of its encompassing sciences. Al-Shafiʿi traces community statement to Sunna, Sunna to Qur’an and Qur’an to divine names, and another authority to the greatest name. Al-Jishumi calls understanding its meanings and rulings the foremost religious science. [18 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); ALUSI-FULL(أبو هريرة,ابن عباس,ابن مسعود,المرسي); ELMALILI(Mürsî,İbn Abbas,İbn Mes‘ûd); BURHAN(ابن مسعود,الحرالي,الشافعي,غيره); JISHUMI]
- 55:2/q18/p2 Its understandings are layered and may vastly outnumber its words or verses. — Al-Burhan reports sixty thousand understandings per verse, and a different estimate of 77,200 sciences by fourfold outer, inner, limit and opening. It reads a report on every verse’s outer and inner and every letter’s limit and opening as layered comprehension rather than Batini esotericism; a person’s share grows with learning. [5 notes: BURHAN(ابن سبع,بعض العلماء,قول آخر)]

## 55:2/q19 What makes learning and teaching the Qur’an especially worthy?  [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · interpretation]
- 55:2/q19/p1 Qur’anic learning and teaching rank above neglect of the Book and bring special merit. — The Prophet calls the best people those who learn and teach it. Fudayl rebukes seekers of human sayings and names grammar, clear and ambiguous verses, law and abrogation as the work of generations, citing 10:57; Sufyan regrets leaving the Book for detailed legal disputes. Hasan wants every verse’s circumstances and meaning known, while Mujahid says God loves those who know revelation best. Abu al-ʿAliya, Qatada and another interpreter connect 2:269’s wisdom with understanding Qur’an, its law or exegesis. Al-Burhan likewise links Qur’anic sciences to 39:9 and 2:269. Qushayri preserves a poem longing for the days of divine Qur’an instruction. [13 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبو العالية,الحسن,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,غيره,قتادة,مجاهد); THALABI(الحسن,الفضيل بن العياض,سفيان الثوري); BURHAN(مجاهد,مقاتل); QUSHAYRI]
- 55:2/q19/p2 Al-Hasan says Qur’anic knowledge is learned only by men. — Al-Thaʿlabi reports this restriction without further explanation. [1 notes: THALABI(الحسن)]

## 55:2/q20 How should a reader learn the Qur’an’s language and interpret its words?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes distinguish verbal evidence and outward exegesis from broader inference while requiring both for sound understanding.
- 55:2/q20/p1 Master Arabic wording, grammar, context and eloquence, using poetry and transmitted explanation where needed. — Al-Mawardi distinguishes vocabulary and inflection: a commentator needs vocabulary; rare nonlegal senses may rest on one or two reports and poetic lines, but action affecting senses require wide transmission and corroborating poetry. Grammatical differences changing law bind reciter and commentator; other inflection still protects recitation, while ignorance remains a defect. Reported prophetic instructions and Ibn Abbas say seek rare vocabulary in Arab poetry; al-Mawardi relates rare words to inimitability. Al-Wahidi adds adab, metaphors, proverbs, similes, wordplay and compressed speech, saying native Arabs knew the idiom while later learners require study; the Prophet clarified what the community needed. Ibn Atiyya repeats prophetic poetry and parsing reports and says grammar underlies law; al-Hasan blames weak Arabic for false speech about God. Al-Burhan says begin with individual words, roots and forms, then syntax, rhetorical implications and bayan, applying Arabic usage and context when no report survives. [34 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس,رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن,رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); BURHAN]
- 55:2/q20/p2 Interpretive understanding needs the outward sense first, then reasoned inference and attention to multiple aspects. — Al-Mawardi assigns ambiguous text, laws and general language to scholarly reasoning and legal scholars guided by sacred law and wording. Al-Burhan warns that inner secrets need the surface first and that later inference can expand transmitted exegesis. Ibn Abbas mocks bare recital without explanation, Iyyas compares a reader without tafsir to a person unable to read a royal letter in darkness, and a prophetic report says full understanding sees a verse’s many aspects. Masruq traveled for an interpreter; Ibn Abbas taught Qur’an, then exegesis, then Hadith. [11 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; BURHAN(ابن سبع); IBNATIYYA-FULL(إياس بن معاوية,ابن عباس,الشعبي,رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 55:2/q21 What is tafsir, and what makes it an Islamic science?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether tafsir is considered explanation of divine intent or the collection of its supporting disciplines.
- 55:2/q21/p1 Tafsir discloses a verse’s context, story, occasion and meaning and is a principal science of God’s intent. — Al-Buraydi relates tafsir to unveiling and the diagnostic tafsira. Ibn Ashur argues that it yields universal rules, like poetry supporting linguistic discoveries; religious and literary disciplines need not consist solely of rational universal propositions. Verbal definitions can yield propositions and proofs, and the field contains rules on abrogation, interpretation and decisive verses. Its early practice trained scholars in Qur’anic style and subtle composition, which Ibn Ashur calls decisive; al-Ghazali calls explanation of God’s intent principal and al-Baydawi the head of Islamic sciences. [9 notes: THALABI(البريدي); IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL+]
- 55:2/q21/p2 Viewed as ancillary study of revelation periods, abrogation, readings and legal method, tafsir is a supplementary religious science. — Ibn Ashur gives al-Ghazali’s classification and notes that exegetes have often focused on details rather than foundational legislative principles. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 55:2/q22 How should Qur’anic commentary be compiled and transmitted?  [* · sciences]
- 55:2/q22/p1 Use reliable chains, careful selection and organized explanation that serves readers. — Al-Thaʿlabi criticizes chainless notebook copying and mixed sound and poor material; teachers pass learning to students, and without chains anyone could claim anything. He says excessive reports and disputes discourage seekers, while early commentators lacked law and resolution of difficulties. He gathered over one hundred tested or heard books and nearly three hundred teachers, organizing, clarifying, shortening or expanding material to meet demand. Al-Wahidi reports complaints about huge low-benefit works and records his own recitation and book studies with al-Busti, al-Hiri, al-Farisi and al-Thaʿlabi, including Ibn Mihran, al-Zajjaj and over five hundred parts of al-Thaʿlabi’s work. Al-Jishumi honors early foundations and later refinement and says his work balances length and brevity for beginners and advanced readers. [13 notes: THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI]

## 55:2/q23 Who is remembered for knowing and teaching Qur’anic interpretation?  [* · reports]
- 55:2/q23/p1 Early interpreters pursued verse meanings intensely and were recognized for distinct expertise. — Ibn Atiyya recounts Ali praising Jabir for interpreting 28:85 and Masruq traveling through Basra to Syria for a verse’s interpreter. Al-Suyuti lists ten prominent Companion exegetes: the four caliphs, Ibn Masud, Ibn Abbas, Ubayy, Zayd, Abu Musa and Ibn al-Zubayr; Ali has most reports among caliphs, possibly because the others died earlier. Ali claimed knowledge of each verse’s time and place and an understanding heart; Ibn Masud is praised for Qur’an and Sunna, said Ali knew the seven readings’ outward and inward, and said he himself knew each verse’s audience and place. Prophetic prayers and Ibn Umar’s report honor Ibn Abbas’s wisdom and interpretation. [10 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الشعبي,علي بن أبي طالب); ITQAN(أبو البختري,ابن عمر,ابن مسعود,علي,علي بن أبي طالب)]

## 55:2/q24 How are transmitted Qur’anic readings authenticated?  [* · readings]
turns on: Whether tawatur belongs to the written consonantal text, each vocalization, or specialist transmission.
- 55:2/q24/p1 The Uthmanic consonantal text is mass transmitted, but individual compatible vocalizations and named chains need not each be mutawatir. — Ibn Ashur says variants outside the codex, such as Ibn Masud’s, fall outside acceptance; Companion-era use without objection made accepted choices collectively widespread, while individual chains remain singular. Ibn al-Arabi, Ibn Abd al-Salam al-Tunisi, Ibn Idris and al-Abyari deny that each reading is mutawatir, and Ibn Ashur endorses this: chains show named readers’ differences, while wording is independently established. He rejects the claim that methods and recensions are all mutawatir, though Nafi has two Companion transmitters. The ten traditions trace to eight Companions; Ibn Arafa and Ibn Lab debated this. [6 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL(الأبياري)+]
- 55:2/q24/p2 Compatible readings are mutawatir because they follow the mass-transmitted script. — Ibn al-Arabi gives this basis; Imam al-Haramayn and Ibn Salama say the readings are mutawatir, though al-Abyari rejects Imam al-Haramayn. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو بكر بن العربي,إمام الحرمين,ابن سلامة الأنصاري)]
- 55:2/q24/p3 Readings are mutawatir among recitation specialists, though not among Muslims generally. — Al-Maziri’s middle position. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(المازري)]

## 55:2/q25 How should grammar be weighed against transmitted readings?  [* · readings]
turns on: Whether grammatical school preference can override transmitted reading.
- 55:2/q25/p1 Sound transmitted readings must be accepted even when grammarians disfavor their syntax. — Ibn Ashur says most parses are mutawatir but allows different parses with the same sense, such as wa-lata hina and hatta yaqula. He rejects Basran or Kufan norms as exhaustive tests of Arabic and says less preferred grammar can preserve pronunciation and yield distinct rhetorical effects. Al-Zamakhshari rejected Ibn Amir’s passive zuyyina with nominative qatlu, accusative awladahum and genitive shuraka’ihim; Abu Ali al-Farisi instead defended readings grammatically in al-Hujja. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(أبو علي الفارسي)]
- 55:2/q25/p2 Some readings are altered or condemned for doctrinal reasons. — Ibn Ashur reports some Muʿtazilites taking Allah accusative in ‘God spoke to Moses’ to avoid divine speech, and condemns a Rafidi dual ‘misleaders’ aimed at Abu Bakr and Umar. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(بعض الرافضة,بعض المعتزلة)]
- 55:2/q25/p3 Al-Zamakhshari judged several transmitted readings grammatically weak. — Ibn Ashur cites his criticism of Ibn Amir’s passive zuyyina construction as an example of the rejected approach. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(الزمخشري); IBNASHUR-FULL-]

## 55:2/q26 How is the Qur’an described as enduring guidance?  [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · interpretation]
- 55:2/q26/p1 It gives life to hearts and has been protected from alteration while superseding earlier religions. — Al-Zarkashi says it is called a spirit because it brings lasting life to the heart, enables people and invites reflection (40:15). Al-Jishumi says it was revealed without addition or omission and made to supersede other religions. [2 notes: BURHAN; JISHUMI]

## 55:2/q27 What kinds of knowledge and argument does the Qur’an convey?  [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · interpretation]
- 55:2/q27/p1 It gives clear proofs in familiar Arabic while allowing deeper rational and rhetorical insight. — Al-Burhan says it contains every kind of proof, demonstration, division and definition; 14:4 explains why messengers address their people’s language. Obscure disputation can show inability to establish a point plainly. Its arguments address ordinary hearers while specialists discern subtleties, using reason, hearing, reflection and memory (2:164). Change of states proves the world’s origination; Abraham’s setting stars, moon and sun extend proof from the lesser bodies to all corporeal things (6:76–78). [8 notes: BURHAN]

## 55:2/q28 What do Qur’anic parables teach?  [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · interpretation]
- 55:2/q28/p1 They make meanings memorable, prompt reflection and convey ethical and legal guidance. — Al-Suyuti cites 39:27 and 29:43 and reports a prophetic fivefold division: lawful, unlawful, clear, ambiguous and parables, each with its response. Al-Mawardi says grasping the represented meaning is essential, comparing neglected representation to an unbridled horse. Al-Shafiʿi requires jurists to know parables of obedience and disobedience. Ibn Abd al-Salam says praise, blame, reward and wasted deeds can indicate rulings and that praise alongside permission can mark recommendation or obligation. Al-Suyuti lists exhortation, deterrence, sensory clarification, proof, reward and punishment; al-Zarkashi adds eloquence and al-Zamakhshari says analogy makes conjectural ideas visible with fitting comparisons. [9 notes: ITQAN(أبو هريرة,الزركشي,الزمخشري,الشافعي,الشيخ عز الدين,الماوردي)]

## 55:2/q29 What does the adjoining word bayān mean?  [* · meaning]
- 55:2/q29/p1 It is evidence leading to knowledge or clarification that distinguishes one meaning from another. — Al-Tabrisi offers these two descriptions; al-Jurjani adds that speech presents knowledge with its qualities and states, while the best speech depends on arrangement rather than isolated words. [3 notes: TABRISI; JURJANI-ASRAR]

## 55:2/q30 Whom may al-insān in the next verse designate?  [* · referent]
- 55:2/q30/p1 It may mean humanity as a kind, Adam, or Muhammad. — Al-Nasafi reports the three alternatives. [1 notes: NASAFI-FULL]

## 55:2/q31 How is the sura’s opening counted and classified?  [* · sciences]
- 55:2/q31/p1 Most verse counters count the opening as one verse, and al-Zarkashi classifies this sura among declarative openings. — Ibn Ashur gives the verse count; al-Zarkashi lists al-Rahman with al-Anbiya and al-Qamar among twenty-three declarative sura openings. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; BURHAN]

## 55:2/q32 What other reading or expression in this sura is noted?  [* · readings]
- 55:2/q32/p1 ʿAmr ibn ʿUbayd read the rose comparison in 55:37 in the nominative, picturing a rose-colored sky emerging. — Al-Zamakhshari classifies the expression as tajrid. [1 notes: BURHAN(الزمخشري)]

## 55:2/q33 How can one Qur’anic statement hold two aspects?  [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · interpretation]
- 55:2/q33/p1 An act may be affirmed of a human in one respect and denied in another because God causes its effect. — Al-Zarkashi illustrates with 8:17: the Prophet threw, but the resulting effect was not his own act. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 55:2/q34 Who first recited the Qur’an publicly in Mecca after the Prophet?  [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · reports]
- 55:2/q34/p1 ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd was the first to recite it publicly in Mecca after the Prophet. — ʿUrwa ibn al-Zubayr reports that Quraysh beat Ibn Masʿūd as he continued reciting, and that he returned ready to repeat the public recitation. [1 notes: IBNHISHAM(عروة بن الزبير)]
