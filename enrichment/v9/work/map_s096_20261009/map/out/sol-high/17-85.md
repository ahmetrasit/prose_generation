# 17:85: 29 questions

## 17:85/q01 Who asked the Prophet about the spirit, and how did the question arise?  [وَيَسْـَٔلُونَكَ ٱلرُّوحِ · reports]
turns on: Reports place the question among Jews in Medina or Quraysh in Mecca after consultation with Jews; one names Abu Jahl.
- 17:85/q01/p1 Quraysh asked after Jews suggested a test. — Reports name Nadr ibn al-Harith and Uqba ibn Abi Muayt as envoys; Bursevi also names Ubayy ibn Khalaf. Jewish scholars suggested the Cave youths, Dhul-Qarnayn, and spirit; answering the first two while leaving the third obscure would attest prophethood in some accounts. Ibn Ashur distinguishes a spirit-only consultation and notes Meccan–Yathrib ties. Ibn Abbas's broader account places Quraysh leaders at the Kaaba, accusing Muhammad of dividing society and offering wealth, rule, or treatment. [50 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(اليهود); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن عباس); BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(ابن عباس); BURSEVI; DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ELMALILI(İbn Abbas); IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNHIBBAN(ابن عباس); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(عطاء عن ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; JISHUMI(الأصم); KASHSHAF-FULL; MUJAHID(قتادة); MUSNAD-AHMAD(ابن عباس); NASAFI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; RAZI-FULL; STUDYQURAN(some reports); SUYUTI-LUBAB(ابن عباس); TABATABAI(ابن عباس); THALABI; TIRMIDHI(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-ASBAB(المفسرون,عكرمة عن ابن عباس); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); YAHYA-SALLAM(الكلبي,قتادة)]
- 17:85/q01/p2 Jews directly questioned him in Medina. — Ibn Masud places the exchange in a field, garden, ruined district, street, or date grove; the Prophet paused, sometimes leaning on a staff or palm stalk, before reciting the revelation. Some Jews warned against asking, regretted asking, or said the answer matched their scripture. Wāḥidī notes Bukhari and Muslim transmit the report. Jishumi says Jewish questioners are one option; al-Zajjaj instead names polytheists. [42 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن مسعود); ALUSI-FULL(ابن مسعود); BAGHAWI-FULL(عبد الله عن النبي); BIQAI-FULL(عبد الله بن مسعود); BUKHARI(عبد الله بن مسعود); DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,مجاهد); ELMALILI(Abdullah b. Mes‘ud); IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن مسعود); IBNATIYYA-FULL(عبد الله بن مسعود); IBNHIBBAN(عبد الله); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن مسعود); IBNKATHIR-FULL(عبد الله بن مسعود); JISHUMI(ابن عباس,قتادة); MUSLIM(عبد الله بن مسعود); MUSNAD-AHMAD(عبد الله بن مسعود); QURTUBI-FULL(عبد الله بن مسعود); STUDYQURAN(some reports); TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,ابن مسعود,قتادة,مجاهد); THALABI(ابن عباس,عبد الله); TIRMIDHI(عبد الله); WAHIDI-ASBAB(عبد الله); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن مسعود); YAHYA-SALLAM(مجاهد)]
- 17:85/q01/p3 The question concerned Abu Jahl and his companions. — Muqatil names Abu Jahl's party as the occasion. [1 notes: MUQATIL]

## 17:85/q02 How long did revelation pause after the test question?  [وَيَسْـَٔلُونَكَ · reports]
turns on: The reports give three, twelve, fifteen, or forty days or nights.
- 17:85/q02/p1 Forty days. — Wahidi and Razi report forty days and connect 18:23–24 with the omission of the exception; Baghawi attributes forty to Ikrima. [4 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(مجاهد وعكرمة); BIQAI-FULL(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس)]
- 17:85/q02/p2 Fifteen days or nights. — Ibn Atiyya and Biqai connect the delay to the Prophet's promise to answer without saying 'if God wills'; Baghawi records a fifteen-day report. [3 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(مجاهد وعكرمة); BIQAI-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 17:85/q02/p3 Twelve nights. — Baghawi attributes this to Mujahid; Biqai lists it as a variant. [2 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(مجاهد وعكرمة); BIQAI-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 17:85/q02/p4 Three days. — Yahya reports Gabriel's delay and his later citation of 19:64: angels descend only by God's command. [2 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM]

## 17:85/q03 What was the test meant to establish?  [وَيَسْـَٔلُونَكَ · reports]
turns on: Reports differ in their test formula and Razi disputes whether the proposed test could prove prophethood.
- 17:85/q03/p1 Answering the two historical questions but leaving the spirit unexplained would identify a prophet. — The Jews' scripture allegedly named but did not explain spirit. Wahidi Asbab says answering all or none disqualified him; Abu Hayyan notes variants. Razi objects that stories could be disbelieved and silence proves nothing. [13 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(اليهود); BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI(İbn Abbas); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(عطاء عن ابن عباس); JISHUMI(الأصم,قيل); KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; WAHIDI-ASBAB(المفسرون); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); YAHYA-SALLAM(الكلبي); RAZI-FULL-]
- 17:85/q03/p2 The question was an obstinate attempt to embarrass him. — Mawardi compares demands for signs; Jishumi's al-Asamm cites the Jewish book; Burhan and Itqan say the word has many referents, so a general answer defeated the trap. Islahi calls it ridicule. [6 notes: BURHAN(صاحب الإيضاح); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ITQAN(صاحب الإفصاح); JISHUMI(الأصم); MAWARDI-FULL]
- 17:85/q03/p3 The delayed answer itself showed revelation was from God. — Biqai argues that the Prophet could not compose an answer even while the delay damaged his reputation. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 17:85/q04 What did the questioners ask about the spirit?  [وَيَسْـَٔلُونَكَ ٱلرُّوحِ · interpretation]
turns on: The answer can be read against inquiries into essence, origination, Gabriel's descent, or the Quran's source.
- 17:85/q04/p1 They asked what the life-giving spirit is, including its essence, location, and relation to the body. — Jishumi lists its definition, varied perception, and need for life; Wahidi Wajiz gives its reality, manner, and place. Razi says the phrase could also invite questions about substance, spatiality, survival, and happiness. Ibn Ashur says the general phenomenon is known intuitively. [16 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الجمهور); JISHUMI(قيل); KASHSHAF-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Şevkânî); NASAFI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(أكثر أهل التأويل); RAZI-FULL; TABRISI(ابن مسعود وابن عباس); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 17:85/q04/p2 They asked whether the spirit is created or eternal. — Mawardi, Tabrisī, Baydawi, Nasafi, Burhan, and Razi report this as a question answered by divine creation. Alusi and Tabatabai dispute that this was asked. [9 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الإمام الرازي); BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURHAN(بعضهم); MAWARDI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL(قيل); RAZI-FULL; TABRISI; ALUSI-FULL-; TABATABAI(بعضهم)-]
- 17:85/q04/p3 They asked about the Quran's form, source, arrangement, or why revelation came to Muhammad. — Jishumi lists composition and bearer; Razi says the question could contrast divine revelation with poetry or soothsaying; Islahi says the concealed complaint concerned God's choice of recipient. [8 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; JISHUMI(أبو مسلم,الحسن، والأصم، وأبو مسلم); RAZI-FULL; TABRISI; THALABI(قوم); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق)]
- 17:85/q04/p4 They asked how a soul from God can be punished in a body. — Ibn Abbas reports the Jewish question; the answer came through Gabriel, whom the Jews then called their enemy, prompting 2:97. [5 notes: DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس); TAB-FULL(ابن عباس); THALABI(ابن عباس)]
- 17:85/q04/p5 They asked about Gabriel's attributes and descent with revelation. — Jishumi attributes this to Ibn Abbas; Alusi reports Hasan and Qatada asking how Gabriel came and delivered revelation. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الحسن,قتادة); JISHUMI(ابن عباس، والحسن، وقتادة)]
- 17:85/q04/p6 They asked about Jesus. — Nahhas says the reply counters the Christian claim about him. [1 notes: NAHHAS(بعض العلماء)]

## 17:85/q05 How is the hamza in 'they ask you' pronounced?  [وَيَسْـَٔلُونَكَ · readings]
turns on: The reading changes pronunciation but not meaning.
- 17:85/q05/p1 Some readers fully pronounce the hamza, while Hafs eases it. — Qiraat-ER says the semantic value remains the same. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER]

## 17:85/q06 How does asking with ʿan work here?  [وَيَسْـَٔلُونَكَ عَنِ ٱلرُّوحِ · grammar]
- 17:85/q06/p1 An information-seeking verb may take its second object directly or with ʿan, more often with ʿan. — Al-Raghib gives this verse as the example, as cited by Itqan. [1 notes: ITQAN(الراغب)]

## 17:85/q07 What does the Arabic word rūḥ mean and how is it formed?  [ٱلرُّوحِ · meaning]
turns on: The sources relate it to wind, breath, movement, life, and other Quranic uses.
- 17:85/q07/p1 It can name the soul or life-principle of a body. — Ibn Qutayba notes God takes it at death; Damghani includes animals and living beings. Ibn Jawzi links Adam; Elmalili distinguishes motive force, biological life, and conscious human life. Tabatabai states ordinary Arabic usage enables sensation and voluntary movement. [11 notes: DAMGHANI; ELMALILI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أهل التفسير); IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; TABATABAI]
- 17:85/q07/p2 It is related to wind or breath. — Ibn Qutayba links rūḥ, rawḥ, and rīḥ through vowels and patterns, comparing other word families. Jishumi's Abu Ali explains waw becoming ya under kasra and gives arwāḥ as plural. Dhu al-Rumma's fire poem uses rūḥ for blowing; Ibn Jawzi glosses its tinder and measured breath. Mawardi and Wahidi derive the animal spirit's name from wind. [9 notes: IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(ابن قتيبة); IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; JISHUMI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT]
- 17:85/q07/p3 It can name Gabriel. — Ibn Qutayba cites Holy Spirit and Trustworthy Spirit in 2:253 and 26:193–194; Kuranyolu surveys usages. [2 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 17:85/q07/p4 It can name divine revelation or the Quran. — Tabatabai calls knowledge and revelation life-giving figuratively; Kuranyolu cites 16:2, 40:15, 42:52. [2 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; TABATABAI]
- 17:85/q07/p5 It can mean motion, expansion, or a motivating force. — Wahidi cites a broad foot, wide bowl, and wine called rāḥ; Elmalili extends the usage to forces such as electricity. [2 notes: ELMALILI; WAHIDI-BASIT]
- 17:85/q07/p6 It can mean blowing or breath that revives fire. — Ibn Qutayba and Ibn Jawzi cite Dhu al-Rumma's line; the blowing is related to wind from the spirit. [3 notes: IBNJAWZI-NUZHA; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL]

## 17:85/q08 What is the spirit asked about here?  [ٱلرُّوحِ · referent]
turns on: Sources use context and other Quranic occurrences to choose among human spirit, Gabriel, Quran, angel, distinct creature, and Jesus.
- 17:85/q08/p1 The animating human or animal spirit. — Wahidi Basit says this is the immediate sense; Ibn Atiyya, Razi, Abu Hayyan, Alusi, and most exegetes prefer it. Razi links the previous verse's discussion of each person's pattern; al-Dimashqi cautions against importing other Quranic uses. The question includes how it animates and enters the body. ## 17:85/q09 What is the human spirit or self in its nature?  [ٱلرُّوحِ · theology]
turns on: Views differ on whether the self is a body, a subtle body, an accident of life, or an immaterial substance.
- 17:85/q09/p1 A simple, immaterial substance governs and survives the body. — Razi argues that identity persists as body parts change or vanish; self-awareness remains without awareness of organs; calling them 'my' organs distinguishes owner and owned; one subject perceives, desires, speaks and acts through different organs; knowledge persists without sense organs; thinking and asceticism strengthen soul while weakening body; accumulated intelligibles do not displace one another; bodily transformations and angelic appearances separate form from essence; martyrs, the dead, and the tranquil soul survive bodily death in 3:169, 40:46, 71:25, 89:27–28, 6:61–62; dreams and practices for the dead corroborate survival; 59:19, 6:93, 23:12–14, 15:29, 91:7–8, and 76:2 distinguish spirit from bodily fashioning and responsibility. He locates its initial influence at the heart, rejects bodily and accident theories, and answers objections from divine likeness, bodily Quranic wording, and martyrs' provision. Ibn Sina's self-awareness and philosophers' survival support the view; Alusi favors an immaterial cognizer given when a prepared body needs governance but regards scriptural proof as suggestive rather than decisive. Razi eliminates pure elements and dense organs and knows no theory of a human body outside the body. Alusi notes possible differences between human and spirit, reports Ibn al-Qayyim's image-based individuation and rebuts an immaterialist difficulty, and says spirit may illuminate scattered original bodily parts. Elmalili describes spirit as shaping disposition toward guidance or error. [89 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أبو القاسم الراغب الأصفهاني,أكثر الإلهيين من الفلاسفة,ابن القيم,ابن سينا,الإمام,الإمام الرازي); BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI(البيضاوي); ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; RAZI-FULL(أكثر الإلهيين من الفلاسفة,القائلون بأن الإنسان شيء مغاير لهذا الجسد,المحققون,المنكرون,النبي,جمهور المتكلمين); TABRISI(بعض العلماء); ALUSI-FULL+; RAZI-FULL+; ALUSI-FULL(ابن القيم)-]
- 17:85/q09/p2 A subtle airy body flows through an animal's passages or members. — Jishumi's teachers, Tabrisī's theologians and al-Murtada, Biqai's Sunni preference, Ibn Kathir's al-Suhayli, and others use airflow or water in a tree or rosewater in a rose. Ali ibn Isa says life is in every part. Nasafi rejects the fine-air definition. Ibn Kathir says al-Suhayli's account of one thing called spirit and soul by aspect is good. [10 notes: ALUSI-FULL(ابن القيم,الإمام الرازي,المحققون); JISHUMI(مشايخنا); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; TABRISI(أكثر المتكلمين,علي بن عيسى); BIQAI-FULL(أهل السنة)+; IBNKATHIR-FULL(السهيلي)+; NASAFI-FULL(قيل)-]
- 17:85/q09/p3 Its precise anatomy or essence remains unknown. — Wahidi reports brain, blood, and whole-body locations, citing 14:17 for the last, but calls them speculation; Baydawi and Bursevi say sense-based knowing reaches attributes rather than essence. Kuranyolu says science has not established the soul's nature. [11 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الخفاجي,الكشف,بعض متأخري الصوفية,بعضهم); BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; WAHIDI-BASIT]
- 17:85/q09/p4 The spirit is in the heart, as a meaning or indivisible occupant. — Jishumi and Tabrisī report a meaning in the heart; Razi reports a heart location and argues free choice and knowledge reside there, citing 7:179, 58:22, 26:193–194. [5 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أقوال مختلفة); JISHUMI(قيل); RAZI-FULL(منهم من يقول); TABRISI(الأسواري)]
- 17:85/q09/p5 The human is the visible body, bodily structure, or its elemental mixture. — Razi cites most theologians, physicians, deniers of survival, Abu al-Husayn al-Basri, and early Mutazilites; they variously specify shape, temperament, or bodies bearing life and power. Razi rejects this through identity, unity of subject, bodily change, and survival arguments; he says a human-shaped angel and a transformed person refute shape as definition. [5 notes: ALUSI-FULL(جمهور المتكلمين); RAZI-FULL(أكثر شيوخ المعتزلة,جمهور الأطباء ومنكري بقاء النفس وأبو الحسين البصري,جمهور المتكلمين); TABATABAI(بعضهم); RAZI-FULL(القائلون بهذا القول,النبي)-]
- 17:85/q09/p6 The spirit is the living human person. — Al-Ikhshid and al-Nazzam identify it with the morally responsible human; Shawkani calls it the essential self, but these formulations vary in whether they mean body or distinct self. [3 notes: JISHUMI(أبو بكر أحمد بن علي الإخشيد); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Şevkânî); TABRISI(ابن الإخشيد والنظام)]
- 17:85/q09/p7 The spirit is blood. — Wahidi and Razi report the inference from blood loss causing death. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أقوال مختلفة); RAZI-FULL(بعضهم); WAHIDI-BASIT]
- 17:85/q09/p8 The spirit is inhaled air or bodily vapor. — Wahidi cites strangulation; Razi reports airy spirits mixed with innate heat generated in heart or brain; Farghani describes a life-bearing vapor from the heart. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(المحقق الفرغاني); RAZI-FULL(منهم من يقول); WAHIDI-BASIT]
- 17:85/q09/p9 A luminous celestial body permeates the prepared human body. — Razi calls this worthy of reflection; investigators and Ibn al-Qayyim call it strong and claim scripture, Sunna, consensus, reason, nature, and 105 proofs. Thabit ibn Qurra links the soul to such bodies; Alusi allows his composite version. [6 notes: ALUSI-FULL(المحققون,ثابت بن قرة); RAZI-FULL(ثابت بن قرة); ALUSI-FULL(ابن القيم,الإمام الرازي)+; RAZI-FULL(من الناس)+]
- 17:85/q09/p10 Spirit is the accident or quality of life. — Abu al-Hudhayl identifies it with life; Mufid, Balkhi and Baghdad Mutazilites define life as the accident allowing power, knowledge and choice. Razi says a human who knows and acts is a substance, not an accident; Alusi rejects postmortem nonexistence on this basis. [2 notes: JISHUMI(أبو الهذيل); TABRISI(المفيد والبلخي); ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)-; RAZI-FULL-]
- 17:85/q09/p11 The spirit is an indivisible part of the brain. — Razi reports this as an alternative to the heart location; Alusi lists it among many definitions. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أقوال مختلفة); RAZI-FULL(منهم من يقول)]
- 17:85/q09/p12 The spirit is innate fiery heat. — Razi reports a view combining heat with cardiac and cerebral spirits; Alusi lists the same. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أقوال مختلفة); RAZI-FULL(من الناس)]
- 17:85/q09/p13 Humanity is the union of body and a distinct soul. — Razi says the union ends at death while the soul persists. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(الفريق الثاني)]

## 17:85/q10 Are spirit and soul the same thing?  [ٱلرُّوحِ · theology]
turns on: Reports use the terms interchangeably, distinguish them, or say usage determines their relation.
- 17:85/q10/p1 They are one in essence or are used interchangeably. — Ibn Zayd says most scholars and reports exchange the words; Abu Hurayra's death report uses soul and spirit for one departure and ascent. Ibn al-Qayyim follows the majority; al-Suhayli says one thing has different aspects. [7 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أبو هريرة,ابن القيم,ابن زيد); IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(السهيلي); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 17:85/q10/p2 The terms coincide in some uses and differ in others. — Alusi concludes contextual usage decides; Elmalili notes the identity question remains disputed. [2 notes: ELMALILI; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 17:85/q10/p3 They are distinct entities or levels. — Ibn Habib makes spirit breath and soul the feeling form that leaves in sleep; Ibn Mandah reports earthy-fiery versus luminous, or human versus divine; hadith scholars contrast worldly desire with afterlife orientation; Farghani makes soul vapor shaped by spiritual spirit; Ibn Barjan makes spirit from soul's inner reality. Sufis say discipline may turn soul into spirit. [9 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أهل الأثر,ابن القيم,ابن برجان,ابن حبيب,ابن منده,المحقق الفرغاني)]
- 17:85/q10/p4 There is no demonstrated answer on whether they are one or two. — Ibn Jawzi rejects unproved medical and philosophical theories and cites early Muslims' restraint. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD]

## 17:85/q11 When did spirits come into being relative to bodies?  [ٱلرُّوحِ · theology]
turns on: Some put spirits before bodies; others at bodily formation, and an ancient view treats them as eternal.
- 17:85/q11/p1 They arise with or after bodily formation. — Ghazali, Aristotle, and Ibn al-Qayyim are cited. Ibn al-Qayyim uses the fetal hadith: an angel breathes spirit after four months; if it existed earlier, the report would say bring and insert. Alusi favors an immaterial cognizer bestowed when bodily temperament becomes ready, and cites 23:14. [8 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أفلاطون,حجة الإسلام الغزالي); IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن القيم)+]
- 17:85/q11/p2 They were created before bodies. — Sulami's Ibn Ata and Wasiti report prior creation, and one report makes Muhammad's spirit first. Al-Khattabi reads the 'gathered troops' hadith this way; Ibn Hazm posits a separate barzakh, though Alusi rejects his claim of consensus and says no scriptural proof establishes it. Ibn al-Qayyim criticizes a two-thousand-year report's chain. [6 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الخطابي,عائشة,محمد بن نصر المروزي); SULAMI(ابن عطاء,الواسطي); ALUSI-FULL(ابن القيم)-]
- 17:85/q11/p3 The soul or spirit is eternal. — Alusi attributes this to Plato and predecessors, while saying Muslims agree on origination; he reports a later attempt to reinterpret Plato subtly. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(أفلاطون,بعض صدور المتأخرين); ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; ALUSI-FULL(المسلمون)-]

## 17:85/q12 Does the spirit die when the body dies?  [ٱلرُّوحِ · theology]
turns on: Death may mean annihilation, bodily separation, or a changed mode of life.
- 17:85/q12/p1 It survives the body's death and continues reward or punishment. — Alusi reports philosophers' reasoning: the body neither causes nor sustains the immaterial soul, and its loss need not destroy it; body is an instrument and initial condition, not a continuing cause. Martyrs' and other spirits are rewarded; Alusi notes annihilation remains possible in his theology even if it does not occur. [15 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الفلاسفة,طائفة); RAZI-FULL(النبي)]
- 17:85/q12/p2 It dies or ceases. — Alusi reports arguments from every soul tasting death, the angels' death, and the two deaths in 40:11. Some say animal spirits cease if animals are not resurrected; others call the spirit a life accident ending at death, which Alusi rejects. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أقوال مختلفة,طائفة); ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)-]
- 17:85/q12/p3 Its death is separation from the body, not annihilation. — Alusi says shock of the trumpet need not mean death, destruction can mean loss of function, and 40:11 does not prove a second annihilation; the spirit returns for lasting reward or punishment. For animal spirits he makes survival conditional on resurrection. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أقوال مختلفة); ALUSI-FULL+]

## 17:85/q13 Where are spirits after bodily death?  [ٱلرُّوحِ · theology]
turns on: Reports propose Paradise, graves, lower heaven, Sijjin, varied abodes, or free movement.
- 17:85/q13/p1 Their abodes differ by rank and state. — Investigators place prophets in highest Illiyyin, martyrs in Paradise, and children in blessed places; the Prophet's last prayer asks for the highest companion. Nasafi gives four believer ranks and Sijjin for unbelievers. Al-Ifsah, Qurtubi, and Suyuti reconcile varied images and movements; Alusi rejects Nasafi's denial that obedient believers enjoy Paradise. Ibn Rajab says the report of return to earth cannot override many Paradise reports and 20:55 concerns bodies. [9 notes: ALUSI-FULL(ابن المبارك,ابن رجب,الإفصاح,الإمام الشافعي,المحققون,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,النسفي); ALUSI-FULL(القرطبي)+; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 17:85/q13/p2 Martyrs' spirits dwell in green birds; other souls may also take bird forms. — Ibn Masud's Muslim report describes green birds; variants mention green or white birds and sparrows for believers' children. Al-Kurani calls these fitting imaginal bodies. Ibn al-Mubarak's Ka'b report places martyrs by a river under a green dome. Alusi answers the objection that two spirits cannot be in one bird by saying the bird may have only the martyr's spirit or contain it as a fetus is contained. [6 notes: ALUSI-FULL(إبراهيم الكوراني,ابن المبارك,ابن مسعود,الإمام الشافعي,بعض المتكلمين)]
- 17:85/q13/p3 Believers' spirits gather in a heavenly house. — Wahb places a white house in the seventh heaven. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(وهب بن منبه)]
- 17:85/q13/p4 Unbelievers' spirits inhabit black birds in Hell. — Umm Bishr reports black birds eating and drinking fire; Nasafi places them in Sijjin beneath the seventh earth while connected to their bodies. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(أم بشر,النسفي)]
- 17:85/q13/p5 Spirits reside in grave courtyards. — Ibn Hazm attributes this to most hadith scholars; Ibn Abd al-Barr favors it except for martyrs; Mujahid says spirits remain above graves seven days. Alusi accepts temporary visits, not permanent confinement; greetings and shown seats can be mediated by a connection. [5 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعض المحققين,عامة أهل الحديث); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عبد البر,مجاهد)+]
- 17:85/q13/p6 All spirits stay in the lowest heaven near Adam. — A group uses the Mi'raj sight of people at Adam's right and left; Alusi replies that being seen there need not mean residence. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(طائفة); ALUSI-FULL-]
- 17:85/q13/p7 A spirit can move, visit, converse, and meet living spirits in dreams. — Malik is cited for release to go where it wishes, though Alusi limits that if sound. Other investigators propose rapid travel or a nonlocal connection. Alusi says dreams can carry meetings but cannot alone establish legal rulings; he cites Thabit's dream about armor, debt and emancipation and Ibn al-Qayyim's parallels. Some debts may detain spirits. [10 notes: ALUSI-FULL(ابن القيم,بعض الآثار,بعض المحققين,بعضهم,ثابت بن قيس بن شماس,مالك)]
- 17:85/q13/p8 The soul remains below while a distinct spirit rises. — Ibn Barjan uses this to explain Moses at his grave and Abraham in heaven; an evildoer's soul is cast down. Alusi says it reconciles seemingly conflicting reports. [4 notes: ALUSI-FULL(ابن برجان)]
- 17:85/q13/p9 An immaterial spirit has no spatial abode. — Alusi says only the body it governs is located; he nonetheless allows connection and perception in various places. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 17:85/q14 Can one spirit govern more than one body?  [ٱلرُّوحِ · theology]
turns on: Sufi accounts allow multiple manifestations; philosophers challenge shared knowledge.
- 17:85/q14/p1 Yes, an original body and imaginal or extraordinary manifestation can share a spirit. — Sufis compare Gabriel appearing as Dihya without leaving the Lote Tree and Quran as a pale man; Alusi says the shared-knowledge objection proves too little. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الفلاسفة,بعض الصوفية)]
- 17:85/q14/p2 No, one soul in several bodies would give them identical knowledge. — Alusi reports philosophers' objection and replies that the inference from different knowledge is too weak. [0 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الفلاسفة)-]

## 17:85/q15 What inward account of spirit and spiritual growth is given?  [ٱلرُّوحِ · inward]
turns on: Mystical notes describe several distinct aspects of spirit, embodiment, divine disclosure, and disciplined ascent.
- 17:85/q15/p1 Spirit is a subtle created light or reality whose effects animate bodies and open unseen perception. — Sulami reports a ray of reality, a delicacy drawn toward divine knowledge, and spirits predating bodies or emerging from divine beauty and holiness; Abu Said al-Haddad links life, intellect, covenant, and accountability, while Ibn Ata describes the spirit as pure and bright, unveiling the unseen. Wasiti says spirits were created and seized before bodies. [8 notes: RAZI-FULL(الواسطي); SULAMI(أبو سعيد الحداد,ابن عطاء,الواسطي,بعضهم)]
- 17:85/q15/p2 The seeker ascends by discipline from outward practice through purification, illumination, and self-effacement. — Bursevi's Najmiyyah gives heart, secret, spirit, hidden and witnessing stages. Alusi lists four stages, then details repentance, ascetic audit, retreat and remembrance, unveiling, tranquility, annihilation, and sober return. He rejects literal transfer of divine attributes into human soul; a saint's motive for jihad may conceal a wish to escape worship. [11 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الصوفية,بعض الأكابر,بعضهم); BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]
- 17:85/q15/p3 Spirit is worship, while only God sustains existence. — Sulami reports this twice, once from Baghdadi voices, emphasizing God's care and preservation. [2 notes: SULAMI(بعض البغداديين,بعضهم)]
- 17:85/q15/p4 Spirits experience divine disclosure, concealment, love, and varying nourishment. — Sulami says disclosure brings bliss and concealment suffering; Wasiti lists love, fear, hope and truthfulness, with gnosis grounded in God's knowledge. Favors may make spirits forget; prophetic spirits feed on subtle address, righteous spirits on regard, and ordinary spirits on food. A poem imagines ascent beyond stars. [5 notes: SULAMI(الواسطي,بعضهم)]
- 17:85/q15/p5 Spirit's presence gives the body's limbs light and proper conduct. — Sulami says its meeting illumines and adorns bodies; veiling causes improper action; some spirits turn from created things through witnessing the Real and receive the name peace. [3 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم)]
- 17:85/q15/p6 Two spirits serve death and ordinary life. — Abu Said al-Qurashi distinguishes departure that ends life from breath enabling eating and drinking. [1 notes: SULAMI(أبو سعيد القرشي)]
- 17:85/q15/p7 All spirits descend from the Prophet's primordial spirit. — Bursevi's Najmiyyah reads the spirit breathed into Adam and Jesus as deriving from Muhammad's spirit, and connects Adam's descendants and the Prophet's banner. [3 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]

## 17:85/q16 Why repeat 'the spirit' in the answer?  [ٱلرُّوحِ ٱلرُّوحُ · rhetoric]
- 17:85/q16/p1 The noun is repeated for emphasis and special concern. — Alusi notes the answer could have used a pronoun. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 17:85/q17 What does 'from my Lord's command' mean?  [مِنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّى · interpretation]
turns on: Amr is read as a matter known to God, His act of creating, a direct immaterial order, revelation, or sovereign power.
- 17:85/q17/p1 Spirit is a matter whose reality belongs to God's exclusive knowledge. — Farra and Tabari read amr as divine knowledge; Wahidi says no servant has this account, and Bursevi takes the possessive as exclusive knowledge. Alusi reads min as partitive or explanatory with amr as affair, and says the phrase honors the spirit and Lord. Ibn Ashur calls it a genus-level redirection, not a physical description. [25 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); BAYDAWI-FULL(قيل); DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ELMALILI; FARRA; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; IBNKATHIR-FULL; JISHUMI(أبو علي); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL; TAB-FULL; TABRISI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; ZAJJAJ; BURSEVI+; BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)-]
- 17:85/q17/p2 It is God's act or creative command: spirit is originated by Him. — Razi cites amr as act in 11:97 and 11:66 and change from ignorance to knowledge as proof of origination; Abu Hayyan likewise cites 11:97. Baydawi and Alusi explain 'Be' without matter or organs; Jishumi argues what can be withdrawn cannot be eternal. Ibn Ashur takes min as origin. Tabatabai accepts direct creation but rejects taking the question as merely temporal origination. [28 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الإمام الرازي,غير واحد); BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURHAN(بعضهم); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; JISHUMI(أبو صالح); MAWARDI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL(قيل); RAZI-FULL; TABRISI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ(قيل); ALUSI-FULL-; TABATABAI(بعضهم)-]
- 17:85/q17/p3 On the Quran reading, it is God's revelation and speech. — Hasan, Zajjaj, Kashshaf, Mawradi, Nasafi, and Tabrisī cite 42:52: it is God's disclosure and proof of prophethood beyond creatures' ability, not human composition. [10 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); BURHAN(الحسن البصري,بعضهم); JISHUMI(الحسن); KASHSHAF-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL(الحسن); NASAFI-FULL(الحسن); TABRISI; YAHYA-SALLAM(الحسن); ZAJJAJ(بعض المفسرين)]
- 17:85/q17/p4 It belongs to the immaterial command realm, brought directly into being without stages, matter, time, or place. — Biqai contrasts staged khalq and direct amr. Bursevi's Najmiyyah distinguishes sensible perishing creation from primordial command realities; Qunawi relates command to divine knowing. Tabatabai cites 7:54, 36:82–83, 54:50, embryology in 23:13–14 and malakut: every thing has a direct dependence on God. Elmalili reads min as initiatory or explanatory and emphasizes Lord's special governance. Alusi doubts that this categorical contrast is established Arabic usage. [27 notes: BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(البيضاوي,التأويلات النجمية,داوود القيصري,صدر الدين القنوي); ELMALILI; QASHANI; STUDYQURAN(some commentators); TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 17:85/q17/p5 It belongs to God's power or sovereignty alone. — Mawardi says only God has power over spirit; Elmalili reads amr as governing acts that nurture creation and denies identifying spirit or self with God. Tabatabai's hadith denies that God has a cavity or part breathed out. [9 notes: ELMALILI; MAWARDI-FULL; TABATABAI(أبو جعفر وأبو عبد الله عليهما السلام)]
- 17:85/q17/p6 On the Gabriel reading, he is a commanded creature. — Ibn Ashur interprets amr as an object of command, while Jishumi says no one else could produce any proposed referent. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; JISHUMI]

## 17:85/q18 Did the Prophet know the spirit's reality, and why was the answer limited?  [قُلِ ٱلرُّوحُ مِنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّى · interpretation]

- 17:85/q18/p1 He may have known but concealed or declined to elaborate. — Qatada reports Ibn Abbas and perhaps himself explained once then stopped; other reports say Ibn Abbas concealed the Gabriel gloss. Baghawi says prophetic silence served the test; Nahhas says explanation would disprove prophethood in the Jews' rule. Ibn Hibban permits withholding knowledge listeners cannot bear. Razi counters that ignorance of a creature is implausible when God is knowable and revelation grants the Prophet knowledge. [35 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قتادة); ALUSI-FULL(الخفاجي,الكشف,عبد الله بن بريدة,معاذ); BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); BIQAI-FULL(السلف); BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية); IBNATIYYA-FULL(قتادة); IBNHIBBAN; IBNKATHIR-FULL(السهيلي,قتادة); ITQAN; MAWARDI-FULL(بعض المتكلمين,قتادة); NAHHAS(بعض العلماء); QURTUBI-FULL(قتادة); RAZI-FULL; TAB-FULL(قتادة); TABRISI(الجبائي); THALABI(قتادة); YAHYA-SALLAM(ابن عباس,قتادة)]

[14 notes: ALUSI-FULL(عبد الله بن بريدة); BIQAI-FULL(السلف); BURHAN; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,عبد الله بن بريدة); IBNKATHIR-FULL; ITQAN; KASHSHAF-FULL(عبد الله بن بريدة); NASAFI-FULL(أبو هريرة); QURTUBI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(عبد الله بن بريدة); BAGHAWI-FULL+; BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)-; RAZI-FULL-]

Ibn Ashur says all possibilities amount to a general definition. Mawradi proposes preventing irrelevant questions, avoiding philosophical diction, or leaving reason to investigate; Biqai warns against neglecting useful Quranic guidance for unprovable speculation. Tabatabai notes that whether the phrase answers or redirects follows each proposed referent. [16 notes: ALUSI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA; MAWARDI-FULL(بعض المتكلمين); TABATABAI]
- 17:85/q18/p4 The reality can be known through reason, unveiling, or divine teaching. — Mawardi mentions rational knowability; Khafaji distinguishes spirit's knowable essence from God's; Kashf assigns unveiling to mystics. - 17:85/q19/p4 Quraysh or the immediate questioners. — Elmalili notes this completes the speech within the command to say; Ibn Ashur lists Quraysh and their Jewish informants. Qurtubi distinguishes questioners from Jews collectively. [3 notes: ELMALILI(bazı müfessirîn); IBNASHUR-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]

## 17:85/q20 How is 'and you were given' read?  [وَمَآ أُوتِيتُم · readings]
turns on: The pronoun differs between second-person and third-person readings.
- 17:85/q20/p1 Third-person plural: 'they were given'. — Ibn Masud and al-Amash read it this way, sometimes attributed to the Prophet; the referent is the questioners or Jews. Baghawi's al-Amash says it is their recitation; Muslim gives Isa ibn Yunus's variant. [8 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن مسعود والأعمش); ALUSI-FULL(ابن مسعود والأعمش); BAGHAWI-FULL(الأعمش); BUKHARI(الأعمش); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن مسعود والأعمش); MUSLIM; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن مسعود); SAMIN-DURR(عبد الله والأعمش)]
- 17:85/q20/p2 Second-person plural: 'you were given'. — The majority and standard Hafs form directly address hearers; Qurtubi prefers it. Muslim notes Waki's transmission has this wording. [2 notes: MUSLIM; QURTUBI-FULL+]

## 17:85/q21 How does 'of knowledge' attach grammatically?  [مِّنَ ٱلْعِلْمِ · grammar]
turns on: Samin rules out one ellipsis because of the exception's scope.
- 17:85/q21/p1 It attaches to 'you were given'. — Samin says it cannot be an omitted circumstance of 'little': material in the scope of illā cannot precede it. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]

## 17:85/q22 In what sense is human knowledge 'little'?  [مِّنَ ٱلْعِلْمِ إِلَّا قَلِيلًۭا · interpretation]
turns on: The quantity can be relative to God's infinite knowledge, limited to what human needs or senses permit, or specifically knowledge of spirit.
- 17:85/q22/p1 All acquired human knowledge is slight beside God's inexhaustible knowledge. — Yahya, Tabari, Zajjaj, Ibn Ishaq, Razi, and others compare finite human learning with divine infinity. Zajjaj says little and much are relational. Khidr's bird sipping from the sea and Bursevi's drop in oceans illustrate it; wisdom can be great for humans yet slight beside God. Elmalili says Adam learned names, not all essences, and the Quran contains all needed religious and legal guidance, not every reality. [60 notes: ALUSI-FULL(اليهود,عكرمة); BAGHAWI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BINTSHATI; BIQAI-FULL; BURHAN; BURSEVI(أبو مدين المغربي,بعض الكبار); DURR-FULL(ابن جرير,عطاء بن يسار); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الخضر,رسول الله); IBNHISHAM(ابن إسحاق); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; IBNKATHIR-FULL(عطاء بن يسار,عكرمة); JISHUMI; KASHSHAF-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; MAWARDI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; QASHANI; QURTUBI-FULL; RAZI-FULL(المفسرون); TAB-FULL(ابن إسحاق,ابن جريج,عكرمة,قتادة); TABRISI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; YAHYA-SALLAM; ZAJJAJ; ELMALILI+; TAB-FULL+]
- 17:85/q22/p2 People receive small portions as needs arise or through limited sensory routes. — Mawardi speaks of successive need; Baydawi, Bursevi, and Alusi explain sense-dependence and how inaccessible essences evade perception. Qashani calls ordinary knowledge sensory beside divine and rooted knowledge. Ibn Ashur compares it with all knowable facts. [8 notes: ALUSI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL; QASHANI]
- 17:85/q22/p3 The limit refers especially to knowledge of the spirit. — Wahidi Basit links it to the Prophet and believers not being told the spirit's reality; Kuranyolu notes scientific uncertainty. Biqai says ignorance of its precise nature does not deny its existence. [3 notes: BIQAI-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; WAHIDI-BASIT]
- 17:85/q22/p4 The address teaches humility while encouraging continued learning. — Elmalili says knowledge is bestowed and can be withdrawn, but divine infinity opens unending progress. Bint al-Shati uses the verse to admit limits of tafsir and Quranic secrets; Abu Madyan says knowledge is a loan. [9 notes: BINTSHATI; BURSEVI(أبو مدين المغربي); ELMALILI]

## 17:85/q23 How did claims about the Torah or great wisdom challenge 'little knowledge'?  [وَمَآ أُوتِيتُم مِّنَ ٱلْعِلْمِ إِلَّا قَلِيلًۭا · reports]
turns on: The challenge treats Torah or wisdom as abundant; the reply compares it to God's inexhaustible words.
- 17:85/q23/p1 The Jews claimed the Torah contains all knowledge, or that wisdom is great good. — Muqatil adds their charge against Gabriel; reports link 2:269, 18:109, and 31:27 to the exchange. Tirmidhi grades his Ibn Abbas route hasan sahih gharib; some accounts include both Jews and Muslims in the question. [35 notes: ALUSI-FULL(اليهود,عطاء بن يسار,عكرمة); BAYDAWI-FULL; BURHAN; DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNASHUR-FULL(اليهود,عطاء بن يسار); IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNHIBBAN(ابن عباس); IBNHISHAM(ابن إسحاق,ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس,عطاء بن يسار,عكرمة); KASHSHAF-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); MUQATIL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,اليهود); MUSNAD-AHMAD(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL; TAB-FULL(عكرمة); TIRMIDHI(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; ZAJJAJ]
- 17:85/q23/p2 Torah and wisdom are useful or great for their purpose, yet small beside God. — Ibn Ishaq says the Torah suffices if followed, but is a little of God's knowledge. Ikrima calls knowledge that saves from Hell much; Baydawi and Alusi say wisdom covers truth and good within human capacity for both lives. Zajjaj and Kashshaf treat 'little' and 'much' as relative. Trees as pens and seas as ink in 31:27 or 18:109 show inexhaustible divine words. [18 notes: ALUSI-FULL(اليهود,عكرمة); BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNHISHAM(ابن إسحاق); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; IBNKATHIR-FULL(عطاء بن يسار,عكرمة); KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; RAZI-FULL; TAB-FULL(عكرمة); ZAJJAJ]

## 17:85/q24 Where or when was this verse revealed?  [* · sciences]
turns on: The Medina eyewitness account and Meccan Quraysh account prompt different datings and reconciliations.
- 17:85/q24/p1 In Mecca. — Ibn Kathir favors the Quraysh report; Ibn Ashur says the majority place these passages there. Tabatabai rejects a Medinan dating because of the sura's unified Meccan context. Ata says Jews later challenged the wording in Medina. Ibn Ashur notes the insertion depends on the verse's revelation between surrounding passages. [12 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; DURR-FULL(عطاء بن يسار); ELMALILI(İbn Abbas); IBNASHUR-FULL(الجمهور); IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(عطاء بن يسار); ITQAN; TAB-FULL(ابن إسحاق); TABATABAI; IBNKATHIR-FULL+]
- 17:85/q24/p2 It descended twice, once for each occasion. — Ibn Kathir, Ibn Hajar, Zarkashi, Biqai, and later commentators reconcile the two reports by repeated revelation. [9 notes: ALUSI-FULL; BURHAN(ابن مسعود); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ITQAN(ابن كثير,الزركشي); SUYUTI-LUBAB(ابن كثير وابن حجر); BIQAI-FULL+]
- 17:85/q24/p3 In Medina. — The Ibn Masud report has Jews asking there; Suyuti prefers this eyewitness and Bukhari version over Ibn Abbas's account. The verse is listed among Medinan exceptions to the Meccan sura. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ITQAN; SUYUTI-LUBAB(ابن مسعود); ITQAN(عبد الله بن مسعود)+]
- 17:85/q24/p4 The earlier verse was recited again as the later answer. — Ibn Kathir offers this, while Burhan says a fresh incident can prompt the Prophet to receive an earlier verse again. Ibn Ashur suggests the Jews' later question received the same answer or another order to deliver it. [3 notes: BURHAN; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 17:85/q24/p5 Historical conflation or later insertion explains the reports. — Ibn Ashur suggests the Cave and Dhul-Qarnayn questions had Christian associations while the spirit came from Jews; their relative sura order might mean the spirit question was separate or the verse inserted later. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL+]

## 17:85/q25 How does the spirit question fit its surrounding verses or sura?  [* · links]
turns on: The link differs with the referent of spirit and the narrative chronology.
- 17:85/q25/p1 It belongs with the surrounding discussion of the Quran and revelation. — Jishumi cites nearby Quran passages; Razi points to 17:82 and 17:86–88; Tabrisī's Abu Muslim cites the later challenge. Burhan links 17:86 to withdrawal of revelation. Alusi says Quran or Gabriel gives close continuity; Elmalili connects the Lord's revelation, Isra and Praised Station. - 17:85/q25/p2 On the human-spirit reading it interrupts the Quran discussion to expose distraction. — Biqai says questioners ignore useful revelation for an obscure physical spirit. Alusi describes this as a deliberate interruption. [4 notes: ALUSI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 17:85/q25/p3 It connects al-Isra with al-Kahf's two other test answers. — Suyuti says this answer closes one sura while the Cave and Dhul-Qarnayn answers appear in the next; the spirit is separated because its reality was not explained. - 17:85/q25/p4 It anticipates 17:86's warning that revelation can be withdrawn. — Wahidi Basit uses 17:86 to read the address as Prophet and believers; Elmalili says all bestowed knowledge is vulnerable to withdrawal. [2 notes: ELMALILI; WAHIDI-BASIT]
- 17:85/q25/p5 It connects the hidden spirit with the Night Journey or Moses and Khidr. — Biqai relates the concealed command realm to ascent; Suyuti compares irrational-seeming journey and spirit, or ascent and descent, citing Ibn Sina's poem. Moses and Khidr illustrate God's immeasurable knowledge after Jewish Torah claims. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL(جلال السيوطي); BIQAI-FULL]
- 17:85/q25/p6 It follows the prior discussion of the soul and human conduct. — Razi connects it with 17:84's claim that each acts according to a pattern. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 17:85/q28 How should uncertain or multivalent interpretation of this verse be handled?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: One approach reserves hidden matters to transmission; another uses evidence-guided reasoning while respecting general wording.
- 17:85/q28/p1 Interpret by transmitted warrant where God has reserved a matter. — Burhan places spirit among divine secrets, admitting Quran, prophetic explanation or consensus; early restraint is proper, and speculative anatomy lacks warrant. Ibn Kathir says scholars have written on its nature and rulings and commends Ibn Manda's book, which he heard. [5 notes: BIQAI-FULL(السلف); BURHAN; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 17:85/q28/p2 For multivalent wording, reason from evidence and the more apparent sense. — Burhan distinguishes interpretation from transmission, permits scholarly derivation but forbids bare opinion, and says the apparent sense governs absent stronger evidence. Bint al-Shati warns against mechanically tying verses to supposed occasions, favors general wording unless context limits it, and attends to revelation order and rhetorical form; she says no synonym fully replaces Quranic diction. [8 notes: BINTSHATI; BURHAN]

## 17:85/q29 What wider Quranic teachings about revelation are cited here?  [* · links]
- 17:85/q29/p1 The Quran's sciences and inimitable arrangement exceed human exhaustion. — Itqan says jurists, grammarians, and rhetoricians draw different knowledge from it; its stories and language overwhelm minds, and only God can produce its composition. Islahi compares Jesus' saying that people live by divine words rather than bread alone. [4 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ITQAN]
