## THE CURRENT MAP
53:36/q01 What does 'am lam yunabba' mean? [أَمْ لَمْ يُنَبَّأْ · meaning]
  53:36/q01/p1 Rather, has he not been told or informed? — reasons: Wahidi al-Basit and Nasafi gloss yunabba as being informed; Abu Hayyan and Alusi explain am as 'rather'; Bursevi frames it as the possibility that he never heard the scriptural teaching.
53:36/q02 How may the final sound of 'yunabba' be read? [يُنَبَّأْ · readings]
  53:36/q02/p1 Its final hamza may be softened to a long ā without changing the meaning. — reasons: Qiraat-ER says the softened ending is less abrupt.
53:36/q03 What rebuke does the question convey? [أَمْ لَمْ يُنَبَّأْ · interpretation] (turns on: The notes differ in whether they stress the failed excuse of ignorance or the obligation to consult earlier revelation.)
  53:36/q03/p1 The person turning away cannot claim complete ignorance of the earlier teaching. — reasons: Razi says one who fully knows needs no instruction and one wholly unaware, like a sleeper, is not addressed; neither condition excuses this person. Bursevi states the question as whether he has never been told.
  53:36/q03/p2 If he rejects Muhammad, he should seek the earlier messengers' teaching on fearing God and escaping punishment. — reasons: Ibn Ashur calls this second interrogative another rebuke.
53:36/q04 Whom does the question address? [أَمْ لَمْ يُنَبَّأْ · referent] (turns on: Razi makes the audience depend on whether bima means the message's shared teachings or the actual contents received by a scriptured community.)
  53:36/q04/p1 It addresses Quraysh primarily and the People of the Book secondarily. — reasons: Islahi says Quraysh claimed Abraham as ancestor and religious leader, while the People of the Book claimed Moses and Abraham.
  53:36/q04/p2 It can address both idolaters and the People of the Book. — reasons: Razi says the Prophet proclaimed teachings such as monotheism and resurrection found in Moses' scrolls, so both audiences heard their substance.
  53:36/q04/p3 It specifically addresses the People of the Book. — reasons: On Razi's alternative, bima points to the scriptures' actual contents, received by that community; he also proposes that the person who ceased giving God's rights when Muhammad came may have possessed a book.
53:36/q05 How does 'bima fi suhufi Musa' point to the earlier teaching? [بِمَا فِى صُحُفِ مُوسَىٰ · interpretation] (turns on: The contrast is between the teachings in substance and the actual scriptural contents, with different implications for the audience.)
  53:36/q05/p1 It points to teachings found in Moses' scrolls, conveyed again by the Prophet. — reasons: Razi gives monotheism and resurrection as examples and says this reading reaches idolaters as well as the People of the Book; Tabatabai says preceding Qur'anic passages present the material as wisdom, admonitions, stories, and lessons without calling them excerpts.
  53:36/q05/p2 It points to the very contents as they stand in those scriptures. — reasons: Razi says this reading especially addresses the People of the Book, who received the scriptures; Zajjaj says the text goes on to state what the scrolls contain.
53:36/q06 Which teachings are recalled from the scrolls? [بِمَا فِى صُحُفِ · interpretation] (turns on: The notes propose a particular nearby rule, an earlier-scriptures statement about the afterlife, or a broader body of common prophetic teaching; Razi favors the nearby rule without excluding further contents.)
  53:36/q06/p1 Chiefly, no soul bears another's burden and each person answers for his own deeds. — reasons: Nahhas says none is punished in another's place; Thalabi and Jishumi connect the teaching to Abraham's message and God's individual accounting. Razi prefers this as the most apparent content from 53:38–39, while allowing the scrolls to contain more.
  53:36/q06/p2 The afterlife is better than worldly life. — reasons: Razi offers this as another proposed content, citing the first scriptures in 87:18–19.
  53:36/q06/p3 Foundational beliefs occur in every scripture, including monotheism and resurrection. — reasons: Razi cites 6:90 for following earlier guidance in religious fundamentals; legal branches may differ between communities. He also names monotheism and resurrection as teachings found in Moses' scrolls.
  53:36/q06/p4 They convey enduring wisdom and ethical truths through admonitions, stories, and lessons. — reasons: Tabatabai says the earlier Qur'anic presentation of these teachings takes those forms; Asad says Moses and Abraham exemplify prophets conveying enduring ethical truths.
  53:36/q06/p5 Prophetic descent alone does not entitle someone to Paradise; the prophets' teachings and sacrifices matter. — reasons: Islahi directs those claiming descent from Abraham and Moses to what the prophets taught and did.
53:36/q07 What does 'suhuf' mean, and why is it plural? [صُحُفِ · meaning]
  53:36/q07/p1 It denotes written sheets or scrolls, plural because there were many parts or tablets. — reasons: Al-Raghib, reported by Bursevi, derives sahifa from a flat spread surface such as a face or writing sheet, gives saha'if and suhuf as plurals, and says a mushaf gathers sheets. Razi prefers numerous scrolls over merely counting Moses and Abraham as two possessors, citing the tablets in 7:154 and 7:150; Tabatabai says Abraham's scripture had many parts. Al-Qahestati, reported by Bursevi, says mushaf has three possible vowels on its mim and denotes a collection containing the Quran.
53:36/q08 What variant reading is reported for 'suhuf'? [صُحُفِ · readings]
  53:36/q08/p1 The word is also read with a light consonant. — reasons: Kashshaf reports the lighter pronunciation without giving a change of meaning.
53:36/q09 What are Moses' scrolls? [صُحُفِ مُوسَىٰ · referent]
  53:36/q09/p1 They are the Torah, described as its books or tablets. — reasons: Moses' scrolls are repeatedly identified as the Torah; Baydawi also calls his tablets the Torah. Razi relates the plural to the many tablets (7:154; 7:150). Ibn Ashur notes that 87:18–19 also names the earlier scriptures.
53:36/q10 Why are Moses' scrolls named before Abraham's? [صُحُفِ مُوسَىٰ · rhetoric] (turns on: The explanations appeal to familiarity and scale of scripture, the audience and context, scripture chronology, or the absence of a required order.)
  53:36/q10/p1 Moses' scripture was more familiar, extensive, accessible, or weighty for the audience. — reasons: Razi proposes that Moses' scrolls were numerous and familiar while Abraham's were less known. Baydawi calls the tablets more familiar and substantial; Burhan cites wider circulation and the verse endings; Biqai says the book was greatest after the Quran and available for consultation; Bursevi invokes the nearer, better-known figure; Ibn Ashur cites broader guidance and law, sparse Abrahamic material, and familiarity through the People of the Book (28:48); Study Quran reports the greater familiarity from Bḍ.
  53:36/q10/p2 The order here reflects the immediate audience, including Jews, rather than an invariant prophetic order. — reasons: Razi offers addressing Jews first as an explanation; he observes that Moses is often named later where the Prophet's dealings with polytheists and Jews and polytheist respect for Abraham shape the context.
  53:36/q10/p3 The order follows the order in which the scriptures descended. — reasons: Razi gives this as a possible rationale for a different ordering in the earlier sura, comparing 87:1.
  53:36/q10/p4 No special rationale is required; either order is eloquent. — reasons: Razi initially says eloquent speech allows either ordering, while later listing possible contextual explanations; he compares the earlier sura at 87:1.
53:36/q11 Why are Moses and Abraham invoked together? [صُحُفِ مُوسَىٰ · rhetoric] (turns on: The notes explain the pairing through audience familiarity and claimed ancestry, shared perennial teaching, or the history of individual accountability.)
  53:36/q11/p1 Their scriptures and legacies were familiar to the hearers who claimed a connection to them. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says Arabs knew Abraham's hanifiyya, sometimes claimed to follow it, as with Zayd ibn Amr ibn Nufayl, and knew Moses' scrolls through the People of the Book (28:48). Islahi says Quraysh claimed Abraham and the People of the Book claimed both. Kuran Yolu says these were the best-known prophets to the audience; Biqai notes Israelite and Arab claims to Abraham's legacy.
  53:36/q11/p2 They attest the enduring truths revealed across prophetic history. — reasons: Asad cites 2:124 for the prophets' transmission of ethical truth; Kuran Yolu calls these core revelations; Study Quran says the section turns to perennial truths in both scriptures.
  53:36/q11/p3 Their scrolls are singled out in connection with the rule against liability for another's offense. — reasons: Qurtubi reports al-Hudhayl ibn Shurahbil's account that between Noah and Abraham people were held liable for relatives' offenses.
53:36/q12 What are the scrolls attributed to Abraham in the continuation? [صُحُفِ · referent] (turns on: Whether 'scrolls of Abraham' means written revelation given to him or later written preservation of teachings transmitted orally.)
