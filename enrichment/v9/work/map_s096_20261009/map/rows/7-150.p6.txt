[JURJANI-ASRAR:v1p278/r4] JURJANI-ASRAR, d. 471 · القاضي | prefers | Al-Jurjani endorses the Qadi’s criterion that a phrase naming both sides, such as Zayd is a lion, is simile rather than metaphor; a phrase suppressing the subject is metaphor.
[JURJANI-ASRAR:v1p278/r5] JURJANI-ASRAR, d. 471 · author | holds | The suppressed-subject form may be called simile when describing the speaker’s intention, but its surface wording applies the borrowed name as though it were the intended referent’s own name.
[JURJANI-ASRAR:v1p278/r6] JURJANI-ASRAR, d. 471 · author | holds | When the compared subject is explicit, listeners cannot take it literally as both a person and a lion; they infer only the lion’s courage, boldness, or force.
[JURJANI-ASRAR:v1p280/r1] JURJANI-ASRAR, d. 471 · author | holds | Even without an explicit comparison particle, saying a man is a lion necessarily signals likeness: one cannot literally be both a man and a lion, only a man with lion-like traits.
[JURJANI-ASRAR:v1p280/r2] JURJANI-ASRAR, d. 471 · author | holds | The suppressed-subject form can remain literal: gazelle may mean an actual animal, sun the sun, and sword a literal weapon rather than a brave man or a successful plan.
[JURJANI-ASRAR:v1p280/r3] JURJANI-ASRAR, d. 471 · author | holds | A metaphorical royal appearance requires removing signs of common status and making the person appear to be a king; merely adding some royal clothes while commoner signs remain does not do this.
[JURJANI-ASRAR:v1p284/r1] JURJANI-ASRAR, d. 471 · author | holds | A direct simile names its subject and asserts likeness; metaphorical naming instead presents the target under another name without first stating a comparison.
[JURJANI-ASRAR:v1p284/r2] JURJANI-ASRAR, d. 471 · author | holds | A predicate asserts a meaning as present, whereas an attributive description clarifies and specifies a meaning already established.
[JURJANI-ASRAR:v1p284/r3] JURJANI-ASRAR, d. 471 · author | holds | The two uses should retain separate technical names: metaphor for the unexpressed comparison and simile for the named comparison.
[JURJANI-ASRAR:v1p284/r4] JURJANI-ASRAR, d. 471 · author | rejects | Do not call every comparison metaphor when a simile particle can readily be inserted; definite images such as the lion are explicit similes.
[JURJANI-ASRAR:v1p284/r5] JURJANI-ASRAR, d. 471 · author | holds | Indefinite image nouns such as sea or lion resist insertion of the simile particle, which helps distinguish metaphorical predication from an overt comparison.
[JURJANI-ASRAR:v1p284/r6] JURJANI-ASRAR, d. 471 · author | holds | Assigning an image an astonishing quality presents an unprecedented member of its class; explicit comparison can obscure that intended novelty.
[JURJANI-ASRAR:v1p288/r1] JURJANI-ASRAR, d. 471 · author | holds | Metaphor can assert an unprecedented instance of a kind with a strange quality; calling it merely like a familiar image would undo the point.
[JURJANI-ASRAR:v1p288/r2] JURJANI-ASRAR, d. 471 · author | holds | When a comparison is entrenched, as knowledge with light or ignorance with darkness, the metaphor is nearly literal and adding as-if weakens it.
