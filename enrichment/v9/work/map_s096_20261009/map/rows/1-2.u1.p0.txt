## THE CURRENT MAP
1:2/q01 What does ḥamd mean and how does it relate to praise and thanks? [ٱلْحَمْدُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether praise and thanks are synonymous, whether ḥamd requires a chosen good, and whether its object or its means is broader.)
  1:2/q01/p1 Ḥamd praises qualities as well as gifts; shukr answers a gift. — reasons: IBNATIYYA-FULL, d. 542 (author): Al-hamd is complete praise, with the definite article covering the whole class of praise; it is broader than thanks, which responds to a favor, while praise may simply describe qualities. TABRISI, d. 548 (author): All beautiful praise belongs to God, who deserves worship as the source of original blessings and the creator, nurturer, and reformer of creation; the verse teaches gratitude and praise. TABRISI, d. 548 (author): Praise, commendation, and gratitude are related: praise contrasts with blame and commendation with satire, while gratitude contrasts with ingratitude. TABRISI, d. 548 (author): Praise need not respond to a favor, whereas gratitude is specifically for a favor; praise can nevertheless substitute for gratitude, as its use with shukran shows. IBNJAWZI-ZAD, d. 597 (author): Praise and gratitude overlap, but praise may be offered initially whereas gratitude is only in response to a blessing. IBNJAWZI-ZAD, d. 597 (ابن قتيبة): Ibn Qutayba defines praise as commending a person's inherent virtues, while gratitude is praise for a favor or kindness received. IBNJAWZI-ZAD, d. 597 (ابن قتيبة): Ibn Qutayba notes that ḥamd can stand for shukr, as in praising someone for a kindness, just as one thanks him for courage. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (author): Al-hamdu lillah is better than ash-shukru lillah because it praises God for every benefit to the worlds, even one not received by the speaker; shukr is personal gratitude. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (المخالف): One reported distinction says hamd thanks God for warding off calamity, while shukr thanks Him for gifts; an objection asks why the lesser benefit is named first. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (author): Razi offers three answers: thanking for the lesser implies gratitude for the greater; warded-off harm is unbounded while gifts are finite; and preventing harm matters more than adding benefit. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (author): Al-hamdu lillah also means praise and thanks are God’s due and property, because His abundant favors make Him inherently deserving of them. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (author): Because full gratitude is beyond human capacity, the verse states that all praise belongs to God rather than commanding people to praise Him. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (author): The ability, motive, and freedom from obstacles needed for gratitude are themselves gifts, so thanking them would require endless further thanks. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (الواسطي): Al-Wāsiṭī calls gratitude shirk when a person imagines that his thanks can equal God’s blessings. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (author): The report’s expanded wording contrasts one immeasurable blessing with the servant’s boundless thanks in saying al-ḥamdu lillāh. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (داود): David asks how he can thank God when even the ability to thank depends on God’s gift; God says recognizing that incapacity is gratitude according to one’s capacity. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (author): Praise thanks God for past blessings and brings future increase, as promised in the Qur’an; it closes barriers and opens the way to knowledge of God, hence the name al-Fātiḥa. | mentions 14:7 RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (author): Scholars disagree whether gratitude is owed by reason or only by revealed law; the latter cite the rule that punishment awaits a messenger and the removal of excuses by messengers. | mentions 17:15, 4:165 RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (author): Al-Rāzī argues that gratitude is due at all times, even before revelation: the verse makes praise God’s right without qualification and grounds it in His nurturing and mercy. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (author): A second group calls praise bad manners: the tiny thanks cannot match immeasurable blessings, recalling blessings distracts from knowing the giver, and praise for benefits seems self-interested. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (author): Contemplating creatures reveals more mercy, favor, and beneficence than evil, because good is essential and evil incidental; therefore praise and thanks predominate. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (author): Praise corresponds to bowing: gratitude looks both to God and His gift, a middle state between turning away and absorption, as bowing lies between standing and prostration; abundant gifts also weigh the back down. QURTUBI, d. 671 (ابن عطية): Ibn ʿAṭiyya counters that adding shukran narrows the praise to gratitude for a particular blessing, which shows the words are not identical. QURTUBI, d. 671 (بعض العلماء): Another view makes praise broader because it includes both gratitude and commendation, can stand in place of gratitude, and is not itself replaced by gratitude. QURTUBI, d. 671 (شقيق بن إبراهيم): Shafīq ibn Ibrāhīm says gratitude has three conditions: recognizing the giver, being content with what was given, and not disobeying God while one retains strength. QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 (ابن عطية): Ibn ʿAtiyya says “praise to God, in thanks” restricts praise to a blessing and therefore argues against equating praise and gratitude. QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 (بعض العلماء): Others say praise is broader: it includes gratitude and commendation, can stand in place of gratitude, but gratitude cannot replace praise. QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 (author): Other explanations say God praised Himself because servants cannot fully praise Him, or because abundant blessings exceed their capacity; divine praise removes the burden of gratitude. QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 (شقيق بن إبراهيم): Shafiq says gratitude requires recognizing the giver, accepting what is given, and not disobeying while one has strength. BAYDAWI, d. 685 (النبي): The Prophet is quoted saying praise is the head of gratitude and that whoever does not praise God has not thanked Him. BAYDAWI, d. 685 (author): Blame is the opposite of praise, and ingratitude the opposite of shukr. BAYDAWI-FULL, d. 685 (النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم): A report calls praise the head of gratitude and says one who does not praise God has not thanked Him. NASAFI, d. 710 (author): Al-hamdu is nominative as a subject, though it can be read accusative with an implied verb as an explanatory verbal noun, like ‘thanks’ or ‘ingratitude’; nominative wording conveys a settled, enduring meaning. NASAFI-FULL, d. 710 (author): A report calls praise the head of gratitude and says a servant has not thanked God without praising Him; verbal mention spreads notice of a favor more than inward belief or ambiguous bodily acts. ABUHAYYAN-FULL, d. 745 (author): Three positions equate praise and thanks, make ḥamd broader, or distinguish thanks for deeds from praise for attributes; Abū Ḥayyān prefers ḥamd as broader. SAMIN-DURR, d. 756 (الراغب): Al-Raghib says hamd can concern chosen and innate qualities, while shukr answers a favor; he also explains mahmud as praised, Muhammad as much-praised, and Ahmad as surpassing others in praise. IBNKATHIR, d. 774 (author): Ibn Kathir questions Ibn Jarir's equation: praise is verbal commendation for necessary or bestowed qualities, while thanks applies only to bestowed qualities and may be inward, spoken, or enacted. IBNKATHIR, d. 774 (author): Ibn Kathir resolves the scope dispute by saying praise covers more kinds of objects but is speech-only, whereas thanks uses more kinds of means but concerns bestowed qualities alone. IBNKATHIR, d. 774 (الجوهري): Al-Jawhari defines praise as the opposite of blame, says taḥmīd is more emphatic than ḥamd, and regards ḥamd as broader than thanks. IBNKATHIR, d. 774 (الجوهري): Al-Jawhari defines thanks as praising a benefactor for what he bestowed and says using the preposition li- is more eloquent. IBNKATHIR, d. 774 (ابن عباس): Ibn Abbas says al-ḥamdu lillāh is a word of thanks; when a servant says it, God says that His servant has thanked Him. IBNKATHIR, d. 774 (ابن عباس): Ibn Abbas explains praise as thanks, humble submission, and acknowledgment of God's favors, guidance, and origination. IBNKATHIR, d. 774 (ابن جرير): Ibn Jarir explains praise as exclusive thanks to God for countless blessings, including faculties for worship, worldly provision, and guidance toward lasting bliss despite no prior entitlement. BIQAI-FULL, d. 885 (author): The Fatiha's many titles converge on مراقبة: it is the Mother of the Book, foundation, oft-repeated, treasure, healing, sufficing, complete, protecting, incantation, praise, thanks, supplication and prayer. BIQAI-FULL, d. 885 (author): The Fatiha is a treasure, cure, sufficiency, fulfillment, protection and incantation; it gathers praise, gratitude and supplication, with prayer as their greatest assembly. BIQAI-FULL, d. 885 (author): The root and forms of al-hamd suggest reaching an end, extension, encompassing and circularity; these imply lowering the head and can lead to contentment and gratitude.
