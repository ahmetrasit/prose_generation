53:34/q06 What fault or lesson does this act illustrate? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the criticism chiefly concerns imagined transfer of sins and apostasy, meager interrupted generosity and false confidence in reward, or a broken payment promise; Bursevī and Islahi explicitly challenge competing emphases.)
  53:34/q06/p1 Turning from faith while trusting a paid promise to transfer one's afterlife punishment is the central error. — reasons: Bursevī prefers this focus and calls cessation of the gift incidental and ironic; 53:35 challenges supposed knowledge of the unseen. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the man implicitly recognized turning away as a crime but wrongly assumed its consequences could be transferred. Al-Rāzī says money cannot carry another's sins. Biqāʿī infers that no one can know God's future judgment even of oneself, much less certify another's standing. Asad asks how limited giving proves there is no judgment.
  53:34/q06/p2 Stinginess, breaking off generosity, and presuming a favorable standing in the Hereafter are censured. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr sees even small-scale stinginess as blame beyond disbelief; Islahi portrays those who assume Abrahamic or Mosaic descent ensures Paradise while ignoring those prophets' teaching and sacrifice. His misers give under pressure, then clench their fists and attack exhorters; 90:6 records their boast of vast spending. Kuran Yolu sees fleeting compassion overtaken by worldly attachment. Study Quran says interrupted good may nullify earlier efforts, like a well never reaching water. Bursevī rejects Muqātil's small donation to companions as disconnected from following verses.
  53:34/q06/p3 The giver's stopping after a promise is blameworthy even if payment was not owed. — reasons: Al-Rāzī answers the objection that a voluntary gift could harmlessly stop: noble Arab custom honors promises, and the proposed payment could never transfer sins. Islahi objects that the Qurʾān is not censuring al-Walīd for a broken promise.
  53:34/q06/p4 A seeker who abandons a little spiritual striving for pleasure reveals unreadiness for union. — reasons: Bursevī treats life's capital as the resource briefly spent on the path before the sensual ego reclaims it.
53:34/q07 How does this verse fit the surrounding argument? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the emphasis falls on the immediate unseen-knowledge question, the preceding recompense promise, or the sūra's broader scriptural argument; Asad's excerpt does not identify its proposed earlier connection.)
  53:34/q07/p1 It leads into 53:35's challenge to the person's claimed knowledge of unseen judgment. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī prefers the al-Walīd report because the next question fits a man buying relief from punishment. Bursevī treats the false transfer belief as the focus. Asad asks how scant giving establishes that judgment will not come. Islahi hears sarcasm toward misers behaving as though they already see Paradise's luxury.
  53:34/q07/p2 It follows 53:31's promise of recompense with astonishment at a man who misunderstands what God rewards. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the preceding fāʾ frames this response; each person has only what he strives for, whereas this man tries to outsource consequences. He distinguishes the prior turning away as blame from the astonishment introduced by the small gift.
  53:34/q07/p3 It leads from the third section's account of creation to enduring teachings in Moses' and Abraham's scriptures. — reasons: Study Quran describes the sūra's third section as a call to reflect on creation before the scriptural transition. Islahi invokes those prophets' teaching and sacrifice against presumed inherited entitlement to Paradise.
  53:34/q07/p4 Its wording returns to an earlier theme in the sūra. — reasons: Asad says his rendering of 53:33–34 and its bracketed insertions follow Rāzī; the excerpt ends before naming the earlier verses.
