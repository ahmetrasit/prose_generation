[ITQAN:v3p97/r2] ITQAN, d. 911 · الجمهور | reports | The majority holds that figurative expression occurs in the Qur’an.
[ITQAN:v3p97/r3] ITQAN, d. 911 · الظاهرية وابن القاص وابن خويز منداد | reports | The Zahiris, Ibn al-Qass, and Ibn Khuwayz Mindad deny Qur’anic metaphor, arguing that it resembles falsehood and is used only when literal speech is inadequate, which cannot apply to God.
[ITQAN:v3p97/r4] ITQAN, d. 911 · author | rejects | Suyuti calls this objection false: excluding metaphor would remove half the Qur’an’s beauty; rhetoricians agree it is more eloquent than literal speech, and the objection would also exclude ellipsis, emphasis, and repeated stories.
[ITQAN:v3p97/r5] ITQAN, d. 911 · author | holds | Ibn Abd al-Salam wrote a treatise on metaphor; Suyuti says he summarized it, with extensive additions, in his own book on Qur’anic metaphor.
[ITQAN:v3p97/r6] ITQAN, d. 911 · author | holds | One figurative mode is syntactic or rational attribution: an action is assigned to something associated with its true agent, through their relation.
[BURSEVI:v10p334/r1] BURSEVI, d. 1127 · author | holds | A soul stabilized in spiritual ascent is protected from reverting to natural and human traits and from descending to the commanding self.
[BURSEVI:v10p334/r13] BURSEVI, d. 1127 · بعض أهل الإشارة | reports | The soul settled in worldly life is told to return to God by abandoning it and following the path of the afterlife.
[BURSEVI:v10p334/r5] BURSEVI, d. 1127 · author | holds | Returning to the Lord means reaching the promised honor and nearness; this figurative destination defeats corporealists' argument and indicates the spirit was created first.
[BURSEVI:v10p334/r6] BURSEVI, d. 1127 · author | holds | The soul is pleased with its enduring bliss and accepted by God.
[ALUSI:89:28/r1] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Return means from the place where the soul has been judged.
[ALUSI:89:28/r2] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Your Lord means the place of God's special care and station of honor; reports indicate that the blessed have a special place before accounting where they are spared others' exhaustion and later called to judgment.
[ALUSI:89:28/r3] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | Alternatively, return means turn solely to God, laying aside concern for one's deeds and whether they will be accepted; the call reassures the soul after its account and acceptance are complete.
[ALUSI:89:28/r4] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Calling her tranquil reminds her of the quality that should prompt return, like urging a famous brave man to advance; this rationale fits inward return better than return after judgment. | mentions 89:27
[ALUSI:89:28/r5] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Addressing her as returning to your Lord is especially tender in either interpretation, unlike simply saying return to God.
[ALUSI:89:28/r6] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Rāḍiya means content with the endless gifts bestowed on her; contentment with a light account and accepted deeds is also proposed, but al-Ālūsī finds that less persuasive.
[ALUSI:89:28/r7] ALUSI, d. 1270 · قيل | reports | Another view glosses rāḍiya as pleased with her Lord and marḍiyya as pleasing to Him; it is called clearest, though the contextual objection is itself questioned.
[ALUSI:89:28/r8] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Marḍiyya means approved and well-pleasing to God.
[ALUSI:89:28/r9] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Both adjectives are accusative circumstantial expressions; the first is apparently prospective, though a simultaneous state is also proposed, and the second advances to the greater honor of divine approval, as in 9:72. | mentions 9:72
[ALUSI:89:29/r10] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | Another analysis takes nafs as the soul and rabb as its owner, the body: the soul returns to its former body, then enters the servants and the abode of reward.
[ALUSI:89:29/r12] ALUSI, d. 1270 · ابن زيد وجماعة | reports | Ibn Zayd and a group say the address comes at death, with the soul understood as the spirit returning to the sacred realm and then joining the close servants.
[ALUSI:89:29/r13] ALUSI, d. 1270 · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Ibn Jubayr reports that, after the tranquil-soul verse was recited before him, Abū Bakr called it beautiful and the Prophet said an angel would address him with it at death; no grading is stated.
[ALUSI:89:29/r14] ALUSI, d. 1270 · أبو بكر الصديق | reports | A similar death-time report is transmitted in al-Ḥakīm al-Tirmidhī’s Nawādir al-uṣūl through Thābit ibn ʿAjlān and Sulaym ibn ʿĀmir from Abū Bakr; no grading is stated.
[ALUSI:89:29/r18] ALUSI, d. 1270 · أبو صالح | reports | Abū Ṣāliḥ says the return is at death, meaning departure from worldly life, while the two entry commands are spoken at resurrection.
[ALUSI:89:29/r19] ALUSI, d. 1270 · محمد بن كعب القرظي | reports | Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb al-Quraẓī places the address after death but before resurrection: the believer sees his Garden home, returns to the body, and awaits questioning by Munkar and Nakīr; no grading is stated.
[ALUSI:89:29/r21] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | rejects | An inward reading applies the commands throughout earthly life: turn to God in every matter, join the sincere servants through good works, and enter the Garden potentially; al-Ālūsī says this is not a sound interpretation.
[ALUSI:89:29/r25] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | Some readings imply that souls existed before bodies in the realm of dominion; al-Ālūsī notes that most theologians, like Plato and his followers, instead hold that souls are created when bodies become ready for them.
[ALUSI:89:29/r5] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | One reading takes ‘return to your Lord’s promise’ as the promised Garden and company of the righteous, spoken after the reckoning; the objection that this implies prior residence is answered by a potential existence in Adam’s loins while he was in the Garden.
[ALUSI:89:29/r6] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | The proposed prior existence is compared with reports from ʿAlī and Ibn ʿAbbās that maʿād in 28:85 means Paradise rather than Mecca; this works only if Adam’s Garden was the eschatological Garden, a strongly disputed identification discussed by Ibn al-Qayyim. | mentions 28:85
[ALUSI:89:29/r7] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | Another interpretation makes ‘return to your Lord’s command’ mean return to one of His affairs: His treatment of her without distress, placement in an honored station, or something similar.
[ALUSI:89:29/r8] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | A further view understands the return as return to God’s general favor, from the barzakh after death or from the period after resurrection but before judgment.
[ALUSI:89:29/r9] ALUSI, d. 1270 · عكرمة والضحاك | reports | ʿIkrima and al-Ḍaḥḥāk place the address at resurrection; on one analysis nafs means the person, Lord means God, and an implied noun supplies the station to which she returns.
