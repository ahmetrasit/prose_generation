[IBNASHUR-FULL:v1p162/r3] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | Ibn Ashur derives Allah from al-ilah, a common noun for deity; its root may mean worship or states such as bewilderment, stillness, fear, or ardor, all associated with submission and reverence.

[IBNASHUR-FULL:v1p162/r6] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | Ibn Ashur finds no evidence that Arabs used the definite singular al-ilah for an idol; they used a possessive phrase, often rabb, or a plural, and a conquest report speaks of idols in the Kaaba.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v1p162/r7] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | He says frequent use led Arabs to drop the hamza in al-ilah when referring to God, as in al-nas from al-unas, though they retained it in some speech.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v1p163/r1] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | The divine name became a unique proper form whose article is treated as part of the word, as the vocative يا الله shows; its formation has no parallel among ordinary names.

[IBNASHUR-FULL:v1p165/r1] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | A poet's use of لاه and fixed expressions such as لله أبوك support treating لاه as a shortened form of Allah.
