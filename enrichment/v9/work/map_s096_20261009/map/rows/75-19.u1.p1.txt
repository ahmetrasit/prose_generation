75:19/q09 Why is God bound to clarify ambiguous revelation? [عَلَيْنَا بَيَانَهُۥ · theology] (turns on: Whether the source of obligation is promise and grace or wisdom.)
  75:19/q09/p1 The duty follows from God's promise and grace. — reasons: Al-Rāzī states his school's ground for God's obligation to explain ambiguous wording.
  75:19/q09/p2 The duty follows from divine wisdom. — reasons: Al-Rāzī attributes this ground to the Muʿtazilites.
75:19/q10 How are clear and ambiguous Qur'anic passages understood in connection with clarification? [بَيَانَهُۥ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether clarity is a property of wording and rulings, access to a single intended meaning, or the reader's ability to follow interlocking passages.)
  75:19/q10/p1 Muḥkam is firm through commands and prohibitions, especially clarification of lawful and unlawful matters; mutashābih can have several senses or need another passage. — reasons: Al-Zarkashī calls competing definitions close: the clear may bear one meaning; ambiguous wording may bear several or stand by reference to another passage. He says limited readers can mistake subtle interlocking meanings and Qur'anic ordering for obscurity, citing 6:141.
  75:19/q10/p2 Muḥkam is unrepealed or abrogating material, obligations and promise or threat, a single-sense text, immediately recognized meaning, or recurrent wording. — reasons: Al-Zarkashī reports the alternatives separately, citing 6:151 and 17:23 for one set and al-Ikhlāṣ and 42:11 for immediate clarity.
  75:19/q10/p3 Mutashābih can mean similar-looking wording with different senses, or Qur'anic parts resembling one another in truth, inimitability, promise, warning, and divine origin. — reasons: Al-Zarkashī compares garden fruit similar in appearance but different in taste (2:25). In the divine sense, similarity of Qur'anic parts is positive; blame falls on people who exploit seeming ambiguity for discord.
  75:19/q10/p4 Reported mutashābih categories include abrogated text, stories, parables, opening letters, hidden matters, and texts needing interpretation or another passage. — reasons: Al-Zarkashī includes the Hour, rain, death, and multiply interpretable wording, citing 11:37, 39:56, and 31:34. Other reports leave the hidden matters' knowledge with God.
  75:19/q10/p5 The Qur'an is not inherently obscure or enigmatic, although some passages need clarification. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī draws this conclusion from the passage. Al-Zarkashī distinguishes what appears ambiguous to limited readers from the intended divine category, and calls the most reliable explanation one that expresses the true intended meaning.
75:19/q11 What happened as the Prophet received revelation? [* · reports] (turns on: Whether haste arose chiefly from fear of loss, inquiry into meaning, or the need for continuing guidance under pressure; the explanations can coexist.)
  75:19/q11/p1 He moved his tongue or lips in eagerness or fear of forgetting; God told him to listen to Gabriel and recite afterward. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās and Saʿīd demonstrated the lip movement; al-Bukhārī and Muslim transmit the report by several routes. Ibn Kathīr cites al-Bukhārī's detail that the Prophet lowered his head when Gabriel came. Al-Durr says revelation strained him; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says fear of forgetting drove memorization. Farāhī adds fear of accountability for losing even a letter and responsibility to guide his people. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the fit of similar Ibn Jubayr reports with 75:36–39 is obscure.
  75:19/q11/p2 He hurried to memorize the words and to ask about difficult meanings during Gabriel's recitation. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf gives both motives; al-Rāzī relates reciting with Gabriel to 75:18 and questions about meaning to 75:19; Abū Ḥayyān says the command defers questions until recitation ends.
  75:19/q11/p3 He sought further revelation under the pressure of public mission and opponents' demands. — reasons: Islahi says only revelation guided his warning, strategy, future circumstances, and spiritual, moral, and intellectual needs. Opponents asked for a Qur'an all at once and obstructed the mission; its arrival renewed his heart. Qur'an and Hadith depict his looking heavenward during divinely wise pauses and eagerness when Gabriel recited. Farāhī adds both preservation and eagerness for guidance as motives.
75:19/q12 What lesson does this promise give about haste and patient reception? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the exhortation is confined to the Prophet's reception of revelation or extends to every believer's study.)
  75:19/q12/p1 The Prophet should listen and trust God's timing, preservation, recitation, and explanation. — reasons: Al-Baghawī and Ibn Kathīr present stages of listening, retention, recital, and explanation. Islahi says the Prophet's motives were noble but gradual revelation served divine wisdom; he should accept each portion and trust that every task has its appointed time. Islahi compares 20:114–115. Al-Bayḍāwī says if haste is rebuked even in religion's foundation, it is blameworthy elsewhere.
  75:19/q12/p2 Every reader or listener should study the whole Qur'anic message patiently and seek understanding beyond fluent sound. — reasons: Asad extends the prophetic instruction to believers, linking 20:114; isolated verses invite hasty conclusions, and beautiful sound or mechanical fluency alone can miss the message. Muhammad ʿAbduh says the Qur'an properly read is its own best commentary.
75:19/q13 How is the promised explanation supplied through the Qur'an's later verses and arrangement? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The notes describe complementary means: later interpretive verses and their divinely guided placement.)
  75:19/q13/p1 Later explanatory verses clarify, specify, supplement, narrow, complete, replace, or abrogate earlier material at the proper time. — reasons: Islahi names explanatory verses that clarify an earlier directive, abrogate it, or complete it; the formula “thus does God explain” often marks them. Elmalılı lists confirmation, interpretation, alteration, replacement, and necessity. Farāhī adds specifying general directives, supplementary rulings, and narrowing after collection and arrangement; he says explanatory verses appear after their subject or at a sūrah's end for the whole theme and signal their function.
  75:19/q13/p2 The Prophet and Companions transmitted revelation in its assigned sūrah sequence, making its arranged explanation available. — reasons: Farāhī appeals to Qur'an and Hadith: the Prophet recited whole sūrahs in order, the Companions memorized and followed it, and he directed them to place each newly revealed verse in its assigned location.
75:19/q14 How does the revelation passage fit the surrounding sūra? [* · links] (turns on: Whether a passage addressed to the Prophet may interrupt resurrection discourse, whether it is linked by contrast and gathering imagery, or whether the whole passage must retain a Judgment referent.)
  75:19/q14/p1 Verses 16–19 briefly interrupt the resurrection discourse to instruct the Prophet, after which the prior theme resumes. — reasons: Al-Khaṭṭābī records an objection that this divides a discourse running from 75:14–15 to 75:20–21. Al-Itqān says the interruption occurs when revelation reaches 75:15, compares 20:114, and reports al-Rāzī's teacher who pauses to regain a pupil's attention. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says an interruption needs no surrounding contextual marker and resembles 20:114.
  75:19/q14/p2 It is integrated with the surrounding account of conscience or creates a meaningful contrast. — reasons: Elmalılı links the account of inner conscience to the start and end of the passage. Abū Ḥayyān contrasts the man who denies resurrection and signs with the Prophet eager to learn and present them. Al-Sāmarrāʾī links the sūra's gathering of bones and sun with moon to the earthly gathering of the Qur'an.
  75:19/q14/p3 A supposed lack of connection was alleged as evidence of alteration of the Qur'an. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān reports al-Rāzī's account of an early extremist Rāfiḍī allegation; Abū Ḥayyān answers with the contrast between denier and Prophet.
  75:19/q14/p4 The verses continue the Judgment scene by describing a person's fearful reading of his record. — reasons: Al-Qaffāl cites surrounding self-witness and 17:14 but concedes no report supports the proposal. Al-Bayḍāwī says the reader stumbles; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects this contextual objection by noting an interruption needs no sign.
  75:19/q14/p5 The verses continue the Judgment theme as a prohibition on hastening to ask when it will come. — reasons: The reported account says God gathers those destined for Judgment and explains it at the trumpet blast; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects it.
75:19/q15 How does the following kallā relate to this promise? [* · links] (turns on: Whether kallā resumes with rebuke of haste, opens an emphatic assertion or attention call, or functions as a response or future marker; grammar and a tanwīn reading also bear on its classification.)
  75:19/q15/p1 It rebukes haste in recitation and restrains or denies the preceding conduct. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba gives general rebuke and restraint usages, citing 70:39, 74:52, 75:20, 104:4, 82:9
