## THE CURRENT MAP
2:32/q01 Who says “They said,” and to what challenge do they reply? [قَالُوا۟ · referent]
  2:32/q01/p1 The speakers are the angels, answering God's challenge to name what Adam learned. — reasons: Muqātil and Jishumī identify the angels. Muqātil says God confronted their claim to knowledge of unseen, uncreated things by asking about visible things they did not know. Ibn Masʿūd's report relates the challenge to their earlier prediction of human corruption. Al-Ālūsī and Bursawī read this as a fresh answer to whether they fulfilled the task.
2:32/q02 Why does “they said” lack a linking particle? [قَالُوا۟ · grammar]
  2:32/q02/p1 Direct, successive dialogue needs no conjunction when its sequence is clear. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān cites the dialogue in 2:30, 2:32, 5:27, 2:259, 2:260 and 26:10; al-Ālūsī notes frequent Qurʾānic usage, especially al-Shuʿarāʾ. Ibn ʿĀshūr specifically explains the absence of fa as direct dialogue.
2:32/q03 What does “Subḥānaka” mean? [سُبْحَٰنَكَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the expression is explained by disavowal and transcendence or a movement metaphor.)
  2:32/q03/p1 It glorifies God by declaring Him transcendent, free of evil, defect, or blame. — reasons: Majāz cites al-Aʿshā's poetic repudiation of ʿAlqama's boast; Akhfash uses the same line for disavowal. Ibn ʿAbbās says free of evil; ʿAlī calls it a word God loves; Maymūn calls it a divine name of magnification; al-Ḥasan says humans cannot claim it. Al-Zajjāj reports philologists' agreement on freedom from defect. Abū Ḥayyān contrasts taqdīs as purification, supported by Bayt al-Maqdis, holy land, quds vessel and qidās string of pearls; he also records al-Zamakhsharī's travel derivation and an anonymous poetic qidās.
  2:32/q03/p2 It denotes rapid movement toward God. — reasons: Al-Naḍr transmits a dream interpreter's gloss, relating it to a horse's swift swimming motion.
2:32/q04 What is the form and derivation of subḥān? [سُبْحَٰنَكَ · grammar] (turns on: Its morphological source, lexicalized status, and the evidence of rare poetic forms.)
  2:32/q04/p1 It is a verbal noun or noun standing for tasbīḥ, normally accusative in an understood glorification construction. — reasons: Akhfash, Ṭabarī, al-Khalīl, Sībawayh and others give an implicit verb; Sībawayh distinguishes the verbal noun tasbīḥ from its substitute subḥān. Bayḍāwī compares ghufrān and maʿādh Allāh and notes an exceptional predicate use. Ibn ʿĀshūr considers a lighter sabbaḥa derivation; al-Itqān says the verb fell out of use. Samīn records both verbal noun and ism maṣdar analyses.
  2:32/q04/p2 It is a conventional proper name for glorification or transcendence. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and Samīn discuss standalone poetic subḥān: tanwīn may be poetic declension or indefiniteness, and the untanwinned form diptote through properness plus alif-nūn. Al-Ālūsī cites the al-ʿAlqama poem for naming and the counterclaim of omitted Allāh; Ibn ʿĀshūr cites al-Nābigha's analogous names, while Sībawayh explains loss of tanwīn by definiteness. Bursawī and Elmalılı also call it a proper name.
  2:32/q04/p3 An unusual derivation links it to raising the voice in praise. — reasons: Al-Mufaṣṣal derives it from sabaḥa and cites a poem of Taghlib and pilgrims praising God.
  2:32/q04/p4 It is a reduplicated form denoting repeated sanctification. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān rejects this because it requires unattested singular subḥā; al-Ālūsī objects to the required dual or nominative form and unexplained fixed nūn.
2:32/q05 How is “Subḥānaka” governed and what does its suffix mark? [سُبْحَٰنَكَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether it is an accusative cognate noun, vocative, or governed by the quotation verb; whether its suffix denotes object or subject.)
  2:32/q05/p1 It is accusative with an implied glorification verb, and the kāf is its object. — reasons: Al-Khalīl and Sībawayh supply 'we glorify You'; Abū Ḥayyān says the cognate verb must be omitted and favors object kāf. Samīn also reports annexation to the object.
  2:32/q05/p2 It is an annexed vocative, ‘O Your Glory.’ — reasons: Al-Kisāʾī proposes the vocative; Abū Ḥayyān rejects it for lacking a transmitted vocative particle, Samīn says most grammarians reject it, and al-Ālūsī finds no proof.
  2:32/q05/p3 Its accusative is governed by the verb ‘they said.’ — reasons: Samīn explicitly names the verb of saying as the governing ʿāmil.
  2:32/q05/p4 The kāf can be the subject of the annexation: ‘You are transcendent.’ — reasons: Samīn reports the subject annexation; Abū Ḥayyān allows that some take the suffix as subject, while favoring object.
  2:32/q05/p5 Al-Kisāʾī explains its accusative by its lack of an adjective. — reasons: Naḥḥās transmits this additional grammatical explanation alongside al-Kisāʾī's vocative allowance; Thaʿlabī says it lies outside ordinary description.
  2:32/q05/p6 Standalone subḥān is cut from annexation and therefore takes tanwīn. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says grammar works reject this account; an alternative likens the untanwinned standalone form to qabl and baʿd.
2:32/q06 What are the angels doing by opening with “Subḥānaka”? [سُبْحَٰنَكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the opening is primarily disavowal, apology for the prior question, or courtly praise.)
  2:32/q06/p1 They clear God of objection, error, or lack of wisdom and disclaim their own claim. — reasons: Ṭabarī and Ibn ʿAbbās relate it to God alone knowing the unseen; Thaʿlabī, Wāḥidī, Jishumī, Ṭabrisī and Abū Ḥayyān relate it to His judgment and governance. Abū ʿAlī stresses reverent speech even before knowing the wisdom. Bursawī says God is free of purposeless acts.
  2:32/q06/p2 They apologize or repent for their earlier question or presumption. — reasons: Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān and Ibn ʿĀshūr describe apology and courtesy, comparing Moses' 7:143 and Jonah's 21:87; Bursawī notes the same formulas. Those who think angels sinned see repentance; al-Ālūsī says it resembles repentance for a less preferable act without breaching infallibility. Qatāda and al-Ḥasan report the angels had privately claimed superiority.
  2:32/q06/p3 They begin with reverent praise before acknowledging inability. — reasons: Qushayrī says praise precedes excuse; Ibn ʿĀshūr emphasizes majesty. Wāḥidī refers back to the angels' tasbīḥ in 2:30.
2:32/q07 What do the angels mean by “We have no knowledge except what You taught us”? [لَا عِلْمَ لَنَآ إِلَّا مَا عَلَّمْتَنَآ · interpretation] (turns on: Their prior question in 2:30, whether it was culpable, and what God had previously taught them.)
  2:32/q07/p1 Their knowledge is received from God, and the requested names were not among what He taught them. — reasons: Muqātil, Ṭabarī, Ibn Isḥāq and many others read it as admitting the limit of angelic knowledge. Jishumī and Ṭabrisī specify the names; al-Ālūsī adds inability for the succession task. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the statement admits incapacity rather than informing God. Study Quran stresses limited comprehensive scope.
  2:32/q07/p2 They ask tactfully to be taught and repent for their uninformed question about Adam. — reasons: Wāḥidī reports that their ignorance of names reveals greater ignorance of unseen outcomes and warns against rating their own obedience; the reading ties back to 2:30.
  2:32/q07/p3 They express astonishment at being asked about something unknown. — reasons: Ṭabrisī reports this further reading.
  2:32/q07/p4 They repent because their earlier speech was a sin. — reasons: Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān transmit this from those who deny angelic infallibility; al-Ālūsī reports and qualifies it.
  2:32/q07/p5 They make an innocent inquiry: God had told them of future corruption but not the names. — reasons: Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān distinguish the earlier divinely supplied prediction from the names; Bayḍāwī, Bursawī and Elmalılı deny an explicit claim to succession or sinful objection.
2:32/q08 How does the wording “no knowledge except what You taught us” add to a bare admission of ignorance? [لَا عِلْمَ لَنَآ إِلَّا مَا عَلَّمْتَنَآ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the exception acknowledges knowledge already granted or is read hypothetically of future teaching.)
  2:32/q08/p1 The exception praises God, thanks Him for teaching, and denies independent knowledge. — reasons: Wāḥidī says 'we do not know this' would omit recognition; Jishumī and Ṭabrisī say a bare denial would miss gratitude and duty. Al-Ālūsī says the broad wording avoids self-assertion and a narrow denial of names. Abū Ḥayyān points to lā with an indefinite noun excluding every kind of untaught knowledge.
  2:32/q08/p2 The wording allows future learning without affirming past teaching. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān sketches a strained disconnected exception with conditional mā: 'whatever You teach us, we shall know'; he says it conflicts with reports of prior divine teaching or knowledge from the Preserved Tablet, unless the angels discount that specific information.
2:32/q09 How is “lā ʿilma lanā” constructed? [لَا عِلْمَ لَنَآ · grammar] (turns on: Whether lā is the genus-negating particle or operates like laysa.)
  2:32/q09/p1 Lā negates an indefinite noun generally; lanā is its omitted locative predicate. — reasons: Naḥḥās compares lā rayba fīhi; Ṭabrisī supplies an omitted element governing lanā. Abū Ḥayyān says the indefinite denies every category of knowledge.
  2:32/q09/p2 Lā can instead function as laysa. — reasons: Naḥḥās gives the alternative analysis of 'we have no knowledge.'
2:32/q10 What does ʿilm mean here? [عِلْمَ · meaning]
  2:32/q10/p1 It can mean the thing known, so the sentence says they know no object except what God taught. — reasons: Nasafī explicitly glosses ʿilm as maʿlūm; Samīn uses that sense to explain an omitted resumptive pronoun in the relative clause.
2:32/q11 What is the grammatical role and sense of “mā” after “except”? [مَا عَلَّمْتَنَآ · grammar] (turns on: Whether mā is relative, verbal-noun, or conditional; whether the exception is connected, and what can govern mā.)
