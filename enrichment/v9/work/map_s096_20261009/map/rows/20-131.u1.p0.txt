## THE CURRENT MAP
20:131/q01 What does extending one's eyes forbid? [وَلَا تَمُدَّنَّ عَيْنَيْكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether 'extending' describes a lingering gaze itself or grief over the wealth missed.)
  20:131/q01/p1 A prolonged gaze of admiration, desire, or attachment to others' worldly goods; a passing glance is excused. — reasons: Kashshaf says extending the gaze means lingering without turning away; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Qurṭubī find it stronger than mere looking. Rāzī, Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, Bursevī, and Ālūsī excuse a brief glance. Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls fixed sight a metonym for love. Zamakhsharī and Rāzī invoke Qārūn's spectators (28:79–80). Ibn ʿĀshūr says amazement, rather than admiration, is intended; Islāḥī attributes the Prophet's concern to compassion for elites, not greed.
  20:131/q01/p2 It forbids regret or grief over others' fortune rather than a literal look. — reasons: Māwardī reports grief as a second sense; Jishumī reports a regretful gaze. Abū Muslim explicitly opposes a literal looking interpretation in Rāzī.
20:131/q02 Why say 'extend your eyes'? [تَمُدَّنَّ عَيْنَيْكَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the wording emphasizes duration of looking or also evokes inward sight.)
  20:131/q02/p1 The expression intensifies looking into sustained attention and longing. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Qurṭubī contrast it with ordinary looking. Ibn ʿĀshūr compares extending a hand toward a desired object; Bursevī traces madd to stretching and notes imdād often concerns desirable things, madd harm. Ālūsī proposes ellipsis or figurative attribution.
  20:131/q02/p2 The two eyes signify both outer sight and inner vision. — reasons: Bursevī, following al-Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya, connects divine vision to freeing both forms of sight from attachment; he cites 18:24.
20:131/q03 To whom does the warning apply? [وَلَا تَمُدَّنَّ عَيْنَيْكَ · referent]
  20:131/q03/p1 It addresses the Prophet and instructs believers as well. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says he was least attached to adornment; Ālūsī reports the communal intent. Jishumī says it disciplines believers in renunciation; Muqātil specifies Meccan unbelievers as those whose riches are viewed.
  20:131/q03/p2 It speaks to the Prophet's concern for the Meccan and Ṭāʾif elites. — reasons: Islāḥī says they offered wealth if he stopped preaching, and his concern was compassionate. Study Quran identifies Makkans rejecting him.
20:131/q04 What are the things God gave them to enjoy? [مَا مَتَّعْنَا بِهِۦٓ · meaning]
  20:131/q04/p1 Worldly goods and pleasures, especially the wealth of affluent people. — reasons: Mujāhid names the rich. Rāzī and al-Kabīr list sights, sounds, scents, clothes, and marriage. Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī include children, homes, transport, and food; Ibn ʿĀshūr adds gardens, livestock, and women (28:60). Jishumī explains imtāʿ as perceived pleasure and intensive mattaʿa as giving enjoyment; Rāzī says the intensive form suggests repeated abundance. Bursevī links matāʿ to extended benefit or household goods.
  20:131/q04/p2 They are God-given resources, entrusted to their recipients. — reasons: Islāḥī rejects treating wealth simply as inheritance or intelligence; Bursevī cites a hadith that people are successors watched by God and agents over entrusted wealth.
20:131/q05 What does 'أزواجا منهم' denote? [أَزْوَٰجًۭا مِّنْهُمْ · referent] (turns on: Whether azwāj names paired or grouped recipients, their households, or the varieties of goods; Asad broadens the recipients.)
  20:131/q05/p1 Groups, kinds, or counterparts among the unbelievers. — reasons: Māwardī, Jishumī, Rāzī, and Ṭabāṭabāʾī derive it from paired resemblance; Ubayy says they resemble one another in turning from right. Ibn ʿAbbās calls them classes. Kashshāf, Nasafī, Ālūsī, and Abū Ḥayyān identify disbelieving groups; Bursevī includes polytheists, Jews, and Christians. Asad reports this majority view; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the indefinite plural minimizes them.
  20:131/q05/p2 Men or individual people among them. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ and Zajjāj say men; Rāzī transmits al-Kalbī and Zajjāj. Abū Muslim suggests individuals successively; Jishumī reports a reading that points to people.
  20:131/q05/p3 Their male and female pairs, spouses, families, or households. — reasons: Jishumī reports males and females; Ibn ʿĀshūr says shared children, homes, and furnishings are enjoyed by each spouse, including each other's beauty; Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports the household view.
  20:131/q05/p4 Varieties of the worldly provisions themselves. — reasons: Jishumī and Ālūsī report azwāj describing different delights or an omitted object of such kinds.
  20:131/q05/p5 Many others generally, beyond the truth deniers. — reasons: Asad prefers a broad injunction against envy, rendering the phrase 'so many others.'
20:131/q06 How does 'أزواجا' function grammatically? [أَزْوَٰجًۭا مِّنْهُمْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether azwāj is the direct object or a ḥāl after the relative clause.)
  20:131/q06/p1 It is the object of mattaʿnā, with minhum modifying or explaining it. — reasons: Qurṭubī calls it the object; Abū Ḥayyān says minhum modifies it; Ālūsī says min is explanatory and the phrase is fronted for emphasis. Samīn also allows this parse.
  20:131/q06/p2 It is a circumstantial accusative linked to the pronoun, while minhum supplies the people affected. — reasons: Kashshāf, Nasafī, Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān, and Samīn relay this; Samīn says mā is followed once according to its form and once by its plural meaning.
20:131/q07 What is the 'flower of worldly life'? [زَهْرَةَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا · meaning] (turns on: Whether zahrah is the bloom of worldly goods or a plural adjective for those who enjoy them.)
  20:131/q07/p1 The world's adornment, brightness, freshness, or beautiful bloom. — reasons: Qatāda, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Suddī, and many commentators gloss adornment. Ibn Qutayba and Wāḥidī's linguists compare tree blossoms; Ṭabrisī gives luminous complexion, radiant suras, and a radiant day. Qurṭubī contrasts the plant flower with ẓuhrah, a star; Ibn ʿĀshūr includes clothing, livestock, gardens, women, children (28:60). Damghānī links 18:46 and 28:60. Bursevī notes a dinar is called a flower for brief freshness; the Prophet in Ibn Kathīr and Durr names earth's blessings worldly bloom.
  20:131/q07/p2 It can describe radiant worldly people, as the plural of zāhir. — reasons: Zamakhsharī compares the bright faces and finery of the indulgent with the pallor and rough clothes of the devout; Samīn cites the plural patterns fājir/fajarah and bārr/bararah. Rāzī, Abū Ḥayyān, and Ālūsī transmit it; Ālūsī objects that the adjective denotes a stable quality and the circumstantial parse is poor.
20:131/q08 Why call worldly life a flower? [زَهْرَةَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا · rhetoric]
  20:131/q08/p1 It dazzles briefly and fades like a plant's blossom. — reasons: Jishumī and Ibn ʿAṭiyya stress quick disappearance; the Prophet calls the world sweet and green in Jishumī and Bursevī. Ṭabarī calls its attractiveness a passing deception. Ibn Kathīr, Ibn ʿĀshūr, and Islāḥī stress transience; Bursevī compares it with honey's bitter aftermath, carrion once seen through, and Ḥāfiẓ's deceitful woman or costly bride.
  20:131/q08/p2 The word itself can rebuke a base, misguided attraction. — reasons: Ālūsī answers an objection to a censure reading by saying the image names a low desire despite natural attraction.
20:131/q09 How is زهرة read? [زَهْرَةَ · readings] (turns on: Whether hāʾ is quiescent or opened; some explain the latter as dialectal or a plural.)
  20:131/q09/p1 With a still hāʾ, the general or majority reading. — reasons: Zajjāj, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ṭabrisī, Abū Ḥayyān, Samīn, N​​ashr, and Ibn ʿĀshūr record it; both vocalizations carry the same root sense per Qirāʾāt ER.
  20:131/q09/p2 With fatḥa on hāʾ, attributed especially to Yaʿqūb and other readers. — reasons: Jishumī and Bayḍāwī call it a dialect form; Ibn al-Jawzī names Ibn Masʿūd, Ḥasan, Zuhrī and Yaʿqūb; Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī add Abū Ḥaywah, Ṭalḥah, Ḥumayd, Salām, Sahl and ʿĪsā. Qirāʾāt ER says syllable weight changes, not root sense. Ibn Jinnī contrasts Basran restriction to attested glottal dialects with Kūfan extension to pharyngeals; Ālūsī records that Kūfan rule.
20:131/q10 How is 'زَهْرَةَ' parsed? [زَهْرَةَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا · grammar] (turns on: The accusative's governor and its relation to mā, bihi, or azwājan; particular objections turn on definiteness and intervening syntax.)
  20:131/q10/p1 An accusative object or result of mattaʿnā, or an implicit 'made/gave.' — reasons: Farrāʾ adduces a Banū Fuqʿas poem, though Ṭabarī finds its prepositional governance weak. Zajjāj says God made life a flower for them; Wāḥidī, Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī and Qurṭubī transmit this. Kashshāf, Rāzī, Bayḍāwī, Samīn, Bursevī and Ālūsī include a second object or elided verb.
  20:131/q10/p2 A circumstantial accusative describing mā, bihi, or the granted goods. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya invokes incomplete definiteness; Ṭabrisī transmits Zajjāj's two possible anchors. Qurṭubī prefers ḥāl with dropped tanwīn and al-ḥayāh appositive to mā (36:40); Samīn supports tanwīn elision at two sukūns with poetry. Makkī prefers the ḥāl in Abū Ḥayyān; Ibn ʿĀshūr and Islāḥī use it.
  20:131/q10/p3 An appositive to bihi or its syntactic place. — reasons: Kashshāf, Rāzī, Qurṭubī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Samīn and Ālūsī cite it. Ibn al-Ḥājib finds an accusative substitute from a prepositional phrase weak and substitution from a pronoun disputed.
  20:131/q10/p4 An appositive to azwājan, with omitted 'possessors of' or by identification. — reasons: Kashshāf, Rāzī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Samīn and Ālūsī report it; Ālūsī rejects directly identifying people as enjoyment.
  20:131/q10/p5 An appositive to the syntactic position of mā. — reasons: Samīn reports the proposal; Ālūsī repeats it. Qurṭubī objects that li-naftinahum belongs to mattaʿnā; Samīn and Abū Ḥayyān report the same intervening-clause objection.
