QURTUBI, d. 671 (طائفة): Another group considers la ilaha illa Allah better because it repels unbelief and associating partners, and people fight on its basis; they cite the Prophet’s command to fight until people say it. QURTUBI, d. 671 (author): In Arabic, al-ḥamd is complete praise; the definite article encompasses every kind of praise, all of which God deserves because He has the most beautiful names and highest attributes. QURTUBI, d. 671 (author): The poet’s use of muḥammad for a person rich in praiseworthy qualities explains the name Muhammad; another verse says the Throne’s Lord is Maḥmūd and the Prophet is Muhammad. QURTUBI, d. 671 (رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم): Anas reports that God is pleased when a servant eats or drinks and praises Him for it; Qurtubi cites this from Muslim. QURTUBI, d. 671 (ابن عطاء): Al-Sulami transmits from Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq and Ibn ʿAṭāʾ the synonymy view; Ibn ʿAṭāʾ explains praise as gratitude for God enabling people to learn and praise Him. QURTUBI, d. 671 (الطبري): Al-Ṭabarī argues the terms are synonymous because Arabic permits the phrase al-ḥamdu lillāhi shukran, praise to God in gratitude. QURTUBI, d. 671 (بعض العلماء): Some scholars say gratitude is broader because it can be expressed by tongue, limbs, and heart, while praise is verbal alone. QURTUBI, d. 671 (author): Qurtubi’s preferred distinction is that praise commends attributes without requiring prior kindness, whereas gratitude praises a benefactor for a favor; praise is broader because it includes commendation as well as thanks. QURTUBI, d. 671 (الحسن): Al-Hasan says that no blessing exists for which praise of God is not better than the blessing itself. QURTUBI, d. 671 (جعفر الصادق): Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq gives a symbolic reading of the letters: ḥāʾ signifies unity, mīm dominion, and dāl permanence; knowing these is knowing God. QURTUBI, d. 671 (شقيق بن إبراهيم): Shafīq ibn Ibrāhīm says gratitude has three conditions: recognizing the giver, being content with what was given, and not disobeying God while one retains strength. QURTUBI, d. 671 (author): God praises Himself and opens His Book with praise, while people are forbidden to claim purity for themselves; Qurtubi cites the command not to praise yourselves and the report to throw dust in flatterers’ faces. | mentions 53:32 QURTUBI, d. 671 (author): God’s self-praise precedes any creature’s praise and is without cause, while created praise is mixed with ulterior causes. QURTUBI, d. 671 (قيل): One explanation says God praised Himself eternally because His servants cannot fulfill the duty of praising Him; even the Messenger declared that he could not enumerate praise to God. QURTUBI, d. 671 (النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم): The Prophet’s confession that he cannot enumerate praise to God illustrates the limits of human praise. QURTUBI, d. 671 (author): A poet says that when people praise God rightly, He is as they praise Him and greater than their praise can express. QURTUBI, d. 671 (قيل): Another explanation says God praised Himself eternally because He knew His blessings would overwhelm servants’ ability to repay them, thereby easing the burden of indebtedness and making the blessing more enjoyable. QURTUBI, d. 671 (رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم): Anas reports from the Prophet that when God gives a servant a blessing and the servant praises Him, the praise is better than what was received. QURTUBI, d. 671 (سيبويه): Sībawayh says nominative al-ḥamdu lillāh conveys that praise from the speaker and all creatures belongs to God, while the accusative reports only the speaker’s own praise. QURTUBI, d. 671 (الطبري): Al-Ṭabarī says God’s praise of Himself implicitly commands His servants to praise Him, as though the wording said, Say praise belongs to God. QURTUBI, d. 671 (رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم): A report in Nawādir al-uṣūl from Anas says that if someone possessed the whole world and then praised God, the praise would be better than it. QURTUBI, d. 671 (author): Rabb can also mean the deity, as shown by a poet’s line about a lord upon whose head a fox urinates. QURTUBI, d. 671 (بعض العلماء): Some scholars call Rabb the greatest name of God because callers frequently invoke Him by it and it conveys the bond of care, mercy, and need between Lord and servant. QURTUBI, d. 671 (author): One derivation traces Rabb to tarbiya, since God manages and nurtures creation; the stepdaughter is called rabība because her mother’s husband raises her.
