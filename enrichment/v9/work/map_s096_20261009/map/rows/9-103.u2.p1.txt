9:103/q11 What property liability and payment form follows from this command? [أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ صَدَقَةًۭ · law] (turns on: Whether zakat attaches to the person or asset, and whether sin capacity is a condition.)
  9:103/q11/p1 Zakat is a debt on the owner's liability, not shared ownership of the threshold property. — reasons: Al-Rāzī reasons from calling the wealth 'theirs'; al-Shāfiʿī therefore says liability remains if delayed zakat property perishes. The verse's generality also covers debtors' wealth and guaranteed wealth.
  9:103/q11/p2 Prescribed zakat must be given in its specified kinds and quantities, not as monetary value. — reasons: Al-Shāfiʿī cites the camel schedules as an instance.
  9:103/q11/p3 Children's property is exempt because purification from sin presupposes capacity to sin. — reasons: Abū Ḥanīfa argues children cannot sin.
  9:103/q11/p4 Children's and mentally incapacitated persons' property is zakatable. — reasons: Al-Shāfiʿī says loss of one purifying cause does not negate the rule.
9:103/q12 What are the monetary zakat thresholds and rates? [أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ · law] (turns on: The disputed increments above silver nisab and the competing gold nisabs.)
  9:103/q12/p1 Silver reaches nisab at 200 dirhams after a year, with one fortieth due; every excess amount is taxed. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī records agreement that an uqiyya is forty dirhams and five dirhams are due at 200. Mālik, al-Layth, al-Shāfiʿī and many others charge every excess, relying on the year requirement reported by al-Tirmidhī.
  9:103/q12/p2 Silver above 200 dirhams incurs no further zakat until forty more dirhams accrue. — reasons: Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyib, al-Ḥasan, ʿAṭāʾ, Ṭāwūs, al-Shaʿbī, al-Zuhrī, Makḥūl, ʿAmr ibn Dīnār, and Abū Ḥanīfa require another dirham at 240.
  9:103/q12/p3 Gold becomes zakatable at twenty dinars; half a dinar is due, then one at forty. — reasons: The majority cites ʿAlī's report. Al-Tirmidhī authenticates two chains; al-Bājī calls the chain weak but the accepted practice corroborative. Al-Qurṭubī cites ʿAlī, Ibn ʿUmar, and ʿĀʾisha against the forty-dinar view.
  9:103/q12/p4 Gold is not zakatable until forty dinars. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan, al-Thawrī, and some followers of Dāwūd ibn ʿAlī dissent.
9:103/q13 What livestock zakat schedules are cited? [أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ · law] (turns on: The schedules differ at 121 camels, 301 sheep, and below thirty cattle.)
  9:103/q13/p1 At five camels one sheep is due; at 121 camels the collector may choose three bint labūn or two ḥiqqas. — reasons: Mālik's 121-camel option is shared by ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz ibn Abī Salama, Ibn Abī Ḥāzim, and Ibn Dīnār. Al-Qurṭubī reports consensus below five and cites Abū Bakr's letter to Anas for schedules.
  9:103/q13/p2 At 121 camels three bint labūn are due until 130, then one ḥiqqa and two bint labūn. — reasons: Ibn Shihāb gives this schedule; Ibn al-Qāsim prefers it.
  9:103/q13/p3 For sheep, three are due at 201 through 399, four at 400, then another per hundred. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī cites the majority, with agreement on the subsequent hundred increment.
  9:103/q13/p4 For sheep, four are due at 301, five at 401, then another per hundred. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan ibn Ṣāliḥ ibn Ḥayy and Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī are reported to hold this.
  9:103/q13/p5 For cattle, one yearling is due at thirty and one mature cow at forty. — reasons: Muʿādh's Yemen report includes this schedule and a dinar or Maʿāfir cloth from each adult as poll tax; al-Tirmidhī authenticates it. Abū ʿUmar says the scholars agree. He notes some versions are mursal, stopped, or interrupted; Baqiyya's connected version is disputed and al-Ḥasan ibn ʿUmāra is weak. Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr says Ibn al-Mundhir confused attributions.
  9:103/q13/p6 One sheep is due for every five cattle up to thirty. — reasons: Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyib, Abū Qilāba, al-Zuhrī, and Qatāda dissent from the yearling-at-thirty rule.
9:103/q14 How are trade goods and hidden assets collected? [أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ · law] (turns on: Whether property is visible to collectors, and how initial ownership and public shares are conceived.)
  9:103/q14/p1 Visible livestock, crops, fruits, and circulating trade goods are collected by officials at their locations. — reasons: Elmalılı reports the Prophet's collectors visited livestock at watering places; the Thaqīf condition meant no assembly of herds. ʿUmar extended collection to open trade goods and no Companion objected; ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz followed this. For Muslims, a year, nisab, and ownership are required; ʿUmar's rates were quarter-tithe for Muslims, half-tithe for dhimmīs, full tithe for enemies. ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz reports half a dinar per twenty from Muslim traders and one from dhimmīs.
  9:103/q14/p2 Owners presented hidden-asset zakat voluntarily; later direct payment to the poor was allowed. — reasons: Elmalılı says the Prophet and first caliphs received it, because homes and shops could not be searched. ʿUthmān ordered debts paid first and allowed owners to choose recipients.
  9:103/q14/p3 Zakat and similar shares arise when new wealth appears, while the remainder belongs to the worker or owner. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī argues from 2:29 and 4:5 that wealth might initially be society's, with an individual's share assigned; after ownership settles, further charges are usurpation except emergency needs.
9:103/q15 How did Ibn Zayd identify the people addressed? [أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ · referent]
  9:103/q15/p1 They were hypocrites who missed Tabūk, confessed hypocrisy and doubt, and renewed repentance by offering charity. — reasons: Ibn Zayd links the passage to 9:84's prohibition on prayer over dead hypocrites; a separate report says they had promised to fight and give charity.
9:103/q16 What kind of charity is meant? [صَدَقَةًۭ · law] (turns on: Whether the occasion fixes the kind of alms or the general wording and purifying function indicate prescribed zakat.)
  9:103/q16/p1 It is the obligatory zakat. — reasons: ʿIkrima, al-Jubbāʾī, and many jurists hold this. Al-Rāzī argues purification from sin fits an unpaid duty and willing payment tests repentance for attachment to wealth; al-Ṭabrisī says the apparent wording is general and gives nisāb categories. Al-Ālūsī reports al-Jubbāʾī's concern that rejecting their payment would liken them to hypocrites. Elmalılı allows extra gifts. Asad says state acceptance recognizes Muslim standing.
  9:103/q16/p2 It is a voluntary expiatory gift from the repentant men. — reasons: Ibn Zayd, Ibn Kaysān, al-Ḥasan and others place it after accepted repentance; al-Rāzī's advocates say a new zakat ruling would break the surrounding passage's coherence. Al-Ālūsī argues zakat had already been prescribed. Al-Bursevī says obligatory zakat is not collected this way; al-Qurṭubī connects one-third to a vow. Ṭabāṭabāʾī regards the specific-wealth report as weak.
  9:103/q16/p3 The verse's occasion was voluntary expiation, while its legal rule also covers obligatory zakat. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī explicitly separates historical occasion and general legal scope; al-Rāzī says the one-third report is compatible with his zakat reading.
9:103/q17 When and how did fixed zakat become obligatory? [صَدَقَةًۭ · sciences] (turns on: Whether 9:103 begins zakat's obligation or applies a prior institution.)
  9:103/q17/p1 This passage establishes a new command to collect obligatory zakat. — reasons: Most jurists in al-Rāzī, al-Bursevī, and Kur'an Yolu infer a general ruling.
  9:103/q17/p2 Fixed-threshold zakat was probably obligated in year two after Ramadan, with regular state collection perhaps beginning in year nine. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu distinguishes eight Meccan uses of zakat as a flexible duty from twenty-two definite Medinan uses and twelve of ṣadaqa; it acknowledges uncertainty in the dating. An al-Ṣādiq report says the Prophet announced it in Ramadan, then after a year dispatched collectors.
  9:103/q17/p3 Zakat was already obligatory when this verse addressed the repentants. — reasons: Al-Rāzī's second view says they sincerely professed Islam and offered due zakat; al-Ālūsī uses prior obligation to favor voluntary expiation instead.
9:103/q18 What social purpose and distribution policy does zakat serve? [صَدَقَةًۭ · law]
  9:103/q18/p1 It supplies society's needs while improving individuals and reducing class differences. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī compares zakat and khums to earlier communal provisions, describes zakat's eight and khums's six recipient classes, and argues public shares should aid people rather than prestige works. Kur'an Yolu calls zakat moral and economic worship benefiting giver and society, paired often with prayer. The Study Quran calls charity a prominent sūra theme.
  9:103/q18/p2 An authority's acceptance recognizes the repentant giver as a Muslim. — reasons: Asad notes the Prophet refused zakat from some exposed hypocrites; the later state can accept from those whose deeds show repentance.
9:103/q19 How is 'a charity' construed, and why is it called that? [صَدَقَةًۭ · grammar] (turns on: The accusative construction and the moral etymology supplied by the sources.)
  9:103/q19/p1 Ṣadaqa is the object taken from their wealth. — reasons: The commentators treat it as the alms accepted on command; its unspecified form invites legal specification.
  9:103/q19/p2 The name ṣadaqa derives from truthfulness of faith. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī says it shows agreement of inner and outer belief, against those who disparage voluntary donors in 9:79; al-Bursevī calls it truthful servitude.
  9:103/q19/p3 Ṣadaqa is a circumstantial accusative: take from their wealth while giving charity. — r
