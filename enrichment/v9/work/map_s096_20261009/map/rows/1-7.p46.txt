Nearly all commentators equate God's condemnation with the harmful consequences people incur by knowingly rejecting guidance and disobeying its commands.
[ASAD-NOTES:1:6#n4/r2] ASAD-NOTES, d. 1412 · Zamakhshari | reports | Zamakhshari reads the final two descriptions as qualifying the blessed people: they were not condemned and did not go astray.
[ASAD-NOTES:1:6#n4/r3] ASAD-NOTES, d. 1412 · Baghawi and Ibn Kathir | reports | Baghawi and Ibn Kathir reject Zamakhshari's qualification reading and take the final phrases as distinct groups on a wrong course, as Asad does.
[ASAD-NOTES:1:6#n4/r4] ASAD-NOTES, d. 1412 · Al-Ghazali and Muhammad 'Abduh | reports | They distinguish informed rejection of a recognized message from straying when truth never arrived or arrived too corrupted to recognize.
[ABUDAWUD:779/r1] ABUDAWUD · سمرة بن جندب | reports | Samura reports two pauses in the Prophet’s prayer, at the opening takbir and after al-Fatiha’s closing words; Imran disputed his memory, but Ubayy ibn Kaʿb affirmed that Samura had preserved it.
[ABUDAWUD:780/r1] ABUDAWUD · قتادة | reports | Qatada explains that one pause was when prayer began and another after recitation, adding a clarification associating the latter with saying al-Fatiha’s final words.
[ABUDAWUD:821/r2] ABUDAWUD · الله تعالى | reports | In a hadith qudsi transmitted by Abu Hurayra, God divides al-Fatiha between Himself and the servant: praise and glorification receive divine responses, worship is mutual, and the guidance petition belongs to the servant.
[ABUDAWUD:934/r1] ABUDAWUD · أبو هريرة عن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Abu Hurayra reports that when the Prophet recited al-Fatiha’s closing words, he said Amin loudly enough for the first row to hear.
[ABUDAWUD:935/r1] ABUDAWUD · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم عن أبي هريرة | reports | The Prophet instructs followers to say Amin after the imam recites these words; when their utterance coincides with the angels’, their previous sins are forgiven.
[ABUDAWUD:972/r1] ABUDAWUD · أبو موسى الأشعري عن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Abu Musa relays the Prophet’s prayer instructions: align rows, follow the imam’s takbir, bowing, and prostration; say Amin after 1:7 and praise God after the imam’s praise; the taught tahiyyat has an Ahmad transmission with two omissions.
[BUKHARI:4475/r1] BUKHARI · أبو هريرة | reports | Abu Hurayra reports the Prophet instructing worshippers to say amin after the imam recites this close of al-Fatiha; agreement with the angels brings forgiveness for past sins.
[BUKHARI:782/r1] BUKHARI · أبو هريرة | reports | Abu Hurayra reports the Prophet instructing worshippers to say amin after the imam recites this close of al-Fatiha; agreement with the angels brings forgiveness for past sins.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r1] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The concatenated specification of the right path distinguishes it from rival paths to God, while leaving the groups’ identities partly open.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r10] CORPUSCORANICUM · Josef van Ess | reports | Van Ess highlights a theological difference: the Fātiḥa locates evil in the individual soul, which must be protected from temptation.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r11] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The Fātiḥa and Lord’s Prayer differ theologically: the Quran makes keeping the right path salvific, unlike Christian redemption from sin; their strongest common ground is the genre of community prayer.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r12] CORPUSCORANICUM · Anton Baumstark | reports | Baumstark reconstructs a monotheistic liturgical genealogy for the Fātiḥa, whose formula and structure reflect an early Christian daily-prayer doxology.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r13] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | If the Basmala is omitted in worship, the Fātiḥa opens with a universalized doxology like the beginnings of the Chrysostom and Basil liturgies.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r14] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The Greek doxology and response together parallel praise, eschatological kingship, rule over present and eternity, and mercy in the first part of the Fātiḥa. | mentions 1:2, 1:3, 1:4
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r15] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | Parallels between the Fātiḥa’s second half and the litany after the hymn strengthen the view that it opens worship; it likely functioned both as community prayer and liturgical preface during Quranic formation.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r2] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | Those favored by God can mean righteous people generally, but here may refer especially to middle Meccan Christians whose protagonists are linked with divine favor and mercy. | mentions 19:58, 19:2
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r3] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The phrase may also include monotheistic predecessors generally, favored by God.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r4] CORPUSCORANICUM · Nöldeke | reports | On the hypothesis that the Fātiḥa draws on Christian traditions, Nöldeke’s reading takes the second group as Jews, stereotyped in Christian polemic as subject to divine wrath.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r5] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | Identifying the wrath-stricken with Jews would be anachronistic for the Fātiḥa’s first proclamation: this image appears only in Medina and never becomes a Quranic stereotype.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r6] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The category may inherit a preexisting topos stripped of political targeting; since Jewish opponents were not yet in view, hearers fill in its referent.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r7] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | Al-ḍāllīn is a standard middle Meccan designation for pagan opponents, already found in early Meccan passages. | mentions 56:51, 56:92
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r8] CORPUSCORANICUM · Winkler and Goitein | reports | They note the Fātiḥa’s relationship to the Christian Lord’s Prayer, especially its kingdom and deliverance-from-evil motifs.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:1:7:kommentar/r9] CORPUSCORANICUM · Martin Bauschke | reports | Bauschke argues more strongly that the Fātiḥa records Muhammad’s authentic engagement with Christ and traces anonymous traces of Jesus into Islam’s principal prayer.
[IBNMAJAH:844/r1] IBNMAJAH · سمرة بن جندب | reports | Samura reports two Prophetic pauses, at prayer's start and after recitation of this ending; Imran objected but Ubayy confirmed Samura, and Qatada says the pause lets the reciter regain breath.
[IBNMAJAH:846/r1] IBNMAJAH · أبو هريرة | reports | Abu Hurayra reports the Prophet's prayer instructions: followers listen during the imam's recitation and say amin when he finishes with these words.
[IBNMAJAH:853/r1] IBNMAJAH · أبو هريرة | reports | Abu Hurayra says people had stopped saying amin; the Prophet said it aloud after the Fatiha's closing words, loud enough for the first row to hear and the mosque to reverberate.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:1:7/r1] KURANYOLU-TEFSIR · author | holds | History offers evidence for the sound and unsound paths: seekers should study those who found happiness by following prophets and those who rejected divine guidance and incurred wrath.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:1:7/r2] KURANYOLU-TEFSIR · author | reports | Some reports identify the strayed as Christians and those under wrath as Jews; the author says these are nearby examples in time and place, not an exhaustive identification.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:1:7/r3] KURANYOLU-TEFSIR · Allah Teâlâ | reports | A sacred hadith in Muslim presents Fatiha as divided between God and the worshipper: God answers each praise or request, and grants the servant’s plea for guidance.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:1:7/r4] KURANYOLU-TEFSIR · author | holds | Amin means asking that the prayer be accepted; it is not part of Fatiha or a verse, and hadiths say the Prophet said it after Fatiha and instructed others to do so.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:1:7/r5] KURANYOLU-TEFSIR · author | reports | Hadiths say angels also say amin with the worshipper and that the prayer is accepted in both the visible and unseen realms.
[KURANYOLU-TEFSIR:1:7/r6] KURANYOLU-TEFSIR · author | holds | Because sound hadiths support saying amin aloud after audible Fatiha, most legal schools adopt that practice; Hanafis always say it silently in prayer.
[MALIK:187/r3] MALIK · الله تبارك وتعالى | reports | The qudsi report assigns the final plea for guidance to the servant and says the servant receives what he asks.
[MALIK:194/r1] MALIK · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Abu Hurayra reports that the Prophet tells the congregation to say amen after the imam's final phrase; agreement with the angels brings forgiveness of past sins.
[MUSLIM:878/r3] MUSLIM · الله تعالى | reports | When the servant asks for the guided path and names those favored rather than those under anger or astray, Allah says this request is for the servant and grants what was asked.
[MUSLIM:904/r1] MUSLIM · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Abu Musa reports the Prophet’s prayer instructions: keep the rows straight, follow the imam’s takbīr, bowing, and prostration after him, and recite the sitting testimony.
[MUSLIM:904/r2] MUSLIM · أبو موسى الأشعري | reports | Abu Musa says the Prophet taught worshippers to answer the recitation’s ending with Amin, so that God answers them; the teaching arose after a man spoke during prayer.
[MUSLIM:920/r1] MUSLIM · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | The Prophet promises forgiveness of previous sins when the reciter and those behind him say Amin in unison with the heavenly utterance.
[NASAI:1064/r1] NASAI · أبو موسى | reports | Abū Mūsā reports that the Prophet taught worshippers to say amin after the imam recites 1:7, so God answers them.
