53:33/q07 How dependable are the named occasion reports? [* · reports] (turns on: Historical establishment, the character of ʿUthmān, the chain of the al-Walīd report, and how its details fit the Qurʾānic censure.)
  53:33/q07/p1 The ʿUthmān account is false or unestablished. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya declares ʿUthmān innocent. Rāzī says the account is unestablished and contrary to his character. Ibn ʿĀshūr transmits Ibn ʿAṭiyya's reason that he would not heed Ibn Abī Sarḥ. Abū Ḥayyān attributes a rejection of the accounts to Ibn ʿAṭiyya.
  53:33/q07/p2 The al-Walīd story is unsound or ill-fitted to these verses. — reasons: Islāḥī says it has no sound chain and, even if true, its connection is unclear because the Qurʾān does not rebuke him for breaking a promise; treating al-ladhī as a proper name led exegetes to invent an ill-fitting incident. Kuranyolu says polytheist reports are unreliable and their details conflict with Qurʾānic principles.
53:33/q08 What conduct does the verse rebuke or warn against? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the main censure falls on abandoned faith and the imagined transfer of punishment, on stinginess after limited giving, or on complacency about earlier deeds.)
  53:33/q08/p1 Turning from obedience or truth and believing another can carry one's punishment. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr links the censure to 75:31–32, which describes neither believing nor praying but denying and turning away. Bursevī says the false transfer of punishment and turning away are the chief rebukes; the stopped gift narrates the episode ironically. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the man acted as if payment could erase the consequences of abandoning Islam, although his action presupposed both its guilt and transferability of afterlife sins.
  53:33/q08/p2 His turning is blame, while the astonishment also prepares rebuke for giving little and then stopping. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr distinguishes the blame for turning from the astonishment; the small gift leads to a further charge of stinginess even beyond disbelief.
  53:33/q08/p3 A person who trusts past good deeds or self-purity and ceases good works may lose even the fruit of earlier deeds. — reasons: Study Quran extends the warning beyond the reported person to anyone who stops doing good.
  53:33/q08/p4 Wealthy claimants to Abrahamic or Mosaic standing expect Paradise while withholding charity, despite those prophets' teachings about personal deeds and burdens. — reasons: Islāḥī says claimed descent cannot secure Paradise; no one carries another's burden, and each receives the fruit of his own deeds. He presents a miserly idolater as representative of wealthy Quraysh and notes sarcastic disparagement of those who give grudgingly yet act certain of Paradise's luxuries. Kuranyolu likewise stresses wealth attachment and enduring stinginess.
  53:33/q08/p5 The listener should consider a man who gave only a little toward God's cause and then stopped. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī reads the following verse as clarifying that the turning was from spending.
53:33/q09 How does this passage fit the surrounding argument of the sūra? [* · links] (turns on: The commentators connect the example to different surrounding themes: recompense, the scriptural case for personal responsibility, ancestral pride, parallels elsewhere, or the sūra's recitation.)
  53:33/q09/p1 It moves from God's knowledge and recompense to a pointed example of one person's mistaken conduct. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī says the passage singles out one idolater after exposing the idolaters' ignorance. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the question is astonishing after 53:31's promise of recompense, and contrasts its particular man with 53:29's group. Rāzī connects the warning to God's knowledge and human need for prophetic instruction in 53:29–32; Asad bases his rendering on Rāzī's return to an earlier theme, although the excerpt breaks before naming the verses.
  53:33/q09/p2 The example introduces the scriptures' truth about personal responsibility against the polytheists' reliance on idols and intercession. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī says citations from Moses' and Abraham's scriptures answer the claim that idols portray God's angel daughters who intercede. Creation and governance imply a second life of recompense, revelation, and duties; the destroyed peoples of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the overturned cities testify. The sūra then identifies this warner with earlier warners, announces the near Hour, and commands worship and prostration. Study Quran calls this its third section, from creation to enduring truths in those scriptures.
  53:33/q09/p3 The scriptures of Abraham and Moses expose the error of expecting Paradise through ancestry while refusing charity. — reasons: Islāḥī contrasts boastful Abrahamic and Mosaic association with the prophets' teaching and sacrifices: no one bears another's burden, and each meets the outcome of his own acts.
  53:33/q09/p4 The verse's turning away parallels the denial and turning in 75:31–32. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr connects the condemnation of disobedience with that passage's sequence of neither believing nor praying, then denying and turning away.
  53:33/q09/p5 The framing recalls apostasy after the Night Journey and the unexpected prostration by all groups at this sūra's end. — reasons: Biqāʿī cites Bukhārī for Muslims, polytheists, jinn, and humans prostrating together, even some who later apostatized.
53:33/q10 Were the verses beginning here revealed in Medina? [* · sciences]
  53:33/q10/p1 Some except the nine verses beginning here from al-Najm's Meccan classification. — reasons: Al-Itqān reports a view extending the exception through 53:41; Study Quran says some extend the proposed Medinan dating to verse 33.
53:33/q11 What inward path does the turning away portray? [تَوَلَّىٰ · inward]
  53:33/q11/p1 A seeker begins to spend life's capital on the spiritual path, then tires, returns to the sensual ego, and spends the rest on worldly pleasure. — reasons: Bursevī says the relapse shows a lack of readiness for union.
53:33/q12 What image explains the stopping of the gift in the continuation? [* · meaning]
  53:33/q12/p1 Akdā pictures a well digger striking hard rock and being stopped. — reasons: Study Quran derives the verb from al-kudyah, the hard rock met while digging a well.
