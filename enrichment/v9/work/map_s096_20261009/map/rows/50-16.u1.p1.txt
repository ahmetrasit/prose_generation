50:16/q13 Are unspoken whispers morally recorded or punished? [مَا تُوَسْوِسُ بِهِۦ نَفْسُهُۥ · law]
  50:16/q13/p1 Passing inward whispers are pardoned unless spoken or acted upon, and unsettled thoughts are not written. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr cites a sound prophetic report of pardon for the Muslim community. Elmalılı distinguishes passing thoughts from intention, resolve and decision in the angelic record.
50:16/q14 What does God's being nearer than the jugular mean? [وَنَحْنُ أَقْرَبُ إِلَيْهِ مِنْ حَبْلِ ٱلْوَرِيدِ · theology] (turns on: The dispute turns on whether the first-person plural names God's knowledge or power, both attributes, His angels, or a deeper nonspatial relation; the following angels and the prior knowledge clause are key contextual evidence.)
  50:16/q14/p1 God's knowledge and witness encompass the person more intimately than any physical closeness. — reasons: Ṭabarī, al-Naḥḥās, Muqātil, Wāḥidī, Baghawī, Ṭabrisī, Rāzī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, Biqāʿī, Ālūsī, Elmalılı and Ibn ʿĀshūr make this reading. Bodily parts or flesh conceal one another or the vein, but nothing screens God. Bayḍāwī's close observer and Elmalılı's omitted 'whoever' explain the comparator; one possible observer is the person himself. Al-Itqān applies the rule of interpreting impossible literal attributes by their implication. The image is not spatial presence.
  50:16/q14/p2 God has greater power, mastery and command over the person than the vein. — reasons: Reported by Arabic interpreters, Akhfash, Māwardī, Ṭabrisī, Rāzī and Elmalılı. Ibn ʿAṭiyya stresses dominion and grasp without bodily proximity; Rāzī compares command in the person with blood moving through veins. Qurṭubī contrasts the person's control of the vein; al-Ḍaḥḥāk and an Abū Saʿīd prophetic report assert the comparison, the latter joining it to God intervening between heart and person and holding forelocks.
  50:16/q14/p3 The clause conveys knowledge and power together, with care and effects such as kindness or wrath. — reasons: Thaʿlabī and Qushayrī join knowledge and power; Muqātil as quoted by Qurṭubī combines both. Elmalılı prefers the combination: power entails knowledge, and the added nearness clause goes beyond the preceding knowledge clause. He weighs the proposed bar on combining metaphors against context cues and says a representative metaphor can unite them.
  50:16/q14/p4 The near ones are God's angels placed near the human, rather than divine indwelling. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr argues from 56:85, where unseen nearness to the dying is angelic, and 15:9, where 'We sent down' acts through angels. God enables angelic prompting alongside satanic prompting; the Prophet likened Satan's movement to blood. Study Quran reports this reading, while noting interpreters seek to avoid literal divine nearness. Ibn Kathīr says the wording does not demand a mere knowledge reading.
  50:16/q14/p5 God is nearer in perception than the jugular would be if it perceived. — reasons: Ṭabrisī reports this further gloss.
  50:16/q14/p6 The nearness is a real, abiding ontological relation in which each created self and faculty depends on God. — reasons: Qāshānī rejects spatial conjunction but says particular existence stands only through divine existence; he cites ʿAlī that God is with things without conjunction. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says God mediates between soul and itself and actions (8:24), while the familiar image only approximates greater nearness. Ibn ʿArabī, as Elmalılı reports, places the Near One above bodily life channels, makes faculties dependent on God, and describes nearness with distance; devotional nearness through prescribed acts makes God the servant's hearing and sight without identity. Study Quran reads divine immanence throughout creation (2:115, 57:4). Elmalılı accepts essential and active nearness yet insists creation, divine acts, and the servant remain distinct, citing 37:96 and 8:17; unity language must not become union. He says God's encompassing cannot be spatial, and common exegesis uses attributes to prevent that impression.
  50:16/q14/p7 God remains more powerful and knowledgeable than the two recording angels when they arrive. — reasons: Study Quran reports a reading tied to 50:17: God needs no angels to know acts. Elmalılı suggests the omitted close comparator could be the recording angels, whom God exceeds in nearness.
50:16/q15 What is the jugular or warīd named in the comparison? [حَبْلِ ٱلْوَرِيدِ · referent] (turns on: The sources locate the vessel in the neck, heart, or branching network, sometimes treating these as names for connected parts.)
  50:16/q15/p1 A vein or pair of veins in the throat or sides of the neck. — reasons: Mujāhid and Ibn ʿAbbās identify throat or neck; Ibn Qutayba and Ṭabarī place it between throat and upper chest or collarbones. Zajjāj cites a poet's dual وريداه; al-Thaʿlabī cites a swelling vein in poetry and plural awrida. Abū al-Haytham describes pulsing vessels beside the throat notch and a life vessel carrying breath rather than blood; al-Layth says anger swells the pair. Farrāʾ, Abū ʿUbayda and Zajjāj locate it within the neck; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Qurṭubī note right and left veins. Zamakhsharī connects the pair to the watīn. Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies an artery from the left heart chamber, the aorta and its carotid branches; Elmalılı gives competing accounts of venous and arterial blood, the two circulations, and says their colors are only general labels.
  50:16/q15/p2 A vessel connected to the heart, called the watīn or vital heart cord. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan and al-Aṣamm give the heart vessel; Muqātil says it reaches the heart. Ibn ʿAbbās is also reported as calling it the heart's vital cord. Rāghib describes a liver–heart vessel carrying spirit or blood, for which some compare God to a person's spirit.
  50:16/q15/p3 A blood-carrying vein or vessel that branches throughout the body. — reasons: Language scholars in Wāḥidī name upper arm, forearm and hand branches; Baghawī, Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī, Rāzī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī describe distribution. Al-Athram names watīn in the heart, abhar in the back, akḥal/nasā in arm/thigh and aslam or sulaym in the little finger. Elmalılı allows the generic singular to denote the entire vein network or vena cava and explains blood returning to the heart.
50:16/q16 Why is the vessel called ٱلْوَرِيدِ? [ٱلْوَرِيدِ · meaning]
  50:16/q16/p1 Its name relates to arrival or passage of life or spirit through it. — reasons: Bayḍāwī reports that spirit goes to it; Zamakhsharī says spirit arrives there from the head. Samīn and Ālūsī allow active or passive participle readings, the arriving vessel or the one arrived at. Elmalılı says the vessel gathers blood toward the heart.
50:16/q17 What does حَبْلِ mean here and in cited usage? [حَبْلِ · meaning] (turns on: Context selects the bodily cord here from the word's wider rope and bond senses.)
  50:16/q17/p1 Here it denotes a bodily cord or vessel resembling an ordinary rope. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī defines ordinary ḥabl as twisted fiber, cotton or wool and includes neck vein as a Qurʾanic sense; Ibn ʿĀshūr describes thick bodily vessels. Ālūsī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī say the vessel is called a cord by resemblance; Elmalılı gives rope, bond and vessel senses.
  50:16/q17/p2 Elsewhere ḥabl can denote a covenant, safe-conduct or the Qurʾan. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī says a covenant is a rope one holds to reach a goal (3:112); al-Aʿshā uses plural ḥibāl for guarantees or safety. The literal rope appears at 26:44 and 111:5; the Qurʾan as a bond appears at 3:103.
50:16/q18 How does the annexation حَبْلِ ٱلْوَرِيدِ work? [حَبْلِ ٱلْوَرِيدِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the genitive identifies the same vessel, specifies a member of a class, or relates neighboring cords.)
  50:16/q18/p1 It names one vessel twice; the second, specific name explains the first general name. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, Ṭabarī, Thaʿlabī, Baghawī, Ibn al-Jawzī, Qurṭubī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Biqāʿī, Ālūsī, Elmalılı, Ibn ʿĀshūr and Ṭabāṭabāʾī give identity or explanatory annexation. They compare differing names, 50:9's 'grain of harvest,' مسجد الجامع and shajar al-arāk; Elmalılı allows a rope-like simile.
  50:16/q18/p2 Ḥabl is a broad category specified by the kind warīd, rather than self-annexation. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya explicitly rejects calling this a thing added to itself; Samīn reports a comparable specification of a broad term, as in بعير سانية.
  50:16/q18/p3 It can mean the cord belonging to or near the vein in the same neck region. — reasons: Samīn reports a possessive analysis like مسجد الجامع or a neck cord added to the vein because both share an organ. Abū Ḥayyān offers a shoulder cord in the jugular's region; Elmalılı reports Zamakhsharī's base-of-neck sense, allowing a lāmī genitive and neck or body metaphor.
50:16/q19 Why compare divine nearness to the jugular? [مِنْ حَبْلِ ٱلْوَرِيدِ · rhetoric]
  50:16/q19/p1 It is a familiar image of the closest bodily connection, made vivid by a hidden vessel. — reasons: Qushayrī calls it the nearest part to oneself; Bayḍāwī cites death nearer than the vein. Biqāʿī, Ālūsī and Elmalılı call it a proverbial image; Dhu al-Rumma uses it poetically. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the vessel is both intimate and unnoticed, making unseen knowledge intelligible, and judges it better than midwife-seat or belt images; he cites Zuhayr's hand-mouth and Ḥanẓala's sandal-strap comparisons. Elmalılı says it evokes the closest vessel or life itself; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the image approximates a greater nearness.
