11:112/q10 Does shared repentance imply the Prophet had disbelieved? [وَمَن تَابَ مَعَكَ · theology]
  11:112/q10/p1 No; sharing the act of turning need not mean sharing the same past sin. — reasons: Bursevī says prophets are protected from unbelief and deliberate major sins before and after revelation; he can call repentance a leaving of a former state even without actual unbelief. He reports that the Prophet said ʿAlī never disbelieved, and an invitation to reject al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā does not show otherwise. Ālūsī notes the Prophet's practice of seeking forgiveness more than seventy times a day, while shared repentance may have distinct objects.
11:112/q11 Must the Prophet and his companions have equal degrees of steadfastness? [وَمَن تَابَ مَعَكَ · interpretation]
  11:112/q11/p1 No; conjunction shares the general duty without equal rank. — reasons: Ālūsī reports that the Prophet's commanded degree exceeds the companions', with 4:18 cited.
11:112/q12 What does ṭughyān mean as a word and form? [تَطْغَوْا۟ · meaning] (turns on: The notes give a shared limit sense and a separate morphological observation.)
  11:112/q12/p1 Crossing or rising beyond a proper limit. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī, Jishumī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Ṭabrisī, Qurṭubī, and Biqāʿī give the limit sense. Jishumī cites floodwater by analogy and variant root forms; Ibn ʿAṭiyya cites the flood and Pharaoh in 69:11, 20:24, 20:43, and 79:17. Qurṭubī cites 69:11. Biqāʿī connects the root's rising and covering to night, flood, sea, fertile land, and Ghūṭa. Ibn ʿĀshūr says its older sense is arrogance and audacity, here daring to violate commands, citing 2:15 and 20:81. Ṭabāṭabāʾ recalls water passing its banks.
  11:112/q12/p2 The verb's form follows ṭaghawtu ṭaghā, like maḥawtu maḥā. — reasons: Al-Akhfash gives this morphological analogy.
11:112/q13 What conduct does do not transgress forbid? [وَلَا تَطْغَوْا۟ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether ṭughyān is read as the inclusive crossing of all limits or a specified kind of excess.)
  11:112/q13/p1 Every departure from God's limits, including excess and neglect, disobedience, oppression, and distortion of commands. — reasons: Ṭabarī and Ibn Zayd identify disobedience; al-Wāḥidī, Baghawī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Ṭabrisī, Qurṭubī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, and Ālūsī state the general limit. Al-Ṭabrisī specifies additions and omissions; Rāzī prefers the inclusive reading that embraces unlawful rulings, excess, and abandonment of humility. Biqāʿī says religion disciplines people rather than meeting a divine need, that modest sound practice suffices, and that barring excess also bars deficiency a fortiori. Bursevī says both extremes are blameworthy and the word highlights common excess. Ālūsī says crossing sacred law is heresy. Ibn Kathīr emphasizes oppression even of polytheists as ruinous; Asad attributes a universal conduct rule toward believers and unbelievers to Ibn Kathīr following Ibn ʿAbbās and reports the equity reading from several commentators. Ṭabāṭabāʾ frames the boundary as human servitude and warns against proud refusal like earlier nations.
  11:112/q13/p2 It specifically forbids arrogance toward anyone and requires humility before God. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās gives this gloss in al-Wāḥidī and Rāzī; al-Wāḥidī's Wajīz and Asad repeat it. Qurṭubī reports it as an alternative, with oppression also mentioned.
  11:112/q13/p3 It forbids declaring lawful or unlawful beyond what God commanded. — reasons: Al-Kalbī says this in al-Wāḥidī; Abū Ḥayyān ascribes it to Ibn ʿAbbās, and Rāzī and Ālūsī include it among the interpretations.
  11:112/q13/p4 It warns that prosperity and blessings may produce pride or leave the straight path. — reasons: Al-Jubbāʾī gives the warning in al-Ṭabrisī; Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Biqāʿī, and Rāzī mention this danger. Rāzī's inclusive reading also includes abandoning gratitude.
  11:112/q13/p5 It forbids mixing monotheism with doubt or idolatry. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān attributes this to Muqātil; Ālūsī reports the same gloss.
11:112/q14 Why does the prohibition use plural address? [وَلَا تَطْغَوْا۟ · rhetoric]
  11:112/q14/p1 It addresses the believers included with the Prophet, while the singular positive command honors him. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies the plural recipients; Abū Ḥayyān calls the alternation eloquent. Elmalılı says the positive command grammatically addresses the Prophet but applies to all, whereas the prohibition addresses the community because their transgression is possible and the Prophet's inconceivable; he links this to the Prophet's burden in Hūd. Ālūsī says this account need not conflict with prophetic infallibility.
11:112/q15 How does the ban on transgression connect with other commands? [وَلَا تَطْغَوْا۟ · links]
  11:112/q15/p1 It parallels the command in 42:15 against following unbelievers' desires. — reasons: Bursevī says resisting those desires preserves full steadfastness, while following them constitutes transgression.
11:112/q16 How is the final verb read? [تَعْمَلُونَ · readings] (turns on: Direct address in the preceding clauses or a shift to third-person reference.)
  11:112/q16/p1 The common reading has second-person tāʾ, matching the direct address. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports the majority; Samīn calls it the general reading.
  11:112/q16/p2 Al-Ḥasan, al-Aʿmash, and ʿĪsā al-Thaqafī read third-person yaʾ. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya names al-Ḥasan and al-Aʿmash; Abū Ḥayyān adds attribution to ʿĪsā. Samīn says the shift resembles 11:111; Ālūsī also notes the third-person reading.
11:112/q17 What does God being baṣīr of deeds convey? [إِنَّهُۥ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌۭ · interpretation]
  11:112/q17/p1 He sees and knows every deed, including hidden obedience and disobedience, and recompenses them. — reasons: Ṭabarī says nothing escapes God; Ibn ʿAbbās in al-Wāḥidī adds that He knew acts before they occurred. Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, al-Wāḥidī's Wajīz, and Ibn Kathīr stress nothing is hidden. Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ālūsī, and Elmalılı add recompense or punishment; Ālūsī prefers this as the explanation of both command and prohibition.
11:112/q18 Why close with God's sight of deeds? [بَصِيرٌۭ · rhetoric]
  11:112/q18/p1 It warns against concealed disobedience and gives God's knowledge vivid force. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says seeing conveys clear, powerful knowledge even of transgression people hide; Bayḍāwī and Ālūsī connect it causally with the command, prohibition, and recompense.
11:112/q19 What legal method does the command to stand firm support? [فَٱسْتَقِمْ كَمَآ أُمِرْتَ · law] (turns on: Whether text-grounded analogy can count as obedience to the command and whether conjunction imposes legal sequence.)
  11:112/q19/p1 Follow the explicit order of divine texts, without specifying general wording by analogy or juristic preference. — reasons: Rāzī uses commanded steadfastness to require the stated sequence in ablution and like livestock for zakāt; he objects to qualifying a general text by analogy. Bayḍāwī likewise bars alteration by analogy or juristic preference. Ālūsī reports the inference but distinguishes analogy based on textual causes as authorized ijtihād; he rejects the ablution-order proof because it would impose order on every pair joined by wa, such as prayer and alms, and unfairly condemn Ḥanafīs who do not require that order.
  11:112/q19/p2 Fidelity to texts also permits analogy grounded in textual causes. — reasons: Ālūsī reports this qualification as authorized ijtihād and objects to Rāzī's blanket rejection of specifying general texts by analogy, particularly its ablution-order and Ḥanafī implications.
11:112/q20 What inward steadfastness does the verse describe? [فَٱسْتَقِمْ · inward]
  11:112/q20/p1 Fulfill divine and human rights through God while witnessing unity without egoic appropriation or neglect of duties. — reasons: Qāshānī says the Prophet returns to creation and upholds commands while witnessing unity, and cites his swollen feet and reply that he should be a grateful servant despite forgiveness in 48:2. Ālūsī similarly names God's and creatures' rights, guidance of creation, and seeing multiplicity in unity and unity in multiplicity without losing reverence. Junayd distinguishes steadfastness with fear and hope for worshipers, awe and hope for the near ones, and no self-regard for gnostics.
11:112/q21 What inward transgression does the verse warn against? [وَلَا تَطْغَوْا۟ بَصِيرٌۭ · inward]
  11:112/q21/p1 Assigning unqualified divine perfection to the limited ego veils God's reality. — reasons: Qāshānī says God sees whether acts are through Him or through the self.
11:112/q22 What is reported about the Prophet's burden on receiving this command? [فَٱسْتَقِمْ · reports] (turns on: The reports describe different effects of the verse, with varying sura names in the graying account.)
  11:112/q22/p1 Ibn ʿAbbās called it the hardest verse for the Prophet, connecting it to his graying. — reasons: Jishumī, Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, Rāzī, Qurṭubī, Biqāʿī, Ibn ʿĀshūr, and Ṭabāṭabāʾ transmit this. The reported sura wording varies: Hūd and its sisters in several, Hūd and al-Wāqiʿa in al-Ṭabrisī and Ṭabāṭabāʾ; al-Ālūsī reports Ibn ʿAbbās also called it hardest.
  11:112/q22/p2 After its revelation, the Prophet urged earnestness and was not seen laughing. — reasons: Ālūsī transmits al-Ḥasan's report; Ṭabāṭabāʾ says Ibn Abī Ḥātim and Abū al-Shaykh transmit from al-Ḥasan that believers girded themselves and the Prophet ceased laughing.
