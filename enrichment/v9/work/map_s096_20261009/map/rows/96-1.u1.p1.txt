KURANYOLU-TEFSIR: The unspecified object of reading includes revelation, cosmic signs, and everything to study and understand for lessons and useful results; knowing the Creator is knowledge and religion's chief aim.
  96:1/q02/p5 The command initiates reading without specifying an object. — reasons: IBNASHUR: The object of “read” is omitted either because the verb is used intransitively to mean begin reading, or because context makes clear that the object is what will be revealed of the Qur'an. Against—TABATABAI: The command does not simply use read intransitively with its object omitted, nor does it mean ‘recite to people’ with an omitted complement.; cites 17:106
96:1/q03 How could the unlettered Prophet obey the command to read? [ٱقْرَأْ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the command prepares him for dictation, miraculously enables recitation, or concerns visible writing.)
  96:1/q03/p1 Gabriel's repeated command prepares him to receive and recite the words about to be dictated. — reasons: Qushayrī reports Muhammad twice saying he was not a reader; Jishumī reports his asking what he should read before Gabriel supplied the words. ELMALILI: At Hira the angel first commanded only “read” twice; because Muhammad had not read before, he answered that he was not a reader, so these were preparatory prompts to begin recitation. ELMALILI: The third command began the Qur'an and simultaneously miraculously made the unreading Prophet able to read, fixed the text to be recited, and taught him to introduce the Creator, as related to 29:48 and 42:52.; cites 29:48, 42:52 IBNASHUR: The imperative is literal, asking for reading now or in the near future; the Prophet is to say what will shortly be dictated, since no text had yet been dictated or given to him. IBNASHUR: The command resembles a teacher telling a student to write before dictating: it prepares the student for the text that is about to be supplied. IBNASHUR (عائشة): Aisha's report in the two Sahih collections describes Gabriel pressing the Prophet three times; each time he answered that he could not read, and at the third command Gabriel recited the opening verses through “what he did not know.” IBNASHUR: Aisha's wording that Muhammad returned bearing the revelation and Khadija asked Waraqa to hear him shows he understood and recited the verses after the third command; Gabriel's repetitions familiarized him with an unlearned act. IBNASHUR-FULL: The imperative asks for immediate or near-future recitation of words about to be dictated, since no text had previously been read or handed to him. IBNASHUR-FULL (عائشة): Aisha narrates in the two Sahihs that Gabriel came to Muhammad in Hira, commanded him three times, embraced him forcefully each time, then recited the opening through 'what he did not know.' IBNASHUR-FULL: Aisha's narration is directly from the Prophet; her account that he returned trembling with the passage means he retained and recited what had been dictated. IBNASHUR-FULL: Khadija's request that Waraqa hear him further indicates Muhammad recited the revealed passage immediately; Gabriel's repeated command repeated God's wording to accustom him to reading.
  96:1/q03/p2 God enabled the unlettered Prophet to retain, recite, and teach the revelation without prior reading or writing. — reasons: ELMALILI: A book need not already be written to be a book, and reading does not require writing; the fullest recitation is from memory. ELMALILI: The Prophet's own recitation was the fullest kind, from memory by divine instruction, whether for himself, prayer, teaching others, or dictation. ELMALILI: The command miraculously gave the unlettered Prophet a book to recite, teach, and dictate without writing himself; beginning with the basmala accords with God's lordship. ELMALILI: The attachment to “your Lord” explains the command through God's role as creator and caretaker, leaving no doubt that He can teach an unlettered recipient. ELMALILI: The Prophet needed no writing to recite, while his unlettered reading establishes prophethood and the Qur'an's divine origin; 29:48 states this explicitly.; cites 29:48 ELMALILI: The Prophet never wrote with his own hand and needed no writing to retain revelation because God promised he would not forget in 87:6; he recited and dictated the revelation to scribes for the community.; cites 87:6 ELMALILI: The third command began the Qur'an and simultaneously miraculously made the unreading Prophet able to read, fixed the text to be recited, and taught him to introduce the Creator, as related to 29:48 and 42:52.; cites 29:48, 42:52 IBNASHUR: The surah teaches Muhammad the Qur'anic speech and its recitation despite his prior lack of recitation; it signals that God can directly teach as He enabled people to learn writing. IBNASHUR: Opening with “Read” foretells that the Messenger will recite a book despite having recited no book before the Qur'an, as 29:48 states; he told Gabriel, “I am not a reader.”; cites 29:48 IBNASHUR-FULL: The surah teaches Muhammad to recite Qur'anic speech though he did not previously know recitation, and suggests that God can directly enable knowledge as He enabled writing. IBNASHUR-FULL: Opening with 'Recite' announces that the Messenger will become a reciter of scripture although he had not recited scripture before revelation; it is a fitting opening for the Qur'an.; cites 29:48 IBNASHUR-FULL: The initial command and reference to pen teaching imply that Muhammad remained unlettered, an attribute completing the Qur'an's miracle, as another verse states.; cites 29:48 BINTSHATI: The opening command to recite is striking because it begins revelation to an unlettered Prophet among an unlettered, pagan Bedouin people, with the Book as his miracle.
  96:1/q03/p3 The repeated command was initially unspecified and clarified by the subsequent revealed words. — reasons: ALUSI (الآمدي): Al-Āmidī uses the repeated command and question in the beginning-of-revelation report as evidence that clarification may be delayed: Gabriel says read three times before specifying what to read. ALUSI-FULL (الآمدي): Al-Amidi cites the repeated command Read followed by this passage as an example of clarification delayed until after the original command; al-Alusi accepts the passage as the clarification but rejects making the whole passage or basmala the object of the earlier command. TABATABAI: The apparent sense of iqraʾ is to receive and read what the angel reveals; the command is embedded within the revealed book, like an instruction at a book’s opening.
96:1/q04 How is the imperative iqraʾ parsed? [ٱقْرَأْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether its ending is a jussive with an omitted particle or an imperative form.)
  96:1/q04/p1 A jussive verb with an omitted imperative lām. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās attributes this to the Kufans, with al-Farrāʾ supplying an unspoken lām.
  96:1/q04/p2 An imperative with a fixed ending, rather than jussive inflection. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās attributes the contrary account to Basrans, who say the imperative is fixed because it does not resemble nouns.
96:1/q05 How is iqraʾ pronounced in transmitted readings? [ٱقْرَأْ · readings] (turns on: The hamza's retention, lightening, substitution, or loss.)
  96:1/q05/p1 A transmitted reading lightens or drops the hamza. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī reports Abū Jaʿfar's light hamza; Abū Ḥayyān records al-Aʿshāʾ via Abū Bakr from ʿĀṣim dropping it, explained from the imperfect verb. Al-Samīn adds a poetic parallel. QIRAAT-ER: In pause, Ibn Kathīr and others drop hamza, giving a long final a; this canonical reading softens the command phonetically.
  96:1/q05/p2 The common reading retains a quiescent hamza. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn identify the majority reading as retaining a quiescent hamza.
  96:1/q05/p3 Hamza is substituted according to a reported pronunciation. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās reports Abū Zayd and al-Kisāʾī and cites Zuhayr's poetic yebdaʾ; a comparable explanation is offered at 2:61.
96:1/q06 Why does 'Read' precede 'in your Lord's name'? [ٱقْرَأْ بِٱسْمِ رَبِّكَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the command is foregrounded or the prepositional phrase belongs to the later repetition.)
  96:1/q06/p1 The verb comes first to emphasize the urgent, initial command to read. — reasons: Al-Burhān gives the urgency of the act in the first revealed sūra as reason to put the verb first. BURHAN: Two explanations are offered for placing the verb before “in the name of your Lord”: the act is foremost because this was the first revealed sūrah, or the phrase attaches to the second “recite.”; cites 96:1 BIQAI: The verb is placed before its prepositional phrase for greater impact, especially as this was the first revelation and reading was the urgent command; its object is omitted to indicate that the reading commanded is the Qurʾān, comprehensive of all good. BIQAI-FULL: The active verb is placed before its prepositional phrase because this is more forceful, especially in the first revelation, where the command to read is paramount. ALUSI: Al-Ālūsī prefers attaching “in your Lord’s name” to the adjacent verb; placing the verb first suits this opening surah, where recitation is especially important. ALUSI-FULL: The fronted command suits the importance of reading in the first surah revealed; even if another passage preceded it, the sense is read beginning with God's name, so other openings would not comply, while moving the name first changes the focus.
  96:1/q06/p2 The prepositional phrase can be construed with the second 'Read'. — reasons: BURHAN: Two explanations are offered for placing the verb before “in the name of your Lord”: the act is foremost because this was the first revealed sūrah, or the phrase attaches to the second “recite.”; cites 96:1 ALUSI (بعضهم): Another analysis leaves “in God’s name” with its customary attachment, treats recite as initiating the reading, and attaches “your Lord” to the second command.
