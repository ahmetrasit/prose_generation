## THE CURRENT MAP
2:31/q01 How does ‘taught Adam the names’ work grammatically? [وَعَلَّمَ ءَادَمَ ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the verb is active or passive and how its causative form governs objects.)
  2:31/q01/p1 A passive reading makes Adam the nominative subject standing for the suppressed agent. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān ascribe this to al-Yamānī and Yazīd; Ibn Jinnī reports Yazīd al-Barbarī and explains Adam's prominence as an effect of object foregrounding.
  2:31/q01/p2 In the active reading, God is subject, Adam the first object, and the names the second object of the causative verb. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās calls this the grammarians' agreement. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn explain the doubled verb as a causative from one-object ʿalima; al-Ḥarīrī's proposed third object is rejected.
2:31/q02 How did God teach Adam? [وَعَلَّمَ ءَادَمَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether knowledge was created directly, given through speech or an angel, or bestowed as a capacity for gradual naming.)
  2:31/q02/p1 God directly implanted or inspired necessary knowledge in Adam. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī says God created it in Adam's heart; al-Qurṭubī prefers necessary inspiration. Al-Bayḍāwī allows direct casting into awareness; al-Ṭabrisī calls it an extraordinary opening of heart and tongue.
  2:31/q02/p2 God showed Adam each thing and taught its name, purpose, benefit, and harm. — reasons: Al-Jishumī describes presentation of individual referents in every language; al-Nasafī mentions horse and camel. Ibn ʿAṭiyya records both physical showing and verbal description.
  2:31/q02/p3 God taught through an angel or the angels' language. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Ṭabrisī report angelic speech or teaching of an angelic language. Al-Ālūsī doubts the intermediary explanation without a sound report.
  2:31/q02/p4 God endowed Adam with a faculty to develop names and language gradually. — reasons: Elmalılı says an innate human naming power unfolded under nurture, perhaps before or with the spirit. Ibn ʿĀshūr says human linguistic capacity is divinely inspired regardless of how many words were taught.
  2:31/q02/p5 God taught Adam by direct speech. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān finds direct instruction most apparent; Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports divine speech before earthly descent while preserving Moses's distinction.
2:31/q03 What is the origin and grammatical status of Adam's name? [ءَادَمَ · meaning] (turns on: Arabic derivations from earth or complexion compete with a foreign-name analysis; these yield different declension explanations.)
  2:31/q03/p1 Adam is named for the earth's surface from which he was made. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and others connect adīm with visible surface soil; reports of a handful from all earth explain the descendants' variety. Some grammarians treat the derivative as declinable when indefinite; al-Ṭabarī calls it a verbal proper name and diptote.
  2:31/q03/p2 Adam derives from adama, a complexion term, with reports varying between dark or reddish and white. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ says adama differs between people and camels or gazelles; Dhū al-Rumma and al-Aʿshā are cited for color usage. Al-Naḍr says white, al-Ḍaḥḥāk dark brown. This account can make the name diptote even when indefinite.
  2:31/q03/p3 Adam is a foreign proper name; Arabic etymologies are secondary or morphologically unsound. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn and others point to properness plus foreignness for diptote status. Al-Ālūsī allows Arabic derivation only after Arabization; Elmalılı cites Hebrew, Syriac and other forms while preferring an original first-language name.
  2:31/q03/p4 Adam is a proper name on the Arabic afʿal pattern, with disagreement about declining it when indefinite. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās records consensus on definite Adam; Khalīl and Sībawayh keep it diptote when indefinite, al-Akhfash declines it, and Abū Isḥāq favors Sībawayh. The plural varies with adjectival versus proper use.
  2:31/q03/p5 Other accounts derive Adam from affinity, model, Hebrew or Syriac soil, or identify him with Kayumers. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī calls affinity strained; al-Samīn lists a Hebrew soil derivation and al-Ṭabarī's quadriliteral verb. Elmalılı reports the Kuyumers identification but notes disagreement.
2:31/q04 Who is Adam in the account? [ءَادَمَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the named individual also represents the human race.)
  2:31/q04/p1 Adam is the first human and representative of all humanity, including women and men. — reasons: Asad cites the angels' reference to future corruption and 7:11; Elmalılı treats Adam's original faculties as inherited by the species. Study Quran notes that the Qur'an does not explain the transition from primordial Adam to the first couple.
  2:31/q04/p2 Adam bears the kunyas Abū Muḥammad and Abū al-Bashar. — reasons: Al-Baghawī and al-Suhaylī report these; al-Suhaylī connects Abū Muḥammad with the final prophet and distinguishes Paradise from earth.
2:31/q05 Which names did Adam learn? [ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ كُلَّهَا · referent] (turns on: The scope of 'all' and whether the masculine display pronoun narrows the named beings.)
  2:31/q05/p1 The names of every created thing, great and small, in all kinds and times. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, Qatāda and many others mention ordinary vessels, animals, landforms and even bodily terms. Al-Jishumī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr and al-Ālūsī favor breadth from 'all'; the intercession report has Adam taught every name. Reports vary on inclusion of future beings and crafts.
  2:31/q05/p2 Only the names of the angels. — reasons: Attributed to al-Rabīʿ and other early transmitters; the reports vary in his exact identity.
  2:31/q05/p3 Only the names of Adam's descendants. — reasons: Ibn Zayd says the descendants were taken from Adam's back and displayed; Elmalılı notes the pronoun's rational plural and suggests seedlike or intellectual forms.
  2:31/q05/p4 Names of the creatures and things of earth, especially animals and birds. — reasons: Muqātil individually names horse, mule, donkey; Ibn Qutayba and al-Kalbī limit scope to earth. Ibn ʿĀshūr restricts initial vocabulary to concrete things needed in humanity's first world.
  2:31/q05/p5 Names of Adam's descendants and the angels together. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī prefers this because ʿaraḍahum ordinarily denotes rational persons. Later authors transmit his view; al-Rabīʿ is also reported with this combination.
  2:31/q05/p6 God's own names, or a hidden divine name by which Adam learned all names. — reasons: Al-Jurayrī speaks of a hidden name; al-Ḥakīm al-Tirmidhī of divine names. Al-Qushayrī and Bursevī include both divine and creaturely names, arguing that divine names imply still greater honor.
  2:31/q05/p7 The making of every kind of thing, including crafts. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī and al-Baghawī report the narrower craft explanation. Jishumī, Ṭabrisī and later authors include tools, cultivation, medicine, mining and life-sustaining skills within the broad reading.
  2:31/q05/p8 Only stars' names. — reasons: Reported from Ḥumayd al-Shāmī; Abū Ḥayyān also reports an attribution to Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid and Qatāda.
  2:31/q05/p9 Names of broad kinds rather than each individual or later subtype. — reasons: ʿIkrima gives human, angel, jinn and bird; another report names mountains, horses and valleys, with later descendants assigning other labels. Al-Zajjāj's linguists call them genera.
  2:31/q05/p10 A word or power of naming that unlocks every name. — reasons: Al-Naqqāsh reports one word from Ibn ʿAbbās; Abū Ḥayyān reports the majority's act-of-naming reading; Kur'an Yolu describes a capacity to invent language.
  2:31/q05/p11 The hidden living realities preserved with God, of which worldly names are descended signs. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī argues from the living plural referents and 15:21's imperishable treasuries; their plurality is of ranks, not separate individuals. Verbal labels cannot account for Adam's distinction over angels.
  2:31/q05/p12 Adam was taught everything, even advanced grammar such as Sībawayh's. — reasons: Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī is reported with this extreme claim; Ibn ʿAṭiyya explicitly calls it wrong on several grounds.
2:31/q06 What does ‘names’ mean here: labels, referents, or realities? [ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether ism denotes uttered labels, the things named, or properties that reveal what a thing is.)
  2:31/q06/p1 The names are the meanings, attributes, properties and uses of things, with labels as signs. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj's theorists say knowing a horse's use is more informative than uttering 'horse'; al-Māwardī, Jishumī and Ṭabrisī say bare labels confer no benefit. Rāzī reports arguments from sima and sumu and Adam's superiority, though al-Shihāb objects that properties are not called names.
  2:31/q06/p2 The names are expressions or labels that denote things. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya prefers this because teaching and the later request concern names. Al-Qurṭubī cites common usage and 87:1, 55:78, 53:23; al-Nasafī rejects teaching objects in place of labels. Al-Bayḍāwī distinguishes ordinary from grammatical usage.
  2:31/q06/p3 Adam learned both the labels and what they signify. — reasons: Al-Māwardī gives both as an interpretation because names without meanings are useless. Al-Ṭabrisī calls it stronger; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī distinguishes Adam's direct essential knowledge from angels' later verbal information.
