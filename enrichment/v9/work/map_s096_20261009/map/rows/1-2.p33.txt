[QUTB-ZILAL:1:7/r2] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · رسول الله | reports | Abu Hurayra reports in Sahih Muslim that God calls al-Fatiha a prayer divided between Himself and His servant: praise, mercy, and glorification are the Lord's; worship and aid are shared; guidance and the servant's request belong to the servant, who receives what he asks.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r1] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Important unfamiliar teaching should be preceded by preparation that removes resistance and mental obstacles, so hearers can benefit from guidance.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r10] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Al-Fatiha serves as the Quran's proem or a sermon introduction; this literary arrangement aids understanding and attentive reception.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r11] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | A preface should be concise so listeners are not tired by waiting; the shortness of Al-Fatiha explains its placement before the longer surahs.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r12] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | A good opening hints at its subject, preparing receptive hearers and allowing advanced hearers to assess or complete the argument; this displays the speaker's command of it.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r13] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | A preface should use concise, comprehensive expression, a principle discussed by rhetoricians for places requiring careful composition.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r14] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | A fourth rule for a preface is to begin with praise of God.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r15] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The surah's preparation rejects atheism and materialism, polytheism, stubbornness, and errors corrupting sound knowledge or true law; this is why it is called the Mother of the Quran. | mentions 1:4, 1:5, 1:6, 1:7
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r16] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Arabic dignitary address begins with praise before a request; Umayya ibn Abi al-Salt says that praise of the addressee can itself satisfy the petitioner.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r17] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · أبو هريرة عن النبيء صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | A report from Abu Hurayra, attributed to the Prophet, says an important matter not begun with praise of God is cut off; Ibn Ashur gives no grading.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r18] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Muslims consequently called valuable speech lacking opening praise abtar; Ziyad ibn Abi Sufyan's Basran sermon was nicknamed al-Batra for this omission.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r19] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Hamd is praise of a beautiful quality, whether an intentional action such as generosity or aiding the distressed, or another quality such as courage.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r20] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Thanā is the broader category of favorable mention and is not the opposite of hamd; some anomalously use it for mention regardless of praise or blame.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r21] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · ابن القطاع | reports | Ibn al-Qatta is associated with the anomalous view that thanā can mean mention generally, including neutral mention; Ibn Ashur says a hadith wording misled him.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r22] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Calling blameworthy mention thanā in a hadith is figurative word matching that indirectly urges people either to speak well of Muslims or refrain.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r23] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Nithā, with initial n, applies to praise or blame and is more commonly used for blame.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r24] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · الجمهور | reports | The majority distinguish madh from hamd by making madh broader: it can praise voluntary or nonvoluntary qualities.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r25] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · صاحب الكشاف | reports | Al-Zamakhshari calls hamd and madh equivalent; a proposed reading takes his word 'brothers' to mean derivational relatives like jazb and jadhb.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r26] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Ibn Ashur rejects the derivational-sibling reading: the commentators' synonym reading fits al-Zamakhshari's gloss in al-Fa'iq and his treating blame as hamd's opposite.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r27] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · السيد الشريف | reports | Al-Sayyid al-Sharif, apparently also Sa'd al-Din, reads the synonyms as retaining the voluntary-quality restriction, citing al-Zamakhshari on 49:7. | mentions 49:7
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r28] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · عبد الحكيم السلكوتي | reports | Abd al-Hakim al-Salkuti takes synonymy to cancel the voluntary-quality restriction for both words, presenting this as a possible or transmitted reading rather than a preference.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r29] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | To allow praise of God's essential attributes under the majority's definition, scholars offer three replies: the attributes count as voluntary by their perfection, by their effects, or because God acts freely even when the praised attribute is not voluntary.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r30] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | prefers | Ibn Ashur answers that nonvoluntariness is a defect in human qualities but perfection in God's eternal, necessary attributes; no analogy to human choice is needed, as with God's having no child.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r31] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Hamd is the strongest language available to people for praising God, though it cannot fully convey realities beyond ordinary human experience.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r32] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Al-hamdu is nominative as the subject in every transmitted reading, and li-llah is its predicate, related to the general existential predicate.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r33] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · سيبويه | reports | Sibawayhi explains that praise expressions originally use an omitted verb in the absolute accusative, as in 'we praise God with praise'; the verbal noun replaces the verb.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r34] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Sibawayhi distinguishes supplicatory accusative sources from declarative ones such as hamdan and shukran, whose omitted verbs mean 'I praise' or 'I thank'.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r35] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The cited grammar says a definite source such as al-hamdu can be raised as a subject because it is declarative and definite; the sense of the omitted verb remains.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r36] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · صاحب الكشاف | reports | Al-Zamakhshari also describes hamd's original accusative with an omitted verb and its shift to nominative as a means of indicating stability.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r37] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The nominative makes a nominal sentence for permanence, the generic article broadens it, and fronting highlights it; the accusative construction cannot convey all three effects.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r38] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · سيبويه | reports | A reading with al-hamda in the accusative and definite article is a Tamimi dialect form attested by Sibawayhi; its implied verb limits praise to the speaker or intended group.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r39] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Raising hamd announces praise from the speaker and all creation, whereas the accusative announces only the speaker's praise.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r40] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Although anomalous, the accusative reading documents an Arabic stage in this familiar construction, showing that some speakers retained its absolute-object origin even with the article.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r41] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The nominative is more eloquent than the accusative, and definite accusative is more expressive than indefinite accusative; raising marks permanence and stability.
