## THE CURRENT MAP
9:103/q01 What occasion prompted the command to take charity? [خُذْ صَدَقَةًۭ · reports] (turns on: Whether the command addresses the repentant stay-behinds, Abū Lubāba alone, or a different alms episode.)
  9:103/q01/p1 Repentant men who stayed behind offered their wealth; the Prophet awaited revelation, then accepted charity. — reasons: Muqātil says three men brought the wealth that held them back. Al-Farrāʾ, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḍaḥḥāk, Zayd, and later exegetes connect the offer to release from mosque pillars and 9:102; some report he took a third. Ibn ʿĀshūr relates their gift to repairing Tabūk losses. Al-Biqāʿī calls it confirmation of accepted repentance. Qatāda confirms the Tabūk group. Elmalılı allows that the Prophet's initial refusal concerned earlier refusal of alms from people such as Ḥāṭib ibn Saʿlaba.
  9:103/q01/p2 The command arose from Abū Lubāba's repentance over warning Banū Qurayẓa by pointing to his throat. — reasons: Mujāhid and al-Bāqir identify him alone; reports describe his binding himself to a pillar and fasting until forgiven. Al-Zuhrī instead connects his binding and seven days of abstention to missing Tabūk.
  9:103/q01/p3 A report connects the verse to Thaʿlaba's refusal to pay alms, contrasted with a man of Sulaym who willingly gave good camels. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī reports Thaʿlaba called the collectors' demand a tax.
9:103/q02 Who is to collect or distribute the charity? [خُذْ · law] (turns on: Whether the imperative is a continuing office, a Prophetic privilege, or permits direct distribution.)
  9:103/q02/p1 The imam or later authority collects and administers it. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Wāḥidī, al-Qurṭubī, Elmalılı, and Asad derive an official role from the imperative. Ibn al-ʿArabī explains that address to the Prophet can be shared in practice with the community, citing prayer and divorce commands. Elmalılı connects the collectors to 9:60 and says the Prophet collected as imam, not for himself.
  9:103/q02/p2 Only the Prophet could receive these alms; the obligation ended with him. — reasons: Post-Prophetic refusers argued that only his prayer could purify them; al-Qurṭubī preserves their poem questioning Abū Bakr. Abū Bakr vowed to fight those separating prayer from zakat; Ibn al-ʿArabī and al-Rāzī reject the claim, citing the poor's independent right in 9:60 and 51:19.
  9:103/q02/p3 Eligible people may sometimes pay zakat directly to the poor. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī ties direct payment to individual authority, though a ruler may forbid it for public interest. Elmalılı says ʿUthmān ended the imam's collection right for hidden assets and permitted direct payment; collectors could not inspect protected homes and shops, but collected visible trade assets.
9:103/q03 May officials compel payment, and does payment to an unjust collector discharge it? [خُذْ · law] (turns on: Whether collection power includes force, and whether intention suffices under unjust collection.)
  9:103/q03/p1 The imam can forcibly collect from a refuser. — reasons: Al-Ikhtiyār cites the verse for collection and placement where due; Abū Bakr fought withholders, swearing even an ʿiqāl or ʿanāq formerly paid must be given.
  9:103/q03/p2 Officials should not seize zakat by force; the owner must pay personally. — reasons: Al-Ashbāh reports this as the relied-upon school position. Al-Muḥīṭ says forceful seizure without consent does not discharge the obligation and imprisonment may compel payment.
  9:103/q03/p3 Payment to an unjust official can discharge the duty if made with charitable intent. — reasons: Al-Mabsūṭ applies its sounder view to charity, tithes, jizya, kharāj, levies, and confiscations, while another view advises repeating payment if the official knew its nature.
9:103/q04 What does 'take' mean here? [خُذْ · meaning]
  9:103/q04/p1 It means accept the offered charity. — reasons: Al-Dāmghānī lists covenant, judgment, alms and pardon as senses of acceptance, while a cited passage denies acceptance of ransom. The Prophet's delay until revelation shows the issue was authorization to accept.
9:103/q05 How much of their wealth was taken or required? [مِنْ أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ · law] (turns on: Whether 'from' and the occasion establish a third, or external zakat specifications govern the portion.)
  9:103/q05/p1 The Prophet took one third and left two thirds. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī and later reporters infer this from 'from their wealth,' not all wealth. Al-Zuhrī says the Prophet told Abū Lubāba one third sufficed for his vow; Mālik likewise says one third fulfills a vow to give all one's property. Al-Rāzī accepts the report while still preferring a zakat reading.
  9:103/q05/p2 The command concerns a known, previously specified due portion rather than an arbitrary amount. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says a single grain cannot be the intended minimum. Al-Qurṭubī says Sunnah and consensus specify thresholds and rates. Al-Shāfiʿī requires payment in the specified kinds and quantities rather than monetary value.
  9:103/q05/p3 The wording alone leaves the amount open, and even a small voluntary amount can meet it. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says this on the legal reading of the broad wording.
9:103/q06 How does 'from their wealth' work grammatically? [مِنْ أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ · grammar] (turns on: Its attachment to the verb or to the following indefinite charity.)
  9:103/q06/p1 Min is partitive and the phrase attaches to 'take'. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls this strongest; al-Ṭabrisī says all wealth need not be given. Al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī allow attachment to the verb. Reported one-third collections illustrate partitivity.
  9:103/q06/p2 The phrase modifies 'charity' as an advanced circumstantial expression. — reasons: Al-Samīn says it was originally an adjective of ṣadaqa and advanced; al-Ālūsī allows an omitted circumstantial modifier.
9:103/q07 How many penitents were involved, and who were they? [أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ · reports] (turns on: The reports count different groups and distinguish the pardoned penitents from the deferred three.)
  9:103/q07/p1 Seven penitents were involved, four identified as Jadd ibn Qays, Abū Lubāba, Ḥarām, and Aws. — reasons: Al-Ḍaḥḥāk and Qatāda give seven; Qatāda's four had promised striving, spending, and charity. Saʿīd's separate report describes illness and blindness among three.
  9:103/q07/p2 The penitents were distinct from the three whose case was deferred. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan and Qatāda say so; all count traditions cited by al-Baghawī include Abū Lubāba.
  9:103/q07/p3 Ten men delayed, seven tied themselves to pillars and three were deferred. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās's report in al-Durr links the seven's pardon to 9:102 and the three's later case to 9:117; another Ibn ʿAbbās report in al-Baghawī counts ten including Abū Lubāba.
  9:103/q07/p4 Eight penitents were involved. — reasons: Al-Baghawī attributes the count to Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and Zayd ibn Aslam.
  9:103/q07/p5 Five penitents were involved, including Abū Lubāba. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās through ʿAṭiyya gives this count.
9:103/q08 Whom does 'their' refer to, and how general is the command? [أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ · referent] (turns on: Whether the historical occasion limits the pronoun or the wording yields a general collection rule.)
  9:103/q08/p1 It first refers to the penitents who confessed, but the command or ruling extends to Muslims who owe charity. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī identifies those confessing sin. Al-Wāḥidī distinguishes occasion from general law; Ibn Kathīr rejects confinement to them. Al-Ālūsī distinguishes the expiation and zakat readings. Asad applies it to later authorities accepting repentant Muslims' zakat.
  9:103/q08/p2 It refers generally to owners of zakatable wealth. — reasons: Jurists and the majority read a new zakat command without a previously named group; al-Ālūsī says the pronoun can have this reference on that reading.
  9:103/q08/p3 It concerns the specific repentant confessors and their property. — reasons: Some commentators limit the pronoun to 9:102; al-Ṭabarī's account is specific. Ibn ʿĀshūr says another group of confessors; the report of charity specific to their wealth is judged weak by Ṭabāṭabāʾī.
9:103/q09 What does 'wealth' mean here? [أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ · meaning] (turns on: The notes distinguish ordinary inclusive ownership from narrower regional or technical usages.)
  9:103/q09/p1 It includes owned property generally, in multiple kinds. — reasons: Al-Jishumī gives māl's derivation from acquisition and increase and contrasts singular uniform wealth with plural varied assets. Abū ʿUmar appeals to ordinary Arabic and a Prophetic saying; Abū Qatāda called a garden acquired for armor his first wealth. Some of Daws called clothing and goods al-amwāl, as Abū Hurayra's Khaybar report illustrates.
  9:103/q09/p2 It denotes coined gold and silver. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī reports this usage from others.
  9:103/q09/p3 It means camels specifically. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī cites the saying that wealth is camels.
  9:103/q09/p4 It denotes livestock generally. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī reports this usage.
  9:103/q09/p5 Gold or silver below the zakat threshold is not called wealth. — reasons: Thaʿlab cites a poem about never attaining the threshold.
9:103/q10 What kinds of property may the command cover? [أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ · law] (turns on: Whether wealth is taken in its broad owned-property sense or specified by zakat law and intention.)
