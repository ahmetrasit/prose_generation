## THE CURRENT MAP
53:39/q01 How is وَأَن parsed and linked? [وَأَن · grammar] (turns on: Which earlier construction governs the conjunction.)
  53:39/q01/p1 It continues the preceding clauses introduced by an, as part of the scriptures' reported contents. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās and al-Wāḥidī coordinate it with the previous clause. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the majority read fatḥa, while Abū al-Sammāl reads kasra from v. 42. Al-Samīn permits genitive, nominative, or accusative coordination and says lightened an is not separated from nonconjugating laysa. Bursevī gives li-l-insān as predicate and mā saʿā as subject; al-Ālūsī follows the preceding wa-anna.
  53:39/q01/p2 It may coordinate with the earlier prepositional phrase or with the earlier allā clause. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says these produce reduced inna and explanatory an respectively; both reinforce 53:38.
53:39/q02 What does لِلْإِنسَٰنِ mean grammatically? [لِلْإِنسَٰنِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether lām signifies entitlement or is read as ʿalā.)
  53:39/q02/p1 The lām marks a person's due, possession, or particular entitlement. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya makes strict entitlement the pivot; al-Qurṭubī says ownership or obligation; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī distinguishes lasting true ownership from possessions lost at death. Elmalılı says ikhtiṣāṣ and prefers verbal-noun mā because sought objects need not be obtained. Al-Rāzī contrasts lām for benefit with ʿalā for liability.
  53:39/q02/p2 The lām can mean ʿalā: what is upon or owed by a person. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī reports this parse and Ibn ʿĀshūr lists it among harmonizations. Al-Ālūsī rejects it as contrary to apparent wording and the warning against the one who turned away and gave little.
53:39/q03 Who does الْإِنسَٰنِ include? [الْإِنسَٰنِ · referent] (turns on: Whether a generic human noun has universal or restricted scope.)
  53:39/q03/p1 It is generic and includes believers and unbelievers. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says a generic noun under negation is universal; Abū Ḥayyān calls this apparent. Al-Rāzī maintains it despite objections concerning kin's charity, fasting and prayer and tenfold reward; he calls the restricted reading weak.
  53:39/q03/p2 It means the unbeliever; believers may also receive others' efforts. — reasons: Al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas is repeatedly cited. Al-Rāzī objects that restricting the noun is weak.
53:39/q04 What does سَعَىٰ mean? [سَعَىٰ · meaning] (turns on: Whether saʿy is work, intention, causal contribution, or relinquished effort.)
  53:39/q04/p1 It denotes one's work, deeds, or earning. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī says deeds; al-Baghawī cites 92:4. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says earning. Bursevī and al-Rāghib define literal brisk walking slower than running, extended to earnest effort in good or evil; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the extension metaphor or metonymy. Al-Rāzī says seeking an object would normally take fīhi.
  53:39/q04/p2 It means what the person intends. — reasons: Al-Warrāq cites resurrection according to intention. Ibn ʿAṭāʾ distinguishes seeking God's pleasure from seeking reward. Asad cites a widely transmitted intention hadith and includes deliberate omissions and expressed belief in conscious action.
  53:39/q04/p3 One's striving includes one's causal contribution to another's deed. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī names a call to faith; Ibn al-Zāghūnī names kin, child, friend and service to religion; al-Biqāʿī and Islāḥī include ties, teaching, example and financial help.
  53:39/q04/p4 It is the effort one repudiates as one's own. — reasons: Al-Wāsiṭī says a person has only what they disclaim of their striving.
53:39/q05 Why is سَعَىٰ in the past tense? [سَعَىٰ · rhetoric]
  53:39/q05/p1 Past tense urges actual good work; future promises have not yet been earned. — reasons: Al-Rāzī contrasts already performed prayer and charity with unperformed promises, which are not recorded.
53:39/q06 What is مَا in مَا سَعَىٰ? [مَا سَعَىٰ · grammar] (turns on: Whether mā denotes the act or its object.)
  53:39/q06/p1 It is a verbal noun denoting striving itself. — reasons: Bursevī and al-Ālūsī allow this. Elmalılı favors it because not every sought object becomes one's property; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ prefers its broader sense in an echo of this verse.
  53:39/q06/p2 It is a relative pronoun denoting what one strove for. — reasons: Bursevī and al-Ālūsī allow this. Al-Rāzī says the work itself is preserved with God, citing 99:7, or its reward is intended.
53:39/q07 What does the verse say a person receives? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the limit applies to all deeds, a kind of deed, strict entitlement, or material earnings.)
  53:39/q07/p1 One receives recompense for one's own deeds, good for good and evil for evil. — reasons: Muqātil locates it in the Hereafter; Ibn Qutayba and al-Wāḥidī stress preparation for it. Al-Zajjāj and Abū Isḥāq include good and evil; al-Rāzī reports this as well known. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī contrasts enduring deeds with wealth, children and status lost at death.
  53:39/q07/p2 The apparent point concerns benefits from good deeds. — reasons: Al-Rāzī and Ibn ʿĀshūr cite lām for benefit rather than ʿalā for liability. Al-Rāzī says v. 41's full recompense suits good while evil can be matched, reduced or forgiven.
  53:39/q07/p3 The restriction may concern evil deeds specifically. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī cites a sound Muslim report: an unperformed good intention is credited, accomplished good multiplied up to seven hundred, and accomplished evil counted once.
  53:39/q07/p4 Only one's own earned deed is owed; more can come by divine grace. — reasons: Al-Ḥusayn ibn al-Faḍl reconciles the verse with multiplication, citing 2:261 or 2:245; al-Thaʿlabī tells Ibn Ṭāhir's questions about Cain and God's daily act and recounts al-Ḥusayn's reply and the other view of Cain's regret. Ibn ʿAṭiyya names intercession, relatives, multiplication and forgiveness. Al-Qurṭubī cites tenfold to seven-hundredfold and Abū Hurayra's two-millionfold report. Al-Rāzī says even tenfold reward is sought in hoping for God's promise, citing 6:160.
  53:39/q07/p5 Another's gift can benefit someone without becoming that person's earned entitlement. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī compares children admitted to Paradise without deeds of their own. Elmalılı distinguishes true ownership from gifts and commanded cooperation. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says bodily and financial good can benefit both doer and recipient, citing Saʿd's charity for his mother.
  53:39/q07/p6 The verse denies reliance on another's work. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr lists this among harmonizations with reports of others' good deeds.
  53:39/q07/p7 Earnings should be proportional to one's labor. — reasons: Some readers infer material earnings proportionate to labor; Islāḥī rejects this as unrelated to the verse's context.
  53:39/q07/p8 Worldly provision tests gratitude and patience; the Hereafter rewards earned deeds. — reasons: Islāḥī distinguishes the distribution of worldly provision from final reward and punishment; Kur'an Yolu affirms honest labor's value before God and places voluntary choice within a test whose full secrets exceed human knowledge.
53:39/q08 Can another person's deed benefit someone under this verse? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether entitlement, causal connection, proxy intention, or limited scope governs others' deeds.)
  53:39/q08/p1 Another's deed gives no earned reward apart from one's own effort. — reasons: The preceding ban on bearing another's burden is parallel, according to al-Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Ālūsī, Ibn ʿĀshūr and Study Quran. Islāḥī cites 2:134, Noah's son, Abraham's father, Lot's wife and Pharaoh's wife in 66:11 against automatic inherited reward.
  53:39/q08/p2 Another's deed can count as one's own through faith or causal effort. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, al-Nasafī, al-Ālūsī and Ebussuud require faith, good conduct or a causal role. Al-Ālūsī recounts al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil's vow of a hundred camels, Hishām's fifty sacrifices and the Prophet's conditional statement about monotheism. Ibn Kathīr treats endowment, useful teaching and a child's prayer as traces of one's efforts, citing 36:12 and a hadith that a child is one's earning. Islāḥī adds ties of faith, teaching and aid.
  53:39/q08/p3 Another's deed can count through a proxy intending it for the recipient. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī's second answer contrasts self-directed deeds with work for the deceased. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī and al-Ālūsī treat the performer as agent; al-Ālūsī notes figurative usage.
  53:39/q08/p4 Believers can receive others' efforts because the restriction concerns unbelievers. — reasons: Al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas is transmitted by these sources.
  53:39/q08/p5 Muslims can benefit from others because the rule concerned Abraham's and Moses's peoples. — reasons: ʿIkrima's account is cited repeatedly; Ibn ʿĀshūr treats acceptance of another's deed as a special concession.
  53:39/q08/p6 An unbeliever's good is paid in worldly life, leaving none for the Hereafter. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī and Ibn al-Jawzī recount ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy's shirt for al-ʿAbbās and the Prophet's reciprocal shroud.
  53:39/q08/p7 Reports show benefit through charity, pilgrimage, prayer, or continuing deeds. — reasons: They include Saʿd's charity for his mother, a dead father's or sister's Hajj, a child's Hajj, and posthumous prayer. Al-Qurṭubī calls charity undisputed, Bursevī cites many reports, and an unnamed report in al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says even a nāṣib's burden can be lightened by a gifted deed.
