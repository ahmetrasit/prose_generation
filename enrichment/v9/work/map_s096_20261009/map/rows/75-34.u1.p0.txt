## THE CURRENT MAP
75:34/q01 What does awlā laka mean as a warning? [أَوْلَىٰ لَكَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the idiom is heard as a curse, approaching harm, deserved punishment, an elliptical expression, or an ironic predication of his conduct; Iṣlāḥī explicitly disputes the 'befitting' rendering.)
  75:34/q01/p1 A menace or curse: woe to you, may the hated calamity befall you. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba calls it a fixed warning, comparing 47:20; his anonymous poet, al-Khansāʾ, a line addressed to Aws, and a poem cited by Qurtubī repeat awlā as a threat. Al-Zajjāj explains Arab usage as a curse; al-Kashshāf and al-Rāzī call it a prayer for a hated outcome. Al-Ṭabrisī and al-Qurṭubī report the idiomatic woe gloss; al-Burṣawī combines woe with approaching harm. Elmalılı likens it to a prayer of deserved calamity; Iṣlāḥī says wayl expresses anger, reproach, and hatred and cites al-Khansāʾ. The Study Quran records a fourfold woe rendering. Samarrāʾī explains the invocation in the context of hastening sins.
  75:34/q01/p2 Harm or destruction has drawn near to the addressee. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās says the formula addresses someone already ruined or nearly so, and elsewhere describes nearly perishing but escaping. Al-Aṣmaʿī cites poems for nearness, praised by Thaʿlab; al-Jawharī calls this the best explanation. Thaʿlab, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī, al-Jishumī, and al-Itqān connect it with walī and 9:123. Al-Ṭabrisī applies it to Abū Jahl; al-Bayḍāwī says God brings his hated fate close. Al-Ālūsī compares suḥqan and quotes poetry. Al-Burṣawī cites al-Qāmūs. Samarrāʾī says its comparative form signals proximity; Study Quran records both a hated outcome and punishment now nearer. Asad takes the passage to say the human end draws nearer hourly.
  75:34/q01/p3 The addressee is more fittingly entitled to the punishment or destruction he deserves. — reasons: Some scholars in al-Thaʿlabī and al-Baghawī stress merited punishment. Al-Jishumī gives both entitlement and nearness. Al-Qurṭubī reports suitability for punishment; al-Burṣawī's al-Qāmūs has destruction as the more fitting fate. Al-Ālūsī reports a favored reading with an implied Fire. Elmalılı takes woe and ruin as befitting him. Iṣlāḥī expressly disputes this rendering on Arabic usage and context.
  75:34/q01/p4 Blame or condemnation is more fitting for him than leaving it unsaid. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Itqān, and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī describe an omitted completion, lost through frequent idiomatic use until the phrase meant woe.
  75:34/q01/p5 It rebukes him and urges him to stop his conduct. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it an Arabic rebuke and compares 47:20. Al-Biqāʿī says it can urge abandonment of the present behavior for something beneficial.
  75:34/q01/p6 It means separation and ruin in this life and the next, or distance from their good things. — reasons: Al-Qāḍī in al-Rāzī speaks of severed worldly and afterlife affairs; al-Jubbāʾī in al-Ṭabrisī speaks of exclusion from both worlds' goods.
  75:34/q01/p7 His present conduct is 'better for him' ironically: its burden and punishment now belong to him. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī supplies an omitted subject referring to the person's behavior and calls the predication metonymic; he says the reported comparative-Hell analysis comes close but does not fully fit.
  75:34/q01/p8 A separate lexical gloss of al-ala denotes a condition or a funeral bier. — reasons: Al-Māwardī records both senses of al-ala; the note does not explain how either applies to this threat.
75:34/q02 How is awlā formed and construed? [أَوْلَىٰ · grammar] (turns on: Its status as comparative, verb, verb-like noun, or proper name, and which subject or omitted completion its syntax requires; feminine awlāt is cited against a simple comparative, while al-Ālūsī challenges the metathesis and proper-name case.)
  75:34/q02/p1 It comes from the root for nearness, originally a comparative describing approaching ruin. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya derives it from walī; al-Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī, and al-Itqān cite 9:123 for nearness. Al-Ālūsī calls it originally comparative, later used as a threat. Samarrāʾī and his ḥalaqāt account retain the comparative force. Al-Jishumī permits both entitlement and nearness.
  75:34/q02/p2 It is a comparative with an omitted element: Hell or punishment is more fitting for him. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports an implied Fire as subject; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports Hell as the omitted predicate or the addressee as deserving it. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says this approaches but fails to match his own account of conduct as the omitted subject.
  75:34/q02/p3 It is a comparative with the addressee's conduct as its omitted subject. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī makes the conduct the subject and assigns the consequent burden and punishment to it by metonymy.
  75:34/q02/p4 It is a fixed proper name for a threat or ruin, serving as subject with laka as predicate. — reasons: Al-Mahdawī denies that it is a comparative, citing Abū Zayd's feminine awlāt. Al-Qurṭubī calls it indeclinable like Aḥmad. Abū al-Baqāʾ gives faʿlā with an augmentative alif or afʿal, and Abū Zayd's feminine form retains no declension. Al-Itqān notes dispute over its pattern. Abū ʿAlī in al-Ālūsī derives the name for ruin from awīl by metathesis, patterned afʿal; al-Ālūsī objects that wayl is already indeclinable, the yawm aywam analogy is nonstandard, and neither inversion nor a generic proper name is grounded.
  75:34/q02/p5 It is an indeclinable imperative or verb-like noun meaning successive evil is coming. — reasons: Abū al-Baqāʾ and al-Itqān call it an ism fiʿl; laka then explains the address. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī and Samarrāʾī report the same analysis as woe or evil after evil.
  75:34/q02/p6 It is a past verb or optative with destruction understood as its subject. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports al-Aṣmaʿī's verbal analysis with an implicit subject, destruction, and a possibly extra lām. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports the optative 'may destruction approach you.' Al-Naḥḥās also supplies 'destruction' after laka in a nearly perished reading.
  75:34/q02/p7 It is a past imprecative verb with God as subject, bringing near what he hates or destroying him. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī compares an extra lām with 27:72. Al-Burṣawī describes a shift from triliteral to ifʿāl to govern two objects. Al-Ālūsī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī report God's agency; the latter distinguishes extra lām ('God gives you what you hate') from original lām ('God brings you near it') and separately records a wish for uniquely near destruction.
  75:34/q02/p8 It is an inversion of awīl or wayl, yielding a word for woe. — reasons: Abū al-Haytham in al-Thaʿlabī cites another scholar and comparable Arabic inversions. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Itqān, and Elmalılı report the proposal, with al-Khansāʾ's line as evidence in al-Itqān. Abū ʿAlī applies the inversion to a proper-name analysis; al-Ālūsī disputes its grounds.
  75:34/q02/p9 A frequent ellipsis leaves 'blame is more fitting than silence' implicit. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī says the omitted completion can no longer be expressed in the fixed idiom; al-Qurṭubī, al-Itqān, and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī also report this analysis.
75:34/q03 What is the force of the lām in laka? [لَكَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether awlā is a noun, verb-like noun, or verb, and whether the preposition contributes meaning, merely explains the addressee, or intensifies the curse.)
  75:34/q03/p1 It emphasizes the threat. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says the lām strengthens the warning.
  75:34/q03/p2 It is extra in a verbal construction. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī compares 27:72; al-Ālūsī says some take it as extra with destruction the verb's implicit subject; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says extra lām permits 'God gives you what you hate.'
  75:34/q03/p3 It is the predicate of the subject awlā. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī, Abū al-Baqāʾ, and al-Itqān use this with the indeclinable proper-name analysis.
  75:34/q03/p4 It merely clarifies whom the verb-like noun addresses. — reasons: Abū al-Baqāʾ and al-Itqān call it explanatory with the ism fiʿl analysis.
  75:34/q03/p5 It is original to the verbal construction and denotes bringing the addressee near what he hates. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī distinguishes this from the extra-lām reading.
75:34/q04 Is there a transmitted reading relevant to awlā here? [أَوْلَىٰ · readings]
  75:34/q04/p1 Abū ʿAmr opens the listed non-final words on routes that incline verse endings. — reasons: Al-Nashr adds that for Mūsā he uses his customary intermediate vowel where imāla is transmitted; this note does not specify a separate variant for this verse.
75:34/q05 Who receives this warning? [أَوْلَىٰ لَكَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the reported address to Abū Jahl limits the verse or provides an example of the general description; Ibn ʿĀshūr uses the opening of the sūra and the flexibility of occasion language for the broader reading.)
  75:34/q05/p1 Abū Jahl ibn Hishām is its immediate addressee. — reasons: Muqātil and numerous occasion reports place the confrontation with him; Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls this the majority interpretation of the passage. Al-Thaʿlabī and al-Baghawī name him as the threatened man; the report also calls him this community's Pharaoh. Al-Rāzī records al-Qaffāl's enemy as its target.
  75:34/q05/p2 It addresses every disbeliever who shares those traits, with Abū Jahl as an example. — reasons: Elmalılı extends the plainly applicable warning beyond its reported occasion; Ibn ʿĀshūr cites the sūra's opening about resurrection and self-witness (75:3, 75:14) and cautions that early scholars can speak loosely of an occasion; the Study Quran records all swaggering rejecters. Ibn Kathīr calls the addressee a swaggering unbeliever, comparing the taunting threats of 44:49, 77:46, 39:15, and 41:40. Al-Qaffāl allows a new divine warning to unbelievers.
