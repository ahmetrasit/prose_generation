[ALUSI:1:2/r31] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Ḥamd is generally commanded, as in the saying that whoever does not thank people does not thank God, whereas madḥ is not universally praised and flatterers are rebuked.
[ALUSI:1:2/r32] ALUSI, d. 1270 · الزمخشري | reports | Al-Zamakhsharī appears to treat ḥamd and madḥ as synonymous; al-Ālūsī rejects that position and maintains that madḥ can praise what is involuntary.
[ALUSI:1:2/r33] ALUSI, d. 1270 · بعضهم | reports | Some distinguish shukr from ḥamd by reserving shukr for bodily action and ḥamd for speech.
[ALUSI:1:2/r34] ALUSI, d. 1270 · بعضهم | reports | Other definitions reserve shukr for apparent blessings or, alternatively, for inward blessings.
[ALUSI:1:2/r35] ALUSI, d. 1270 · الراغب | reports | Al-Rāghib points to a definition of shukr as verbal expression for a blessing, while the familiar account includes grateful speech, service by the limbs, and belief and love in the heart.
[ALUSI:1:2/r36] ALUSI, d. 1270 · الطيبي | reports | Al-Ṭībī says the technical usage of the legal theorists means worship, which is complete only through speech, bodily acts, and the heart.
[ALUSI:1:2/r37] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Qurʾān 34:13 uses shukr for action, supporting the broader usage of gratitude beyond speech. | mentions 34:13
[ALUSI:1:2/r38] ALUSI, d. 1270 · النواس بن سمعان | reports | Al-Ṭabarānī reports from al-Nawwās b. Samʿān that the Prophet vowed to thank God if his she-camel returned; when it did, he said al-ḥamdu lillāh, and the Companions expected further prayer or fasting.
[ALUSI:1:2/r39] ALUSI, d. 1270 · صاحب التجريد | reports | The author of al-Tajrīd adds a fourth form of gratitude: thanking God by God, since no one but God can give Him His due thanks.
[ALUSI:1:2/r4] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | The act of ḥamd involves five elements: what is praised by, what is praised for, a praiser, a praised one, and an expression indicating the praised quality.
[ALUSI:1:2/r40] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | People generally recognize three modes of shukr; it overlaps with ḥamd in some respects, but ḥamd is its strongest branch because it openly discloses the blessing.
[ALUSI:1:2/r41] ALUSI, d. 1270 · ابن عمر | reports | A report from Ibn ʿUmar calls ḥamd the head of gratitude and says a servant has not thanked God unless he praises Him; al-Ālūsī notes its chain is interrupted.
[ALUSI:1:2/r42] ALUSI, d. 1270 · أنس | reports | Al-Ālūsī cites a supporting report from Anas in which God tells Abraham that praise is the key to gratitude and gratitude ascends to the Lord of the Throne.
[ALUSI:1:2/r43] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Calling ḥamd the head of gratitude suits its status as the most visible and principal form; this also explains why the Qurʾān opens with praise and begins the precious sūra with what is most precious.
[ALUSI:1:2/r44] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Hamd is more elevated as worship than shukr because it need not be prompted by a benefit received, unlike gratitude for a gift.
[ALUSI:1:2/r45] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | An additional rationale is that shukr responds to finite gifts, while ḥamd can praise even for the unbounded benefit of averting harm, which is more urgent than gaining benefit.
[ALUSI:1:2/r46] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | In the common account, ḥamd has a limited means but a general object, whereas shukr has a general means but is directed specifically toward blessings.
[ALUSI:1:2/r47] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Although tasbīḥ commonly precedes taḥmīd, the sūra omits it because praise includes declaring God free of defects while the reverse is not always true; tasbīḥ elsewhere serves a different purpose.
[ALUSI:1:2/r48] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Tasbīḥ signals that God is free of defects, while ḥamd also points to His positive beneficence and the knowledge, power, and sufficiency that make it possible.
[ALUSI:1:2/r49] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Alternatively, every tasbīḥ is a kind of praise, but ḥamd is broader because it includes both negative and positive attributes while tasbīḥ addresses negative attributes.
[ALUSI:1:2/r5] ALUSI, d. 1270 · الإمام الرازي | reports | Al-Rāzī allows the praised quality to be positive or privative, transitive or intransitive, and voluntary or involuntary; al-Dawwānī and others endorse this account.
[ALUSI:1:2/r50] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Al-Ālūsī takes the article in al-ḥamdu as generic: it points to the familiar notion of praise, as in Labīd’s verse describing the camels.
[ALUSI:1:2/r51] ALUSI, d. 1270 · لبيد | reports | Labīd’s line, cited as linguistic evidence, uses al-ʿirāk for the camels pressing together at the watering place.
[ALUSI:1:2/r52] ALUSI, d. 1270 · الزمخشري | reports | Al-Zamakhsharī calls the exhaustive interpretation of al-ḥamdu a mistaken assumption held by many.
[ALUSI:1:2/r53] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | rejects | Some tie the generic reading to the Muʿtazilī account that human actions belong to their agents; al-Ālūsī replies that good acts still depend on God’s enabling and empowering.
[ALUSI:1:2/r54] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | rejects | Al-Ālūsī rejects arguments that the generic article means only the perfect kind or that a verbal noun cannot convey exhaustive scope; context can make either reading exhaustive.
[ALUSI:1:2/r55] ALUSI, d. 1270 · الزمخشري | reports | Al-Zamakhsharī paraphrases the phrase as we praise God with a praise, then treats إياك نعبد as an answer explaining how that praise is performed.
[ALUSI:1:2/r56] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | prefers | Al-Ālūsī prefers an exhaustive reading here: the sūra’s encompassing attributes of lordship, mercy, and sovereignty support all praise belonging to God, and the shift to إياك نعبد marks a new address rather than an explanation.
[ALUSI:1:2/r57] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Al-Zamakhsharī’s statement in 64:1 that sovereignty and praise belong exclusively to God supports a comprehensive reading, though the present context gives further reason to include every instance of praise. | mentions 64:1
[ALUSI:1:2/r58] ALUSI, d. 1270 · أبو العباس المرسي | reports | Al-Mursī says the article is covenantal because God praised Himself eternally on behalf of creatures unable to encompass His praise, supported by the prophetic supplication that He is as He praised Himself.
[ALUSI:1:2/r59] ALUSI, d. 1270 · بعض ساداتنا الصوفية | reports | Some Sufis understand the praise as belonging to God Himself, like sovereignty and creation-command: He is both praiser and praised, and all things are His manifestations. | mentions 7:54
[ALUSI:1:2/r6] ALUSI, d. 1270 · العلامة في شرح التهذيب | reports | A view reported and adopted in Sharḥ al-Tahdhīb requires the quality praised and the ground for praise to be voluntary.
[ALUSI:1:2/r60] ALUSI, d. 1270 · الماتريدي | reports | Al-Māturīdī says God praised Himself in order to teach creatures, and this is fitting because He inherently deserves praise.
[ALUSI:1:2/r61] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | The transmitted and standard reading has al-ḥamdu in the nominative as a subject with lillāhi as its predicate.
[ALUSI:1:2/r62] ALUSI, d. 1270 · الحسن البصري وزيد بن علي | reports | Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and Zayd b. ʿAlī read with the dāl assimilated toward the following lām; Ibrāhīm b. Abla and Bedouins assimilate the reverse way.
[ALUSI:1:2/r63] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | This cross-word assimilation is permissible because the two frequently paired words are treated as if they were one word.
[ALUSI:1:2/r64] ALUSI, d. 1270 · بعضهم | reports | Some prefer Ibrāhīm’s reading because following the first sound is easier than the reverse and because the dāl’s inflection is stronger than the lām’s fixed vowel.
[ALUSI:1:2/r65] ALUSI, d. 1270 · بعضهم | reports | Others prefer al-Ḥasan’s reading because the second sound more commonly follows the first and the uninflected sound should follow the inflected one.
[ALUSI:1:2/r66] ALUSI, d. 1270 · الزمخشري | reports | Al-Zamakhsharī calls Ibrāhīm’s reading أشف, a word that can indicate either greater clarity or greater obscurity.
[ALUSI:1:2/r67] ALUSI, d. 1270 · هارون بن موسى | reports | Hārūn b. Mūsā reads al-ḥamda in the accusative, a construction common among Arabs for a definite verbal noun with an implied verb such as نحمد.
[ALUSI:1:2/r68] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | The implied verb for the accusative is in the first-person plural, matching naʿbudu and nastaʿīn and the humility of worship, rather than a majestic first-person singular.
[ALUSI:1:2/r69] ALUSI, d. 1270 · الغزالي | reports | Al-Ghazālī interprets the congregational prayer report as the worshippers speaking collectively in the first-person plural.
[ALUSI:1:2/r7] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | rejects | The fact that people do not praise stature or a bright face, or that a pearl goes unpraised, cannot by itself prove whether the restriction lies in the praised quality or the ground for praise.
[ALUSI:1:2/r70] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | prefers | The nominative reading is strongest because the nominal sentence, in this context, conveys settled and enduring praise rather than a renewed act of praising.
[ALUSI:1:2/r71] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Grammarians may treat an underlying verbal form as the source construction for verbal nouns, but here the nominative form answers the rhetorical need for an established state.
