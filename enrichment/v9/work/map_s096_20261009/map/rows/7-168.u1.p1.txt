7:168/q12 Who are those 'below that'? [وَمِنْهُمْ دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ · referent] (turns on: Whether ṣalāḥ means faith itself or a higher degree of obedience, and whether the paired communities are geographically identified.)
  7:168/q12/p1 Unbelievers, especially Israelites who rejected Muhammad or remained Jewish. — reasons: Muqātil contrasts believers with unbelievers. Saʿīd identifies nonbelievers in Muhammad; al-Kalbī says disbelievers. Ibn ʿAṭiyya's condition is that ṣalāḥ means faith. Al-Rāzī cites 7:169's call to return to argue they had persisted in Judaism; Abū Ḥayyān prefers unbelievers for the same reason. Al-Samīn and Abū Ḥayyān reject making dūn lexically synonymous with ghayr, while accepting that the lower group is different. Kuranyolu reports the early convert-versus-Jew classification and rejects making it exclusive.
  7:168/q12/p2 Those lower in righteousness, including sinners or disobedient believers as well as possibly unbelievers. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya says if ṣalāḥ means worship and goodness beyond faith, lesser believers fit. Al-Ṭabrisī describes those obeying some commands and violating others before apostasy. Al-Rāzī reports a lesser-righteous objection as closer to the surface wording but rejects it on 7:169. Al-Ālūsī says they believe but sin; Ibn ʿĀshūr sees varying degrees. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Zamakhsharī include unbelievers and sinners; Study Quran identifies Jews who do not follow their law.
  7:168/q12/p3 Jews on this side of the river, as opposed to the righteous community beyond China. — reasons: Al-Kalbī's geographical report in al-Baghawī sets the two communities on opposite sides of the river Udaf.
7:168/q13 What does 'below' mean as a word? [دُونَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether 'other than' is the word's meaning or merely follows from its sense of lower rank.)
  7:168/q13/p1 Originally short of a spatial limit, extended to lower rank. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān derives rank from locative use. Sibawayh describes falling short of a limit, and al-Ṣaffār says specifically short of the next limit, with lower knowledge a metaphor. Al-Zarkashī notes spatial directions, noun and adjective uses, including an adjective meaning bad.
  7:168/q13/p2 It means 'other than' in this context when righteousness means faith. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya's gloss yields unbelievers. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn reject exact lexical synonymy with ghayr but accept that whoever is below a group is necessarily other than it.
7:168/q14 How is 'among them are those below that' parsed and inflected? [وَمِنْهُمْ دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether an omitted noun supplies the subject, whether adverbial form is retained, and whether dūna can function as the predicate here.)
  7:168/q14/p1 A fronted predicate 'among them' and an omitted subject such as 'people' or 'group', described by 'below that'. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī cites 37:164 and 'some of us departed, some stayed' for omitted described nouns. Al-Taftāzānī says the first of two adverbials is predicate and the second subject, with reversal less apt. Al-Ālūsī says omission after min or fī is normal. Ibn ʿĀshūr says dūna can describe an omitted group; al-Ṭabrisī and al-Majmaʿ give the same group analysis.
  7:168/q14/p2 Dūna is underlyingly nominative as subject, but retains an adverbial accusative form. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās and al-Akhfash know no reader who raises it. Al-Ṭabrisī and Abū al-Ḥasan compare 6:94 and 60:3; al-Majmaʿ similarly compares 'between' in 6:94 and another judgement expression, cited as 3:94 and 6:159 in its note. Al-Zarkashī records two dialects of omitted-noun inflection and nominative and accusative readings.
  7:168/q14/p3 Dūna is nominative as the adjective standing in for an omitted noun. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī points to 37:164. Al-Nasafī and Abū Ḥayyān give an omitted people/group analysis. Al-Zarkashī notes that some dialects decline the adjective when its noun is suppressed.
  7:168/q14/p4 Dūna is an adverb or predicate in the construction. — reasons: Abū al-Baqāʾ compares 6:94. Al-Samīn rejects making it a predicate here. Ibn ʿĀshūr alternatively makes min a noun meaning 'some', minhum the subject and dūna the predicate.
7:168/q15 What does 'that' in 'below that' point to? [ذَٰلِكَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the demonstrative points to the quality of righteousness or the group bearing it.)
  7:168/q15/p1 Righteousness itself, with 'people of' understood where needed. — reasons: Al-Samīn says this balances the two groups; al-Ālūsī reports the same alternative.
  7:168/q15/p2 The righteous group, with singular 'that' used for a plural referent. — reasons: Al-Bursawī notes a singular demonstrative can point to dual or plural; al-Samīn says this requires no omitted 'people of'.
7:168/q16 What does 'We tested them' mean and how does it join the earlier clause? [وَبَلَوْنَٰهُم · grammar]
  7:168/q16/p1 It means God tested them and is coordinated with 'We divided them'. — reasons: Al-Jishumī explicitly joins the verbs: God divided them into groups and tested them, while some were righteous and others less so. Al-Wāḥidī says God dealt with them as a tester would.
7:168/q17 What are the good and bad things used in the test? [بِٱلْحَسَنَٰتِ وَٱلسَّيِّـَٔاتِ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the pairs designate experienced worldly conditions, divine recompense, or inward effects of deeds.)
  7:168/q17/p1 Worldly blessings and afflictions, especially prosperity versus hardship, abundance versus drought, and health versus illness. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba adds good and bad fortune; al-Ṭabarī details comfort, provision, austere living, calamities and lost wealth. Ibn ʿAbbās gives al-ḥaṣb for good and tribulation or punishment for bad; Mujāhid gives ease. Al-Dāmghānī specifies plentiful versus scarce rain and cites 7:95 and 30:36; Ibn al-Jawzī cites 7:131 and 7:95. Al-Ṭabrisī mentions harms to life and property. Al-Biqāʿī recalls historical blessings and calamities; Elmalılı adds wealth and encounters with Magi and Romans; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the terms cover what pleases or troubles, citing 7:131, and characterize God's distribution of fortune. Study Quran points to general worldly provision and adversity.
  7:168/q17/p2 Reward and punishment. — reasons: Al-Māwardī includes this as one explanation alongside blessings and afflictions and fertile and barren years.
  7:168/q17/p3 Inwardly, good works can bring self-admiration while misdeeds can bring remorse and repentance. — reasons: The Najmiyya interpretation contrasts Iblis' self-admiration with Adam's repentant return in 7:23.
7:168/q18 What responses do good and bad trials test or prompt? [وَبَلَوْنَٰهُم بِٱلْحَسَنَٰتِ وَٱلسَّيِّـَٔاتِ · interpretation] (turns on: The general purpose of testing and the reported response of this people.)
  7:168/q18/p1 Blessings test gratitude and draw hope for continued or greater good; hardship tests patience and moves people to seek relief and obey. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba says both are tests, as is fitna. Al-Wāḥidī's people of meaning, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabrisī and al-Bursawī explain hope of more or fear of loss and desire for relief. Ibn ʿĀshūr contrasts patience and gratitude with distress and disbelief. Kuranyolu adds fulfilling duties and steadfast worship, with worldly unity and afterlife salvation as benefits; Study Quran cites 21:35 for the universal scope. Al-Bursawī quotes a Persian touchstone poem on tests exposing truth.
  7:168/q18/p2 These Israelites failed both tests, showing neither gratitude in ease nor patience in hardship. — reasons: Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb says they did not endure prosperity; al-Sulamī says they failed both. Kashifi says they were arrogant in blessing and in hardship insulted God as poor and His hand as bound, citing 3:181 and 5:64.
7:168/q19 What return is sought from them? [لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْجِعُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether 'return' presupposes prior obedience, or can mean a first turn toward truth or a return to innate disposition.)
  7:168/q19/p1 Repentance, leaving wrongdoing or unbelief and returning to God, truth and obedience. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan and Ibn ʿAbbās gloss it as repentance. Al-Ṭabarī specifies obedience and repentance from sins; al-Zamakhsharī says cease wrongdoing. Al-Wāḥidī's people of meaning picture a turn from falsehood toward truth. Al-Ṭabrisī adds compliance with God's command, al-Qurṭubī return from unbelief, and Ibn ʿĀshūr return from covenant breaking; he says blessings and hardships or the contrasting groups' fortunes may bring reflection. Elmalılı says some repented and others had not by Muhammad's era; Asad says return to righteousness.
  7:168/q19/p2 Turning toward the right path or returning to an original innate disposition, even if they had not followed the command before. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī compares urging someone in danger to return to the straight path and offers innate disposition as another sense of return.
7:168/q20 How is the hoped-for return worded? [لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْجِعُونَ · rhetoric]
  7:168/q20/p1 The hope is expressed from a human observer's perspective, and the phrase explains the preceding tests. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the wording is from the human observer's standpoint. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it an explanatory resumption after the tests.
