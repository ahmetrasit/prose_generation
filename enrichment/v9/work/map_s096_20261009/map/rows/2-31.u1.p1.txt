2:31/q07 Which languages did Adam learn, and how did languages later differ? [ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ كُلَّهَا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether all historical languages were taught, only an original language was taught, or later people coined or differentiated languages.)
  2:31/q07/p1 Adam learned all languages; his descendants inherited them, dispersed, retained different tongues and forgot the others. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī infers this from 'all'; al-Ṭabrisī allows differentiation after Noah. Al-Qurṭubī prefers Adam as first human speaker of all languages and restricts rival 'first Arabic' reports to later groups. Bursevī reports seven hundred thousand languages, lost and recovered.
  2:31/q07/p2 Adam learned names in his own or one original language; later peoples agreed on further names. — reasons: Al-Māwardī and Ibn ʿAṭiyya report a single language. Ibn ʿĀshūr places concrete first words in humanity's first language, with event nouns, verbs and adjectives developing later.
  2:31/q07/p3 Arabic began with Gabriel, Noah, Ishmael, or Yaʿrub rather than Adam. — reasons: Kaʿb reports Gabriel to Noah and Shem; a Prophetic report calls Ishmael the first clear Arabic speaker at age ten; another gives Yaʿrub. Al-Qurṭubī reconciles these as first speakers in a particular lineage or group.
  2:31/q07/p4 Language development grew from Adam's divinely given capacity to name, think and speak. — reasons: Elmalılı considers naming, knowledge and speech intrinsic to human nature; later linguistic diversity arose from this capacity. Kur'an Yolu gives language invention as a possible reading.
  2:31/q07/p5 Adam learned every language except the address used by God; that one arose by convention. — reasons: Al-Jishumī reports this alternative.
2:31/q08 Does the verse establish who first assigned words to meanings? [وَعَلَّمَ ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ · theology] (turns on: Whether God's teaching entails divine assignment of all languages, human convention, a mixture, or leaves the issue undecided.)
  2:31/q08/p1 God instituted the original languages and taught their established meanings to Adam. — reasons: Al-Ashʿarī, al-Jubbāʾī and al-Kaʿbī infer prior assignments from 'taught the names' and angelic verification; Ibn Khuwayz Mindād sees detailed divine institution. Al-Ālūsī rejects an infinite regress from requiring prior human convention.
  2:31/q08/p2 People established word meanings by convention, with God's teaching able to convey that convention. — reasons: Rāzī says gestures could establish meanings and God could inspire or teach human-coined languages; al-Ālūsī attributes human convention to the Muʿtazila and Abū Hāshim requires a prior language.
  2:31/q08/p3 Some assignments are divine and others conventional. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī attributes this distribution to al-Ustādh and notes al-Ashʿarī allows some later human naming.
  2:31/q08/p4 The verse does not decide the origin of language because it leaves the teaching method open. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says all sides accept divine inspiration of linguistic capacity; al-Māzirī says the practical consequence concerns altering revealed words versus ordinary language, which Ibn ʿĀshūr endorses.
  2:31/q08/p5 All peoples acquired different languages in one night. — reasons: Al-Māwardī reports this explanation of linguistic diversity but rejects it as impossible by common custom.
2:31/q09 What force does ‘all of them’ give to the names? [ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ كُلَّهَا · grammar] (turns on: Whether the article and kull require absolute exhaustiveness or a contextually known set.)
  2:31/q09/p1 The definite plural and kull emphatically include every name without restriction. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī says the generic plural and reinforcement establish certainty; al-Jishumī and al-Ṭabrisī analyze kull as encompassing parts. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects partitive limitation.
  2:31/q09/p2 Kull is a dependent emphasis of the names, with syntactic freedom unlike ajmaʿūn. — reasons: Al-Samīn says kullahā follows al-asmāʾ; al-Ṭabrisī cites 15:30 for ajmaʿūn following kull.
  2:31/q09/p3 The article refers to the customary set in Adam's world, and kull exhausts that set. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says this set suffices to show Adam's merit; the plural is needed for each name and referent, while the claim that a definite singular is always more inclusive is imprecise.
2:31/q10 Is anything omitted from ‘the names’? [ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether an unspoken genitive, omitted first construct noun, or no ellipsis best explains the pronoun and later request.)
  2:31/q10/p1 The phrase means 'the names of the named things,' with the genitive omitted. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī and al-Zamakhsharī treat the article as replacing the omitted complement, citing 19:4; al-Samīn and Abū Ḥayyān say this substitution is not Basran.
  2:31/q10/p2 No ellipsis is needed: all names are taught, while their referents are understood through the name relation. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn say teaching literal names achieves the purpose. Ibn ʿĀshūr says naming is relational and therefore naturally evokes the referent.
  2:31/q10/p3 'Names' itself denotes the referents. — reasons: This is an alternative metonymic account; later mention of their names may still distinguish labels from objects.
  2:31/q10/p4 The phrase means 'the referents of the names,' with the first construct noun omitted. — reasons: Al-Samīn gives this as an alternative motivated by ʿaraḍahum, which normally cannot refer to an abstract nonrational plural; he compares an omission in 24:40.
2:31/q11 What does ‘then’ indicate between teaching and display? [ثُمَّ · rhetoric] (turns on: Temporal delay versus an increase in the rank of what follows.)
  2:31/q11/p1 It marks elapsed time, allowing Adam's knowledge to settle before the angels' sudden test. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī and Abū Ḥayyān contrast Adam's preparation with the angels' immediate demand; al-Samīn calls thumma temporal. Ibn ʿĀshūr acknowledges this reading.
  2:31/q11/p2 It marks the display's higher rhetorical rank: the public proof of wisdom surpasses the lesson alone. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr prefers this; al-Ālūsī says thumma and the later fa show special attention to Adam.
2:31/q12 What does ‘displayed’ mean? [عَرَضَهُمْ · meaning] (turns on: Different root analyses converge on making something manifest.)
  2:31/q12/p1 It means making something visible or manifest to another. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī gives goods, troops and a book as examples; al-Layth's poem uses aʿraḍat for appearing and al-Farrāʾ compares 18:100. Al-Jishumī traces it to appearance or a side; Bursevī stresses recognition of its condition.
  2:31/q12/p2 Its original sense is side or aspect, extended to presentation, width and reputation. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj and al-Ḥaralī explain showing an object's side; ʿirḍ also denotes a person's protected honor or qualities. Al-Jishumī connects accident with altered appearance.
2:31/q13 What did God display to the angels? [عَرَضَهُمْ · referent] (turns on: Whether the object was the named beings or the names themselves, and how the pronoun and demonstrative bear on it.)
  2:31/q13/p1 God displayed the named beings, their forms or images. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ, al-Akhfash and many exegetes read the masculine pronoun and 'these' as pointing to beings. Ibn ʿAṭiyya prefers kinds shown first to Adam and then angels; al-Māwardī and Ibn Kathīr favor referents. The objects could be material or representational.
  2:31/q13/p2 God displayed the names themselves. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās and Qatāda reports take this view. Ubayy's and Ibn Masʿūd's feminine readings support a names antecedent; al-Jishumī objects that abstract names cannot be displayed.
  2:31/q13/p3 The display was of Adam's descendants in particular. — reasons: Ibn Zayd says they were drawn from his back; Elmalılı favors them from the rational plural and offers seedlike or intellectual presentations.
  2:31/q13/p4 God displayed the hidden living realities behind worldly names. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī argues that the animate plural, unseen knowledge and 15:21 show exalted, knowing realities preserved with God.
2:31/q14 How were the displayed beings shown? [ثُمَّ عَرَضَهُمْ · reports] (turns on: Materially created beings, representative kinds, small particles, mental forms, or unseen realities.)
  2:31/q14/p1 God represented their images or forms in the angels' minds. — reasons: Al-Māwardī and al-Ṭabrisī allow mental impression before creation; al-Ālūsī allows intellectual forms or an imaginal realm in which abstract meanings take shape.
  2:31/q14/p2 God created the beings and showed their actual forms, possibly one exemplar of each kind. — reasons: Muqātil and al-Ṭabrisī describe visible entities; an exemplar leaves angels less excuse than a description. Al-Māwardī allows display after creation.
  2:31/q14/p3 God displayed tiny particles representing descendants or other referents. — reasons: Ibn Zayd's descendants come from Adam's back; Bursevī cites dustlike figures, Elmalılı likens them to seeds or microbes.
  2:31/q14/p4 God described the beings without displaying physical forms. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya records this as a possibility; al-Ālūsī allows a general account of future beings and the knowledge needed for life.
  2:31/q14/p5 The angels saw the named things within Adam's own comprehensive constitution. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports the macrocosm within the human body; al-Qāshānī speaks of human faculties through which angels receive knowledge.
