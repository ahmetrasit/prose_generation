7:150/q20 How do sources assess Moses' treatment of Aaron? [وَأَخَذَ بِرَأْسِ أَخِيهِ · theology] (turns on: The significance of custom, prophetic impeccability, sincere zeal, and claims of humiliating discipline.)
  7:150/q20/p1 The seizure could be read as humiliating or disciplining Aaron. — reasons: WAHIDI-WAJIZ (author): Aaron asks Moses not to let the calf worshippers rejoice over him by striking or humiliating him, or count him among the wrongdoers. KASHSHAF-FULL (author): Moses is asked not to do what the enemies hoped for by humiliating or mistreating Aaron; a reading instead addresses the enemies, forbidding them to rejoice. IBNJAWZI-ZAD (author): The people who had weakened Aaron were the calf worshippers, and they had humiliated him. RAZI-FULL (الطاعنون في عصمة الأنبياء): Those who challenge prophetic impeccability say Moses pulled Aaron’s head toward him to humiliate and belittle him. QURTUBI-FULL (العلماء): The fourth view says Moses embraced Aaron to learn what had happened; Aaron feared Israel would mistake it for humiliation. QURTUBI-FULL (العلماء): A second interpretation says Moses wanted to whisper about the tablets revealed during the private conversation and conceal them from the Israelites; Aaron objected lest the gesture look humiliating. NASAFI-FULL (author): The enemies are the calf worshippers; Moses should not do what they wish by humiliating or harming Aaron, or treat him as one of the wrongdoers by sharing his anger with them. ABUHAYYAN-FULL (author): One view says Moses grabbed Aaron only to tell him privately that the tablets had descended during his communion, and Aaron stopped him lest the Israelites mistake the private exchange for humiliation. ABUHAYYAN-FULL (author): Another view says Moses pulled Aaron close to learn what happened; Aaron feared the Israelites would think Moses was humiliating him and explained their oppression. BIQAI-FULL (author): Aaron says the people intimidated him and nearly killed him for objecting; he asks Moses not to let their enemies rejoice at his humiliation or count him among the wrongdoers. BURSEVI (author): Moses threw down the tablets of the Torah and pulled Aaron by the hair toward himself in reproach, not to humiliate him, supposing he had failed to restrain the people; Aaron was older, gentle, and loved by the Israelites. ALUSI-FULL (author): Moses did not intend to humiliate Aaron; the physical reproach addressed what seemed a failure of duty under his leadership, born of religious zeal. ALUSI-FULL (author): Aaron says the people overpowered and humiliated him and disregarded him because he had few supporters. IBNASHUR-FULL (author): Throwing the tablets was not punishment of the people, since tablets containing what would reform them could not appropriately be treated as prophetic discipline. STUDYQURAN (author): Aaron asks Moses not to let enemies gloat over his further humiliation at Moses’ hands.
  7:150/q20/p2 The two prophets' different practical judgments are compatible with impeccability. — reasons: JISHUMI (author): Their exchange suggests that Moses had a distinct authority even though both brothers were prophets; his deputyship lasted until his return. KASHSHAF-FULL (author): Some say Aaron was Moses' full brother; if so, Moses invoked their shared mother to awaken tenderness, stress their bond, and honor their believing mother and her suffering. TABRISI (أبو علي الجبائي): One explanation is that Moses, thinking deeply in anger, treated Aaron like himself and grabbed him as a person may grab his own beard or lip; customs can make the same act honor in one setting and insult in another. RAZI-FULL (المثبتون لعصمة الأنبياء): Those who affirm prophetic impeccability say Moses drew Aaron close in order to speak privately and learn what had happened. QURTUBI-FULL (محمد): The Prophet forbade gloating over one's brother, warning that God might relieve him and test the one who gloats. IBNKATHIR-FULL (ابن عباس عن النبي): The Prophet says Moses did not throw the tablets when God informed him that his people had fallen into trial; seeing them himself moved him to throw them, for seeing is unlike hearing. ALUSI-FULL (author): Other explanations for mentioning their mother are that she raised Moses through dangers, or that Aaron's visible mercy made the maternal reference fitting; the latter draws on his prophetic gift and merciful speech to the people. | mentions 19:53, 20:90 TABATABAI (author): A fourth explanation says Moses took Aaron's head compassionately because Aaron shared his grief, while Aaron feared people would mistake the gesture for insult. TABATABAI (ابن عباس): The Prophet says God have mercy on Moses: after hearing that his people had been tested he did not throw the tablets, but seeing them himself, he threw them and some broke; seeing is not like hearing. QURTUBI-FULL (العلماء argues against): A third interpretation is that Moses suspected Aaron sided with the calf-worshippers; Qurtubi says this suspicion is impermissible for prophets.
  7:150/q20/p3 The act did not dishonor Aaron: custom or sincere zeal explains it. — reasons: ABUHAYYAN-FULL (author): Another view treats the gesture as affection rather than insult, like Arabs seizing a brother's beard. MAWARDI-FULL (author): One answer is that custom changes whether such an act counts as humiliating, and it may not have been humiliating then. MAWARDI-FULL (author): Another answer compares Moses' action to someone now taking hold of his beard or biting his lip. QUSHAYRI (author): Harun's appeal softened Moses, who returned to God in supplication and confessed his need for forgiveness and mercy. TABATABAI (author): An alternative says Moses handled Aaron as people handle themselves in anger, gripping a beard or biting a lip, because he regarded his brother as himself. QURTUBI-FULL (الترمذي الحكيم): Al-Tirmidhī al-Ḥakīm says Moses was permitted to act because he was God's intimate interlocutor and anyone who reached toward him with harm had committed a grave offense. ASAD-NOTES (author): Against the Biblical account, Asad says the Qur'an does not accuse Aaron of making or worshipping the calf. IBNABIHATIM (author): Ibn Abbas: Moses excused Aaron and sought forgiveness. QURTUBI-FULL (أبو الفرج الجوزي argues against): One cannot compare the states of prophets with those of foolish people. TABATABAI (author argues against): Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says most or all of these alternative explanations do not fit the passage's context.
7:150/q21 What is the syntax of يَجُرُّهُ إِلَيْهِ? [يَجُرُّهُۥٓ إِلَيْهِ · grammar] (turns on: The circumstantial clause's attachment and whether a proposed modifier has a linking pronoun.)
  7:150/q21/p1 Jarr means to pull or drag. — reasons: KASHSHAF-FULL (author): Taking Aaron by the head means by his hair, dragging him by the forelock in the shock that clouded Moses' judgment as he suspected Aaron had failed to restrain the people. ABUHAYYAN-FULL (author): Jarr means pulling or dragging. IBNASHUR-FULL (author): An unnamed report describes Moses as quick-tempered and severe in anger, linking this to striking the Egyptian and dragging Aaron; his reaction signals the enormity of the people’s deed, not discipline. | mentions 28:15, 7:150 TABATABAI (author): Moses threw down the Torah tablets and seized Aaron by the head, dragging him toward himself; Ta-Ha recounts Moses asking why Aaron did not follow the people when they strayed. | mentions 20:93
  7:150/q21/p2 Yajurruhu is a circumstantial clause, primarily of Moses; other attachments are debated. — reasons: SAMIN-DURR (author): يجره إليه can be circumstantial for Moses, the hidden subject of أخذ: he took him while dragging him toward himself. SAMIN-DURR (أبو البقاء): Abū al-Baqāʾ also attaches the circumstantial clause to أخيه; the author calls it weak because a circumstantial clause from a genitive possessor is rare or disallowed, though some permit it when the possessor's part is meant. ALUSI-FULL (author): The two descriptions are either parallel circumstantial clauses or nested, with the second describing the hidden pronoun in the first; Abu al-Baqa also allows the second to replace the first as a full substitute. ALUSI-FULL (author): The clause يجره إليه is circumstantial and may modify Moses' pronoun, the head, or Aaron; Abu al-Baqa considers these attachments weak. SAMIN-DURR (author): The author accepts the genitive-possessor construction in cases where the possessed noun is a part of its possessor. SAMIN-DURR (أبو البقاء argues against): Abū al-Baqāʾ proposes that the clause modifies رأس, but the author objects that this reading lacks a linking pronoun.
7:150/q22 How is ٱبْنَ أُمَّ read? [ٱبْنَ أُمَّ · readings] (turns on: The transmitted fatḥa, kasra, and retained-yāʾ forms.)

TAB-FULL (author): Kufans read kasra mīm. TAB-FULL (author): Tabari prefers damma tāʾ, kasra mīm and accusative enemies by consensus; rejects anomaly. NAHHAS (author): Abu Jafar rejects Humayd's kasra mīm tashmit as morphologically invalid. FARISI-HUJJA (author): Ibn Amir, Abu Bakr, Hamza, Kisai read kasra mīm. WAHIDI-BASIT (author): The phrase ابن أم is read with either kasra or fatha on the mīm.
