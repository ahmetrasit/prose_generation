2:32/q21 Where does Ibn ʿĀshūr defer further discussion of wisdom? [ٱلْحَكِيمُ · links]
  2:32/q21/p1 He defers the fuller discussion to 2:269. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr gives 2:269 as the later locus for the discussion of wisdom.
2:32/q22 What is the force of “Indeed You are the All-Knowing, the Wise” in this reply? [إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْعَلِيمُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the closing titles express a general divine perfection or point specifically to Adam's appointment.)
  2:32/q22/p1 It confirms their limited knowledge by affirming God's comprehensive knowledge and wise decision. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says Adam's learning shows wisdom in the vicegerency; al-Ālūsī says the clause confirms their denial and corrects a possible misunderstanding. Ibn ʿĀshūr says inna gives an explanation: God grants knowledge according to creatures' capacity. Qushayrī says His acts are beyond blame. Al-Ālūsī prefers unrestricted objects of both names over a narrow restriction to commands and decrees.
  2:32/q22/p2 It names the particular decision to make Adam vicegerent. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās, as quoted by Rāzī, takes the close to refer to God's decree of Adam's earthly succession.
2:32/q23 What conduct does the angels' admission teach a person asked about what they do not know? [لَا عِلْمَ لَنَآ · interpretation]
  2:32/q23/p1 Admit ignorance humbly, say ‘I do not know’ or ‘God knows best,’ and accept correction. — reasons: Qurṭubī cites a prophetic warning that ignorant opinion misleads, the Prophet's consultation with Gabriel and Michael on places, Abū Bakr on inheritance, ʿAlī and Ibn ʿUmar on 'God knows best,' Qāsim and Mālik's practice, and the poem of Yazīd. His reports of ʿUmar, ʿAlī and Bakkār show fairness in accepting correction; Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr calls fairness a condition of learning. Bursawī adds Abū Yūsuf and the pulpit scholar; one should also not conceal knowledge one has.
  2:32/q23/p2 Seeking status through disputation undermines honest admission of ignorance. — reasons: Qurṭubī judges his age worse than Mālik's, reporting Mālik's remark that fairness was scarce; rivalry hardens hearts and harms piety.
2:32/q24 Does the command to name the things prove God may impose an impossible task? [لَا عِلْمَ لَنَآ إِلَّا مَا عَلَّمْتَنَآ · theology]
  2:32/q24/p1 Yes: God asked the angels to report names He knew they could not know. — reasons: Thaʿlabī explicitly draws this inference; Qushayrī says no one can blame God for testing the unable. Ṭabrisī says the test displayed dependence on His teaching and elevated Adam.
2:32/q25 What did the angels' inability demonstrate about Adam and vicegerency? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether knowledge of names by itself establishes fitness, or deeper human representation is needed.)
  2:32/q25/p1 Adam's taught knowledge showed his fitness and rank over the angels. — reasons: Ṭabrisī says God raised Adam by the names; Bayḍāwī calls knowledge central to vicegerency and superior to worship, citing 39:9. Nasafī says names knowledge surpasses worship, even more so divine law. Elmalılı and al-Sāmarrāʾī say the comparison tested Adam's fitness and prepared angelic submission.
  2:32/q25/p2 The angels' failure alone cannot establish unfitness for succession; a deeper representative capacity matters. — reasons: Elmalılı argues that ignorance of all names would imply no speech, while ignorance of descendants' or new names would not disqualify them. Human representative speech and direct angelic cognition differ; humans can also err by confusing words or concepts with truth.
  2:32/q25/p3 Adam's comprehensive inward and outward constitution makes him uniquely representative of God. — reasons: Bursawī says he gathers spiritual, bodily, celestial and earthly realms, uniquely received God's spirit (17:70), and mirrors divine attributes. Angels know God yet not themselves and all attributes; the human inner lamp images the spirit's manifestation in just and merciful action, citing 38:26, 9:128, 48:29.
2:32/q26 What does the scene imply about the origin of language and the angels' speech? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether 'teaching names' concerns prior divine designation or how angelic communication itself occurs.)
  2:32/q26/p1 God instituted the names and their meanings before Adam used them. — reasons: Bayḍāwī reasons that teaching words with meanings presupposes prior designation, and no creature before Adam could designate them.
  2:32/q26/p2 Angelic dialogue is direct illumination or knowledge, unlike human indirect representative speech. — reasons: Elmalılı says angels' speech cannot be ordinary naming whether they lacked all names or just some; God first addressed them by direct reality and their glorification likewise expresses illumination. Adam's speech conveys knowledge indirectly via signs or deputies.
2:32/q27 How does this passage connect to God's previous claim to know what the angels did not? [* · links] (turns on: How the scene functions in the surrounding narrative and later address.)
  2:32/q27/p1 The names test and its answer display the hidden knowledge and wisdom asserted in 2:30. — reasons: Bayḍāwī says the follow-up challenge restates the 2:30 claim in fuller form and exposes hidden conditions. Bursawī links al-ʿAlīm to that claim; Abū Ḥayyān says Adam's learning reveals why God appointed him.
  2:32/q27/p2 The story prepares the audience for the call to the Children of Israel. — reasons: Elmalılı says divine lordship and embedded human gifts lead into 2:40; Ṭabarī says the account recalls favors to their ancestors and warns of Iblis-like punishment for continued rebellion.
2:32/q28 What does recounting this unseen dialogue establish for Muhammad's audience? [* · links]
  2:32/q28/p1 Revelation of inaccessible events proves Muhammad's prophethood and the divine source of his message. — reasons: Ṭabarī connects the information in 2:30–31 with a proof to Jewish hearers that Muhammad knew unseen events only by revelation.
2:32/q29 What report explains the angels' prior private claim and subsequent repentance? [* · reports]
  2:32/q29/p1 They expected no creature more knowledgeable or honored; Adam's knowledge and commanded prostration corrected them. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān transmits their expectation. Al-Durr reports Qatāda and al-Ḥasan that the angels privately claimed superiority and repented when unable to answer. Al-Baghawī's report says prostration was ordered after Adam's spirit was breathed, citing 15:29.
2:32/q30 What inward lesson is drawn from the angels' answer? [* · inward] (turns on: Whether the inner emphasis lies on humility before God or the limit of the angelic spiritual station.)
  2:32/q30/p1 Reliance on God purifies the self of pretension and brings recognition of one's limits. — reasons: Qushayrī links reliance to purity and confessed ignorance. Abū ʿUthmān al-Maghribī, with a parallel from Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, says creatures suffer from pretensions: angels claimed worship then admitted ignorance.
  2:32/q30/p2 The angels' fixed perfections disclose their shortfall from human spiritual perfection. — reasons: Qāshānī reads their answer as testimony that they cannot acquire the ranks of human knowledge.
2:32/q31 What broader truths does the episode establish about human knowledge and speech? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the episode is used to stress knowledge's rank or the risks of representation.)
  2:32/q31/p1 Knowledge is central to Adam's honor and human vicegerency. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī says God has comprehensive knowledge, angels know what He teaches, and Adam receives what equips him for succession. Bayḍāwī cites 39:9 for knowledge's superiority to worship.
  2:32/q31/p2 Human speech can carry truth but can also enable illusion and evil. — reasons: Elmalılı says speech represents reality indirectly; identifying concepts with reality leads to error and compounded ignorance and gives Satan a route to mislead.
2:32/q32 What does the verse teach about claims to hidden knowledge? [* · interpretation]
  2:32/q32/p1 Claims about unseen events require reliable transmitted report or proof. — reasons: Ṭabarī rebukes soothsayers, diviners, fortune tellers and astrologers who claim unseen knowledge without either. Rāzī argues that angels' ignorance makes independent human access still less plausible.
