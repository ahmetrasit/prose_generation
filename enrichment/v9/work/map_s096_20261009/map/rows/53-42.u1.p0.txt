## THE CURRENT MAP
53:42/q01 How is the opening clause read and connected to the earlier scriptures? [وَأَنَّ · readings] (turns on: Whether the hamza is opened, coordinating the clause with the earlier anna, or broken with kasra to begin a new sentence.)
  53:42/q01/p1 With fatḥa on anna, this clause continues the statements in the scrolls of Moses and Abraham. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf says the fatḥa makes all these clauses part of the scriptures; Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it the majority reading and coordinates it with the understood anna of verse 38. Al-Razi calls it the common reading. Al-Samin says the coordinated hamzas share it. Al-Nasafi, al-Zajjaj, Ibn al-Jawzi reporting al-Zajjaj, Ibn Ashur, and Study Quran place the statements in those scrolls; Ibn Ashur says it has the syntactic position of verse 40 and notes Abraham's going to his Lord in 37:99 as a possible parallel. Study Quran says verses 42–50 except 46 begin wa anna; its introduction places these perennial truths after the call to reflect on creation. Islahi says Abraham's and Moses' real teachings and sacrifices challenge those assuming descent from them ensures Paradise.
  53:42/q01/p2 With kasra on inna, this and following clauses begin independent statements outside the earlier scriptures' contents. — reasons: Abu al-Sammal Qanab is named by Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Samin as reading kasra here and onward. Al-Kashshaf, al-Razi, al-Baydawi, al-Alusi, and Study Quran explain the break; Study Quran gives the independent sense as 'truly,' 'surely,' or 'verily.'
53:42/q02 Who is addressed by 'your Lord'? [رَبِّكَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the singular second-person address has an unrestricted listener or the Prophet as its referent.)
  53:42/q02/p1 Any listener or rational person is addressed, so each person's end is before God. — reasons: Al-Razi says this warns wrongdoers and encourages the righteous. For this general addressee, he takes the definite al-muntaha as the promised return already announced in revelation and by the Prophet. Ibn Ashur says the shift from third person to second person leaves the addressee unidentified and effectively says each returns to his own Lord.
  53:42/q02/p2 Muhammad is addressed specifically. — reasons: Al-Razi says this confirms his religion of the one self-sufficient Lord on whom contingent beings depend, the Lord of lords and cause of causes. It also consoles him about opponents, recalling God's knowledge of their words and their eventual return in 36:76 and 36:83. On this address, al-muntaha is generic: every terminus is to the Lord who is its origin.
53:42/q03 What is the grammatical form and case of al-muntaha? [ٱلْمُنتَهَىٰ · grammar] (turns on: The notes discuss different grammatical aspects: its hidden accusative case ending and its nominal or verbal-noun form.)
  53:42/q03/p1 It is the accusative noun of anna, with an unpronounced case ending because it is a defective noun. — reasons: Al-Nahhas identifies its function and explains why the accusative vowel cannot appear.
  53:42/q03/p2 It is a verbal noun meaning ending, in a faʿlā pattern that can function as a noun. — reasons: Al-Nasafi and al-Tabatabai call it a verbal or infinitive noun; al-Tabatabai draws breadth from its unrestricted use. Al-Burhan gives al-shura, al-rujʿa, and al-ʿulya as parallels for faʿlā as a noun.
53:42/q04 What does al-muntaha mean lexically? [ٱلْمُنتَهَىٰ · meaning]
  53:42/q04/p1 The end, endpoint, termination, or final return; it can be the destination where action stops. — reasons: Al-Wahidi explicitly glosses it as endpoint or termination; al-Kashshaf as the end; al-Nasafi as a verbal noun for the end where creatures arrive and return, citing 3:28. Al-Jishumi adds the destination at which action ceases. Al-Bursevi glosses it as end or return. Al-Tabatabai calls it an infinitive noun and takes its unrestricted wording to include every kind of ending.
53:42/q05 What does the definite article in al-muntaha specify? [ٱلْمُنتَهَىٰ · grammar] (turns on: Al-Razi ties the article's reference to whether 'your' addresses people generally or the Prophet.)
  53:42/q05/p1 For a general addressee it identifies the already promised return. — reasons: Al-Razi says revelation and the Prophet have previously announced this return.
  53:42/q05/p2 For the Prophet as addressee it is generic, encompassing every terminus. — reasons: Al-Razi says every terminus goes to the Lord who is its origin; in the corresponding account perception starts from outward things and through reflection reaches God and stops.
53:42/q06 What ultimately ends with the Lord? [إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ ٱلْمُنتَهَىٰ · interpretation] (turns on: The notes dispute whether the unrestricted 'ending' denotes bodily return, final outcomes, hopes, the limit of thought, causal explanation, or all kinds of ending; al-Tabatabai argues against restricting its scope.)
  53:42/q06/p1 Creatures return to God after death or at resurrection for judgment and recompense. — reasons: Al-Nahhas, al-Thaʿlabi, al-Baghawi, al-Qurtubi, and others add recompense for good and bad deeds; al-Wahidi calls this the exegetes' interpretation. Ibn ʿAtiyya calls it the gathering and destination after death, relative to worldly life. Al-Razi calls resurrection the well-known interpretation and says it answers when the prior recompense occurs: gratitude is rewarded and ingratitude punished. Al-Kashshaf cites 35:18; al-Nasafi cites 3:28. Ibn Kathir specifies Judgment Day, Study Quran says all then stand before God, and al-Alusi says no other being has an independent or shared part in their return. Al-Bursevi cites 42:53 and says beginning and end are in God. Al-Biqai includes bodily resurrection and the return of deeds; Elmalili includes full recompense and calls God First and Last. Islahi says no one can evade accountability by appealing to another and God's decisions are final. Kuranyolu says judgment gives full recompense without the slightest injustice, while grace and forgiveness remain God's choice and believers should hope without complacency. Al-Tabatabai rejects making this the only meaning as too narrow.
  53:42/q06/p2 The ultimate destination is specifically God's reckoning, reward in Paradise, or punishment in Hell, where earthly action ends. — reasons: Ibn ʿAtiyya contrasts Paradise or Hell as the absolute endpoint with the postmortem gathering relative to worldly life, and posits an omitted genitive: the end of God's mercy or punishment. Al-Tabrisi names divine reward and punishment where deeds cease; Ibn Kathir reports Muʿadh ibn Jabal saying the return ends in Paradise or Fire. Al-Alusi reports reckonings, Paradise, and Fire as commentators' glosses. Study Quran cites 13:35 for the Garden of the reverent versus the Fire of disbelievers. Al-Tabatabai rejects restriction to reward and punishment or reckoning as unsupported narrowing.
  53:42/q06/p3 All beneficence begins with God and all hopes, or security, finally end with Him. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabi and al-Baghawi report beneficence and hopes; al-Qurtubi has favor and security. Al-Jishumi lists grace and hopes among reported senses.
  53:42/q06/p4 Human inquiry and thought reach their limit at God, whose essence and attributes cannot be encompassed. — reasons: Al-Wahidi permits reasoning about created things but says divine knowledge, essence, and modality elude comprehension. Al-Jishumi reports the limit of thought; al-Alusi applies it to divine essence and attributes. Reports from Ubayy, Anas, Abu Hurayra, Ibn Abbas, and Abu Dharr support stopping or turning to creation, detailed in the reflection question. Al-Razi interprets Ubayy's and Anas's reports as reaching the uncaused necessary existent; he says reflection from outward objects stops at God. Al-Tabatabai rejects making the limit of thought the verse's only sense, while treating the Imam al-Sadiq's instruction as an expanded application of 'ending.'
  53:42/q06/p5 All beings, their effects, governance, deeds, knowledge, hopes, and every other ending depend on God, their origin as well as final end. — reasons: Al-Tabatabai reads the unrestricted infinitive noun absolutely: God creates beings and their relations, directly or through causes, and all governance returns to Him; he cites 39:62–63 and 7:54. Al-Biqai includes bodily return and deeds, knowledge, hopes, affairs, place, and time. Asad includes the universe's beginning and end in time and space, God as source and goal. Bursevi cites 42:53 for affairs returning to God because there is no other deity. Al-Husayn's explanation of tawhid, transmitted by al-Sulami and Bursevi, says contingent effects arise from God, return to Him, and vanish while their cause remains; al-Sulami cites 'He is the First' in 57:3. Al-Razi reads the generic article as every terminus belonging to its origin.
  53:42/q06/p6 The chain of causes and rational inquiry ends at God as the uncaused Necessary Being and source of existence. — reasons: Al-Razi argues contingent things require causes and an explanatory chain cannot finish with another contingent thing. He takes Ubayy's and Anas's stop-at-God reports in this sense. He warns that extending the same reading to every return or ascent verse, such as 35:10, lacks apparent support in some cases though it is clear here. Study Quran reports this understanding from R. Al-Jishumi mentions God as final judge among several reported views, distinct from the causal proof.
  53:42/q06/p7 All knowledge ultimately ends with the Lord. — reasons: Muqatil states the epistemic endpoint without further argument.
