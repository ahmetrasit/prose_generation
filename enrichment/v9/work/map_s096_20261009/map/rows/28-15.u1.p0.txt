## THE CURRENT MAP
28:15/q01 Which city did Moses enter? [ٱلْمَدِينَةَ · referent] (turns on: Whether 'city' names Egypt broadly, its royal capital, or a nearby settlement; the Qur'an itself does not name it.)
  28:15/q01/p1 Egypt, or its capital city where Pharaoh resided. — reasons: Ibn Shajara and Ibn Ishaq say Egypt; al-Jishumi prefers Egypt or its land. Al-Alusi calls it the best-known and most apparent identification. Islahi distinguishes the elite capital from the Israelites' settlement.
  28:15/q01/p2 Memphis (Manf), in Egypt. — reasons: Al-Suddi's itinerary has Moses follow Pharaoh there; markets were closed and streets empty. Ibn Ashur calls it the northern capital; Bursevi places it west of the Nile and describes its royal history.
  28:15/q01/p3 A village called Khanin, Habin, Hain, or Habin, about two farsakhs from Egypt. — reasons: Muqatil locates Moses initially at Khanin and describes the city as a village; later tellings vary its spelling. Razi reports a town at that distance as the majority's account.
  28:15/q01/p4 Heliopolis or Ayn al-Shams. — reasons: Al-Dahhak and later authorities give this identification.
  28:15/q01/p5 Alexandria. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and Alusi list this further possibility.
28:15/q02 When were the city's people unaware? [عَلَىٰ حِينِ غَفْلَةٍۢ مِّنْ أَهْلِهَا · reports] (turns on: Whether ghafla denotes a daily rest, a festival's distraction, or failure to remember Moses.)
  28:15/q02/p1 At midday, during the siesta, when streets and markets were empty. — reasons: This is attributed to Ibn Abbas, Qatada, Sa'id ibn Jubayr, Ikrima, al-Suddi, and others. Razi prefers it because the wording makes the inhabitants inattentive, not merely Moses secretive.
  28:15/q02/p2 Between sunset and nightfall or the evening prayers. — reasons: Ibn Abbas, al-Qurazi and Wahb are cited; variants call the endpoints sunset, night prayer, isha and atama.
  28:15/q02/p3 On a festival day, while people were playing. — reasons: Al-Hasan and Ali are named for this report; some combine it with the account of Moses' return after expulsion.
  28:15/q02/p4 After a long absence, when people had forgotten Moses. — reasons: Ibn Zayd connects the absence to Pharaoh's expulsion of Moses after a childhood staff or ember incident. Wahidi rejects this chronology, saying Moses' return after a long interval followed Shuayb and prophethood.
  28:15/q02/p5 An inattentive interval when entering the city was unusual; its exact hour is unspecified. — reasons: Baydawi and Bursevi stress the unusual opportunity. Tabari declines to decide the reason or hour; Razi says the Qur'an provides no evidence to rank the reports. Asad translates the general sense; Tabatabai includes midday or midnight as examples.
28:15/q03 Why and in what circumstances did Moses enter the city? [وَدَخَلَ ٱلْمَدِينَةَ عَلَىٰ حِينِ غَفْلَةٍۢ · reports] (turns on: The reports put Moses either within Pharaoh's household, in fearful opposition, or returning from an earlier exile.)
  28:15/q03/p1 He followed Pharaoh there and arrived during Pharaoh's midday rest. — reasons: Al-Suddi says Moses had Pharaoh's son's status, clothes, and mounts; Pharaoh rode without him, so he followed to Memphis.
  28:15/q03/p2 He entered secretly because his opposition to Pharaoh had produced mutual fear. — reasons: Ibn Ishaq says Moses had Israelite adherents and repudiated Pharaoh's religion. Al-Dahhak says Moses deliberately chose an inattentive hour. Islahi says he visited to see his oppressed people.
  28:15/q03/p3 He returned after Pharaoh had expelled him years before. — reasons: Ibn Zayd's childhood account includes the wife's protection and the ember test causing his speech impediment. Wahidi argues the long-return chronology conflicts with this stage of the narrative.
  28:15/q03/p4 The verse does not establish why he entered or when this happened relative to prophethood. — reasons: Tabari retains the verse's wording without selecting a story; Razi says waw need not mark temporal sequence and the reports lack decisive Quranic evidence.
28:15/q04 How does 'upon a time of heedlessness' work? [عَلَىٰ حِينِ غَفْلَةٍۢ مِّنْ أَهْلِهَا · grammar] (turns on: Whether ala is read temporally with 'entered', as circumstance, or with hin treated as extra.)
  28:15/q04/p1 It attaches to 'entered'; 'upon' marks time, effectively 'during' or 'in'. — reasons: Al-Nahhas says the preposition focuses heedlessness itself; Burhan and Itqan cite 2:102 for temporal use. Al-Alusi prefers this straightforward syntax. Ibn Ashur instead describes metaphorical elevation that conveys security.
  28:15/q04/p2 The phrase is circumstantial of Moses, the city, or the entry. — reasons: On the subject reading Moses entered furtively; Abu al-Baqa also allows a qualifier of the city. Al-Alusi objects that this evades the ordinary construction.
  28:15/q04/p3 'Time' is superfluous and the meaning is 'upon heedlessness'. — reasons: Wahidi reports al-Farra's analysis.
28:15/q05 How is 'time' read? [حِينِ · readings] (turns on: The treatment of the adverb under annexation and the explanation for its open ending.)
  28:15/q05/p1 The common genitive form is ḥīni. — reasons: Al-Alusi treats it as the obvious construction governed by ʿalā.
  28:15/q05/p2 Abu Talib reads ḥīna with fatḥa. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and al-Samin explain building the adverb because of the following verbal sense; Al-Alusi reports an assimilation explanation too but finds the proposed grammar unconvincing.
28:15/q06 How is 'from its people' attached? [غَفْلَةٍۢ مِّنْ أَهْلِهَا · grammar]
  28:15/q06/p1 It qualifies 'heedlessness': the inattentiveness came from the city's people. — reasons: Al-Alusi says the indefinite phrase is more eloquent than annexation because nunation magnifies the heedlessness.
28:15/q07 What does 'fighting' describe and what were the men doing? [رَجُلَيْنِ يَقْتَتِلَانِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the post-indefinite verb is an adjective or an allowable circumstantial clause.)
  28:15/q07/p1 The verb clause qualifies the two men; they were fighting one another. — reasons: Al-Samin and Al-Alusi prefer adjectival attachment; Bursevi explains reciprocal form; Ibn Ashur says the fighting was severe mutual beating and Biqai describes grappling, strangling, and striking.
  28:15/q07/p2 The clause is circumstantial: Moses found them while they fought. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya gives this parse; al-Samin notes that Sibawayh permits circumstantial clauses from indefinite nouns more broadly than most grammarians.
28:15/q08 What caused the fight? [يَقْتَتِلَانِ · reports] (turns on: Whether the quarrel arose from forced labor, religious opposition, or an unspecified worldly matter.)
  28:15/q08/p1 The Copt tried to force the Israelite to carry wood to Pharaoh's kitchen or bakery. — reasons: Reports call the Copt a cook or baker and say the Israelite refused; one says the Copt then threatened Moses with the work. Ibn Ashur calls this coercion the aggression.
  28:15/q08/p2 They disputed over religion. — reasons: Abu Ali is named for this explanation.
  28:15/q08/p3 They disputed over some worldly affair, unspecified. — reasons: Jishumi and Alusi report this possibility.
28:15/q09 How is the fighting verb read? [يَقْتَتِلَانِ · readings] (turns on: Whether the two tāʾs are articulated separately or assimilated.)
  28:15/q09/p1 The ordinary reading retains the two tāʾ sounds in yaqtatilān. — reasons: The two men are grammatically dual.
  28:15/q09/p2 Nuaym ibn Maysarah assimilates the two tāʾs, transferring the first vowel to qāf. — reasons: Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and Alusi transmit this reading.
28:15/q10 Who was the man from Moses' party? [هَٰذَا مِن شِيعَتِهِۦ · referent] (turns on: Whether the affiliation is ethnic, religious, a particular named person, or an army.)
  28:15/q10/p1 An Israelite, from Moses' people or followers. — reasons: Most reports name an Israelite; some describe him as a religious adherent or believer, others as kin by lineage. Muqatil and al-Suddi say both fighters were unbelievers; Razi cites the next day's rebuke, but prefers a Muslim ally because partisan usually implies shared way.
  28:15/q10/p2 Specifically the Samaritan, who was an Israelite partisan. — reasons: Reported by the commentators and repeated by later sources.
  28:15/q10/p3 A member of Moses' army. — reasons: Al-Damghani gives the army sense of shiʿa.
28:15/q11 What does 'party' mean here? [شِيعَتِهِۦ · meaning] (turns on: Which aspect of affiliation shiʿa denotes.)
  28:15/q11/p1 A group affiliated with someone: followers, helpers, companions, or kin. — reasons: Ibn Faris gives helpers and factions; a cited poem uses shiʿa for what follows after. Ibn Ashur compares 28:4.
  28:15/q11/p2 An army. — reasons: Al-Damghani gives this lexical sense.
28:15/q12 Who was the man called Moses' enemy? [هَٰذَا مِنْ عَدُوِّهِۦ · referent] (turns on: Whether 'enemy' refers to Egyptian ethnicity and Pharaoh's party, disbelief, or a named royal employee.)
  28:15/q12/p1 An Egyptian Copt from Pharaoh's people. — reasons: This is the prevailing identification. Mujahid adds that Pharaoh was Persian from Istakhr; Zajjaj reports the slain man as Coptic or from Istakhr. The Biblical Exodus account explicitly calls him Egyptian.
  28:15/q12/p2 Specifically Pharaoh's cook or baker, with varying names: Falyathun, Fathun, Fatun, Faq'un, or Qanun. — reasons: Reports disagree over his job and name; Sa'id ibn Jubayr calls him the baker, while others call him cook. The spelling varies and is not resolved.
  28:15/q12/p3 Perhaps another Israelite who disbelieved. — reasons: Study Quran reports M's speculation; it notes the first opponent is Egyptian in Exodus, while the different wording in verse 19 may concern a distinct second opponent.
