18:34/q11 How are مالا and نفرا construed? [مَالًۭا نَفَرًۭا · grammar]
  18:34/q11/p1 Both are accusative specifications of the comparative degree. — reasons: Zajjāj and Ālūsī label both accusative specifications; Ibn ʿĀshūr explicitly calls nafaran specification of the comparison.
18:34/q12 What does أعز express in the boast? [وَأَعَزُّ · meaning]
  18:34/q12/p1 Greater strength, honor and social standing. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr links strength and honor to numerous courageous kin. Others summarize it as a stronger retinue, clan or greater status.
18:34/q13 What is omitted after أعز, and how does the comparative work? [أَكْثَرُ مِنكَ وَأَعَزُّ · grammar]
  18:34/q13/p1 The second comparative inherits the standard منك from the first. — reasons: Burhān says omitted comparison standards are often inferred when afʿal is a predicate; it compares 87:17. The same source outlines construct, definite and unmarked-plus-min patterns and conditions for definite, plural and collective nouns after afʿal, citing 63:8, 26:111, 18:103, 6:123 and 11:27.
18:34/q14 Who are the owner's نفر? [نَفَرًۭا · referent] (turns on: Whether social power is counted by dependents, male offspring, or the wider kin group; later references to offspring favor the first two for some authors.)
  18:34/q14/p1 His retainers, servants and attendants. — reasons: Qatāda and Ibn ʿAbbās identify servants; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says this reading permits the two men to be brothers. Ṭabāṭabāʾī prefers servants and children by the later comparison at 18:39. Some combine attendants with children.
  18:34/q14/p2 His children, especially sons. — reasons: Muqātil adduces the later mention of wealth and offspring (18:39); Ibn ʿĀshūr cites verse 40. Al-Kashshāf, Bayḍāwī and Nasafī explain that sons accompany their father unlike daughters. Some pair sons with servants and helpers.
  18:34/q14/p3 His clan, kinsmen and fighting supporters. — reasons: Abū Muslim and Abū Sulaymān take clan; Rāzī says they defend and accompany him. Ṭabrisī says the group accompanies him on errands; Ibn Qutayba's examples present many brothers as protection and support for the back. Some sources combine clan and general retinue.
  18:34/q14/p4 A small group of men, conventionally three to ten. — reasons: Ibn Fāris gives three to ten, comparing qawm and rahṭ as collectives without a singular of the same form. Qurtubī says fewer than ten, and Bursevī specifies male helpers and an upper limit of ten.
18:34/q15 What background is reported for the two men and their garden? [* · reports] (turns on: Whether the parable is a specific historical incident, a differently located tale, or a general figure; names, sums and settings vary across reports.)
  18:34/q15/p1 They were brothers who inherited wealth; the believer spent for God and the unbeliever built worldly prosperity. — reasons: Muqātil has the impoverished believer seek generosity and describe lending his inheritance to God for himself and his children; the unbeliever mocks him. Māwardī contrasts afterlife spending with status seeking. Qurtubī recounts charity, mosque building, the believer seeking work and being expelled after resurrection was denied; another account details heavenly land, house, wife and servants. Jishumī connects the story with 37:50.
  18:34/q15/p2 They were Meccan Makhzūmī brothers, Abū Salama and al-Aswad ibn ʿAbd al-Asad. — reasons: Al-Kalbī says each inherited four thousand dinars and the believer spent his share for God; Qurtubī cites 37:51.
  18:34/q15/p3 They were Israelite brothers, the believer Judah or Tamlīkha and unbeliever Qarṭūsh. — reasons: Qurtubī attributes Judah to Ibn ʿAbbās and Tamlīkha/Qarṭūsh to Muqātil; the names vary in the reports.
  18:34/q15/p4 They were partners with eight thousand dinars. — reasons: ʿAṭāʾ supplies the partnership and sum.
  18:34/q15/p5 The gardens were at Lake Tinnīs, which flooded overnight. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports that one brother sold his share, spent in obedience and was mocked before the lake flooded.
  18:34/q15/p6 The wealthy owner is Fatrus or Qutrus. — reasons: Thaʿlabī calls him Fatrus and depicts his tour, boast, doubt in the Hour and hope of a better return; Nasafī calls him Qutrus. These are name variants in the accounts.
  18:34/q15/p7 A fishing test and an angelic vision expose the difference between worldly luck and final fate. — reasons: Qurtubī says the unbeliever caught many fish invoking an idol while the believer caught none invoking God; an angel protested, and Gabriel showed each man's abode, so both judged earthly fortune irrelevant. God took the believer and ruined the unbeliever, who was seen in Hell as in 37:51–52.
  18:34/q15/p8 The parable may be a general example for this community rather than an account of particular men. — reasons: Māwardī reports it as an exhortation to renounce worldly life and desire the hereafter; Qurtubī says the context argues against this.
  18:34/q15/p9 The pair can stand for the Prophet and Meccans, believers and disbelievers, or ʿUyayna's party and Salmān's. — reasons: Qurtubī reports these further identifications alongside the link with 37:51; they are separate generalized applications grouped here as variants rather than an agreed historical identification.
18:34/q16 How does this verse fit the surrounding parable and other verses? [* · links] (turns on: The sources compare the boast with adjacent admonition, a later eschatological dialogue, and the parable's destruction.)
  18:34/q16/p1 The boast answers the earlier call to remain with believers and exposes the arrogance of rich opponents. — reasons: Ṭabarī and Ibn ʿAṭiyya compare ʿUyayna and al-Aqraʿ asking the Prophet to dismiss Salmān, Khabbāb and Ṣuhayb as inferior; Qurtubī links the parable to patience with believers at 18:28. Abū Ḥayyān likens the pride to Quraysh leaders; Iṣlāḥī sees Quraysh's outward and inward arrogance.
  18:34/q16/p2 The worldly exchange here has an afterlife counterpart in 37:51–61. — reasons: Qurtubī says the latter describes the brothers' afterlife situation, with the believer recalling his companion in Hell; Jishumī also connects the inherited-wealth account with 37:50.
  18:34/q16/p3 The garden's collapse later demonstrates the error of trusting wealth and followers. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the companion refutes the claim verbally and ruin demonstrates it. Iṣlāḥī contrasts the owner's assumption of indestructibility with Dhū al-Qarnayn's gratitude and trust in God's promise.
18:34/q17 What does the owner's boast teach about wealth and status? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The outward moral of worldly pretension and an inward symbolic reading of its images.)
  18:34/q17/p1 Worldly wealth and strong followers can feed arrogance and are no proof of lasting divine favor. — reasons: Qatāda calls them the wicked person's ambition; Qushayrī says the unbeliever failed to thank his Provider and regretted ruin. Iṣlāḥī says opponents treat prosperity as proof of belief and expect a better hereafter. Study Quran compares 18:46, 9:55, 9:85, 23:55–56, 3:10, 3:116, 9:69, 26:88 and 34:35, arguing wealth and children cannot shield punishment. Ṭabāṭabāʾī has the later ruin refute self-sufficiency.
  18:34/q17/p2 In an inward reading, the gardens and fruit represent worldly desire and cravings. — reasons: Al-Nīsābūrī maps wealth and status to blameworthy traits, imagined better return to delusion about God, and turning hands in regret to wasted life and good potential.
18:34/q18 What details about the gardens before the boast are supplied? [* · links] (turns on: The immediately preceding garden description and whether its word for 'both' is singular in form or genuinely dual.)
  18:34/q18/p1 The two gardens bore full produce, with palms, crops and flowing water. — reasons: Qurtubī describes palms encircling them, grapes and crops, and a river through the middle; ḥifāf means an encircling edge. Jishumī says the gardens contained other trees too. Ṭabrisī describes the later calamity erasing their green growth.
  18:34/q18/p2 The preceding كلتا is grammatically singular in form while denoting two gardens. — reasons: Qurtubī cites singular ātat and Jarīr's singular predicate and prefers the Basran analysis. The Basrans reject al-Farrāʾ's dual derivation because oblique case with an overt noun would require yāʾ; poetic shortening is necessity. Sībawayh treats alif as feminine and tāʾ as replacing wāw; al-Jurmī reverses what is root and added. Qurtubī compares naḥnu as singular form for multiple referents.
  18:34/q18/p3 The preceding كلتا is a dual derived from كل. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ says lām is lightened and dual alif added; it is always annexed and has no standalone singular. Basrans and Qurtubī dispute the analysis from case and agreement.
18:34/q19 What ancillary examples of wording and authority appear in the collected excerpts? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: These excerpts concern separate cited examples and are not direct interpretations of the verse.)
  18:34/q19/p1 Sentence conjunction depends on the strength of the connection. — reasons: ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī says adjective/noun or emphasis form such a unity that conjunction would coordinate a thing with itself; distinct sentences sharing syntactic role are conjoined; wholly unrelated sentences are not. Omitting conjunction may indicate maximal connection or complete separation.
  18:34/q19/p2 Fronting an object challenges the target's fitness for the action. — reasons: Al-Jurjānī's example a-Zaydan taḍrib questions whether anyone would dare strike Zayd.
  18:34/q19/p3 Several lexical and proverbial explanations address support, relief and hidden danger. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba glosses taṣfarr as lacking affection, makfūf
