## THE CURRENT MAP
20:44/q01 How does this command continue the joint mission? [فَقُولَا · links]
  20:44/q01/p1 God renews the address to Moses and Aaron together as partners in delivering the message. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī links the renewed dual command to the preceding joint address and warning against slackening. Sāmarrāʾī traces the shared mission, fear, reassurance, delivery, and Pharaoh's joint address across 20:29, 20:42, 20:63, 20:90, and 20:92.
20:44/q02 What does gentle speech mean? [قَوْلًۭا لَّيِّنًۭا · meaning] (turns on: The notes distinguish the plain manner of address from its figurative description.)
  20:44/q02/p1 Soft, courteous, accessible speech that invites rather than affronts the hearer. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr extends physical pliancy to fine meanings in speech, citing ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm's تلينا. Jishumī calls the description figurative and eloquent. Ibn ʿAṭiyya insists the full message be conveyed courteously. Ibn Kathīr says accessible words reach hearts. Ibn ʿĀshūr adds that the listener is treated as able to judge truth.
  20:44/q02/p2 Calling speech gentle is a figurative transfer of physical softness to verbal meaning. — reasons: Jishumī calls it Qurʾānic metaphor; Ibn ʿĀshūr explains the source sense as moist, pliant touch and quotes ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm.
20:44/q03 How is لَّيِّنًۭا read? [لَّيِّنًۭا · readings]
  20:44/q03/p1 A lightened form līnan is reported instead of layyinan. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī records līnan. Ibn al-Jawzī names Abū ʿImrān al-Jawnī and ʿĀṣim al-Jaḥdarī and describes a quiescent yāʾ. Al-Samīn attributes a lightened form to Abū Muʿādh, likening it to the two forms of ميت. Al-Ālūsī reports a light nūn from Abū Muʿādh; the descriptions vary.
20:44/q04 What particular words or manner make the address gentle? [قَوْلًۭا لَّيِّنًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: The accounts differ over the actual wording and whether an honorific, an invitation, or promised benefits satisfy the command.)
  20:44/q04/p1 Use a courteous, complete invitation, exemplified by Moses' offer of purification and guidance in 79:18–19 and the message in 20:47. — reasons: Muqātil, Ibn Masʿūd, and others cite 79:18–19; Rāzī and al-Ālūsī add 20:47. Al-Bayḍāwī and Abū Ḥayyān stress question, offer, and counsel. Ibn ʿAṭiyya prefers conveying the full meaning gently. Abū Ḥayyān reports that some identify the whole message through the greeting to followers of guidance. Ibn al-Jawzī also transmits this invitation through Abū Ṣāliḥ from Ibn ʿAbbās and Muqātil.
  20:44/q04/p2 Address Pharaoh respectfully by his kunya. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿIkrima, al-Suddī, and others are named. Qurṭubī cites the Prophet's honoring a people's notable and use of Abū Wahb for Ṣafwān and Abū Ḥubāb for ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy. Rāzī's critics allow this as part of the command but say a kunya alone is too slight to exhaust it.
  20:44/q04/p3 Offer faith in exchange for continuing youth, rule, worldly pleasures, and, in many reports, Paradise. — reasons: Reports vary in details: Wāḥidī mentions health and wealth, ʿAmr ibn Dīnār gives Pharaoh's 409-year lifespan, and Qurṭubī has four hundred extra years. Some accounts include Hāmān's intervention. Rāzī's critics object that such benefits could compel faith and undermine responsibility.
  20:44/q04/p4 Say the testimony that there is no god but God. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī reports Muʿādh and Ibn ʿAbbās; Ibn Kathīr names ʿIkrima. Abū Ḥayyān says its gentleness is ease on the tongue. Al-Ālūsī calls this the most remote view and favors Ibn ʿAbbās's general courteous invitation.
  20:44/q04/p5 Tell Pharaoh he has a Lord and a return, with Paradise for faith and Fire for unbelief. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan's formulation is transmitted by Ibn al-Jawzī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Ālūsī; Ibn Kathīr calls it giving Pharaoh an excuse before destruction.
  20:44/q04/p6 Lead with a promise before a threat. — reasons: Al-Māwardī says enticement softens Pharaoh first. Abū Ḥayyān cites a poet's instruction; al-Ālūsī cites a maxim that promises soften ignorant tribes.
  20:44/q04/p7 Tell Pharaoh that God is nearer to pardon and forgiveness than to wrath. — reasons: Wahb ibn Munabbih gives this wording as reported by Ibn Kathīr.
20:44/q05 Which kunya is reported for Pharaoh? [لَهُۥ · reports] (turns on: Reports assign different kunyas to Pharaoh, with lists of two, three, or four.)
  20:44/q05/p1 Abū al-Walīd. — reasons: This is among the kunyas listed by al-Māwardī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī, Rāzī, Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, and Abū Ḥayyān; accounts vary over how many kunyas he had.
  20:44/q05/p2 Abū Murra or Abū Murrah. — reasons: ʿIkrima is cited for Abū Murra; several authors list it among three or four alternatives. Spelling varies.
  20:44/q05/p3 Abū al-ʿAbbās. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī, Rāzī, Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, and Abū Ḥayyān report this variant.
  20:44/q05/p4 Abū Muṣʿab. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās's report in al-Tustarī uses it; Ibn al-Jawzī attributes it to Abū Sulaymān al-Dimashqī; Abū Ḥayyān counts it among four.
20:44/q06 Why was gentle speech commanded? [فَقُولَا قَوْلًۭا لَّيِّنًۭا · rhetoric] (turns on: The sources give distinct purposes: persuasion, a caregiver's claim, proof, Moses' formation, and the order of admonition.)
  20:44/q06/p1 It makes admonition more persuasive and less likely to provoke a tyrant into obstinacy or violence. — reasons: Rāzī and al-Burṣawī say harshness increases tyrants' defiance; al-Burṣawī adds the danger of Pharaoh striking or killing Moses. Abū Ḥayyān calls the question and offer delicate. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says gentle wording better reaches the aim. Al-Tustarī cites the Prophet's saying that hearts love benefactors and hate mistreatment.
  20:44/q06/p2 Pharaoh had raised Moses, so gentleness acknowledges the caregiver's claim. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī likens it to a father's claim; Rāzī and al-Burṣawī extend it to respect owed parents. Al-Bayḍāwī offers this as another reason. Al-Ālūsī says the linking ف points chiefly to Pharaoh's transgression, not merely parental care.
  20:44/q06/p3 It establishes clear proof and removes Pharaoh's excuse before punishment. — reasons: Al-Tustarī adds that the magicians and others known to be guided could hear. Al-Zajjāj says proof requires messengers, since God's private knowledge is no human proof. Al-Zamakhsharī invokes 20:134, Abū Ḥayyān 17:15, and al-Burṣawī says delivery before punishment is God's custom.
  20:44/q06/p4 It trains Moses in patient restraint and gracious speech. — reasons: Al-Tustarī says the aim was to stir hearts, not predict Pharaoh's faith. Al-Burṣawī describes Moses as sharp tempered and says the injunction softens him; another account contrasts Muḥammad's merciful temperament with Moses' forcefulness and appeals to the maxim 'Adopt God's qualities' and 9:73.
  20:44/q06/p5 It begins the encounter softly, leaving room to consider, with firmness permitted after obstinacy. — reasons: Rāzī says gentleness comes first. Al-Burṣawī cites al-Asʾila al-Muqḥima and 16:125, a Persian couplet joining gentleness with firmness, and contextual judgment. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 29:46 and Moses' later warning in 20:48.
20:44/q07 What conduct does this command teach callers and family members? [قَوْلًۭا لَّيِّنًۭا · law] (turns on: The practical applications differ by audience and stage of admonition.)
  20:44/q07/p1 Invite and correct people gently, even the wicked or powerful, with wisdom and good counsel. — reasons: Qurṭubī connects 2:83 and says protection matters when admonishing rulers; Ibn Kathīr cites 16:125. Ṭabrisī says gentleness encourages acceptance. Asad applies the lesson to every effort at conversion; Iṣlāḥī describes a teacher's sympathy, rejects arrogant prophetic self assertion and compares worldly propaganda. Kuranyolu stresses method and tone.
  20:44/q07/p2 A child may admonish a parent calmly but must avoid harsh rebuke or striking, and should fall silent when the parent becomes angry. — reasons: Al-Burṣawī cites al-Ḥasan via al-Iḥyāʾ and distinguishes a student confronting a scholar who fails to practice knowledge.
  20:44/q07/p3 It is permissible to use an unbeliever's respectful kunya. — reasons: Qurṭubī says the hope of conversion is useful but not a necessary condition. Al-Ālūsī draws the same permissibility from the transmitted kunya account.
  20:44/q07/p4 Firm speech can follow if gentle invitation fails. — reasons: Rāzī, al-Burṣawī, and Ibn ʿĀshūr present the shift as conditioned by stubborn rejection; al-Burṣawī says either manner may be praised or blamed according to circumstances.
20:44/q08 What inward meaning is drawn from the gentle address? [قَوْلًۭا لَّيِّنًۭا · inward] (turns on: The sayings draw different spiritual analogies from the same gentle command.)
  20:44/q08/p1 God's gentleness to a defiant claimant of divinity suggests still greater gentleness toward servants and worshippers. — reasons: Yaḥyā ibn Muʿādh is repeatedly quoted, often weeping. Al-Nahrajūrī contrasts God's enemy with a worshipper. Other sayings contrast the one who harms with one harmed for God, or the enemy with God's allies. Al-Faḍl and Yazīd al-Raqqāshī say God endears Himself even to opponents.
  20:44/q08/p2 The heart and intellect should gently invite the soul to obey divine command and law. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī reads Moses and Aaron as heart and intellect, Pharaoh as soul, and the hoped for softening as obedience.
  20:44/q08/p3 Gentleness repays Pharaoh's kindness in raising Moses. — reasons: Al-Nahrajūrī reports God telling Moses that Pharaoh's early kindness had not otherwise been repaid; al-Māwardī repeats the explanation.
