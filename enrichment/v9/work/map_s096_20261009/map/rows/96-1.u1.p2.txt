96:1/q07 Why is 'Read' repeated later in the opening passage? [ٱقْرَأْ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether repetition reinforces one command or distinguishes purposes of two readings.)
  96:1/q07/p1 The second command reinforces and comforts after the Prophet's hesitation. — reasons: BURHAN (الأصمعي): The anonymous poetic lines repeat النوى, separation, and al-Aṣmaʿī jokes that the second version deserved a she-goat, illustrating the effect of repeated wording. BURHAN: Poetic repetition of حديث and the beloved's dwelling shows how repeating a subject can delight the listener. BIQAI-FULL: Repeating “Read” emphasizes the command, comforts the Prophet, and distinguishes the One who brings revelation from the false objects of worship; the Prophet had said he could not read. BURSEVI: Iqra is a command, repeated to reinforce the obligation and introduce a new sentence about the most generous Lord; a permissible pause follows خلق because the Prophet had objected that he could not read. ALUSI-FULL: Al-Alusi says the clearest interpretation is that the second Read reinforces the first; the proposal to attach the basmala's باسم to the second command to make God's name the exclusive starting point is far-fetched because it normally attaches to the adjacent command. IBNASHUR-FULL: The second command to recite repeats the first to give it additional emphasis. BINTSHATI: She prefers the straightforward context: the command to recite is reiterated, and revelation specifies what the Prophet should read in his Lord’s name.
  96:1/q07/p2 The first is personal or general reading; the second is public proclamation. — reasons: ALUSI-FULL (الجبائي): Al-Jubbai says the first Read commands personal reading or reading generally, while the second is reading for proclamation; another view connects the latter to prayer, and the Lord's generosity clause could be circumstantial or independent. BINTSHATI (بعض المفسرين): Some exegetes, as al-Razi transmits, say the first اقرأ is for the Prophet himself and the second for proclamation to others.
  96:1/q07/p3 The two commands distinguish reading in prayer from reading outside it. — reasons: ALUSI-FULL (الجبائي): Al-Jubbai says the first Read commands personal reading or reading generally, while the second is reading for proclamation; another view connects the latter to prayer, and the Lord's generosity clause could be circumstantial or independent. BINTSHATI (بعض المفسرين): A third reported interpretation says the first recitation is in prayer and the second outside prayer.
  96:1/q07/p4 The first is learning; the second teaching. — reasons: BINTSHATI (بعض المفسرين): A second reported interpretation makes the first command to read for learning and the second to teach.
96:1/q08 What does 'in the name of your Lord' direct the reader to do? [بِٱسْمِ رَبِّكَ · interpretation] (turns on: The force of bāʾ: beginning with invocation, direct object, aid, dedication, accompaniment, or permission.)
  96:1/q08/p1 Begin recitation by invoking God's name, often with the basmala. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj supplies an understood 'I began'; al-Māwardī and al-Kashshāf take the phrase as beginning recitation while saying bismillah. Ibn ʿAṭiyya cites 11:41, the command to board in God's name. Al-Qurṭubī parses it as a circumstantial accusative; al-Nasafī likewise says say bismillah and then read. BIQAI: Reading in the Lord's name may mean beginning with the basmala, seeking help from the beneficent Lord, or mentioning His name; on the last reading, the name stands for His sublime attributes. BIQAI: The opening functions like a longer, more reassuring basmala: it alludes to the Greatest Name through the full set of perfect attributes, universal mercy through creation, and particular mercy through honor and teaching that guides to divine approval. BIQAI-FULL: “In the name of your Lord” means beginning with the basmala, seeking the benefactor's aid, or mentioning His name; in the last reading, the name stands for His exalted attributes. ALUSI: The bāʾ is original and marks association; it attaches to iqraʾ or to an omitted circumstantial expression, so the command means begin or open by God's name. ALUSI (قتادة): A reading attributed to Qatāda takes the bāʾ phrase as an omitted circumstantial expression: read while beginning with your Lord's name. ALUSI: On this reading one must begin by saying in God's name; opening with another name would not fulfill the command. ALUSI (قتادة): Qatāda explains the command as beginning in the Lord’s name: say “In God’s name,” then recite; without the basmala, or with an idol’s name, the command would not be fulfilled. ALUSI-FULL (قتادة): According to a reading attributed to Qatada, the command means begin or open with your Lord's name, saying the basmala and then reading; this supports requiring basmala when reading and has been used to argue it belongs to every surah, though al-Alusi notes debate. ALUSI-FULL: Al-Alusi prefers the ba as accompaniment because that avoids implying God's name is an instrument; it may alternatively indicate seeking aid. ELMALILI: The command miraculously gave the unlettered Prophet a book to recite, teach, and dictate without writing himself; beginning with the basmala accords with God's lordship. ELMALILI: The command means to begin reading in the name of the Lord, starting with God's exalted name. ELMALILI: The third command began the Qur'an and simultaneously miraculously made the unreading Prophet able to read, fixed the text to be recited, and taught him to introduce the Creator, as related to 29:48 and 42:52.; cites 29:48, 42:52 IBNASHUR: One analysis begins the recitation with “In God's name”: bi marks assistance and attaches either to an omitted “begin” or to a second “read,” fronted to emphasize God's name; it implies Muhammad uttered the phrase on receiving the revelation. TABATABAI: ‘In the name of your Lord’ attaches to an implied ‘beginning’ or to ‘read’ with the ba of accompaniment; the basmala is part of the surah and God teaches by having the Prophet begin with it. BINTSHATI: The command is to recite in the name of the Creator, the rightful Lord rather than the idols the Arabs had fashioned and then worshiped. BINTSHATI (الفخر الرازي): Al-Razi takes the command to recite in God’s name as implying that every Quran recitation should begin with God’s name. CORPUSCORANICUM (Nöldeke and Schwally): Nöldeke and Schwally object that qaraʾa elsewhere in the Quran means recite from a text or memory, supporting the usual translation recite in the name of your Lord.; cites 16:98, 17:93, 69:19, 73:20, 87:6 KURANYOLU-TEFSIR: Reading can mean in the Creator Lord's name or for the Creator; every reading or activity should begin with, serve, and seek help from God rather than another being.
