20:54/q13 Why is reason called al-nuhā? [ٱلنُّهَىٰ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the name derives from restraining oneself or others, or from reaching and following authoritative judgment.)
  20:54/q13/p1 Reason restrains its possessor from shameful, forbidden, false, or destructive conduct and directs good action. — reasons: Tabari adds intellect, comprehension, and religion as restraints on harm. Zajjaj and Ibn Jawzi say reason also leads to good. Lihyani, reported by Wahidi, says the mind stops at what it is commanded and does not overstep. Abu Ali derives it from confinement, like a place holding water; Wahidi says the etymologies converge. Bursawi and Ibn Ashur compare ʿaql and ḥijr; Study Quran notes the root's sense of restraint or prohibition. Tabatabai specifies following desire as what it restrains.
  20:54/q13/p2 They follow their reached judgment, or others defer to their opinions as the endpoint of judgment. — reasons: Zajjaj approves the linguists' explanation; Abu Ishaq, reported by Wahidi, considers following reason's judgment better than the restraint derivation. Mawrdi, Jishumi, Qurtubi, Nasafi, and Tabrisi also give the idea that judgment returns to these people.
  20:54/q13/p3 They restrain other people from indecencies. — reasons: Tabrisi's formulation makes others, rather than the possessor, the object of restraint.
20:54/q14 Why are the signs assigned to people of al-nuhā? [لِّأُو۟لِى ٱلنُّهَىٰ · interpretation]
  20:54/q14/p1 People of discerning reason reflect on the signs, learn from them, and take admonition, so they benefit fully. — reasons: Tabari stresses reflection and learning. Bursawi says the signs address everyone but those with intellect benefit. Alusi says only rational people recognize their full force and explains why people, rather than livestock, receive the address. Biqai says blindness to the signs makes unused reason effectively absent, and the point is stressed against deniers. Wahidi's Ibn Abbas report calls the signs a lesson for people who restrain disobedience.
20:54/q15 Why is al-nuhā used here among words for understanding? [لِّأُو۟لِى ٱلنُّهَىٰ · rhetoric]
  20:54/q15/p1 Synonymous expressions are placed in distinct Qur'anic locations. — reasons: Itqan contrasts Taha's لأولي النهى with وليذكر and أولو الألباب elsewhere.
20:54/q16 Who speaks the verse in the surrounding Moses and Pharaoh passage? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the first-person 'We brought forth' marks a shift from Moses' address to direct divine speech.)
  20:54/q16/p1 God speaks directly, interrupting the past account to address the present audience. — reasons: Razi argues that making the prior phrase Moses' speech conflicts with the shift to 'We brought forth.' Abu Hayyan says God continues after Moses' proof. Islahi calls four verses a divine insertion addressing Quraysh, identifying their true Lord and rightful object of worship and obedience; the first-person wording confirms God's speech.
  20:54/q16/p2 Moses says this to Pharaoh. — reasons: Muqatil places the lesson in divine oneness within Moses' address to Pharaoh. Razi rejects parses that keep this speech with Moses because of the subsequent first-person shift; Islahi also reads the verses as inserted divine speech.
20:54/q17 Who is credited with the cited poem challenging Pharaoh? [* · reports] (turns on: The differing attributions reported from Ibn Ishaq and Ibn Hisham.)
  20:54/q17/p1 Zayd ibn Amr ibn Nufayl. — reasons: Biqai reports Ibn Kathir citing Ibn Ishaq's Sira attribution. The poem challenges Pharaoh to claim he made unsupported earth, raised an unpillared sky, gave it light, brings the sun, and grows crops and grain.
  20:54/q17/p2 Umayya ibn Abi al-Salt. — reasons: Biqai reports Ibn Hisham's alternative attribution of that same poem, whose challenge names earth, sky, light, sun, crops, and grain.
