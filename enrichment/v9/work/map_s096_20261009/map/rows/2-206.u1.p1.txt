2:206/q10 How does the conditional clause connect syntactically with what precedes? [وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the conditional is independent or extends the earlier syntactic description.)
  2:206/q10/p1 It is a fresh sentence. — reasons: Abu Hayyan offers an independent sentence, recalling the comparable conditional passive at 2:11; Samin also gives a fresh statement.
  2:206/q10/p2 It is coordinated within the preceding relative description. — reasons: Abu Hayyan gives inclusion in the relative clause; Samin gives coordination with yuʿjibuka. Both point to a comparable passive construction at 2:11; Samin notes debate about its deputy subject.
2:206/q11 What does the response to advice teach? [ٱتَّقِ ٱللَّهَ أَخَذَتْهُ ٱلْعِزَّةُ بِٱلْإِثْمِ · interpretation] (turns on: The moral and practical implications drawn from accepting or rebuffing counsel.)
  2:206/q11/p1 Rejecting sincere correction from pride is a grave sin, and one should accept advice humbly. — reasons: Ibn Mas'ud repeatedly calls 'mind yourself' to an admonisher one of God's gravest sins, or an unforgiven sin; Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi warn believers against such conduct. Jishumi calls arrogant refusal a major sin approaching unbelief; Kur'an Yolu says pride obstructs honest deliberation and taqwa.
  2:206/q11/p2 Humility when admonished is exemplified by Umar and others. — reasons: Baghawi, Abu Hayyan and Bursavi report Umar putting his cheek on the ground; Durr adds Umar's statement that there is no good without candid admonition. Sufyan reports Malik ibn Mughawil likewise laying his cheek down; Harun al-Rashid dismounted, prostrated and granted a Jewish petitioner's request when the verse came to mind.
  2:206/q11/p3 A judge may discipline a litigant for 'be just', but not for 'fear God'. — reasons: Alusi distinguishes the two forms of rebuke in judicial speech.
2:206/q12 How is ٱتَّقِ pronounced here? [ٱتَّقِ · readings]
  2:206/q12/p1 Its qāf has either pure kasra or a blend of ḍamma and kasra; both are canonical and mean the same. — reasons: Qiraat-ER says the blended vowel preserves an archaic pronunciation.
2:206/q13 What does فَحَسْبُهُۥ جَهَنَّمُ mean? [فَحَسْبُهُۥ جَهَنَّمُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether 'suffices' is read as requital, reversal of pride, exclusive fate, or an inward foretaste.)
  2:206/q13/p1 Hell is sufficient punishment or recompense for his wrongdoing. — reasons: Tabari, Tha'labi and many later authors give this reading; Wahidi and Razi compare a dirham sufficing someone, while Imru al-Qays's verse uses hasb for contentment and drink sufficing as wealth. Ibn Ashur says sufficiency usually means fitness, here fitting recompense, citing Abu al-Tayyib and 3:173; Study Quran observes positive and negative Quranic use.
  2:206/q13/p2 Hell is the humiliation that answers his worldly pride. — reasons: Ibn al-Anbari's alternative and Abu Hayyan set his pride against ultimate abasement; Biqa'i relates the place to how he prepared himself for corruption.
  2:206/q13/p3 Its sufficiency implies no further punishment beyond Hellfire. — reasons: Jishumi infers this from the wording.
  2:206/q13/p4 It includes present inner alienation, darkness and constriction before the greater punishment. — reasons: Qushayri reads a present lesser torment and cites 32:21, 'nearer punishment before the greater'.
  2:206/q13/p5 His conduct leaves him no destination except Hell. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu draws this consequence from the clause.
2:206/q14 What is the grammatical function of حَسْبُهُۥ and جَهَنَّمُ? [فَحَسْبُهُۥ جَهَنَّمُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether ḥasb is an ordinary inflected noun, participle-like predicate, or verb-like expression.)
  2:206/q14/p1 Ḥasbuhu is the subject and Jahannam its predicate. — reasons: Samin and Alusi call this primary; Abu Hayyan describes ḥasb as a noun for sufficiency. He and Samin say it takes annexation without becoming definite, has no number or gender inflection, may take an extra bāʾ when subject, and lacks it as predicate. Wahidi says the triliteral verb is obsolete, while aḥsabani al-shayʾ means that a thing sufficed me.
  2:206/q14/p2 Jahannam is the subject and ḥasb an active-participle-like predicate whose agent fills its predicate slot. — reasons: Abu al-Baqa' gives this analysis, supported by the linking fāʾ; Abu Hayyan and Alusi report related analyses.
  2:206/q14/p3 Ḥasb is an indeclinable noun or particle of action meaning 'sufficed' or 'suffice'. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and Samin reject this: ḥasb takes prepositions, is adjectival and inflects for case. Alusi also questions it.
2:206/q15 What does the name جَهَنَّمُ refer to? [جَهَنَّمُ · referent] (turns on: Whether Jahannam names the whole abode or one of its levels.)
  2:206/q15/p1 It is a proper name for the abode or fire of punishment. — reasons: Wahidi's grammarians call it the afterlife's fire; Baydawi says a proper name of the abode, originally a synonym for fire or a loanword; Ibn Ashur notes the Quran calls it fire and describes people and stones as fuel at 2:24.
  2:206/q15/p2 It may name the lowest level of Hell. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and Alusi report this narrower referent as an alternative.
2:206/q16 Is جَهَنَّمُ Arabic or borrowed, and how is it declined? [جَهَنَّمُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the deep-well evidence reflects a native root or comparison, and whether faʿnal is an Arabic pattern.)
  2:206/q16/p1 It is a foreign or Arabized proper name, indeclinable through foreign origin and definiteness. — reasons: Yunus and most grammarians say this; Ibn al-Anbari agrees. Abu Hayyan, Samin and Alusi report derivation from Persian Kehnam/Kahanam with kāf changed to jīm and alif lost; Ibn Ashur also reports a Hebrew possibility and says most grammarians prefer foreign origin. Ibn Jawzi cites Ru'ba and al-A'sha's nondeclension as Arabicized foreign usage; Elmalılı reports kehennam.
  2:206/q16/p2 It is Arabic, connected with depth or harshness; its nūn is extra and its pattern faʿnal, indeclinable as feminine proper name. — reasons: Others derive it from the deep bottom of the fire, supported by Ru'ba's deep-well word. Abu Hayyan supplies jahm, harshness, and extra nūn, defending faʿnal with ḍaghanṭ, safanj, hajanf and zūnak, including Hassan's verse. Samin prefers this and rejects denial of faʿnal; Alusi reports the derivation but rejects a proposed faʿnall pattern. Elmalılı gives a bottomless foul well.
  2:206/q16/p3 It is Arabic with an original nūn and a faʿlal pattern. — reasons: Samin reports this rival analysis, based on the claim faʿnal is absent; he and Abu Hayyan rebut that claim with attested patterns.
  2:206/q16/p4 The deep-well expression does not establish an Arabic etymology. — reasons: Ibn Ashur argues the well may simply be likened to Hell for depth and fire; he treats etymological pursuit as unnecessary under the prevailing foreign-name analysis.
2:206/q17 Why use the name جَهَنَّمُ here? [جَهَنَّمُ · rhetoric] (turns on: The significance assigned to the particular name in this context.)
  2:206/q17/p1 Its harsh reception fits the man's confrontational response to counsel. — reasons: Biqa'i says the name evokes grim face-to-face reception, and reports al-Harali's principle that the Fire's particular name suits each judgment.
  2:206/q17/p2 Its depth and darkness express the lowest spiritual state. — reasons: Qashani reads Jahannam as ultimate depth and darkness of a low inner rank, using the deep-pit name.
2:206/q18 How is Hell depicted as fire? [جَهَنَّمُ · interpretation]
  2:206/q18/p1 A dark fiery oven fueled by people and stones. — reasons: Ibn Ashur cites a poem attributed uncertainly to Waraqa ibn Nawfal or Umayya ibn Abi al-Salt lamenting Zayd ibn Amr, alongside hadith and 2:24.
2:206/q19 What does ٱلْمِهَادُ mean? [ٱلْمِهَادُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the concrete bed image or the extended sense of settled abode is foregrounded.)
  2:206/q19/p1 A bed, bedding, cradle, or prepared place for sleeping or lying. — reasons: Majaz, Ibn Qutayba and many others give bed or resting place; Ibn Qutayba derives it from preparing a bed and a child's cradle. Wahidi links it to leveling; Ibn Atiyya to one's own mattress; Razi cites 51:48 and 30:44 for spreading and preparation; al-Harali adds a comfortable trodden surface.
  2:206/q19/p2 A settled abode or evil settlement. — reasons: Al-Hasan emphasizes permanence beneath the occupant; Razi compares 14:29's evil settlement, and Tabari calls it what the hypocrite prepared for himself.
2:206/q20 Is ٱلْمِهَادُ singular or plural? [ٱلْمِهَادُ · grammar] (turns on: The morphological analysis of the noun.)
  2:206/q20/p1 It is the plural of mahd, a prepared bed or cradle. — reasons: Wahidi, Qurtubi and Samin explicitly give the plural analysis; Abu Hayyan reports it as one option.
  2:206/q20/p2 It is a singular noun for a prepared bed. — reasons: Samin reports the alternative; other gloss
