  20:71/q12/p1 Opposite limbs, ordinarily the right hand and left foot, or the reverse. — reasons: Muqātil, Yaḥyā, Wāḥidī, Kashshāf, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ṭabrisī, Rāzī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī give right hand and left foot. Baghawī allows the reverse; Biqāʿī describes one limb from each side. Ālūsī says this particular pairing follows external explanation, since the verse itself allows other opposite pairings. Abū Ḥayyān calls the act mutilation; Iṣlāḥī views it as punishment for insurgents, displayed publicly.
20:71/q13 How does مِّنْ خِلَٰفٍ work grammatically? [مِّنْ خِلَٰفٍ · grammar] (turns on: Whether min retains its originative or partitive force, or stands in for another preposition.)
  20:71/q13/p1 Min marks origin or the starting point of cutting, and the phrase is circumstantial: the limbs differ in side. — reasons: Kashshāf, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Rāzī, Samīn and Ibn ʿĀshūr give the circumstantial analysis; Ibn ʿĀshūr compares 7:124 and 5:33. Bursawī distinguishes mere difference from opposition. Ālūsī takes min as partitive or originative, and reports an alternative attachment to an omitted verbal noun, cutting begun from the opposite side.
  20:71/q13/p2 Min may substitute for ʿan or ʿalā, expressing cutting from or on opposite sides. — reasons: Ṭabrisī offers both prepositions and treats the phrase as an accusative circumstantial expression. Al-Farrāʾ allows ʿan, ʿalā and bāʾ in such phrases. Ālūsī explicitly rejects Ṭabrisī's substitution analysis.
20:71/q14 Why specify crosswise amputation? [مِّنْ خِلَٰفٍ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the specified pattern signals exceptional terror or a known penal practice that avoids disabling or killing the victim outright.)
  20:71/q14/p1 It makes the threat more dreadful and concrete. — reasons: Bursawī says this method heightens terror; Ālūsī reports that some call it the more dreadful method and says the specification gives an assured, definite threat.
  20:71/q14/p2 It follows a customary penal pattern that preserves the use of remaining limbs. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says this was apparently the customary mark of criminals rather than a uniquely painful method; he compares the ḥirābah rule in 5:33, which cuts one limb per offense to preserve remaining use. Ālūsī says same-side cutting could kill victims and remove their usefulness.
20:71/q15 What does crucifying فِى جُذُوعِ ٱلنَّخْلِ describe? [وَلَأُصَلِّبَنَّكُمْ فِى جُذُوعِ ٱلنَّخْلِ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether fī is Arabic usage for on, a metaphor of firm enclosure, or a literal account of hollowed trunks.)
  20:71/q15/p1 Fastening them on palm trunks; fī can mean ʿalā or portray the body as firmly held or enclosed by its support. — reasons: Muqātil, Abū ʿUbaydah, Ibn Qutayba, al-Farrāʾ, Ṭabarī, Zajjāj, Wāḥidī, Jishumī, Rāzī, Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ibn ʿĀshūr and others cite Arab usage or poems about al-ʿAbdī, Abū Dhuʾayb, ʿAntara, al-Asadī or Suwayd. Muqātil, Wāḥidī, Ibn al-Jawzī and al-Farrāʾ compare 52:38; al-Farrāʾ contrasts the body aligned along a trunk with elevation upon it and compares 4:54. Jishumī retains containment as fī's core, and Kashshāf, Rāzī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Biqāʿī and others take a container metaphor; Rāzī calls a bare replacement gloss weak. Wāḥidī al-Wajīz specifies the tops; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says they are tied on trunks rather than mounted like a rider. Bursawī locates the palms along the Nile and defines crucifixion as fastening the back to wood; Ibn ʿĀshūr allows binding or nailing. Burhān says fī is more exact for the middle, whereas ʿalā suggests the top.
  20:71/q15/p2 The trunks were hollowed out and the victims placed literally inside, to die of hunger and thirst. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and Samīn report the literal account. Ālūsī also reports it but calls it scarcely credible.
20:71/q16 Why specify palm trunks and say 'in' them? [فِى جُذُوعِ ٱلنَّخْلِ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the choice depicts the position and duration of suffering or suggests ease compared with upward movement.)
  20:71/q16/p1 The wording stresses height, public visibility, firm confinement and the duration or horror of the punishment. — reasons: Nasafī says palms are named for height; Iṣlāḥī says public crucifixion makes an example of them. Biqāʿī says enclosure intensifies horror and deters others; Bursawī says the containment image suggests remaining there a long time. Burhān calls the trunk's middle more exact than its top and reports an image of the trunk as a grave. Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies judhūʿ as plural of judhʿ and compares 19:25.
  20:71/q16/p2 Fī suggests the ease of crucifixion, whereas ʿalā would suggest moving upward. — reasons: Burhān twice reports this explanation.
20:71/q17 Did Pharaoh carry out the threatened punishment? [فَلَأُقَطِّعَنَّ وَلَأُصَلِّبَنَّكُمْ · reports] (turns on: Whether narrative reports can establish an execution that this verse itself only threatens.)
  20:71/q17/p1 Yes: the magicians were cut, crucified and killed, becoming martyrs. — reasons: Al-Suddī reports killing and cutting; Naḥḥās and Qurtubī say the threat was carried out until death. Ibn ʿAbbās says they began the day magicians and ended martyrs after asking for patience and a Muslim death at 7:126. Ālūsī finds the apparent account unobjectionable and says the victory in 28:35 could be victory by proof rather than escape from execution.
  20:71/q17/p2 The Qur'an states the threat but does not settle whether Pharaoh carried it out; the reports are not conclusive. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī distinguishes the Quranic account from later reports; Ālūsī reports Rāzī's objection that reports do not establish the event.
20:71/q18 Was Pharaoh the first to use this punishment? [فَلَأُقَطِّعَنَّ وَلَأُصَلِّبَنَّكُمْ · reports] (turns on: How much historical weight to give the reports that assign Pharaoh first use.)
  20:71/q18/p1 Yes, he was the first to cut crosswise or crucify, or both. — reasons: Saʿīd says both crosswise amputation and palm-trunk crucifixion; Ṭabarī says Pharaoh first cut in that fashion. Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ibn Kathīr's Ibn ʿAbbās report, Bursawī and Ālūsī report priority in crucifixion or this punishment.
  20:71/q18/p2 Priority is not established by the reports. — reasons: Rāzī's reservation is quoted by Ālūsī, who nevertheless accepts the apparent account.
20:71/q19 Who are the two parties in أَيُّنَا? [أَيُّنَآ · referent] (turns on: Whether the earlier pronoun lahu points the comparison to Moses or Pharaoh instead answers the divine punishment invoked by the magicians.)
  20:71/q19/p1 Pharaoh and the Lord of Moses, or God whom the magicians believed. — reasons: Muqātil, Wāḥidī, Baghawī, Ṭabrisī, Qurtubī, Nasafī, Biqāʿī, Ibn ʿĀshūr and Asad take the divine comparison. Wāḥidī contrasts Pharaoh's punishment for belief with the Lord's for unbelief; Biqāʿī connects Moses' warning in 20:48, Ibn ʿĀshūr his warning in 20:61 and their avowal in 26:47. Ibn ʿAṭiyya prefers this as consistent with Pharaoh's fabrication; Bayḍāwī reports it as an alternative. Al-Baḥr al-ʿUlūm's divine referent for lahu supports it.
  20:71/q19/p2 Pharaoh and Moses. — reasons: Yaḥyā, Kashshāf, Rāzī, Bayḍāwī and Bursawī take Moses as the other party; Kashshāf and Bayḍāwī point to the earlier lahu. Rāzī and Kashshāf say Pharaoh vaunts his habitual cruelty and mocks a man who never tortured; Bursawī suggests he thought the magicians feared Moses after the staff swallowed their tools. Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports Ṭabarī's view but prefers the divine comparison; Ālūsī calls Ṭabarī and others' view preferable on the apparent pronoun.
20:71/q20 How is وَلَتَعْلَمُنَّ أَيُّنَآ أَشَدُّ parsed? [وَلَتَعْلَمُنَّ أَيُّنَآ أَشَدُّ · grammar] (turns on: Whether ayy is an independent interrogative that suspends the verb or a built relative object under the recognition sense of ʿalima.)
  20:71/q20/p1 Ayyunā is an interrogative subject, ashaddu its predicate; the question suspends the preceding verb's syntactic government. — reasons: Zajjāj and Abū Isḥāq say the preceding report cannot govern an interrogative, hence nominative ayyunā. Ṭabrisī says the verb still acts on it semantically. Samīn and Ālūsī say the clause fills two object places if ʿalima means know and one if it means recognize. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the two-person interrogative suspends taʿlamunna.
  20:71/q20/p2 If ʿalima means recognize, ayyunā may be a built relative object, with an omitted subject before ashaddu in its relative clause. — reasons: Samīn compares one analysis of 19:69; Ālūsī attributes the built relative analysis to Sībawayh.
20:71/q21 What does عَذَابًۭا modify? [عَذَابًۭا أَشَدُّ أَبْقَىٰ · grammar]
  20:71/q21/p1 It is a tamyīz clarifying 'more severe,' and it is understood with 'more lasting' too. — reasons: Ālūsī gives both the expressed and implied scope of the accusative noun.
20:71/q22 What does أَبْقَىٰ mean? [أَبْقَىٰ · meaning] (turns on: Whether abqā comes from the ordinary root for permanence or a remote allusion to Pharaoh's gifts.)
  20:71/q22/p1 More lasting or enduring, especially of punishment. — reasons: Muqātil, al-Farrāʾ, Baghawī, Bayḍāwī and Nasafī give the direct gloss; Ālūsī derives it from baqāʾ, permanence. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ surveys Qur'anic baqāʾ as opposed to perishing and as enduring good with God, including 20:131, 28:60, 42:36, 55:27, 87:17, 16:96, 18:46, 19:76, 21:86, 37:77, 43:28 and 2:248. Her cited Qatāda, Abū ʿUbayda and Rāghib discuss ghābir as remaining: in Lot's punishment, into old age, or like dust left behind; she prefers derivation from remainder or residue. Ṭabrisī's adjacent lexical glosses of īthār, tazkī and zakāt concern choosing and seeking growth or purification, not a competing gloss of abqā.
  20:71/q22/p2 It could allude to gifts that Pharaoh gives favored people. — reasons: Ālūsī reports this remote alternative, comparing Nimrod's boast, and calls it far-fetched.
