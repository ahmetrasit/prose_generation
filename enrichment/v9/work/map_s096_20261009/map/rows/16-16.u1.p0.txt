## THE CURRENT MAP
16:16/q01 What are the signs (ʿalāmāt)? [وَعَلَٰمَٰتٍۢ · referent] (turns on: Whether the signs are terrestrial route markers, stars that have their own marking role, a reported sea creature, or a broader class; Wāḥidī invokes coordination with earth's features and the separate mention of the star.)
  16:16/q01/p1 Perceptible landmarks and route indicators, especially for daytime travel. — reasons: Yaḥyā says God places them along routes. Ibn ʿAbbās distinguishes daytime landmarks from night stars (Ṭabarī, Māwardī, Ibn ʿAṭiyyah, Qurṭubī, Durr, Ālūsī). Ṭabarī includes trodden roads, mountains and stars; Ibn ʿAṭiyyah includes rivers and roads and favors this breadth. Rāzī, Abū Ḥayyān, Bursevī and Ālūsī add winds or even the smell of soil; Ālūsī connects masāfa with smelling. Bayḍāwī adds plains; Ibn Kathīr hills; Study Quran springs and wind vocabulary with 2:266, 17:69, 41:16, 51:41, 52:27. Ibn ʿĀshūr says God inspires people to establish or recognize markers for distances and safe routes. Wāḥidī derives ʿalāma from ʿilm; Jishumī, Ṭabrisī, Abū Ḥayyān, Biqāʿī, Ālūsī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī define a sign as a perceptible form conveying a hidden/intended meaning through writing, speech, gesture, appearance or convention.
  16:16/q01/p2 Mountains in particular are the signs. — reasons: Muqātil, al-Kalbī and others identify mountains; Muqātil cites a mountain-like landmark comparison and 55:14. Muḥammad b. Kaʿb and al-Kalbī say mountains guide by day and stars by night (Thaʿlabī, Wāḥidī, Baghawī). Wāḥidī favors mountains because the signs are coordinated with terrestrial creations and the following star is distinct; if they meant stars, he says, the wording would use 'by them.' Study Quran also cites mountains among wider landmarks.
  16:16/q01/p3 Stars themselves are the signs; some mark places and others guide. — reasons: Qatāda, Mujāhid and Ibrāhīm distinguish stellar markers from guiding stars (Ṭabarī, Thaʿlabī, Baghawī, Ṭabrisī, Qurṭubī). ʿAṭāʾ transmits this identification from Ibn ʿAbbās, though Wāḥidī rejects it for the terrestrial and grammatical reasons given above. Durr reports Ibrāhīm's markers in the sky and Mujāhid's division of markers and guides; Ibn Kathīr transmits Mālik's unusual formulation that the stars are the mountains.
  16:16/q01/p4 The signs are long, slender, colorful fish marking safe arrival near India. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyyah reports his father hearing an eastern scholar describe twisting fish in the Indian Ocean and reports several eyewitnesses; Abū Ḥayyān repeats the route from Yemen to India. Ālūsī calls this account far-fetched.
  16:16/q01/p5 The signs are mountain streams. — reasons: Al-Suddī is reported with this identification in Durr.
  16:16/q01/p6 The further signs include still undiscovered features of the earth. — reasons: Kuranyolu connects this wording with discoveries to come from physical geography, geology and biology.
16:16/q02 How is ʿalāmāt governed in the sentence? [وَعَلَٰمَٰتٍۢ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the accusative follows the preceding construction, expresses the purpose of what was made, or depends on a supplied verb.)
  16:16/q02/p1 It is coordinated with the preceding created rivers and paths or landmarks. — reasons: Wāḥidī, Ṭabrisī and Rāzī attach it to the preceding list. Islāḥī says Arabic leaves recoverable words unspoken in the path and landmark constructions and supplies them in translation.
  16:16/q02/p2 It is an accusative of purpose or verbal-noun-like indication: these things were made as signs for learning the routes. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyyah says they teach people on every route, including mountains, rivers and roads; Abū Ḥayyān reports this analysis.
  16:16/q02/p3 An implicit verb governs it: God created signs for you. — reasons: Ṭabrisī reports this alternative supplied verb.
16:16/q03 How does the star clause connect to the signs clause? [وَعَلَٰمَٰتٍۢ وَبِٱلنَّجْمِ هُمْ يَهْتَدُونَ · grammar] (turns on: Where the sentence ends after 'signs' and whether the following clause is syntactically new or coordinated.)
  16:16/q03/p1 The sentence ends at ʿalāmāt, and 'by the star they are guided' begins a new statement. — reasons: Thaʿlabī and Baghawī report this stop; Wāḥidī and Rāzī attribute it to al-Akhfash. Ṭabrisī and Qurṭubī report the same possibility. Samin says the prepositional phrase attaches to yahtadūn.
  16:16/q03/p2 Signs and stars are both parts of God's provision of guidance, with the star clause coordinated with creation or guidance before it. — reasons: Qurṭubī reports the reading that both are made for guidance. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the star clause coordinates with the creation clause but expresses God guiding people by stars at night, distinct from daytime markers.
16:16/q04 What does the singular al-najm designate here? [بِٱلنَّجْمِ · referent] (turns on: Whether singular al-najm names the stellar class, a fixed named group, al-Jady alone, the Pleiades, or the pole star; the reported prophetic identification of al-Jady and Arabic usage are cited.)
  16:16/q04/p1 Stars as a class, expressed by a generic or collective singular. — reasons: Yaḥyā says the singular means the navigational group. Zajjāj compares singular dirham used of many coins; Wāḥidī says Zajjāj chooses the collective sense. Māwardī and Ṭabrisī specify fixed stars. Ibn Jawzī and Damghānī give the ordinary celestial sense; Ibn Jawzī also lists plant, emerging horn/tooth, Pleiades and stalkless plant as other Arabic senses. Abū Ḥayyān says the majority read it as a collective singular. Ibn ʿAṣfūr instead calls poetic singular for plural a metrical necessity, citing a star-disappearance line; Abū Ḥayyān supplies a parallel poem using singular-like 'throats'; Samin and Ālūsī transmit this argument.
  16:16/q04/p2 Al-Jady alone, the especially fixed navigational star. — reasons: Māwardī gives its fixed central position. Reports through Ibn ʿAbbās identify the Prophet's answer as al-Jady and connect it with qibla and land/sea navigation (Māwardī, Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ālūsī, Ṭabāṭabāʾī); Ālūsī says it should prevail if sound. Qurṭubī places the pole between al-Jady and the smaller Banāt Naʿsh. Abū Ḥayyān calls al-Jady the seventh star of the smaller Banāt Naʿsh, distinct from its lunar-station namesake. Ālūsī says astrologers use a diminutive to distinguish it from the zodiac sign, though another account calls that dialectal.
  16:16/q04/p3 The Pleiades, Banāt Naʿsh, the two Farqads and al-Jady as a set of guiding stars. — reasons: Muqātil says these remain fixed year-round and work with mountains for land and sea navigation; Thaʿlabī, Baghawī, Rāzī, Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī transmit al-Suddī's list. Names and spellings vary slightly, including Judayy/Jady and Furqad/Farqad.
  16:16/q04/p4 Al-Jady and the two Farqads. — reasons: Farrāʾ identifies these; Wāḥidī says he chooses the view. Abū Ḥayyān says the Farqads are the first two stars of the smaller Banāt Naʿsh. Ibn al-ʿArabī says these conspicuous, fixed stars can guide ordinary travelers, unlike stars requiring expert knowledge of risings and settings.
  16:16/q04/p5 The Pleiades alone. — reasons: Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān report this alternative and cite a poem about its rising at harvest and bent vegetation; Abū Ḥayyān also cites a star guiding Euphrates crossings. Ālūsī says al-najm conventionally names the Pleiades and cites a seasonal-affliction report.
  16:16/q04/p6 The Pleiades and Banāt Naʿsh. — reasons: Wāḥidī transmits this narrower list from al-Suddī.
  16:16/q04/p7 The unmoving pole star or North Star. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says this star does not move; Samin and Study Quran report it among possible particular referents.
16:16/q05 How is al-najm read and how are its forms explained? [بِٱلنَّجْمِ · readings] (turns on: The transmitted vowels and whether the unusual form is a regular plural, a lightening or dialect, or a plural with its wāw omitted.)
  16:16/q05/p1 The common reading has the singular al-najm. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyyah and Abū Ḥayyān call this the majority reading; Samin says it may name one particular star or the generic class.
  16:16/q05/p2 A reading vocalizes both nūn and jīm with ḍamma as a plural. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan is named by Ibn ʿAṭiyyah and Ṭabrisī; Samin names Ibn Waththāb, while Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī transmit differing attributions and forms. The analogies are suquf/saqf and ruhun/rahn. Abū Ḥayyān, Samin and Ālūsī favor a direct faʿul plural over derivation by deleting the wāw of nujūm.
  16:16/q05/p3 A reading has ḍamma on nūn and sukūn on jīm, a lightened plural or dialect form. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyyah names Yaḥyā b. Waththāb; Rāzī reports it among al-Ḥasan's forms. Qurṭubī adduces a verse for shortening; Abū Ḥayyān, Samin and Ālūsī allow ease of pronunciation or an independent dialect.
  16:16/q05/p4 The plural sounding form results from deletion of the wāw of nujūm. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyyah and Rāzī report this explanation; Samin compares a shortened plural and cites Abū al-Baqāʾ's khiyām/khaym analogy. Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī prefer treating the form as an ordinary plural.
16:16/q06 What does 'they find guidance' mean here? [يَهْتَدُونَ · meaning]
  16:16/q06/p1 They know, recognize and discern the route or direction. — reasons: Muqātil contrasts this with 4:98, where not finding a path means not knowing the way. Damghānī glosses hadā as knowing/recognizing and cites 20:82 and 27:92; Ibn Jawzī cites recognition of landmarks and 21:31, 27:41 and 43:10. Burhān and Itqān gloss hudā as knowledge in this very phrase.
