7:19/q15 Why does al-Aʿrāf omit al-Baqara's 'from it' after eating? [فَكُلَا · rhetoric]
  7:19/q15/p1 Al-Baqara repeats the Garden reference to fit its more honorific presentation. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī compares the added pronoun there with its omission here.
7:19/q16 What is the force of 'do not approach' the tree? [وَلَا تَقْرَبَا · interpretation]
  7:19/q16/p1 Avoid it, especially eating from it; barring approach blocks the path to the forbidden act. — reasons: Al-Jishumī glosses it as avoidance; al-Ṭabrisī and al-Ālūsī take eating to be forbidden. Al-Biqāʿī and Ibn ʿĀshūr say banning proximity is stronger than simply banning fruit, because approach may lead to eating. Al-Dāmghānī identifies lā as prohibitive; Ibn ʿAṭiyya notes the verb can be vocalized qaruba/yaqrubu.
7:19/q17 Was the tree prohibition binding, advisory, or a different kind of command? [وَلَا تَقْرَبَا · law] (turns on: Whether the utterance legislates a duty, advises against harm, or operates ontologically; disobedience is cited against the last view.)
  7:19/q17/p1 A binding prohibition and test of obedience. — reasons: Al-Jishumī attributes this to most; Qatāda says all creation is tested and Adam violated the trial. Al-Māwardī says the prohibition preserved knowledge and supplied a recompensable test. Elmalılı says obligation and divine limits applied even in Paradise. Al-Naḥḥās calls the wording a prohibition.
  7:19/q17/p2 A nonbinding discouragement or guidance against self-harm. — reasons: Al-Jishumī reports a nonbinding view; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says Adam was before earthly legal responsibility and the warning guided him away from harm, not violation of legislation.
  7:19/q17/p3 An ontological command. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports this view of the prostration and tree prohibition, but rejects it because ontological commands cannot be disobeyed whereas Iblis and Adam did disobey.
7:19/q18 What kind of thing can be called a 'tree'? [ٱلشَّجَرَةَ · meaning]
  7:19/q18/p1 Anything having a trunk. — reasons: Al-Jishumī gives this lexical range.
7:19/q19 Which tree was forbidden? [هَٰذِهِ ٱلشَّجَرَةَ · referent] (turns on: The species or special character assigned by different reports; al-Māwardī contests the colocynth report and Ibn ʿĀshūr doubts intrinsic knowledge effects.)
  7:19/q19/p1 Wheat or a wheat stalk. — reasons: Muqātil calls it a wheat stalk; al-Zajjāj, Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Māwardī, al-Jishumī, and Study Quran give wheat.
  7:19/q19/p2 A grapevine. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj reports this alternative. Ibn Abī Ḥātim names Ibn ʿAbbās, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, al-Shaʿbī, Jaʿd ibn Hubayra, al-Suddī, and Muḥammad ibn Qays; al-Māwardī attributes it to al-Suddī. Al-Jishumī and Study Quran also report it.
  7:19/q19/p3 A fig tree. — reasons: Ibn Abī Ḥātim attributes it to Qatāda, Mujāhid, and Ibn Jurayj; al-Māwardī gives Ibn Jurayj, and Study Quran records the species.
  7:19/q19/p4 The tree of immortality or eternity, whose fruit angels consumed. — reasons: Muqātil reports angels rubbing against it for immortality; Wahb describes intertwined branches and fruit eaten by angels. Ibn Judʿān calls it the tree of eternity; Study Quran notes the Tree of Everlastingness in 20:120.
  7:19/q19/p5 A barley tree. — reasons: Wahb says each grain was as large as a cow's liver, softer than butter and sweeter than honey.
  7:19/q19/p6 A date palm. — reasons: Abū Mālik gives this identification.
  7:19/q19/p7 A camphor tree. — reasons: Al-Māwardī attributes this to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib.
  7:19/q19/p8 The tree of knowledge. — reasons: Al-Māwardī attributes the name to al-Kalbī; Ibn ʿĀshūr reports a Torah-like explanation that the tree intrinsically awakened knowledge of good and evil, but finds that intrinsic mechanism unlikely. Study Quran contrasts the Biblical knowledge tree with Quranic identifications.
  7:19/q19/p9 A colocynth tree. — reasons: Ibn Isḥāq transmits it from the People of the Two Books; al-Māwardī knows no basis for the report.
7:19/q20 Why is the forbidden tree not specified by name? [هَٰذِهِ ٱلشَّجَرَةَ · interpretation]
  7:19/q20/p1 Knowing its species would bring no benefit. — reasons: Al-Bursevī cites Aḥkām al-Marjān: God would have named it if doing so benefited people. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the species reports are weak and refers the question to al-Baqara; Ibn ʿAṭiyya likewise points back to his Baqara discussion.
7:19/q21 Why was this tree placed under prohibition? [هَٰذِهِ ٱلشَّجَرَةَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the point lay in a moral test, a physical consequence, or a supposed intrinsic cognitive effect.)
  7:19/q21/p1 It served as a trial that set a limit amid abundant permission. — reasons: Qatāda says God tests all creation for obedience. Al-Māwardī says it preserved Adam's knowledge and gave an occasion for recompense. Al-Qushayrī calls spouse and tree means of ordeal within God's hidden allotment; al-Iṣlāḥī calls the one tree the pair's trial. Elmalılı says its vicinity tested their observance of limits. Ibn ʿĀshūr favors a training of resistance to appetite and developing human cognition through the first ancestor; he rejects an intrinsic knowledge-tree mechanism and compares the development with 2:31. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī makes it a manifestation of humanity's general disposition, followed by maturation under earthly commands.
  7:19/q21/p2 Eating its fruit caused bodily evacuation, unsuitable for the Garden. — reasons: Abū al-ʿĀliya supplies this physical consequence.
  7:19/q21/p3 Its own nature awakened knowledge of good and evil. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr reports the Torah-like explanation, but calls it unlikely, preferring human cognition's development through the test and comparing 2:31.
7:19/q22 How is 'this' in 'this tree' understood? [هَٰذِهِ · referent] (turns on: Whether the demonstrative binds the ban to one visible specimen or uses that specimen for the species.)
  7:19/q22/p1 One indicated individual tree. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya records this as a possible scope of the demonstrative; the lapse account attributed to Abū ʿAlī says Adam took the ban this way and ate another of the kind.
  7:19/q22/p2 The indicated tree stands for the whole kind. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya compares generic singular usage. Al-Sulamī's account says God intended the whole kind although Adam understood one individual; al-Jishumī reports the same mismatch.
7:19/q23 How is 'this' pronounced and derived? [هَٰذِهِ · readings]
  7:19/q23/p1 Ibn Muḥayṣin reads hādhī, preserving an older yāʾ form. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the final hāʾ of hādhihi replaces yāʾ at pause and survives connected speech. Al-Bayḍāwī derives hādhī from diminutive dhayyā, with hāʾ substituted for yāʾ. Al-Ālūsī instead treats hāʾ as compensation for deleted yāʾ, not a pause marker; he reports Ibn Jinnī's derivation of dhī from masculine dhā and dhayyā, reduction of one yāʾ and conversion of the other to alif to ease the ending.
7:19/q24 How is 'then you become' parsed? [فَتَكُونَا · grammar] (turns on: Whether fāʾ marks the forbidden act's causal outcome (subjunctive) or coordinates a second prohibition (jussive).)
  7:19/q24/p1 Subjunctive: becoming wrongdoers is the consequence of approaching. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj prefers this; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Ibn ʿĀshūr hold it. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the causation runs from the forbidden action, not from uttering the prohibition. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Burhān, and al-Ālūsī allow it as one parse.
  7:19/q24/p2 Jussive: it is coordinated with the prohibition. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj reports 'do not approach and do not become wrongdoers'; al-Naḥḥās calls the latter a coordinated consequence. Al-Bayḍāwī, al-Burhān and al-Ālūsī allow it. Ibn ʿĀshūr says this parse treats the result as an answer to the prohibiting speech act, by analogy with a condition; al-Burhān cites the formulation as a parallel.
7:19/q25 What does becoming 'wrongdoers' mean? [ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the description foregrounds harm to the pair, lost reward, or breach of God's commanded right; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī denies a legislative breach.)
  7:19/q25/p1 They would wrong or harm themselves. — reasons: Muqātil, al-Jishumī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Bursevī, and Ibn ʿĀshūr give self-harm; al-Jishumī calls the warning a threat. Al-Biqāʿī compares the wrongdoer to someone walking in darkness and says disobedience can destroy a high station. Al-Ṭabrisī specifies forfeiting a great reward. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī insists this is harm under guidance, not breach of a binding legislative command.
  7:19/q25/p2 They would violate God's command and right to obedience. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī calls wrongdoing doing what they had no right to do and gives the same gloss at 2:35. Ibn ʿĀshūr includes violation of God's right as an alternative route to the description; Elmalılı says crossing divine limits makes a person unjust to himself.
