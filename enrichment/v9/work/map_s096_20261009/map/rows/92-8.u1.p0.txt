## THE CURRENT MAP
92:8/q01 Who is the miserly, self-sufficient person? [مَنۢ · referent] (turns on: Whether reported names restrict a general description; al-Alusi objects specifically to Abu Sufyan because of his later Islam.)
  92:8/q01/p1 A general human type; named people are examples rather than limits. — reasons: Al-Razi cites 92:4 and 92:14 for the sūra's general scope. Al-Alusi says the wording is general despite reported occasions and treats named people as instances; Kuran Yolu likewise says its message and rulings remain general. Ibn Ashur describes the traits as those of a polytheist; Qutb and Kuran Yolu describe a selfish disbeliever.
  92:8/q01/p2 Umayya and Ubayy, the sons of Khalaf. — reasons: Ibn Masud names both; al-Durr's report sets Abu Bakr's purchase and freeing of Bilal against their efforts and identifies al-husna as the testimony of faith and the hard path as Hell.
  92:8/q01/p3 Umayya ibn Khalaf. — reasons: Ata and Ibn Abbas name Umayya; other reports contrast him with Abu Bakr, including the account of Bilal's purchase for a garment and ten coins.
  92:8/q01/p4 Abu Sufyan ibn Harb. — reasons: Muqatil and al-Kalbi name him, and reports contrast him with Abu Bakr. Al-Alusi questions this identification because Abu Sufyan later became a strong Muslim in Sunni accounts.
  92:8/q01/p5 The Ansari owner of a palm tree. — reasons: Ata's account says he refused a palm in Paradise when his tree's dates fell into a poor neighbor's yard; Abu al-Dahdah acquired and gave the tree to the neighbor. Ibn Abbas's version says the believer traded forty palms for it; the owner is set against the giver.
  92:8/q01/p6 Abu Jahl. — reasons: Tustari applies the traits to Abu Jahl; a narration via al-Tusti from Ibn Abbas names him as the occasion.
92:8/q02 What does بَخِلَ mean here? [بَخِلَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether withholding names wealth, a specified religious due, or the broader refusal of obedience and self.)
  92:8/q02/p1 He withholds wealth or charitable spending. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and al-Hasan say wealth that will not last; Tabari specifies God's bestowed bounty withheld from commanded uses. Al-Wahidi, Baghawi, and others specify good works or charity. Ibn Atiyya favors wealth because 92:11 asks how wealth helps when he falls. Bint al-Shati cites 92:11 and 92:18 and includes alms, charity, generosity and rightful recipients. Alusi rejects extending it to required actions.
  92:8/q02/p2 He withholds what is due to God, especially required spending. — reasons: Qatada and al-Mawardi say God's due; Tabarsi speaks of seeking sufficiency by withholding it. Biqai broadens it to what God commanded or encouraged; Bint al-Shati includes dues to rightful recipients. Elmalili says avoiding what is due.
  92:8/q02/p3 He withholds zakat after God enriched him. — reasons: Ibn Abbas states this narrower application in al-Tabari, Ibn Abi Hatim and al-Durr; al-Durr's same report glosses al-husna as affirmation of monotheism.
  92:8/q02/p4 He withholds obedience, faith, speech, or acts that religion requires. — reasons: Tustari specifies Abu Jahl's withheld obedience to God and Messenger. Ibn Atiyya extends miserliness beyond wealth to faith and required speech or acts. Al-Alusi finds withholding required actions unconvincing.
  92:8/q02/p5 He withholds both himself and his wealth. — reasons: Qutb treats both forms of withholding as part of the corrupting rejection of God and guidance.
92:8/q03 How does the unexpressed object of بَخِلَ work? [بَخِلَ · grammar]
  92:8/q03/p1 The object is omitted because stinginess with wealth is intended. — reasons: Ibn Ashur explicitly supplies wealth as the omitted object.
92:8/q04 Why does the passage place withholding at the head of this list? [بَخِلَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the ordering explains the traits' causal role or aims to exhort generosity.)
  92:8/q04/p1 Giving and withholding ground the later traits of faith and piety or denial and self-sufficiency. — reasons: Al-Irshad, quoted by al-Alusi, says they open their clauses despite being lower in rank because each is foundational to what follows.
  92:8/q04/p2 Naming withholding beside self-sufficiency urges Muslims to give. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says giving and miserliness are included within piety and self-sufficiency yet singled out to encourage giving.
92:8/q05 What does وَٱسْتَغْنَىٰ mean? [وَٱسْتَغْنَىٰ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the implied source of sufficiency is God, His reward, wealth and self, or worldly life; Corpus Coranicum also records a translation choice.)
  92:8/q05/p1 He considers himself independent of God, His mercy, commands, or guidance. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and Qatada say independent of his Lord. Tabari says he therefore does not seek obedience or more of God's bounty. Tustari calls Abu Jahl's independence a show; Ibn Atiyya says it claims no need of God or mercy. Al-Kashshaf and Nasafi say he fails to fear God. Ibn Ashur says a persistent unbeliever relies instead on idols and his people. Qutb includes guidance; Study Quran calls the independence an illusion, referring to 96:6–7.
  92:8/q05/p2 He feels no need of God's promised reward and does not seek it. — reasons: Al-Nahhas and al-Wahidi make reward the object. Kashshaf says he disdains what is with God and thus fails to fear Him. Thalabi and Baghawi tie this to denial of promised good; Biqai adds vain hopes.
  92:8/q05/p3 He relies on his wealth, possessions, power, or self as sufficient. — reasons: Al-Hasan says wealth; Ibn Atiyya pairs wealth with miserliness to intensify blame. Qashani describes love and hoarding of wealth veiling virtue and God. Elmalili adds imagined freedom from future need and protection. Islahi cites Qarun's boast in 28:78 and says all gain is from God. Kuran Yolu includes one's own power.
  92:8/q05/p4 He treats worldly pleasures or life as sufficient instead of afterlife bliss. — reasons: Kashshaf and Nasafi set worldly desires against Paradise, the opposite of God-consciousness in 92:5. Baydawi and Alusi agree. Islahi describes the one who makes this life ultimate and loses resolve for afterlife reward; Samarrai contrasts the seekers of this life and the next.
  92:8/q05/p5 He seeks independence from people and promised reward by vain hopes. — reasons: Biqai says this leaves him neither helping people nor working for the afterlife.
  92:8/q05/p6 The phrase means self-sufficiency, rather than acting arrogantly. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum prefers consistent 'self-sufficient' across three occurrences, including 96:7, over Paret's 'acting arrogantly'.
  92:8/q05/p7 It means acting arrogantly. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum attributes this rendering to Paret and prefers 'self-sufficient' for consistency.
92:8/q06 What force do the added sīn and tāʾ have in ٱسْتَغْنَىٰ? [وَٱسْتَغْنَىٰ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the augmented form intensifies the verb or expresses seeking.)
  92:8/q06/p1 They intensify the action. — reasons: Ibn Ashur compares istajāba, 'answered'.
  92:8/q06/p2 They mark seeking, so the miser seeks more wealth by withholding it. — reasons: Ibn Ashur reports this analysis as an alternative.
92:8/q07 How do the two sets of traits and fates correspond? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the comparison concerns paired traits within 5–10 or their larger arrangement across the sūra.)
  92:8/q07/p1 They form antitheses: giving/withholding, piety/self-sufficiency, affirmation/denial, and ease/hardship. — reasons: Abu Hayyan pairs giving with stinginess and piety with self-sufficiency. Ibn Ashur calls the pattern الجمع مع التقسيم. Corpus Coranicum also notes symmetry, behavior-to-consequence sequences and Neuwirth's cross-rhyme through vowel variation; the divine first-person plural authorizes the promises alongside the oath.
  92:8/q07/p2 The final stanza mirrors and reverses the earlier treatment of saved and damned. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum says earlier behavior precedes outcome, whereas the final warning and promise precede conduct; the outer blessed sections share ittaqa/atqa. Neuwirth places verse 11 with theological statements in 12–13.
92:8/q08 What do the contrasting descriptions teach about people's paths? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the paths describe present moral formation, withdrawal of grace, or their final destinations; Abu Hayyan objects to the grace explanation.)
  92:8/q08/p1 Human striving divides into generous and miserly paths with opposed outcomes. — reasons: Razi says deeds culminate in reward or punishment. Ibn Ashur reports the two groups as those enabled to ease and hardship or as the two triads in 5 and 8–9; 99:6–8 illustrates even tiny deeds' outcomes. Qutb calls them two ranks and says each receives facilitation. Islahi says 8–10 describe the character and belief ending in Hell. Bint al-Shati places diverse earthly endeavors under the oath of night, day and creation; Corpus Coranicum says virtue and vice receive eschatological outcomes.
  92:8/q08/p2 The miser's chosen course brings evil deeds and ultimately Hell. — reasons: Thalabi and Baghawi say he is turned toward disapproved deeds and punishment, and al-usra may name Hell itself. Qurtubi points to 3:180 for wealth's worldly consequence. Qashani says wealth veils God; Qutb says this rejection brings the greatest corruption. Islahi says God lets worldliness continue smoothly until death and then comes 74:17's steep hardship.
  92:8/q08/p3 God withdraws grace, making obedience especially hard. — reasons: Abu Hayyan reports Zamakhshari's argument from the constricted heart in 6:125 but rejects its Mu'tazili insinuation.
  92:8/q08/p4 The paths are named for their outcomes or are afterlife roads to Paradise and Hell. — reasons: Abu Hayyan reports Zamakhshari's alternative explanation of ease and hardship.
