 brings the first shiver and subsequent softening? [تَقْشَعِرُّ ثُمَّ تَلِينُ · interpretation] (turns on: The sources vary between a warning/mercy sequence and deeper stages of contemplation.)
  39:23/q23/p1 Warnings and punishment cause fear; mercy, promise and reward bring hope and softness. — reasons: Muqātil, Farrāʾ, Ibn Qutayba, Ṭabarī and most commentators make this temporal contrast. Ibn ʿĀshūr notes awe may attract believers but burden deniers; Burhān reconciles trembling with 13:28 and 8:2 as fear of deviation within settled conviction. Ibn Jurayj explicitly pairs warning with Paradise and hoped-for mercy. Biqāʿī notes bodily outer surfaces, intense reverent fear, and remembrance of divine majesty. Qurṭubī compares the response with the mountain's splitting and humility at 59:21.
  39:23/q23/p2 Awe arises from contemplating divine majesty, then serenity from beauty and oneness. — reasons: Rāzī says initial reward-and-punishment response is only the first level: divine transcendence beyond place and pre-eternity make the mind shiver, while reasoning about unity and necessary existence brings calm; Qushayrī and Bursevī allow fear/hope, contraction/expansion, disclosure/concealment; al-Nahrjurī contrasts beginners and advanced knowers. Māwardī also describes awe followed by softness on recitation.
39:23/q24 Whose skins shiver in this verse? [ٱلَّذِينَ يَخْشَوْنَ رَبَّهُمْ · referent] (turns on: Whether the phrase describes all God-fearing hearers, the Companions as exemplars, or a spiritual rank.)
  39:23/q24/p1 God-fearing believers, God's friends, and receptive hearers. — reasons: Ṭabarī, Qatāda and Ibn Kathīr describe friends who weep, shiver and settle. Ibn Kathīr contrasts attentive believers with listeners of song and cites 8:2–4 and 25:73; Ibn ʿĀshūr says believers are singled out because they subsequently soften, though deniers too may feel awe and turn away.
  39:23/q24/p2 Specifically the Prophet's Companions in the cited reports. — reasons: Asma describes their eyes watering and skin shivering during recitation; Study Quran reports some commentators' identification.
  39:23/q24/p3 In an inward reading, God-knowing scholars and seekers affected by luminous disclosure. — reasons: Qāshānī says light descending on hearts reaches their bodies; Nahrjurī distinguishes seekers' outward state from knowers' calm.
39:23/q25 How should hearers behave when moved by recitation? [تَقْشَعِرُّ ثُمَّ تَلِينُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether shivering and composure are the normative limits of sincere response, or swooning can be involuntary.)
  39:23/q25/p1 Weep, shiver, become calm, listen attentively and act; avoid contrived loss of control. — reasons: Qatāda says God's allies do not lose reason; Asma and Ibn ʿUmar contrast the Companions with collapsing hearers and blame Satan. Ibn Sīrīn proposes the rooftop or wall test; an anecdote of Moses rejects torn clothes without an opened heart. Ibn Kathīr values stillness and deliberate prostration; al-Zubayr's family and Ibrahim likewise challenge fainting and visionary claims. Rāzī reports Ghazālī's question why some swoon at love poems more than Quran; Rāzī contrasts Quranic majesty with poems of creaturely union and separation.
  39:23/q25/p2 Involuntary swooning may reflect weak capacity, and the verse need not exclude other states. — reasons: Ālūsī reports Sufi shaykhs' defense: weak hearts cannot bear influx, and the relative clause may concern firm saints rather than all believers. He calls swooning a deficiency in contrast to completed wayfarers, yet says involuntary reactions are not blameworthy.
39:23/q26 What benefit is reported for a shiver or tenderness from fear of God? [تَقْشَعِرُّ تَلِينُ · reports] (turns on: The reports promise remission, protection from Fire, or a propitious time for supplication.)
  39:23/q26/p1 Fear-induced shivering sheds sins like leaves from a dry tree. — reasons: Reports through al-ʿAbbās and others repeat the image; Ibn al-Jawzī, Biqāʿī and Māwardī state no grading here.
  39:23/q26/p2 God forbids the Fire to one whose skin shivers in fear. — reasons: A second report from al-ʿAbbās or Ibn ʿAbbās gives protection from Fire; Ubayy links tears from fear with that protection.
  39:23/q26/p3 Tenderness or gooseflesh is an occasion to pray and expect a response. — reasons: The report of Ubayy's recitation before the Prophet says to seize tenderness for prayer; Umm al-Dardāʾ and an unnamed worshipper describe shivering, fear and tears as signs of answered supplication. Bursevī reports Abraham's heart beating audibly from divinely instilled fear.
39:23/q27 What does talīnu mean here? [تَلِينُ جُلُودُهُمْ وَقُلُوبُهُمْ · meaning]
  39:23/q27/p1 Their bodies and hearts grow tender, calm, hopeful and ready to obey. — reasons: Ṭabarī and Thaʿlabī include acting on and affirming the Book; Qurṭubī adds turning to Islam; Qāshānī makes limbs compliant; Islahi excludes bodily arrogance and notes humble gait; Bursevī distinguishes literal soft skin and figurative inner character. Ibn Kathīr connects softening to God's promise.
39:23/q28 Why does talīnu take the preposition ilā? [تَلِينُ إِلَىٰ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the verb absorbs a motion/settling meaning or the preposition replaces li.)
  39:23/q28/p1 It incorporates settling, tranquility or inclining toward remembrance. — reasons: Zamakhsharī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, Biqāʿī, Bursevī, Ṭabāṭabāʾī and Islahi explain the verb's extended sense.
  39:23/q28/p2 Ilā is used with the sense of li. — reasons: Thaʿlabī reports this alternative prepositional explanation.
39:23/q29 What is the remembrance toward which they soften? [ذِكْرِ ٱللَّهِ · referent] (turns on: Whether dhikr is the servant's remembrance of God, implicit reward, the Quran, or God's remembrance of a servant.)
  39:23/q29/p1 Remembrance of God Himself. — reasons: Study Quran's rendering cites 13:28; Rāzī says the highest lover seeks God for His own sake and reads the phrase as approaching His unsensed presence.
  39:23/q29/p2 His mercy, Paradise, reward and promise, understood by ellipsis. — reasons: Muqātil and Ibn al-Jawzī give Paradise and reward; Ṭabrisī says the object is omitted. Zamakhsharī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Biqāʿī, Bursevī and Ālūsī say God is named because mercy precedes wrath and is first recalled.
  39:23/q29/p3 The Quran as God's Reminder. — reasons: Study Quran reports a Companions interpretation based on al-Dhikr as a Quranic title.
  39:23/q29/p4 God's remembrance of the person. — reasons: Study Quran offers this possibility with 29:45.
39:23/q30 Why are hearts named with softening but only skins with shivering? [جُلُودُ جُلُودُهُمْ وَقُلُوبُهُمْ · rhetoric]
  39:23/q30/p1 Fear in the heart is already implicit, while its outward mark is on skin; both soften in hope. — reasons: Zamakhsharī, Nasafī and Bursevī say fear's heart location is understood; Bayḍāwī and Abū Ḥayyān link inward fear and hope. Biqāʿī avoids implying hardened hearts; Ālūsī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī note hearts or souls do not physically shiver; Rāzī says hopeful disclosure fills heart and spirit more fully.
39:23/q31 Why does the verse say God's remembrance instead of naming mercy? [ذِكْرِ ٱللَّهِ · rhetoric] (turns on: Mercy evoked by the name versus devotion to God Himself.)
  39:23/q31/p1 The divine name evokes mercy before wrath and dignifies the passage. — reasons: Zamakhsharī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Biqāʿī, Bursevī and Ālūsī say mercy is primary or first recalled; Ālūsī cites 'My mercy preceded My anger.' Biqāʿī says explicit God avoids a misleading pronoun that could favor unionism and elevates the designation over discourse or book.
  39:23/q31/p2 The highest love is directed to God, not a distinct benefit. — reasons: Rāzī cites 6:125, 13:28, 2:40 and 2:152 for God-centered remembrance.
39:23/q32 What does dhālika refer to? [ذَٰلِكَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the demonstrative resumes the Book or the bodily and inward effects it brings.)
  39:23/q32/p1 The Quran, the Book just described. — reasons: Ṭabarī, Thaʿlabī, Baghawī, Zamakhsharī, Rāzī, Qurṭubī, Nasafī, Bursevī, Ālūsī, Islahi and Study Quran take the pointer to the Book. Rāzī invokes opening of the chest at 6:125; Bursevī 2:2. Al-Kalbī says verses and suras follow one another.
  39:23/q32/p2 The fear, shivering, hope and softening God grants the hearer. — reasons: Ṭabarī, Zajjāj, Naḥḥās, Zamakhsharī, Ibn al-Anbārī, Ṭabāṭabāʾī and others read the demonstrative as the described effect; Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects the Book reading, while Ālūsī dismisses this one. Biqāʿī includes both the discourse and its effect.
39:23/q33 How does God guide by it, and whom does He guide? [هُدَى ٱللَّهِ يَهْدِى بِهِۦ مَن يَشَآءُ · theology] (turns on: The agency of God's will, the hearer's readiness, and whether guidance denotes the Book or its effects.)
  39:23/q33/p1 God guides receptive servants through the Quran and its proofs. — reasons: Muqātil and Ṭabarī say the Quran guides to religion and belief; Jubbāʾī specifies recipients of its proofs; Ālūsī says reflection on divine origin enables guidance. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the revelation supplies the means fully and God prepares receptivity; Islahi says worthiness follows God's established practice. Ṭabāṭabāʾī connects receptivity with freedom from obstruction by sin. Asad accepts the syntactically possible rendering 'He guides therewith whomever He wills.' Bāqillānī says only divine guidance enables recognition of Quranic excellence and cites 2:26 for guidance and misguidance through it.
  39:23/q33/p2 God grants fear and hope themselves as guidance and enabling. — reasons: Ṭabarī and Zajjāj call the listener's shivering and softening divine enabling; Zamakhsharī calls it God's luṭf, which may even draw observers to imitate the believers.
  39:23/q33/p3 The person who wills guidance is guided. — reasons: Ālūsī considers referring the pronoun in 'wills' to the person, but calls it weak.
  39:23/q33/p4 Guidance can come directly from God or through guides
