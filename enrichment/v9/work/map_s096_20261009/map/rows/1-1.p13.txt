27] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | reports | Ibn Atiyya says that, on their face, the Jabir and Abu Hurayra reports imply the basmala is an ayah of al-Fatiha.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r28] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · أبي بن كعب عن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | An authentic report has the Prophet ask Ubayy to recite the surah; Ubayy begins with al-hamdu lillahi rabb al-alamin and continues to its end, without the basmala.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r29] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · الله تعالى في الحديث القدسي | reports | The sound hadith qudsi describing prayer as divided between God and the servant quotes the servant beginning with al-hamdu lillahi rabb al-alamin, not the basmala.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r3] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | holds | Prayer became obligatory in Mecca, and no report preserves a Muslim prayer without reciting al-Fatiha; this supports its Meccan origin.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r30] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | holds | No record says the Prophet or Abu Bakr, Umar or Uthman recited the basmala in prayer.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r31] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | holds | The surah is counted as seven verses by consensus, apart from the anomalous report that al-Husayn al-Ju'fi counted six.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r32] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · حسين الجعفي | rejects | Al-Husayn al-Ju'fi reportedly counts six verses in al-Fatiha; Ibn Atiyya calls this anomalous and unreliable.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r33] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · عمرو بن عبيد | rejects | Amr b. Ubayd makes iyyaka na'budu a separate verse, yielding eight; Ibn Atiyya also calls this anomalous.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r34] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | prefers | The statement that God gave the Prophet seven oft-repeated verses in 15:87 settles the verse-count question. | mentions 15:87
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r35] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · الشافعي | reports | Al-Shafi'i counts the basmala as a verse of al-Fatiha.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r36] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · قراء مكة والكوفة | reports | Many Meccan and Kufan reciters do not count an'amta alayhim as a verse.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r37] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · مالك، وأبو حنيفة، وجمهور الفقهاء والقراء | prefers | Malik, Abu Hanifa and the majority of jurists and reciters do not count the basmala as a verse.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r38] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | prefers | If sound, the Jabir and Abu Hurayra reports can be understood as lessons: the Prophet saw them rehearsing prayer outside prayer and taught the basmala for that reason, not because it was a verse.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r39] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | holds | The Prophet did not include the basmala in his exchange with Ubayy because he was identifying the special surah and its virtue, whereas that purpose was absent in the instructional exchanges with Jabir and Abu Hurayra.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r4] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · عطاء بن يسار، وسوادة بن زياد، والزهري محمد بن مسلم، وعبد الله بن عبيد بن عمير | reports | Ata b. Yasar, Sawada b. Ziyad, al-Zuhri and Abd Allah b. Ubayd b. Umayr instead regard al-Fatiha as Medinan.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r40] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · ابن المبارك | rejects | Ibn al-Mubarak holds that the basmala is a verse in every surah; Ibn Atiyya calls this an anomalous view rejected by others.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r41] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · الشعبي والأعمش | reports | Al-Sha'bi and al-A'mash report a progression in the Prophet's written basmala: first Bismika Allahumma, then Bismillah, then Bismillah al-Rahman, and after 27:30 the full formula. | mentions 17:110, 27:30
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r42] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · عمرو بن شرحبيل | reports | Amr b. Shurahbil reports that Gabriel first came to the Prophet saying, “Say: In the name of God, the Most Merciful, the Compassionate.”
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r43] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas reports that the first thing Gabriel brought down was the basmala.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r44] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · خديجة عن ورقة | reports | In some versions of Khadija's taking the Prophet to Waraqa, Gabriel first tells the Prophet to say the basmala; the Prophet says it, then Gabriel commands him to read, and he replies that he cannot read.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r45] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · بعض الناس | reports | Some say the nineteen letters of the basmala correspond one by one to the nineteen keepers of Hell in 74:30, whose repeated basmala gives them strength. | mentions 74:30
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r46] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | rejects | Ibn Atiyya calls the letter-count explanation a playful exegetical conceit, not sound scholarship.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r47] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | rejects | He compares that numerology with claims that the Night of Decree is the twenty-seventh night because of a word in its surah, and that thirty-some angels rushed to record a thirty-some-letter praise formula. | mentions 97:3
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r48] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | rejects | A similar letter-count claim says thirty-some angels competed to record “Our Lord, to You belongs praise, abundant, good and blessed praise”; Ibn Atiyya presents it as the same sort of numerology.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r49] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · نحاة البصرة | reports | Basran grammarians attach the preposition bi- to an implied noun such as ibtida'i, mustaqirr or thabit, making the whole basmala nominative in their analysis.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r5] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | holds | There is no disagreement that the surah is called Fatihat al-Kitab because its position makes it the opening of the Book.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r50] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · نحاة الكوفة | reports | Kufan grammarians attach bi- to an implied verb, “I begin,” and parse the basmala as accusative.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r51] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · سيبويه | reports | Ibn Atiyya says Sibawayh apparently attaches bi- to a noun, yet treats the phrase as accusative through attachment to thabit or mustaqirr, like Zayd is in the house.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r52] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | reports | The kasra on bi- is explained as matching its grammatical action, because it governs nouns, or to distinguish it from particles that can also be nouns.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r53] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | reports | Al-A'sha's use of kaf is cited as an example of a particle that can function as a noun, motivating a distinctive genitive mark for bi-.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r54] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | holds | The alif is omitted from the written basmala for brevity and ease because the phrase is so frequently used.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r55] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · الكسائي وسعيد الأخفش، ويحيى بن زياد | reports | Al-Kisa'i and Sa'id al-Akhfash omit the alif in bism al-Rahman and bism al-Qahir; Yahya b. Ziyad says omission applies only with Allah because that phrase is especially frequent. Ibn Atiyya says the alif remains with other names.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r56] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · نحاة البصرة | reports | Basran grammarians derive ism from sumuww, elevation, from sama yasmu; the form is associated with either damma or kasra in the initial consonant.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r57] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | holds | The diminutive sumayy, plural asma', and plural-of-plural asami support the Basran etymology; a poetic form simahu is also cited.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r58] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · نحاة الكوفة | reports | Kufans derive ism from wasm, a mark, since a name marks its bearer; Ibn Atiyya says this sense is apt but morphology counters it, since the forms should be wasim and awsām.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r59] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · الطبري | reports | Al-Tabari says the name-and-named question is not appropriate here and criticizes theologians for debating it and similar questions.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r6] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · الحسن بن أبي الحسن | reports | Hasan b. Abi al-Hasan objects to the title Umm al-Kitab, reserving that name for the lawful and unlawful, as in 3:7. | mentions 3:7
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r60] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | holds | A name such as Zayd or lion can mean its referent, as in Zayd stands, or the word itself, as in lion has three letters; only in the first use does the name mean the named.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r61] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · أكثر أهل العلم | reports | Many scholars take name to mean the named essence in 87:1, 55:78 and 53:23; they cite Labid's line about the name of peace as a greeting. | mentions 87:1, 55:78, 53:23
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r62] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | holds | More commonly, ism denotes the verbal name itself: examples include Adam being taught the names, the hadith of God's ninety-nine names, and grammarians' technical usage. | mentions 2:31
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r63] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · مالك | reports | When asked whether the name is the named, Malik answered that it is neither it nor other than it, meaning this is not true in every context.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r64] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | holds | Allah is the most striking and frequently used of God's names, usually preceding the others, which generally function as attributes.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r65] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · فرقة من أهل العلم | reports | One group of scholars treats Allah as an un-derived proper name, with alif-lam integral to the name rather than a marker of definiteness.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r66] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · كثير من أهل العلم | reports | Many scholars derive Allah from alaha, worship, or ta'allaha, devotion; Ibn Abbas and others gloss the Qur'anic wa-alihatak in 7:127 as your worship. | mentions 7:127
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r67] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · author | prefers | Three accounts explain the change from ilah to Allah: irregular loss of hamza before alif-lam, alif-lam added to ilah with hamza-vowel transfer and lam assimilation, or an original lah; Ibn Atiyya favors the first.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r68] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · الخليل | reports | Al-Khalil says the original was ilah or walah, with hamza substituted for waw as in ishaḥ/wishaḥ and isadah/wisadah.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r69] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · قيل | reports | Another account derives the word from walah, bewilderment: intellects are bewildered by the realities of God's attributes and by thought about knowing Him.
[IBNATIYYA:1:1/r7] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · ابن عباس وغيره | reports | Ibn Abbas and others accept Umm al-Kitab as a name for al-Fatiha.
