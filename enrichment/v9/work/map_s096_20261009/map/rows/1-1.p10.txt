16 · ثعلب | reports | Thaʿlab and the Kufans derive ism from wasm or sima, a mark that signifies the meaning and the named thing.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r21] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | prefers | Baghawi prefers sumuww: ism diminishes as sumayy and its verb is samiitu; a wasm derivation would yield wasīm and wasamtu.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r22] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · الخليل | reports | Al-Khalil and others treat Allah as a proper name specific to God, not derived, like human names Zayd and ʿAmr.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r23] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | reports | Others derive Allah from aliha, worship; Ibn ʿAbbas reads “your gods” in 7:127 as “your worship,” showing that God alone deserves worship. | mentions 7:127
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r24] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | reports | Another view takes Allah from the original ilāh, supported by the statement that if another god were with God each god would take its creation. | mentions 23:91
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r25] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · المبرد | reports | Al-Mubarrad links ilāh to Arabic alihtu ilayhi, finding calm with someone; creatures find repose in God and are comforted by remembering Him, as the cited line illustrates.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r26] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · المبرد | reports | Al-Mubarrad also cites a poetic use of alihtu ilayhi for fleeing or seeking refuge in someone.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r27] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | reports | Another etymology derives Allah from walah, with initial wāw changed to hamza like wishāḥ/ishāḥ: people flee to God in hardship and need as a child clings to its mother.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r28] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | reports | A further explanation derives it from walah as loss of reason at losing someone dear.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r29] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · ابن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbas calls Rahman and Rahim two tender names, one more tender than the other.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r3] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | holds | It is Umm al-Kitāb because it is the Qur'an's origin; umm means origin, as Mecca is Umm al-Qura, where the land was spread out from beneath it.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r30] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | reports | Some regard the names as synonyms meaning “possessor of mercy”; their repetition encourages hope among those who seek God.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r31] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · المبرد | reports | Al-Mubarrad reads the sequence as bounty upon bounty and favor upon favor.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r32] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | reports | Those who distinguish them say Rahman is universal mercy, providing for all creatures in this world, while Rahim is particular mercy and forgiveness for believers in the hereafter.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r33] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | holds | The invocation “Rahman of this world, Rahim of the hereafter” reflects universal Rahman but particular Rahim; people may call another merciful but not Rahman.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r34] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | reports | Mercy may mean God's willing good for its recipients, which makes it an attribute of essence.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r35] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | reports | Alternatively, mercy is withholding deserved punishment and giving good to the undeserving, which makes it an attribute of action.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r36] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | reports | The Medinan and Basran readers and Kufan jurists say the basmala is not part of any surah; it opens them for blessing.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r37] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | reports | Meccan and Kufan readers and most Hijazi jurists count it as part of al-Fatiha only, written to separate surahs.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r38] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | reports | Another position, held by al-Thawri, Ibn al-Mubarak, and al-Shafiʿi in one view, counts it in al-Fatiha and every surah except al-Tawba because it is written like the Qur'an in the codex.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r39] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | holds | All agree al-Fatiha has seven verses: those counting the basmala begin the final verse at “the path of those”; those excluding it begin the first at “praise be to God” and the last at “not those angered.”
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r4] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | reports | Another explanation of Umm al-Kitāb is that it precedes and leads the following surahs, being first written in codices and read in prayer.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r40] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | reports | Those who count the basmala as part of al-Fatiha and the surahs cite its writing in the codex in the same script as Qur'anic text.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r41] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · ابن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbas, through Saʿid ibn Jubayr, identifies the basmala as the seventh verse of the seven oft-repeated, saying God reserved it for this community; it supports counting it in al-Fatiha. | mentions 15:87
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r42] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · أنس بن مالك | reports | Anas reports that Abu Bakr, ʿUmar, and ʿUthman did not recite the basmala when beginning prayer, evidence for excluding it from al-Fatiha.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r43] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · ابن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbas says the Prophet recognized the end of a surah only when “In the name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful” was revealed.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r44] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · أبو مسعود | reports | Abu Masʿud says they did not know the boundary between surahs until the basmala was revealed.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r45] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · الشعبي | reports | Al-Shaʿbi says the Prophet first wrote “In Your name, O God,” then “In God's name, the Compassionate,” and after 27:30 wrote the full basmala, as each cited passage was revealed. | mentions 11:41, 17:110, 27:30
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r5] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | holds | It is the seven oft-repeated because scholars agree that it consists of seven verses.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r6] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | holds | It is called oft-repeated because it is recited in every prayer cycle.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r7] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · مجاهد | reports | Mujahid says God reserved the seven oft-repeated for this community and stored it for them.
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r8] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · author | prefers | Most scholars call the surah Meccan, and Baghawi prefers this because al-Hijr is Meccan, so its seven oft-repeated could not have been promised before its revelation. | mentions 15:87
[BAGHAWI:1:1/r9] BAGHAWI, d. 516 · مجاهد | reports | Mujahid considers the surah Medinan.
