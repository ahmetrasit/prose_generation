4:28/q08 What is the lexical sense and range of ḍaʿīf? [ضَعِيفًۭا · meaning]
  4:28/q08/p1 It denotes weakness, the opposite or absence of strength; here it can mean little patience or inability to restrain desire. — reasons: Jishumi treats weakness and lightness as absences of strength and heaviness rather than independent qualities. Ibn al-Jawzi derives ḍaʿīf from ḍaʿf and gives the noun's two vocalizations, ḍaʿf and ḍuʿf; he glosses this verse as little patience and catalogues other contexts: incapacitated (4:76, 8:66), blind (11:91), chronically infirm (9:91), oppressed (28:5), socially lowly (34:31), and semen (30:54).
4:28/q09 How is ḍaʿīfan parsed in 'the human was created weak'? [وَخُلِقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ ضَعِيفًۭا · grammar] (turns on: The function of the final accusative and whether khalaqa can take a second object or an omitted 'from' phrase.)
  4:28/q09/p1 It is an accusative circumstance describing the human, emphatic on the usual passive reading and also possible on the active reading. — reasons: Nahhas, Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, Samīn and Alusi give the circumstantial parse. Abu Hayyan and Samīn reject a proposed tamyīz or specification parse, though they differ in how they discuss supplying min.
  4:28/q09/p2 It is understood through an omitted preposition and qualified noun: created from a weak substance. — reasons: Abu Hayyan mentions clay or embryonic stages; Samīn gives lowly water or a drop. Alusi says this does not fit the context.
  4:28/q09/p3 On the active reading, it is a second object if khalaqa is taken like jaʿala. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya proposes this; Abu Hayyan objects that no grammarian makes khalaqa govern two objects this way, and Samīn calls it unusual because jaʿala meaning 'create' takes one object.
  4:28/q09/p4 It is a tamyīz or specification accusative. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and Samīn report and reject this proposal; the possibility of inserting min is discussed.
4:28/q10 How is khuliqa read? [وَخُلِقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ ضَعِيفًۭا · readings] (turns on: Whether the verb is passive or active, changing the grammatical subject and case of al-insān.)
  4:28/q10/p1 The majority read passive khuliqa, with al-insān as the passive subject. — reasons: Nahhas calls the verb passive; Ibn Atiyya explicitly contrasts the majority reading with the active variant.
  4:28/q10/p2 Ibn Abbas, and in some transmissions Mujahid, read active khalaqa: God created the human weak. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan include Mujahid; Kashshaf, Qurtubi and Alusi name Ibn Abbas. Al-insān is accusative and God's name is the understood subject; Ibn Atiyya keeps ḍaʿīfan circumstantial or proposes a second object.
4:28/q11 How does human weakness explain God's easing? [يُرِيدُ ٱللَّهُ أَن يُخَفِّفَ عَنكُمْ وَخُلِقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ ضَعِيفًۭا · interpretation]
  4:28/q11/p1 The coordinating clause gives the reason for concessions: weak people struggle under severe obligations, especially marriage restrictions. — reasons: Nahhas and Qurtubi connect easily swayed desire with necessary concessions. Biqai makes the waw causal. Ibn Ashur calls the second clause a concluding generalization explaining the first; Islam is suited to all times and places whereas earlier religions addressed particular conditions, and 8:66 similarly cites known weakness before relief. Elmalili says legislation must avoid both indulgence of lust and oppressive severity; easier marriage and dealings avert fornication and misuse. Kuran Yolu says obligations have concessions in normal and exceptional circumstances. Tabatabai says the cause is general but impediments kept its effects from earlier peoples until repentance and mercy reached this community.
4:28/q12 Does the verse rule out obligations beyond human capacity or support a view of who creates human acts? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether the need for divine easing is compatible with God creating acts and the motives behind them.)
  4:28/q12/p1 God's stated wish to ease rules out duties beyond capacity and human acts created by God. — reasons: Al-Qadi argues that creating unbelief then forbidding it, or withholding faith while commanding it, would impose the greatest burden. Razi rejects the argument by invoking prior replies from divine knowledge and motivating causes.
  4:28/q12/p2 The motives that move people to act ultimately come from God. — reasons: Razi accepts al-Qadi's analysis of motives but argues that their causes must either regress infinitely or originate with God; he calls the latter correct and says it defeats al-Qadi's Muʿtazilite position.
4:28/q13 What legal guidance follows from the verse's promise of ease? [* · law]
  4:28/q13/p1 Religious obligations should be manageable, with concessions for hardship while lawful routes channel desire and protect benefits. — reasons: Ibn Ashur calls ease foundational and says the law can accept a lesser harm to prevent hardship or secure benefit, including slave marriage; he says two kinds of concessions branch from it. Elmalili rules out excessive severity and permissive surrender to lust, and urges easier marriage and dealings to prevent fornication or misuse. Kuran Yolu says sex and wealth should be directed lawfully, with concessions in ordinary and exceptional circumstances; human vulnerability does not erase the capacity to meet the test. Study Quran supplies Ramadan nights and reduction of prayers; Ibn Kathir transmits Moses' counsel on the Miʿraj: fifty daily prayers became five with tenfold reward after repeated requests.
4:28/q14 How does this verse connect to its surrounding passage and other verses about ease? [* · links] (turns on: Which part of the surrounding passage supplies the link: prior divine intentions, the marriage rule, or the subsequent financial address.)
  4:28/q14/p1 It follows God's clarification and repentance in 4:26–27 and states enduring care and ease for the community. — reasons: Ibn Ashur links it to 4:26 and sees its human-weakness statement as a concluding rationale. He compares 8:66, where God lightens a burden knowing believers' weakness. Elmalili and Tabatabai connect the prayer against heavy burdens in 2:286 with 7:157, 2:185 and 22:78. Study Quran also cites 2:185; Asad describes integrated spiritual and bodily guidance.
  4:28/q14/p2 It leads from marriage and inheritance to financial conduct, because marriage depends on means and wealth management protects lineage. — reasons: Biqai says human weakness undermines denying women and minors inheritance and explains the move toward rules for money. Elmalili links the next address, 4:29, to the financial ability needed for marriage and the handling of wealth.
  4:28/q14/p3 Nearby wording about human weakness signals why the conditional slave-marriage rule addresses sexual desire. — reasons: Abu Hayyan notes a series of implied links: reluctance suggests consensual inheritance transfer, restrictions on coercing wives can serve their benefit, the prohibition on fathers' wives recalls pre-Islamic custom, a permission implies an earlier ban, and slave-marriage conditions and weakness signal desire.
4:28/q15 What is reported about the value of this verse among verses of al-Nisāʾ? [* · reports] (turns on: The transmitted enumeration of the eight verses, whose last entries differ across sources.)
  4:28/q15/p1 Ibn Abbas counted it among eight verses better for this community than everything under the sun, usually alongside 4:26, 4:27, 4:31, 4:40, 4:48, 4:110 and 4:147. — reasons: Thalabi gives this list; Kashshaf gives the first three as clarification, repentance and relief and names 4:31 and 4:48; Durr gives only an excerpt; Study Quran names the same eight and calls them signs of mercy. Elmalili's listed references include 4:116 as well as 4:147, producing a variant enumeration.
  4:28/q15/p2 A transmitted list substitutes 4:116 and 3:147 for 4:48 and 4:147. — reasons: Razi gives this enumeration and then prays to be worthy of the blessings of these verses by grace and mercy.
  4:28/q15/p3 A transmitted list substitutes 4:152 for 4:147. — reasons: Alusi attributes this list to al-Bayhaqi from Ibn Abbas.
4:28/q16 What rhetorical qualities does the verse exemplify? [* · rhetoric]
  4:28/q16/p1 Its wording exemplifies the Qur'an's gentle register, suited to its context. — reasons: Ibn Ashur contrasts this register with a forceful warning in 41:13 and also cites 39:53 for gentleness. In his wider discussion he describes taḍmīn as a dependent showing a verb's additional sense; unusually effective Qur'anic proverbial expression (39:39, 4:81, 41:34, surpassing Zuhayr); expansive description that instills fear, illustrated by Ibn Ziyaba's poetry (75:26–29, 56:83–84, 14:43); and concise simultaneous literal and figurative senses where context permits. These are general rhetorical comparisons, not claims that each device occurs in this verse.
4:28/q17 What does the related phrase 'do not kill yourselves' mean? [* · links]
  4:28/q17/p1 It means do not destroy yourselves. — reasons: Abu Ubayda (Majaz) glosses the phrase lā taqtulū anfusakum this way.
