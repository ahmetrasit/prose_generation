23:14/q12 To whom does the pronoun in anshaʾnāhu refer? [أَنشَأْنَٰهُ · referent] (turns on: Whether the antecedent is the person already named or the developing physical entity.)
  23:14/q12/p1 It refers to the human being under formation. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī connects it with the human of 15:26; al-Farrāʾ names the human; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says it refers to the person whose bones were clothed, distinct in essence and properties from the material body.
  23:14/q12/p2 It refers to the bone, drop and lump conceived as one entity, or the thing just mentioned. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī and al-Farrāʾ allow a unified entity of the prior materials; al-Nasafī says it can refer to the immediately mentioned thing.
23:14/q13 What does anshaʾa mean here? [أَنشَأْنَٰهُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the verb simply describes origination or signals a new mode of being.)
  23:14/q13/p1 To bring into being, originate, and nurture a new kind of being. — reasons: Al-Dāmghānī cites 21:11, 56:35, 67:23, 6:133 and 56:61; al-Rāghib, reported by al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, joins originating and nurturing. Al-Biqāʿī reports al-Rāzī that the root letters indicate elevation or loftiness.
  23:14/q13/p2 The shift from khalq to inshāʾ marks the emergence of a new self unlike the prior matter. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says life, knowledge and power were not contained in the earlier physical stages; the pronoun still links the person to that matter.
23:14/q14 How is the new-creation clause read? [ثُمَّ أَنشَأْنَٰهُ خَلْقًا ءَاخَرَ · readings]
  23:14/q14/p1 Ibn Masʿūd's reading adds 'for him' to the new-creation clause. — reasons: ʿAbd al-Razzāq attributes this wording to Ibn Masʿūd.
23:14/q15 What is the other creation? [خَلْقًا ءَاخَرَ · interpretation] (turns on: The phrase may name an event in the womb, birth, later bodily and mental development, or the entire human transformation; some commentators explicitly broaden or reconcile the readings.)
  23:14/q15/p1 The spirit is breathed into the formed fetus, making inert matter a living human. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī prefers this by analogy with Adam's clay and spirit; Ṣāḥib al-Naẓm appeals to the shift in wording. ʿAlī says an angel breathes after four months in three darknesses. Ibn Kathīr says movement, hearing, sight and perception follow and treats later life stages as compatible consequences. Al-Ālūsī notes a rational soul created for or in the body; al-Qāshānī reads God's spirit and divine image inwardly. Many early authorities are named across sources.
  23:14/q15/p2 It is the growth of hair, or hair and teeth. — reasons: Qatāda is reported for hair alone and elsewhere hair plus teeth; al-Ḍaḥḥāk answers an objection about newborn hair by pointing to pubic and armpit hair. Al-Ālūsī derides that answer.
  23:14/q15/p3 It is attainment of full youth or mature adulthood. — reasons: Mujāhid, Ibn ʿUmar and others are reported; Study Quran adds full realization of faculties.
  23:14/q15/p4 It encompasses the person's successive stages after birth, from nursing through maturity and old age. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās lists crying, finding the breast, sitting, crawling, walking, weaning, eating, puberty and travel; al-Ḍaḥḥāk adds hair and age. Al-Rāzī invokes the following death verse (23:15), and Ibn Kathīr says the reading follows from ensoulment. Reports differ in exactly which stages they list.
  23:14/q15/p5 It is the completed human form, face, spirit and faculties together. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī reports the face; al-Bayḍāwī allows form, spirit and faculties singly or all together. Al-Rāzī, al-Nasafī, al-Zamakhsharī and al-Ālūsī describe a living, speaking, hearing, seeing being, unlike inert matter. Elmalılı relates the completed form to 95:4; Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya stresses honor and preparedness.
  23:14/q15/p6 It is the distinction of male and female. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan and other unnamed interpreters are reported; this locates the new creation within the womb.
  23:14/q15/p7 It is the acquisition of reason, understanding, sensory faculties, or capacities. — reasons: Al-Māwardī proposes discernment; al-Qushayrī lists hearing, sight, reason, perception and inspired gifts, while al-Ālūsī reports sensory faculties. Kur'an Yolu sees independent psychological and spiritual personality.
  23:14/q15/p8 It is emergence from the womb into the world, becoming a separate creature. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās is reported for birth; Asad calls the newborn independent of its mother's body.
  23:14/q15/p9 The phrase broadly includes speech, perception, skill, reason and all human development, beginning at ensoulment. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Qurṭubī reject confining it to one stage; Ibn Kathīr reconciles ensoulment and later stages. Ibn ʿAbbās is also reported to specify conduct in worldly affairs. Al-Qushayrī adds rare mature spiritual states of servanthood and effacement.
  23:14/q15/p10 It is especially the inward emergence of a human formed in God's image or prepared for spiritual realization. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī says the human is created yet more than merely a creature; al-Qushayrī describes release from bondage and realization of divine attributes through effacement; Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya calls the person repository of gnosis and divine love.
23:14/q16 Why is this called another creation? [خَلْقًا ءَاخَرَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether otherness denotes life and faculties, bodily differentiation, or a self distinct from its material body.)
  23:14/q16/p1 Life and faculties make the human radically distinct from the inert preceding matter. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says mute matter becomes living and articulate; al-Nasafī adds hearing and sight. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī argues the knowing capable person differs essentially from the body and uses 32:10–11, where the person is taken at death while the body scatters. Ibn ʿĀshūr says thumma marks rank; Islahi sees resurrection power in this transformation.
  23:14/q16/p2 The human has unusually rich and differentiated bodily and personal traits. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī contrasts uniform drop with diverse flesh, bones, hair, nails, nerves, skin, brain and veins, and lists divergent temperaments and traits; al-Ālūsī reports the microcosm verse.
  23:14/q16/p3 The person is a self that uses the body as an instrument. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī likens person and body to writer and pen and reads 32:10–11 as distinguishing them.
23:14/q17 What does the verse imply about the timing or nature of the spirit? [ثُمَّ أَنشَأْنَٰهُ خَلْقًا ءَاخَرَ · theology] (turns on: Whether the human named by the verse is bodily composite or a distinct immaterial self, and when spirit comes to be.)
  23:14/q17/p1 The human is a composite bodily being, against a spirit-only or indivisible account. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī, al-Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān say the described stages refute al-Naẓẓām and the philosophers or Maʿmar; al-Ālūsī reports the Imam's argument. The philosophers identify the person with an immaterial rational soul, neither divisible nor inside or outside the body.
  23:14/q17/p2 The spirit is created after bodily formation and joins or permeates it. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says temporal thumma supports a later rational soul, the view of most Islamic theologians and Aristotle; for animal spirit this is plain. He reports disagreement whether the rational spirit attaches to or permeates the body; most Muslims say permeates.
  23:14/q17/p3 The person is distinct in essence from the prior matter and uses the body as a tool. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī links this to being taken at death in 32:10–11 while the body disperses.
23:14/q18 Does the new-creation wording affect compensation for a usurped egg that hatches? [خَلْقًا ءَاخَرَ · law] (turns on: Whether a new physical identity ends the owner's claim to the transformed property.)
  23:14/q18/p1 The taker owes the egg, not the chick, because the chick is another creation. — reasons: Abū Ḥanīfa's inference is transmitted by al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, Bursevī and al-Ālūsī.
  23:14/q18/p2 The chick remains compensable as part of the owner's usurped property. — reasons: Al-Kashf says distinct creation does not defeat the owner's title under opponents' principles; al-Taqrīb says liability rests on its being part of the property, not on identity of names.
23:14/q19 What does tabāraka mean? [فَتَبَارَكَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether emphasis falls on exaltation, abundance of blessing, the root's permanence, or the verbal form.)
  23:14/q19/p1 God is exalted, holy and worthy of enduring praise. — reasons: Yaḥyā compares 23:116; al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī and Ibn al-Jawzī stress God's permanence; Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it a derived form of bāraka. Al-Rāzī links blessing's increase to rising above others; al-Biqāʿī says God is fixed in perfection and free of defect. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī add holiness and divine knowledge and power.
  23:14/q19/p2 The word derives from firmness, settling or permanence. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Ṭabrisī and Jishumī derive it from abiding; al-Rāghib compares water settled in a pool. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites al-Raḍī that the tāfaʿala form intensifies an established state.
  23:14/q19/p3 God possesses abundant, lasting good and pours blessing on creation. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī relays al-Rāghib's pool image for settled divine good (7:96) and connects blessing with God's measuring and creation; Ibn ʿĀshūr speaks of immense welfare decreed for people and baraka
