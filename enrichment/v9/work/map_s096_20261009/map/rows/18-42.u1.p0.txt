## THE CURRENT MAP
18:42/q01 What does وَأُحِيطَ بِثَمَرِهِۦ say happened? [وَأُحِيطَ بِثَمَرِهِۦ · interpretation]
  18:42/q01/p1 The garden's produce or wealth was completely destroyed. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, al-Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, al-Razi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi, al-Alusi and Tabataba'i explain the image through an enemy encircling and overpowering someone; al-Wahidi compares 2:81, al-Razi and Abu Hayyan 12:66, and Tabataba'i 10:22. Ibn Qutayba's Mushkil says an encircled town approaches ruin. Al-Tabari recalls the owner's confidence in 18:35; al-Mawardi, Ibn Kathir, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi and Study Quran connect the event with the brother's warning. Al-Biqa'i says the passive makes ruin comprehensive and easy without naming an agent. Al-Dahhak attributes it to God's decree; al-Zajjaj, al-Baghawi, Ibn al-Jawzi and al-Qurtubi to God's punishment. Al-Asamm's context in al-Jishumi allows all produce or all wealth. Ibn Atiyya calls it comprehensive punishment; al-Khafaji likens it to a surrounded people annihilated.
18:42/q02 What does ثَمَرِهِۦ encompass? [بِثَمَرِهِۦ · referent] (turns on: Whether the vocalization and the owner's investment point to edible yield, the productive trees, or his entire fortune.)
  18:42/q02/p1 The owner's wealth and all the garden's assets, including produce and property. — reasons: Qatada includes fruit and other property; Mujahid and Ibn Mujahid's father specify gold and silver. Abu Amr includes garden, property, palms and trees under both vocalizations. Al-Wahidi and most exegetes prefer the broad sense because spending went to productive assets; the narrow crop was already established in 18:33–34, whereas the broad term covers fruit and roots. Salam distinguishes one reading for wealth and one for plural fruits; Yunus treats them as equivalent, a conflation al-Wahidi rejects. The Qiraat-ER account calls thumr economic wealth; al-Alusi says the known wealth of both gardens and their contents.
  18:42/q02/p2 The fruit or crop of the gardens, including its several kinds. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi says every kind of fruit; al-Wahidi reports that the common thamar denotes edible fruit and Abu Ishaq prefers it because 18:33 had already mentioned produce. Salam calls the alternative plural fruits, while Yunus equates the forms, against al-Wahidi's distinction. Qiraat-ER calls thamar collective produce and thumur a plural highlighting varieties. Ibn Kathir allows fruits before giving wealth as another interpretation.
  18:42/q02/p3 The fruit-bearing trees and date palms themselves. — reasons: Ibn Zayd in al-Wahidi identifies the trees; Ibn Abbas says the fruit trees and palms, and al-Wahidi argues that investment chiefly goes into the trees and roots. Al-Wahidi's Wajiz says the fruitful trees were destroyed; al-Tabrisi speaks of total ruin of trees and palms.
18:42/q03 How is ثَمَرِهِۦ read, and what do the forms suggest? [بِثَمَرِهِۦ · readings] (turns on: The consonants' vowels and whether the resulting noun is collective fruit, a plural, or wealth; reports differ by reader and occurrence.)
  18:42/q03/p1 Thumur, with ḍamma on both thāʾ and mīm. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid names Ibn Kathir, Nafi', Ibn 'Amir, Hamza and al-Kisa'i; Abu Hayyan adds Ibn Abbas, Mujahid and Medinan readers. Al-Nashr assigns it to the remaining readers in both occurrences, and Abu Hayyan records Ruways transmitting this form in the first occurrence but fath in bi-thamarihi. Ibn Khalawayh explains it as a plural of a plural; Abu Hayyan says plural of thimar; Qiraat-ER says plural thamar emphasizing fruit types. Some Abu Amr transmissions agree with Nafi'.
  18:42/q03/p2 Thamar, with fatḥa on both thāʾ and mīm. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid names 'Asim; Abu Hayyan also names Abu Ja'far, al-Hasan, Jabir ibn Zayd, al-Hajjaj, Abu Hatim and Ya'qub through Ruways. Al-Nashr lists Abu Ja'far, 'Asim and Rawh in both occurrences, Ruways in the first only. Ibn Khalawayh describes a collective with singular ending in ta'; Qiraat-ER calls it collective fruit or produce. Abu Ishaq in al-Wahidi favors thamar in view of 18:33.
  18:42/q03/p3 Thumr, with ḍamma on thāʾ and sukūn on mīm. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid and al-Nashr assign it to Abu 'Amr, though Ibn Mujahid notes transmissions matching Nafi'. Abu Hayyan also names al-A'mash and Abu Raja' and explains it as lightened or as a plural of thamarah. Ibn Khalawayh connects it with invested wealth; Qiraat-ER calls it property.
18:42/q04 How is the passive وَأُحِيطَ بِثَمَرِهِۦ parsed? [وَأُحِيطَ بِثَمَرِهِۦ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the understood passive subject is an omitted verbal noun or the bi-phrase in nominative function.)
  18:42/q04/p1 An implicit verbal noun is the passive subject, with the genitive sometimes construed as nominative. — reasons: Al-Nahhas and al-Qurtubi give this analysis; al-Nahhas also calls 'he began turning' a circumstantial accusative. Al-Biqa'i says leaving the agent unnamed conveys the ease and completeness of the destruction.
  18:42/q04/p2 ثَمَرِهِۦ, though introduced by bi-, functions as the nominative passive subject. — reasons: Al-Jishumi expressly gives this parse; al-Nahhas and al-Qurtubi allow the genitive form to have nominative function.
18:42/q05 What rhetorical figure is the encompassing of the fruit? [وَأُحِيطَ بِثَمَرِهِۦ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the transfer from encirclement to destruction is classified as metaphor or metonymy.)
  18:42/q05/p1 It is a metaphor of an enemy surrounding and destroying someone. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, al-Razi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi, al-Alusi and Tabataba'i explain the transfer from military encirclement; al-Khafaji calls it a representative metaphor of a surrounded people annihilated.
  18:42/q05/p2 It is more apparently metonymy for complete destruction. — reasons: Al-Alusi favors this classification over the proposed kinds of metaphor.
18:42/q06 What does the root of أُحِيطَ and the related garden name convey? [وَأُحِيطَ · meaning] (turns on: The literal enclosing sense and its extension to a garden's name or to annihilation.)
  18:42/q06/p1 Iḥāṭa literally surrounds from every side and here extends to destruction. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi's Nuzha defines surrounding; Ibn Qutayba's Mushkil likens impending ruin to an encircled enemy or town. Al-Damghani and Ibn al-Jawzi list destruction as a sense, with the overtaking sin in 2:81 as another illustration.
  18:42/q06/p2 A garden is called ḥāʾiṭ because it surrounds its fruits or protects its owner. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi gives enclosure of many fruits; Ibn al-Anbari adds surrounding, benefiting and protecting the owner.
18:42/q07 What immediate calamity destroyed the garden? [وَأُحِيطَ بِثَمَرِهِۦ · reports] (turns on: Whether the notes specify a fire and vanishing water or only infer a sudden divine calamity from the scene.)
  18:42/q07/p1 God sent fire that burned it, and its water sank or disappeared. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi, al-Jishumi, al-Baghawi, al-Tabrisi, Abu Hayyan and Bursevi transmit or hold this account; al-Kashshaf reports the fire without water. Al-Alusi objects that the visible collapse makes a fire report unlikely unless the garden denotes the whole estate or the description means general ruin.
  18:42/q07/p2 A sudden, otherwise unspecified heavenly calamity annihilated the garden. — reasons: Al-Khafaji, as reported by al-Alusi, infers sudden destruction from the immediate morning scene; Islahi also speaks of a sudden calamity and fallen fences. Al-Hasan in Yahya says destruction enveloped it overnight; al-Qurtubi compares the garden of al-Qalam, blackened by morning.
18:42/q08 How does وَأُحِيطَ connect to the preceding narrative? [وَأُحِيطَ · grammar]
  18:42/q08/p1 It coordinates with an omitted event implied by the context. — reasons: Al-Alusi reports an unspoken earlier event before the clause of destruction.
18:42/q09 Does فَأَصْبَحَ mark morning or simply becoming? [فَأَصْبَحَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether aṣbaḥa retains its time-of-day sense or has the generalized sense 'became'.)
  18:42/q09/p1 Morning arrived after a night-time destruction. — reasons: Al-Hasan in Yahya says destruction came overnight. Ibn al-Jawzi's Nuzha groups this with al-Qalam 68:17 and 68:20 and cites 46:25; al-Qurtubi compares the sleeping garden owners whose garden was blackened by morning. Abu Hayyan says his awakening suggests night-time loss; al-Alusi and al-Khafaji infer the same from the immediate wording, and Islahi pictures him waking to ruin.
  18:42/q09/p2 It can mean 'became' without fixing the time of destruction. — reasons: Abu Hayyan says the morning inference is apparent but unnecessary; al-Samin and al-Alusi preserve both possibilities.
18:42/q10 What does his turning the two palms signify or do? [يُقَلِّبُ كَفَّيْهِ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether 'hand' names the actual physical gesture of remorse or figuratively denotes the property he surveys.)
  18:42/q10/p1 He makes a visible gesture of grief and regret over his lost spending. — reasons: Sources variously describe striking or clapping one hand against the other (Qatada, Ibn Abbas, al-Kalbi, al-Biqa'i), exposing palms then backs (Abu Hayyan, al-Alusi), placing palm against back of the other (Ibn Atiyya), or simply turning them. Ibn Atiyya specifically rejects 'clapping' as inaccurate. Al-Wahidi cites Arab usage, a poetic line and the parallel of biting a hand; Bursevi's Bahr al-'Ulum also cites biting fingertips and gnashing teeth. Al-Kashshaf, al-Samin and al-Alusi call it metaphor or metonym for remorse; al-Alusi distinguishes 'turning a matter inside out' as shifting topics. Al-Majaz, Ibn Qutayba and the others treat it as conventional idiom. Al-Tabari, Yahya, al-Razi, Ibn Kathir and al-Durr relate it to lost wealth; Bursevi quotes a Mathnawi couplet that grieving what is gone cannot restore it. Al-Itqan lists regret as a less familiar usage.
