 Elmalili says a sinner who turns away without denial may enter but does not remain.
  92:16/q08/p4 The stated traits identify those who approach the Fire, without excluding other sinners. — reasons: Abu Muslim's first explanation of 'none enters it but the most wretched' is that the stated traits mark its intended subject. Jishumi prefers this first explanation and objects that absolute exclusion contradicts consensus, invites sin and conflicts with threats to the miser and sinner. Al-Razi counters that blame, divine anger and loss of honor deter sin, and God can punish otherwise.
  92:16/q08/p5 The Fire is named for the majority or its intended occupants. — reasons: Abu Muslim offers this as a way to read the restriction without excluding every other entrant.
  92:16/q08/p6 The wording names those most deserving of the Fire. — reasons: Abu Muslim offers this alternative; al-Razi rejects reducing ashqa to 'more deserving' because it abandons the apparent meaning without evidence.
  92:16/q08/p7 An ellipsis includes the most wretched and sinners like him. — reasons: Jishumi reports the supplied phrase 'and whoever is like him among the sinners.' Al-Wahidi also reports an abbreviated mention of sinful non-polytheists, adding that entry need not mean eternal abode.
  92:16/q08/p8 Not every unbeliever must have performed each listed act. — reasons: Jishumi reports a view that some unbelievers enter without each specified denial, miserliness and turning away; he prefers Abu Muslim's first explanation.
  92:16/q08/p9 All unbelievers do fit the paired description. — reasons: Al-Razi says every unbeliever denies the prophet's claim and turns from examining its proofs; this answers al-Qadi's hypothetical unbeliever without those traits.
  92:16/q08/p10 The apparent restriction could encourage or virtually permit sin. — reasons: Al-Qadi says a sinful believer might infer that sin cannot harm one who neither denied nor turned away. Jishumi raises the same concern. Al-Razi rejects it: present disgrace, divine anger, withheld honor and reward, and other forms of punishment still deter.
92:16/q09 What do the transmitted sayings say about the denier's fate? [ٱلَّذِى كَذَّبَ وَتَوَلَّىٰ · reports] (turns on: The reports give complementary formulations of refusal of Paradise and qualification for the Fire.)
  92:16/q09/p1 Everyone enters Paradise except one who refuses; the denier who turns away is the refuser. — reasons: Abu Hurayra's report identifies the refuser by reciting this description. Versions through Makhul and Abu Umama say the community enters Paradise except one who flees God like a bad camel from its owner; one calls it the gentlest saying heard. Prophetic versions explain that obedience to the Prophet leads to Paradise and disobedience is refusal.
  92:16/q09/p2 Only a wretch enters Hell: one who neither obeys God nor leaves a sin for His sake. — reasons: The Prophet's definition is transmitted through Abu Hurayra and Abu Umama in the cited collections; the report shifts attention from a named person to conduct.
92:16/q10 What occasion or incident is connected to this warning? [ٱلَّذِى كَذَّبَ وَتَوَلَّىٰ · reports] (turns on: Whether the reports specify the original occasion or serve as applications of a general early Meccan warning.)
  92:16/q10/p1 The passage concerns Umayya ibn Khalaf and others who denied Muhammad. — reasons: Ibn Abbas names Umayya and others who denied Muhammad and earlier prophets; the al-Dahhak route adds those like him. Al-Zamakhshari also offers Abu Jahl or Umayya as the outstanding unbeliever in a balanced contrast.
  92:16/q10/p2 The refusal can be mapped to a Medinan palm owner who mistreated poor children. — reasons: Study Quran reports that a palm owner snatched fallen dates from the children and rejected the Prophet's offer of a Paradise palm; another man bought the tree for forty palms and donated it. Al-Qurtubi Full calls the donor Abu al-Dahdah and the owner an Ansari, mapping ease to Paradise, hardship to Hell and death to loss of wealth.
  92:16/q10/p3 The palm story is unlikely to be the occasion if the sura is early Meccan; the warning is general. — reasons: Study Quran reads the verses as generally condemning those who rejected Muhammad as prophet. Kur'an Yolu likewise says that even if verses 5-19 were revealed about Abu Bakr, the message and rulings remain general.
92:16/q11 Why pair this description with the opposite figure in the sūra? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: The notes explain the contrast both through omitted opposing acts and through the sūra's repeated, reversed arrangement.)
  92:16/q11/p1 The opposed conduct of denier and giver makes their opposite outcomes intelligible. — reasons: Al-Biqai says reciprocal omission lets denial imply its opposite in the pious person and later giving imply its omitted opposite for the wretched. Ibn Ashur says the relative clauses make each action the cause of its outcome. Al-Samarrai says the evil sequence moves from particular acts to a general description, while the good sequence rises from giving to utmost piety and purification; he also says the opposite figure gives his own wealth with maximal sincerity. Islahi calls it a contrast of messenger rejecters and believers rather than grades of virtue.
  92:16/q11/p2 The closing stanza reverses and mirrors the earlier antitheses. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum says the sūra first contrasts conduct and recompense in verses 5-13, then again in 14-21; the final stanza reverses the earlier saved/damned order, links ittaqa at 92:5 to atqa at 92:17, and presents warning or promise before the conduct rather than after it. It says 92:14 reveals the blazing Fire implicit in 92:10, comparable to 104:4-7 and 102:3-8. Islahi says verses 15-21 identify those cast in and shielded, with reward.
92:16/q12 How is the warning directed and received? [* · interpretation]
  92:16/q12/p1 It warns the rejecters of guidance, especially Quraysh, of a blazing Fire. — reasons: Al-Baghawi calls it a warning to Mecca. Islahi addresses persistent Quraysh deniers, setting them against those who spend to purify themselves; Kur'an Yolu says God guides through revelation and prophets and warns against Hell as the consequence of the wrong path.
  92:16/q12/p2 The warning moved Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz to tears in prayer. — reasons: Malik recounts that during Maghrib Umar cried so hard at the blazing Fire warning that he could not continue this sūra and recited another.
92:16/q13 What does the surrounding language say about burning and being spared? [* · interpretation]
  92:16/q13/p1 Salaa means roasting or punishment in blazing fire. — reasons: Bint al-Shati distinguishes it from warmth sought for comfort.
  92:16/q13/p2 The most pious is spared the Fire by God before reaching its harm. — reasons: Al-Samarrai says the passive verb attributes rescue to God, creator of people and Fire, rather than human power, even for the most pious giver; he cites 3:185 and 19:71-72. He distinguishes deliverance after entry into harm from sparing before it, citing 2:49, 19:72 and 21:88; the Qur'an uses deliverance for the pious in general and sparing for the singular superlative. He says the wording also leaves God's mercy open to other believers.
