## THE CURRENT MAP
2:2/q01 What does 'that Book' refer to? [ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْكِتَٰبُ · referent] (turns on: The referent and whether distance is literal, retrospective, or inward.)
  2:2/q01/p1 The Qur’an, present or already known to its first hearers. — reasons: Early authorities gloss 'that' as 'this'; Khafāf's poem supports this usage. Some specify earlier revealed sūras because al-Baqara is Medinan. Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr and al-Ālūsī prefer the Qur’an. Ibn ʿAṭiyya also lists the prior revealed portion.
  2:2/q01/p2 The Qur’an promised earlier to Muhammad or in prior scriptures. — reasons: Reported promises include an unwashable Book, a weighty word (73:5), covenant revelation, and a Book foretold by Moses and Jesus; ʿIyāḍ's Muslim report describes reciting it asleep and awake. Al-Nasafī gives both the earlier scriptural promise and the opening letters.
  2:2/q01/p3 The Preserved Tablet, heavenly Qur’an, or God's fixed decree. — reasons: Saʿīd ibn Jubayr names the Tablet and al-Kisāʾī the Qur’an before descent; inward readings add primordial destiny and mercy written before creation. Abū Ḥayyān also reports the undescended Qur’an.
  2:2/q01/p4 The just spoken opening letters or the path requested in al-Fātiḥa. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī and others point back to completed speech; Abū Jaʿfar ibn al-Zubayr identifies the requested path with the Book; Abū Ḥayyān favors that link, al-Ālūsī objects. Abū Ḥayyān adds the alphabet letters used in the challenge.
  2:2/q01/p5 The Torah and Gospel themselves. — reasons: Some retain a distal reference to absent scriptures. Al-Ṭabrisī rejects this because the Qur’an describes their alteration, and Ibn Kathīr calls it unsupported.
2:2/q02 Why use dhālika and how is it formed? [ذَٰلِكَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether distance is temporal, honorific, or literal, and the role of lām and kāf.)
  2:2/q02/p1 The form contains a demonstrative base, lām, and address kāf. — reasons: Basrans and Kufans dispute the base and lām's role: emphasis, distance, extra support or prevention of false annexation. Dual nūn retention proves kāf is not a genitive noun; the lām's kasra is also explained. Al-Rāzī explains masculine agreement by the named item rather than the feminine word sūra.
  2:2/q02/p2 Discourse and context license a distant form for what is already spoken or present. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ, al-Ṭabarī, al-Zajjāj and Ibn Mālik cite ordinary speech and Qur’anic alternations; Ibn ʿĀshūr says contextual purpose chooses near or far. Visible people remain distinct.
  2:2/q02/p3 Distance elevates the Qur’an or recalls its descent, promise, and hidden depths. — reasons: Al-Sakkākī's honorific explanation is preferred by al-Ālūsī; al-Biqāʿī and Ibn ʿĀshūr link lofty rank to the opponents' inability to imitate it. Other accounts cite descent or inaccessible sciences.
  2:2/q02/p4 Dhālika retains its normal sense of distance. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān rejects reinterpreting the remote form as 'this'; al-Qurṭubī reports this distal premise. Al-Raḍī distinguishes distant reference to absent objects, and al-Rāzī records the objection that the Book seems present.
2:2/q03 What does kitāb signify here? [ٱلْكِتَٰبُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the word names a written product or asserts perfect bookhood.)
  2:2/q03/p1 Written and composed revelation, rooted in joining or gathering. — reasons: Poetic and Arabic examples include sewing, strung beads, assembled troops and joined letters. Al-Wāḥidī prefers written revelation over a restricted sense of duties; Elmalılı insists revealed wording and meaning belong together. Al-Bayḍāwī takes kitāb as an emphatic verbal noun or a passive written-object form, used before actual writing.
  2:2/q03/p2 The complete Book, uniquely deserving its definite title. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī compares 'the man' for complete virtue; al-Rāzī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Ālūsī and Ibn ʿĀshūr discuss rhetorical perfection rather than literal exclusion of other books.
2:2/q04 How is 'that Book' attached to the opening letters and what follows? [ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْكِتَٰبُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the opening letters are a name or completed utterance.)
  2:2/q04/p1 The letters, demonstrative, and Book admit subject, predicate, adjective and apposition analyses. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ, al-Ṭabarī, al-Zajjāj, al-Rāzī and al-Samīn enumerate parses depending on whether the letters name a sūra, sound, or complete utterance. Al-Ṭabarī rejects inconsistent Kufan combinations. Al-Ālūsī also records the variant 'tanẓīl al-kitāb'. Al-Itqān insists any parse respect each grammatical category's constraints, with disputed apposition and adverbial examples.
  2:2/q04/p2 The letters end independently and dhālika al-kitāb starts a fresh clause. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān favors this least forced analysis; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it apparent. Al-Zamakhsharī favors closely linked independent clauses.
2:2/q05 What do the opening letters contribute to 'that Book'? [ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْكِتَٰبُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether their function is a marker, oath, challenge, or concealed secret.)
  2:2/q05/p1 They are an oath for the Qur’an, with the response supplied by 'that Book' or 'no doubt in it'. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba treats the letters as oath; Qāshānī says the answer is omitted and indicated by dhālika al-kitāb. Al-Qurṭubī prefers lā rayba fīhi as its answer.
  2:2/q05/p2 They mark the sūra's opening and draw attention to the following revelation. — reasons: Abū ʿUbayda says the letter names are indeclinable and cites Abū al-Najm; Ibn ʿArabī says their unusual sound arrests idle talk and invites reflection.
  2:2/q05/p3 Their ultimate intended meaning is a hidden secret. — reasons: Al-Bursawī reports knowers who require divine unveiling; Jishumī rejects unknowability because revelation aims to be understood and early scholars interpreted the letters.
2:2/q06 What does rayb mean? [رَيْبَ · meaning]
  2:2/q06/p1 Doubt, often colored by suspicion or inward unease. — reasons: Saʿīda and Ibn al-Zibaʿrī witness doubt, Jamil accusation, a distressed gazelle and time's calamities disturbance; a rare poem gives 'need'. Sībawayh, Abū Zayd and Ibn ʿĀshūr disagree on rāba versus arāba. Al-Ṭabrisī adds severe doubt and distinguishes rābanī from arābanī. Al-Bārizī prefers rayb to shakk for a lighter sound sequence without heavy assimilation.
2:2/q07 What does 'no doubt in it' claim? [لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the statement describes the Book objectively, commands the hearer, or has a restricted audience.)
  2:2/q07/p1 The Book itself leaves no rational ground for doubt despite actual doubters. — reasons: The 2:23 challenge tests its miraculousness; al-Ṭabrisī denies obscurity and contradiction, and Ibn ʿĀshūr distinguishes doubts in hearers from defects in the Book. Elmalılı adds certainty of revelation and trustworthy delivery. Ibn ʿĀshūr ties the denial to familiar letters' miraculous inability to be rivalled.
  2:2/q07/p2 It functions as a prohibition: do not doubt it. — reasons: Supporters compare the prohibitive negation at 2:197; Abū Ḥayyān at one point finds this far-fetched.
  2:2/q07/p3 It is restricted to believers' freedom from doubt. — reasons: Al-Sulamī speaks of unveiled hearts; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān reject narrowing the general wording.
2:2/q08 How does the negation work grammatically and rhetorically? [لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ · grammar] (turns on: The particle's scope, the function of fīhi, and whether word order suggests contrast.)
  2:2/q08/p1 Lā negates the genus, building rayb accusative without tanwīn. — reasons: Sībawayh compares it to inna; Basran, Kufan and other accounts differ on government and omitted tanwīn. The common fatḥa categorically negates doubt; Abū al-Shaʿthāʾ's nominative reading has a different possible scope. Al-Ṭabrisī cites Sībawayh for lā governing the unnunated noun. Other grammarians call lā plus indefinite noun a compound; fīhi can be predicate or adjective.
  2:2/q08/p2 The order foregrounds denial and avoids implying another scripture is doubtful. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī and followers contrast 37:47's Paradise wine; Abū Ḥayyān rejects the exclusivity inference, and al-Ālūsī replies.
  2:2/q08/p3 Fīhi is the predicate, or a modifier with an omitted predicate. — reasons: Al-Fārisī and al-Wāḥidī distinguish surface accusative and syntactic nominative; Abū Ḥayyān prefers a readily supplied predicate. Fī also has literal and figurative containment and other Arabic uses. Al-Burhān reports an independent fīhi hudā parse versus fīhi as lā's predicate with hudā as accusative circumstance.
2:2/q09 How are fīhi and the adjoining sounds recited? [فِيهِ هُدًۭى · readings] (turns on: Reader transmission and whether a glide, assimilation, or nasalization is heard.)
  2:2/q09/p1 Ibn Kathīr lengthens hāʾ with a connecting yāʾ or wāw. — reasons: Ḥafṣ and Qutayba agree at specified exceptions; some readers restore original ḍamma. Sībawayh objects to a sequence of quiescent sounds.
  2:2/q09/p2 The common performance uses a short or light hāʾ vowel. — reasons: Nāfiʿ, Abū ʿAmr and ʿĀṣim have light forms; Ibn al-Sarrī prefers kasra without yāʾ because it is usual and fits the rasm. Abū Ḥātim distinguishes concealment from true assimilation. Al-Akhfash also reports silent pronominal hāʾ in an Asad al-Sarāt poetic example.
  2:2/q09/p3 Assimilation and nasalization vary by reader and sound. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī authenticates ghunnah or its omission before lām and rāʾ; Abū Jaʿfar conceals before khāʾ and ghayn. Abū ʿAmr has bāʾ-to-fāʾ assimilation; al-Kisāʾī pauses on hudā with yāʾ. Al-Akhfash reports assimilation of the adjacent hāʾs in fīhi hudā.
