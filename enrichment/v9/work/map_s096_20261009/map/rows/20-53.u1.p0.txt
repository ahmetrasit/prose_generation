## THE CURRENT MAP
20:53/q01 How is الذي جعل لكم attached grammatically? [ٱلَّذِى · grammar] (turns on: Whether the relative continues Moses' description of his Lord or opens a divine resumption, especially in light of the later first person.)
  20:53/q01/p1 It is the predicate of an omitted subject, such as 'He is.' — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari lists this; Razi insists on it because the later first person is divine; Ibn Ashur takes the omitted subject to resume the Lord of 20:52. Alusi's Imam calls the divine resumption certain.
  20:53/q01/p2 It is an adjective of 'my Lord.' — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari and others allow this; Razi and Abu Hayyan object that it makes Moses continue speaking across the later first-person verbs; al-Samin identifies the same difficulty.
  20:53/q01/p3 It is an accusative of praise or an omitted 'I mean.' — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari, al-Qurtubi, al-Baydawi and others give this parse; al-Samin says it can introduce God's speech; Razi objects if it makes Moses speak onward.
20:53/q02 How is مَهْدًۭا read? [مَهْدًۭا · readings] (turns on: The transmitted reader groups and whether the noun form better fits the context and 78:6.)
  20:53/q02/p1 Mahdan, without alif, with fatḥ on mīm and a still hāʾ. — reasons: Most Kufans, including ʿĀṣim, Ḥamza and al-Kisāʾī, read this here and at 43:10; Abu Hayyan adds al-Aʿmash, Ṭalḥa and Ibn Abi Layla; Ibn Ashur adds Khalaf. Al-Nashr calls a report from Rawḥ erroneous.
  20:53/q02/p2 Mihādan, with alif, kasra on mīm and fatḥ on hāʾ. — reasons: Ibn Kathir, Nafiʿ, Abu ʿAmr and Ibn ʿAmir read this; the sources compare 43:10 and the unanimous alif at 78:6, though al-Baghawi cites 78:16. Abu ʿUbayd, Abu Hatim and al-Nahhas prefer it; al-Tabari accepts both famous readings.
20:53/q03 What do mahd and mihād mean and how are they formed? [مَهْدًۭا · meaning] (turns on: Whether the forms distinguish an action from a prepared object, form a singular and plural, or both function as verbal nouns.)
  20:53/q03/p1 Mihād names a spread, bed or carpet; mahd is primarily the act of preparing or spreading. — reasons: Al-Tabari compares farsh/firāsh; Abu Ali and al-Farisi call this clearest; al-Nahhas says mahd requires an implied 'possessing a cradle.' Al-Raghib says the prepared child's bed extends to a level place.
  20:53/q03/p2 Mahd is itself a noun for a cradle or bed, and mihād may be its plural. — reasons: Al-Farisi allows the faʿl-to-fiʿāl plural; Ibn Atiyya and al-Mahdawi allow singular or plural mihād; Ibn Ashur says the plural can point to many regions; al-Alusi says each place is someone's cradle.
  20:53/q03/p3 Both forms can function as verbal nouns or near synonyms. — reasons: Al-Mufaddal derives both from mahada, preparing a bed; al-Samin records this analysis; Ibn al-Jawzi says both denote a bed or spread.
20:53/q04 In what sense was the earth made a cradle? [ٱلْأَرْضَ مَهْدًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the cradle image denotes present physical habitability or life's preparatory role.)
  20:53/q04/p1 It was spread and prepared as a settled, useful place for human life. — reasons: Muqatil, Yahya, Razi and others describe rest, sitting, standing, sleeping, dwelling, farming and travel; 2:22 and 71:19–20 are parallels. Biqai says most land is habitable amid mountains and rough regions; Ibn Ashur notes rare avoidable bumps.
  20:53/q04/p2 Its comparison with a cradle points to human earthly life preparing for higher life. — reasons: Tabatabai compares the child raised in a cradle for a later, nobler life.
20:53/q05 What does سَلَكَ mean here? [وَسَلَكَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the local sense is opening a route or an image based on inserting something into another thing.)
  20:53/q05/p1 God opened, laid out and made routes passable. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and al-Thaʿlabi gloss it as opening a way; Ibn Abbas says the roads were made easy. Al-Nahhas calls the wording figurative because the paths are made available.
  20:53/q05/p2 Its root means to insert or cause to pass into; paths are described as running through earth. — reasons: Wahidi cites insertion examples, including 74:42, and says visible routes are treated as inserted; Ibn Ashur compares Quranic instances at 72:17, 74:42, 26:200 and 39:21 and al-Aʿsha's nail image.
20:53/q06 What are the سُبُل and what do they enable? [سُبُلًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the ways are physical routes alone, broader resources, or also an image of spiritual travel.)
  20:53/q06/p1 They are roads through the earth for travel and access to needs. — reasons: Qatada glosses roads; sources locate them amid mountains, valleys, plains and sand. Biqai notes some land remains impassable; Ibn Ashur includes natural routes and trails worn by people. Ibn Kathir cites 21:31; Qurtubi cites 71:19–20 and 43:10.
  20:53/q06/p2 The ways include material and intellectual means of livelihood. — reasons: Asad takes the earth's ways and means as resources for earning a living on and from it.
  20:53/q06/p3 The paths also point to humanity's journey toward nearness to God. — reasons: Tabatabai compares worldly paths to the path into God's realm of honor.
20:53/q07 What does فِيهَا refer to? [فِيهَا · referent]
  20:53/q07/p1 It refers to the earth. — reasons: Al-Tabari explicitly identifies the pronoun's antecedent.
20:53/q08 Why is لَكُمْ repeated? [لَكُمْ · rhetoric]
  20:53/q08/p1 It makes human benefit the focus of both earth and routes. — reasons: Al-Alusi says the first occurrence identifies the intended beneficiaries and repetition stresses their benefit.
20:53/q09 What is meant by ٱلسَّمَآءِ? [ٱلسَّمَآءِ · referent] (turns on: Whether 'sky' indicates the direction above, clouds or a heavenly origin.)
  20:53/q09/p1 It can mean the sky's direction or regions. — reasons: Al-Nahhas says its regions; al-Alusi offers 'from its direction.'
  20:53/q09/p2 It can mean the heavenly sphere or the clouds, or heaven itself. — reasons: Bursevi says anything above may be called sky, including the sphere and clouds; al-Alusi reports a literal heavenly origin in some reports.
20:53/q10 What is the water sent down? [مَآءًۭ · referent] (turns on: Whether the mention stops at falling rain or extends to water sourced from it.)
  20:53/q10/p1 It is rain. — reasons: Wahidi, Baghawi, Tabrisi, Baydawi and others identify it as rainfall; Bursevi takes the indefinite wording to mean some water, and Biqai points to observable color and taste.
  20:53/q10/p2 The rainwater also encompasses waters found in springs, rivers and seas. — reasons: Tabatabai links rainfall with terrestrial waters in his description of God's provision.
20:53/q11 Where does Moses' speech end, and who says فَأَخْرَجْنَا? [ٱلَّذِى فَأَخْرَجْنَا · referent] (turns on: The point where the quoted dialogue ends and whether the first-person plural is God, cultivators, or a quoted divine voice.)
  20:53/q11/p1 Moses' speech ends at 'my Lord neither errs nor forgets'; God speaks from 'the One who made.' — reasons: Razi argues that the coordinated fāʾ prevents beginning divine speech only at 'We brought forth,' while eating, grazing and return to earth suit God; Abu Hayyan and Ibn Ashur cite the first person. Islahi sees an insertion addressing Quraysh.
  20:53/q11/p2 Moses' speech ends after 'sent down water'; God says 'We brought forth.' — reasons: The Nazm scholar cites the next command to eat and graze; Ibn Atiyya says God addresses Muhammad and creation. Al-Jurjani calls it iltifāt; al-Samin calls it remote and Razi objects to the coordinating fāʾ.
  20:53/q11/p3 Moses continues speaking, and 'we' means people who cultivate plants with the rain. — reasons: The Nazm scholar cites 56:63 for attributing cultivation to people; Razi allows irrigation but argues people cannot bring forth varied plant natures. Al-Alusi ranks it second to divine speech.
  20:53/q11/p4 Moses continues, but quotes God's first-person words or yields rhetorically to the divine voice. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari says 'for you' rather than 'for us' supports verbatim quotation; Ibn Atiyya allows quotation at 'We brought forth.' Ibn al-Munir prefers a rhetorical move from Moses' own words to divine narration. Al-Alusi reports denial of iltifāt or unchanged quotation.
20:53/q12 What is the force of the shift to فَأَخْرَجْنَا? [فَأَخْرَجْنَا · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the shift mainly marks divine sovereignty, renews attention, reflects Moses' state, or is identified as a formal turn.)
  20:53/q12/p1 The first-person plural emphasizes God's exclusive power, majesty, wisdom and command over diverse creation. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari points to 6:99, 35:27 and 27:60; Baydawi calls it a sign of power and wisdom; Alusi likens it to a great ruler speaking of a great act. Ibn Ashur says nature obeys its creator.
  20:53/q12/p2 The shift refreshes attention and makes the scene vivid. — reasons: Al-Samarrai says changes of person and number renew attention; al-Burhan also reports that striking acts are presented vividly.
  20:53/q12/p3 Moses' absorption in divine majesty effaces him and makes God the true speaker. — reasons: Biqai describes Moses' self-effacement at the moment of the shift.
  20:53/q12/p4 It is a stylistic iltifāt from third-person God to first-person God. — reasons: Al-Nasafi, Elmalili and Ibn Ashur identify the formal turn; Tabatabai cites three parallel passages at 35:27, 27:60 and 6:99.
20:53/q13 What do the fāʾ and 'by it' express? [فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِۦٓ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the fāʾ marks temporal succession or a further causal/willing relation alongside the bāʾ.)
  20:53/q13/p1 The fāʾ marks succession and the bāʾ links plant growth causally to water. — reasons: Abu Hayyan identifies the water as the pronoun's antecedent; Alusi prefers succession in the fāʾ because the bāʾ already conveys causation; Tabatabai says the preposition affirms cosmic cause and effect.
  20:53/q13/p2 The fāʾ makes divine willing follow rain, while the bāʾ links growth to water. — reasons: Al-Khafaji
