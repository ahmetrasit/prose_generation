## THE CURRENT MAP
5:27/q01 What does “recite” command? [وَٱتْلُ · meaning]
  5:27/q01/p1 Muhammad is commanded to read aloud and recount the account. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān say it also means making listeners hear. Jishumī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī derive tilāwa from following in sequence; al-Naḥḥās calls the verb imperative with its wāw dropped.
5:27/q02 Who are “them” to whom the account is recited? [عَلَيْهِمْ · referent] (turns on: The prior addressee and the story's intended warning.)
  5:27/q02/p1 The Children of Israel or People of the Book. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī, al-Naḥḥās, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Ālūsī, Ṭabāṭabāʾī and Asad connect the pronoun to the preceding scriptural audience; their concealed knowledge supplies proof of revelation. Al-Qurṭubī says Jews plotted violence; al-Bursawī names the People of Scripture.
  5:27/q02/p2 The Meccans, idolaters, or Muhammad's people generally. — reasons: Muqātil names Mecca; al-Rāzī reports a general audience; al-Ālūsī reports Muhammad's community; the Study Quran allows Jews and/or idolaters.
5:27/q03 What does nabāʾ mean here? [نَبَأَ · meaning]
  5:27/q03/p1 A report or consequential account. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ibn al-Jawzī and Abū Ḥayyān gloss it as news; al-Biqāʿī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī stress its weight and benefit. Al-Akhfash retains its hamza from anbaʾa and shortens it in pause because it is annexed.
5:27/q04 Who are “the two sons of Adam”? [ٱبْنَىْ ءَادَمَ · referent] (turns on: Whether “Adam” names humanity's first father or a generic human/Israelite, and the crow's burial lesson and 5:32.)
  5:27/q04/p1 They are Adam's biological sons, Hābīl and Qābīl (Abel and Cain). — reasons: This is the majority view in al-Ṭabarī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr and al-Ālūsī. Name spellings vary (Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī). Al-Ṭabarī says a remote Israelite story would add little; al-Naḥḥās, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Rāzī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ṭabāṭabāʾī and Kuranyolu cite 5:31's first burial; Ibn al-Jawzī and Abū Ḥayyān also cite the first-murderer report. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says an alternate Adam needs a clue; Ibn ʿĀshūr distinguishes generic singular “son of Adam” from this dual. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī questions Torah names due to textual transmission.
  5:27/q04/p2 They were two Israelites, called Adam's sons as human descendants, or sons of another Israelite Adam. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan and al-Ḍaḥḥāk are cited; sacrifices and the law in 5:32 are offered as evidence, as is the Israelite request for a fire-consumed offering in 3:183. Al-Rāzī says it exposes ancient Jewish envy. Ṭabāṭabāʾī records the alternative Israelite Adam.
  5:27/q04/p3 Their names are less important than the real moral event. — reasons: Elmalılı says the general wording avoids legend, and the story's use remains even if treated hypothetically; he also reports some call it mythical, while Ömer Faruk Harman argues ancient parallels can preserve a historical event.
  5:27/q04/p4 In an inward reading, they are the intellect and estimative faculty in Adam's heart. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī identifies Hābīl with intellect and Qābīl with estimation, each with a twin faculty; imagination serves estimation, practical reason serves intellect.
5:27/q05 What birth and sibling background do the reports give for the two sons? [ٱبْنَىْ ءَادَمَ · reports] (turns on: Reports vary on birth order, where conception occurred, and whether sibling marriage was lawful.)
  5:27/q05/p1 Eve bore boy-girl twins; Cain and his sister were born before Abel and his sister. — reasons: Names vary: Iqlīmā/Aqlima for Cain's twin and Labūdā/Luwaiza/Liuda for Abel's. Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī report twenty twin births and forty children except Seth, born alone. Ibn ʿAbbās says Adam saw forty thousand descendants. Some place Cain's birth one hundred years after descent; Ibn Isḥāq says Cain was conceived in Paradise before sin, Abel on earth after descent. Al-Kalbī says births were two years apart.
  5:27/q05/p2 Adam's descendants became many and corrupt in his lifetime. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās says he saw forty thousand descendants and witnessed adultery, drinking and corruption; al-Qurṭubī rejects an uncorroborated report of his daughter's fornication, questioning with whom it occurred.
  5:27/q05/p3 A report gives Eve a daughter before Cain and Abel, then a jinn and a heavenly spouse for the brothers. — reasons: Al-Ṣādiq calls the first daughter Luwaiza; al-Qurṭubī's version calls the first transgressing daughter ʿAnāq, killed before the brothers were born. This account denies that Adam married a daughter to a son.
  5:27/q05/p4 Cain and his sister were born after Abel and his sister. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī records this alternate birth order without further evidence.
5:27/q06 How were the brothers to marry, and why did they dispute it? [ٱبْنَىْ ءَادَمَ · reports] (turns on: Whether exceptional cross-sibling marriage was allowed or the brothers received nonhuman spouses.)
  5:27/q06/p1 Adam directed each brother to marry the other's twin; Cain wanted his own more beautiful twin. — reasons: The only available women were sisters, so cross-birth marriages were temporarily allowed, not one's own twin; Ibn Jurayj says this lasted four generations. Cain variously argued that he was older, more entitled, or born in Paradise, and feared his descendants' inferiority. Al-Qurṭubī cites 4:1 for common parentage and says the arrangement was later abrogated; Ibn Kathīr calls it necessity. Al-Ālūsī says Adam knew Cain's offering would fail and did not intend an impermissible match.
  5:27/q06/p2 Sibling marriage was forbidden; Cain and Abel were paired with a jinn woman and a heavenly maiden. — reasons: Al-Ṣādiq's report calls them Jihāna and Nuzla; al-Qurṭubī's version says Jamāla and Bazla and adds a created womb. Al-Ṣādiq reasons Adam's religion matched Muhammad's, which forbade sibling marriage. Al-Qurṭubī doubts this account and prefers the cross-birth report.
5:27/q07 What does “in truth” specify? [بِٱلْحَقِّ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether truth qualifies the account, the manner of recitation, or its purpose.)
  5:27/q07/p1 The report is true, sound, accurate, and free from fabrication. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī say it agrees with earlier scriptures; Ibn Kathīr compares 3:62, 18:13 and 19:34. Al-Biqāʿī says it agrees with reality and earlier books. Elmalılı and Ibn ʿĀshūr warn against legendary embellishment.
  5:27/q07/p2 The reciter must tell it truthfully and sincerely. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī gives this as a manner of telling; Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn, al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī parse it as a circumstantial phrase from the reciter. Al-Samīn says the bāʾ denotes accompaniment.
  5:27/q07/p3 It is a truthful recitation, with the phrase modifying an omitted verbal noun. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī supply the omitted noun; al-Samīn says al-Zamakhsharī may prefer this parse.
  5:27/q07/p4 It marks the proper serious purpose of condemning envy and teaching a lesson. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī apply it to envy against Muhammad; al-Rāzī cites 12:111 against idle entertainment. Ibn ʿĀshūr says recite it seriously, not for amusement.
5:27/q08 How is the mīm of Ādam read before bi-l-ḥaqq? [ءَادَمَ بِٱلْحَقِّ · readings]
  5:27/q08/p1 Abū ʿAmr makes the mīm still before bāʾ. — reasons: Al-Samīn says this applies to any vowel-preceded mīm before bāʾ.
5:27/q09 How does “when they offered” connect grammatically to the account? [إِذْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether idh is governed by nabāʾ, stands for an omitted “time,” or is circumstantial.)
  5:27/q09/p1 Idh is governed by nabāʾ: the account concerns that time. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī allows the verbal force of the noun nabāʾ to govern time. Abū Ḥayyān and Ibn ʿĀshūr agree. Sajawandī requires a stop after “in truth” to keep the future imperative “recite” from governing past idh.
  5:27/q09/p2 Idh is an apposition to nabāʾ with an omitted “account of the time.” — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī proposes this; Abū Ḥayyān and the Shaykh object that idh takes a time noun whereas nabāʾ is not time. Al-Ālūsī reports the reply that the construction remains sound.
  5:27/q09/p3 Idh is circumstantial to nabāʾ through an omitted governor. — reasons: Abū al-Baqāʾ proposes this; al-Samīn finds it unclear and objects to attaching a past time to the future recitation. Al-Ālūsī says parses must be distinguished.
5:27/q10 Why did the brothers make offerings? [إِذْ قَرَّبَا قُرْبَانًۭا · reports] (turns on: The text does not say whether God/Adam ordered the offerings, a marriage contest prompted them, or they were voluntary.)
  5:27/q10/p1 Adam used acceptance to settle the contested marriage. — reasons: The brothers' offerings would decide who could marry Cain's beautiful twin; some say Adam ordered them, while others say they agreed. Al-Ālūsī says the test did not itself license a forbidden marriage.
  5:27/q10/p2 The brothers initiated offerings themselves as voluntary devotion or ritual worship. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says offerings sought nearness to God regardless of command. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Ibn al-Jawzī transmit spontaneous offering; Ibn Kathīr prefers the Qur'an's apparent account without a woman as cause and cites Ibn ʿAbbās that the rite was not charity to a poor recipient. The Study Quran says al-Ṭabarī's emphasis on nearness casts doubt on the marriage reports.
  5:27/q10/p3 God commanded the two brothers to offer. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī reports this but says the text does not settle the issue. A report from Adam in Ibn Kathīr says he expected sacrifice in his descendants and asked his sons to offer; the Study Quran distinguishes this from the marriage account.
  5:27/q10/p4 The immediate occasion was unspecified. — reasons: Al-Māwardī gives a no-particular-reason report; al-Ṭabarī explicitly leaves God's command undecided. The Qur'an's sparse account makes the type of offering irrelevant to al-Biqāʿī.
  5:27/q10/p5 Adam ordered the brothers to offer, without a state
