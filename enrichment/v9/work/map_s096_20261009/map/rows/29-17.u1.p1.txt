29:17/q11 What does the idols' lack of ملك رزقا show? [لَا يَمْلِكُونَ لَكُمْ رِزْقًۭا · interpretation]
  29:17/q11/p1 The idols have no power to give any provision or benefit, so their worship is futile. — reasons: Rāzī distinguishes merit, origin of existence, sustenance and fear as reasons for worship; the idols fail to create or sustain. Bayḍāwī calls provision a second argument. Biqāʿī points to human need and God's sole good; Ṭabāṭabāʾī reasons that the creator of people and provision owns it. Bursevī glosses malaka through 5:25; Elmalılı says idols cannot even aid digestion. Study Quran cites 16:73, 39:3, 5:76 and 25:3 against treating them as intermediaries.
29:17/q12 What does رزقا denote in the clause? [رِزْقًۭا · grammar] (turns on: Whether rizq names the act of giving or what is given, with different accusative analyses.)
  29:17/q12/p1 It is the act of providing, an accusative verbal noun. — reasons: Bayḍāwī and Abū Ḥayyān allow an infinitive sense; al-Samīn makes it a cognate accusative governed by لا يملكون. Kufan analysis supplies 'they do not have power to provide you provision' with the infinitive clause as object. Ālūsī allows an absolute object by meaning or an omitted verb, but calls the latter strained.
  29:17/q12/p2 It is the provision itself, the thing provided or one's share. — reasons: Bayḍāwī and Abū Ḥayyān allow this sense; al-Samīn parses it as direct object. A Burhān note says an earlier verse's rizq can mean one's allotted share, avoiding ellipsis; the note cites 29:17.
29:17/q13 Why is رزقا indefinite while الرزق is definite? [رِزْقًۭا ٱلرِّزْقَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the article expresses totality, known promised provision, or deliberate repetition beyond the usual definite noun rule.)
  29:17/q13/p1 Negated indefinite رزقا excludes even the least provision; definite الرزق encompasses all provision from God. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf, cited also by Rāzī, states the contrast; Bayḍāwī says the indefinite is general. An eminent scholar cited by Ālūsī takes it as even a little being beyond idols. Ālūsī says the repeated definite need not simply identify the earlier indefinite. Burhān calls the verse a counterexample to an unrestricted rule equating a later definite with an earlier indefinite.
  29:17/q13/p2 God's provision is known by His promise, whereas any supposed provision from idols is unknown. — reasons: Rāzī cites God's promise in 11:6 to explain the definite and indefinite contrast.
  29:17/q13/p3 The definite repetition is deliberate and need not obey a simple identity rule. — reasons: Burhān compares the preceding repeated 'Lord' and 21:22; another reported analysis says a derived predicate such as 'god' means worshipped, so repeating such predicates with different objects does not repeat their substance.
29:17/q14 Why is provision singled out in the appeal? [رِزْقًۭا ٱلرِّزْقَ · rhetoric]
  29:17/q14/p1 Provision is a universal, essential benefit that stands for all good and makes the idols' impotence clear. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya says every creature knows this need and one part can stand for the whole kind. Rāzī uses sustenance as the benefit that preserves life. Ālūsī's cited scholar notes its importance in creation; Biqāʿī connects it to perpetual human need.
29:17/q15 Where and how should provision be sought? [فَٱبْتَغُوا۟ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرِّزْقَ · interpretation] (turns on: How reliance on God, obedience, worship and ordinary earning relate to the command to seek His provision.)
  29:17/q15/p1 Seek all provision from God alone, the only one able to provide it. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says this obtains what is sought; Ibn Kathīr finds emphatic restriction in seeking at God's presence, comparing 1:5 and 66:11. Biqāʿī says all provision is with God in an interconnected created order; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says ownership follows creation.
  29:17/q15/p2 Seek provision through trust in God instead of earning. — reasons: Sahl presents earning as the way of ordinary people; Tustarī and Sulamī transmit the same guidance.
  29:17/q15/p3 Seek provision through obedience and turning toward worship. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭāʾ is reported by Sulamī and Bursevī; Bursevī adds that worship brings requests to fulfillment.
  29:17/q15/p4 Seek it with effort, whether in worship and reliance or ordinary means. — reasons: Biqāʿī reads exertion in ابتغاء, says God usually grants provision through effort and rebukes idle wishfulness; he warns that outward earning should not distract from worship and that sin may cost provision.
29:17/q16 What does the command to worship God require and accomplish? [وَٱعْبُدُوهُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the commands emphasize present needs or preparation for the final meeting, and what motive prompts worship.)
  29:17/q16/p1 Worship God alone, sincerely recognizing His worth and provision. — reasons: Muqātil specifies affirming oneness; al-Ṭabarī adds humility and recognition of blessing. Rāzī says worship responds to God's intrinsic worth and benefit; Biqāʿī says sincerity excludes association and that God rewards or punishes. Ibn Kathīr says it brings good and repels harm in both lives. Ṭabāṭabāʾī contrasts God with idols.
  29:17/q16/p2 Worship and gratitude prepare people to meet God after death. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf, al-Bayḍāwī and Ālūsī give this as an alternative to treating the commands as means to obtain worldly provision; Nasafī also connects them to the return.
  29:17/q16/p3 Worship may arise from habit, hope for mercy, desire, or love. — reasons: Yaḥyā ibn Muʿādh enumerates the four motives.
29:17/q17 Why and how should they thank God? [وَٱشْكُرُوا۟ لَهُۥٓ · interpretation]
  29:17/q17/p1 Thank God alone for provision and all His blessings, through devotion and deeds. — reasons: Muqātil links gratitude to the living return after death; al-Ṭabarī to provision and blessing. Qatāda calls it a divine honor and Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb counts every deed for God as gratitude. Rāzī includes creation and ongoing sustenance; Bursevī and Ālūsī say gratitude preserves blessings and draws more bounty; Ibn Kathīr calls God sole giver.
29:17/q18 What is the force of شكروا له rather than شكروا إياه? [وَٱشْكُرُوا۟ لَهُۥٓ · grammar]
  29:17/q18/p1 Both constructions mean to thank someone; the version with له is more eloquent. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī compares shakartuhu and shakartu lahu and prefers the eloquence of the latter. Ibn ʿAṭiyya likewise says the two Arabic expressions are equivalent.
29:17/q19 How is ترجعون read? [تُرْجَعُونَ · readings] (turns on: Whether the verb makes the people agents of returning or recipients of being returned.)
  29:17/q19/p1 The verse has the passive 'you are returned' form. — reasons: The active form is expressly presented as an alternative by al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, Ālūsī and the Qirāʾāt note.
  29:17/q19/p2 An active reading has fatḥa on tāʾ and kasra on jīm: 'you return.' — reasons: Al-Nasafī attributes it to Yaʿqūb; al-Bayḍāwī and Ālūsī mention it. The Qirāʾāt note says it gives the addressees agency.
29:17/q20 What does returning to God mean here? [إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ · interpretation]
  29:17/q20/p1 After death they return to God at resurrection for judgment and recompense. — reasons: Yaḥyā specifies Judgment Day; al-Ṭabarī says they are questioned for worshipping others despite living on His bounty and cites 2:28. Biqāʿī says bodily return and effortless resurrection, with reward or punishment in both abodes. Ibn Kathīr and Study Quran cite judgment and parallel return verses. Ālūsī includes death then resurrection; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says this shifts motive from provision to afterlife happiness.
29:17/q21 How does the return clause relate to Abraham's preceding speech? [إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ · links]
  29:17/q21/p1 It concludes the call to worship, seek provision and give thanks, urging preparation for judgment. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it Abraham's conclusion; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the return shifts motive from worldly provision to afterlife happiness, where faith and worship matter though natural causes govern worldly provision. Ālūsī allows it to conclude all the speech or connect to its opening, making the intervening case against idols parenthetical.
29:17/q22 How does this verse advance Abraham's argument and connect to its context? [* · links]
  29:17/q22/p1 Abraham moves from the call to worship one God to exposing idols' false status and inability to provide, then to return and accountability. — reasons: Bāqillānī calls the interruption and return a rhetorical figure, noting disagreement whether it is a distinct badīʿ type and citing poems and Qurʾānic passages including 29:16, 18 and 24. Rāzī tests inherent worth, benefit and fear; Ibn Kathīr calls Abraham the model ḥanīf. Kur'an Yolu says all prophets share the monotheist principle and Abraham calls his people to reflect and heed earlier nations. Study Quran notes his father and people invoke ancestral practice, citing 6:74, 19:42, 21:52-53 and 26:74.
29:17/q23 Why does the speech use direct 'you worship' and 'you fabricate'? [تَعْبُدُونَ وَتَخْلُقُونَ · rhetoric]
  29:17/q23/p1 The second-person verbs turn narration into Abraham's direct rebuke of his people. — reasons: Burhān calls it a shift from third-person narration to address, citing 29:16-17; Bāqillānī describes the speech moving from a call to worship and fear to direct exposure before narrating the reply.
