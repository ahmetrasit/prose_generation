18:42/q22 How does the garden's ruin answer the believing companion's words? [* · links]
  18:42/q22/p1 It fulfills his warning and exposes the error of trusting wealth and worldly permanence. — reasons: Al-Mawardi, Abu Hayyan, Ibn Kathir and Study Quran link the loss to 18:40–41; al-Tabari recalls 18:35 and Abu Hayyan 18:32. Al-Nasafi says the owner remembered the warning; al-Tabrisi reports fire and the vanished water. Tabataba'i says the companion's words corrected his claim verbally while ruin demonstrated the error of permanence, followers and self-sufficiency. Al-Qushayri contrasts gratitude to the Creator with disbelief in the Provider. Islahi says he wakes to the collapse he thought impossible. Ibn Kathir and Study Quran note that the men or clan he boasted of in 18:34 cannot help him; Study Quran also cites 28:81.

18:42/q24 What does the owner's loss teach about punishment, trials and worldly attachment? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether the particular punishment is generalized into a rule about who suffers affliction; al-Razi denies that generalization.)
  18:42/q24/p1 The calamity answers his transgression and may benefit him by provoking regret. — reasons: Al-Mawardi and al-Qurtubi call it an answer to transgression; al-Jishumi says punishment can benefit the sinner by causing regret. Al-Razi says the man's life of worldly pursuit left both fortune and religious opportunity lost; al-Jishumi adds that loss of the self to eternal punishment warrants even greater regret. Ibn Kathir says neither clan nor the man himself could help, and God's authority was manifest.
  18:42/q24/p2 His ruin does not show that believers are exempt from suffering. — reasons: Al-Razi rejects that inference: believers also face many trials, and a prophetic report ranks prophets, saints and then the best as most tested; he mentions 43:33.
18:42/q25 What inward reading is given to the garden scene? [* · inward]
  18:42/q25/p1 The two gardens symbolize desire and world, their fruits cravings, and the owner's hand-turning regret for wasted life and potential. — reasons: Al-Nisaburi, as reported by al-Alusi, also reads wealth and status as inclinations and blameworthy traits, and expectation of a better garden as delusion about God.
18:42/q26 How are variant extra yāʾs in the sūra read? [* · readings]
  18:42/q26/p1 Nine of 37 extra yāʾs differ among readers. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid says Nafi' opens all listed forms except the three ma'i sabran; Abu 'Amr quiesces that trio and satajiduni; Ibn Kathir follows except min duni awliya'; Hafs opens the trio. 18:42/q27 Why does the written alif occur in لَٰكِنَّا هُوَ ٱللَّهُ رَبِّى? [* · readings]
  18:42/q27/p1 It reflects an original لكن أنا with deleted hamza and assimilated nūn. — reasons: Ibn Khalawayh says readers omit the alif in liaison on account of the nūn's fatḥa, while those retaining it at pause follow the written script. The phrase is from the neighboring passage.
18:42/q28 What principles and examples does al-Nashr give for major assimilation? [* · readings]
  18:42/q28/p1 Major assimilation lightens articulation, with detailed letter classes and counted examples. — reasons: Al-Nashr says the two letters remain articulated rather than literally entering one another, contrary to some; only manasikakum and ma salakakum assimilate within one word (2:200, 74:42). Alif and hamza neither assimilate nor accept assimilation. Its letter categories have five meeting no like, kind or neighbor; six meeting only identicals; five only kind or near letters; and eleven all, with totals 17 and 16. Ba' examples include la-dhahaba bi-sam'ihim and al-kitab bi-l-haqq, totaling 57 or 59 depending on joining sura endings to basmala. Ta' totals fourteen including finals becoming ha' at pause; tha' has three; ha' two including al-nikah hatta and wa-la abrahu hatta in al-Kahf; ra' totals 35; sin has three examples in al-Hajj and Nuh. These are broader reading notes.
18:42/q29 How are an apparent word's possible meanings resolved? [* · rhetoric]
  18:42/q29/p1 Verbal and contextual clues determine the intended sense; attached wording can redirect or clarify it. — reasons: Al-Burhan distinguishes words explicit in only one sense from apparent words permitting alternatives and gives 2:196 as an illustration.
18:42/q30 What does Ibn Ashur say about the origins and methods of tafsir? [* · sciences]
  18:42/q30/p1 Tafsir began with prophetic-era explanation and developed through transmitted and reasoned approaches. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says companions asked the Prophet meanings; prominent early interpreters include Ali, Ibn Abbas, Zayd, Ubayy, Ibn Mas'ud and Abdullah ibn Amr. Non-Arab converts increased the need, and he calls tafsir the foremost Islamic science. He says Ibn Jurayj (80–149 AH) first compiled it, gathering reports especially from Ibn Abbas's pupils Ata and Mujahid. Later approaches varied: Malik and al-Dawudi exemplify transmitted reports and al-Tabari their best-known extant representative; others reasoned or relied on Israelite stories, leading to fabricated material. Al-Zamakhshari and Ibn Atiyya both examined Arabic and earlier views, the former emphasizing rhetoric and language, the latter law.
18:42/q31 How does Ibn Ashur assess early tafsir chains and claims about Ali? [* · reports]
  18:42/q31/p1 Several reports attributed to Ibn Abbas and Ali are unreliable; chains must be distinguished. — reasons: Ibn Ashur reports criticism of the al-Kalbi–Abu Salih route, Abu Salih's accusation of lying, and the addition of Muhammad ibn Marwan al-Suddi as a 'chain of lies,' contrasted with the Malik–Nafi'–Ibn Umar golden chain. He reports an allegation linking al-Kalbi to Abdullah ibn Saba's extreme claims that Ali would return. He prefers Ali ibn Abi Talha's Ibn Abbas reports over Muqatil and al-Dahhak because al-Bukhari used that route. He says fabricators hid behind Ibn Abbas's fame. Most reports attributed to Ali are fabricated unless soundly chained; Abu Juhaifa reports Ali denying extra family revelation beyond the Quran, while allowing God-granted understanding of it.
