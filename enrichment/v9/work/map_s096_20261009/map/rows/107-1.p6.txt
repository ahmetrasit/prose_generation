[IBNASHUR:107:1/r7] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | Most verse counters count six; al-Ālūsī says this is the Iraqi Basran and Kufan count, while ʿAlī al-Nūrī al-Ṣafāqusī reports seven for Ḥimṣ and six elsewhere, contradicting al-Ālūsī.
[IBNASHUR:107:1/r8] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The surah exposes denial of resurrection through aggression and contempt toward the weak, withholding food from the poor, and neglect of prayer and almsgiving.
[IBNASHUR:107:1/r9] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The opening question expresses astonishment at deniers and the evil conduct their denial produces; denial itself is common, so the listener waits for the surprising orphan abuse revealed next.
[TABATABAI:107:1-7/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The surah warns people professing religion whose traits resemble hypocrites: neglecting prayer, acting ostentatiously, and withholding aid, all incompatible with belief in requital.
[TABATABAI:107:1-7/r11] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The consequence-marker applies the denier of requital to these praying people; they display belief while practically denying requital, so they are not free of hypocrisy.
[TABATABAI:107:1-7/r14] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | reports | Al-Qummi's tafsir says the opening was revealed about Abu Jahl and the Quraysh unbelievers.
[TABATABAI:107:1-7/r2] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The surah may be Meccan or Medinan; another view says its first half is Meccan and second half Medinan.
[TABATABAI:107:1-7/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The seeing in the opening may be literal vision or recognition; the Prophet is addressed as a listener and thus the address extends to every listener. Here din means requital, making its denier a denier of resurrection.
[TABATABAI:107:1-7/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · unnamed view | reports | Another view takes al-din here to mean religion or religious law.
[ASAD-NOTES:107:1#n1/r1] ASAD-NOTES, d. 1412 · author | holds | Asad reads din as objective validity of religion and moral law.
[ASAD-NOTES:107:intro/r1] ASAD-NOTES, d. 1412 · author | holds | The surah was revealed in the mission's early years, probably after surah 102; its title comes from al-ma'un in the last verse.

[BINTSHATI:v2p183/r14] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | The normal question seeks information the asker lacks; when the asker already knows, the interrogative shifts from its literal linguistic meaning into rhetorical figurative use.

[BINTSHATI:v2p184/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | prefers | The author sees the rhetorical secret in asking about what people think is obvious: a surprising question sharpens attention for an unexpected account of what denying religion means.
[BINTSHATI:v2p184/r4] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | People often assume that professing the creed and performing obligatory worship suffice to show belief in religion.
[BINTSHATI:v2p184/r5] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | In Arabic dīn means obedience and submission; the servant is called madīn because servitude subjects him, while dayyān can mean subduer, judge, or ruler.

[BINTSHATI:v2p185/r1] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | Arabic kadhib covers false appearances and failed expectations (such as a camel thought pregnant or lightning mistaken for rain), sensory error, false dreams or hopes, and self-delusion; kadhdhab bi means deny or disbelieve.
[BINTSHATI:v2p185/r2] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | Qur'anic denial most often concerns God, His signs, and His messengers, and extends to denying the warnings, Resurrection, meeting God, the afterlife, Judgment Day, Hell, and punishment.
