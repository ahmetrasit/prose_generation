20:132/q12 What does iṣṭabir ʿalayhā ask of the Prophet? [وَٱصْطَبِرْ عَلَيْهَا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the stress is ordinary persistence, the intensified form's effort, or plentiful voluntary observance.)
  20:132/q12/p1 Persist patiently in performing prayer, enjoining it, and observing its demands. — reasons: Muqātil, Ṭabarī, Māwardī, Jishumī, Baghawī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ṭabrisī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ibn Kathīr, al-Ālūsī, and Islahi describe continued performance. Ṭabarī specifies prayer's limits; Rāzī says the Prophet should embody what he enjoins; Islahi likens persistence to a farmer tending a field until it yields.
  20:132/q12/p2 It calls for strenuous effort beyond simple patience. — reasons: Burhān says the augmented form is stronger than aṣbir; Biqāʿī says iftiʿāl signals effort even when prayer consumes livelihood time; al-Ālūsī infers that fully observing worship is difficult for the soul.
  20:132/q12/p3 It is abundant voluntary prayer, with the Prophet asked to persist even more than his family. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr takes iṣṭibār as restraint and its figurative sense as plentiful voluntary prayer, citing 73:1–2 and 17:79.
20:132/q13 How do inward readers distinguish iṣṭibār from ordinary patience? [وَٱصْطَبِرْ عَلَيْهَا · inward] (turns on: The inward accounts differ on whether the added quality is stillness, struggle, or being comforted beyond pain.)
  20:132/q13/p1 It is inner stillness under trial, in the heart and secret as well as the lower self. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭāʾ calls this the greatest or hardest patience, contrasting it with ordinary patience of the self; Sulamī and Bursevī transmit the formulation.
  20:132/q13/p2 It is a station of struggle, whereas patience is a station of witnessing. — reasons: Bursevī reports the distinction from ʿArāʾis al-Baqlī.
  20:132/q13/p3 It surpasses patience because divine support carries the person without felt pain. — reasons: Qushayrī describes the extra quality of istiṣbār in these terms.
20:132/q14 What inward meaning is given to the family's prayer? [وَأْمُرْ أَهْلَكَ بِٱلصَّلَوٰةِ وَٱصْطَبِرْ عَلَيْهَا · inward] (turns on: Whether inward prayer is a maintained bond, graded forms of presence, or discipline of inner faculties.)
  20:132/q14/p1 It means joining God and steadfastly maintaining that connection. — reasons: Sulamī reports Junayd, who says only the divinely supported can bear this connection.
  20:132/q14/p2 It means presence of heart and a graded prayer of body, soul, heart, secret, and spirit. — reasons: Sulamī reports learning prayer as presence. Yaḥyā ibn Muʿādh likens prayer and fasting to the warp and weft of worshippers' robes. Bursevī expands this into bodily obligatory and voluntary prayer, the soul's ascent, the heart's vigilance, the secret's exclusive witnessing, and the spirit's annihilation and abiding through God, citing 89:29–30, 23:2, and 4:80.
  20:132/q14/p3 It directs inner faculties toward presence, obedience, struggle, and unveiling. — reasons: Qāshānī treats ahl as psychic and spiritual faculties rather than human relatives.
20:132/q15 What is the force of the command to persist compared with 18:28? [وَٱصْطَبِرْ عَلَيْهَا · links]
  20:132/q15/p1 The excerpt raises the contrast but gives no answer. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī asks how keeping oneself steadfast in 18:28 differs from persisting in prayer with one's family here.
20:132/q16 What does 'We ask you no provision' deny? [لَا نَسْـَٔلُكَ رِزْقًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: The object implicitly supplied for rizq: God's own provision or payment, the Prophet's and family's livelihood, other creatures' livelihood, or compensation for prophethood.)
  20:132/q16/p1 God asks no sustenance or payment from the Prophet for Himself; He asks worship, which benefits people. — reasons: Muqātil and Abū Muslim compare 51:56–57. Farrāʾ says no payment is asked; Ṭabarī contrasts bodily work with wealth. Jishumī compares worldly kings' extraction; Rāzī, Ṭabrisī, Ibn ʿĀshūr, and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī stress divine independence. Ibn ʿĀshūr compares taxes and tribute and cites 51:56–58; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects the alternative that the object is provision for creatures or oneself.
  20:132/q16/p2 He is not required to secure livelihood for himself or his family. — reasons: Yaḥyā prefers this by extending al-Ḥasan's gloss of 51:57; Ibn Qutayba, Rāzī, Qurṭubī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, Bursevī, and al-Ālūsī link it to freedom for worship and the Hereafter. Al-Thawrī says God does not burden him with seeking provision. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects reading the request this way.
  20:132/q16/p3 He is not charged with providing for God's other creatures. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, Ibn Muslim, Jishumī, Baghawī, Ṭabrisī, and Biqāʿī explicitly include others' sustenance; Māwardī extends God's provision to every creature. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī objects to taking the request as provision for creatures.
  20:132/q16/p4 He owes no provision in return for the gift of prophethood. — reasons: Yaḥyā reports this explanation from some interpreters.
20:132/q17 What does the wording of the provision statement do here? [لَا نَسْـَٔلُكَ رِزْقًۭا نَّحْنُ نَرْزُقُكَ · rhetoric] (turns on: The commentators locate the rhetorical point in reassurance, rejection of tribute, an answer to anxiety, or independence from wealthy opponents.)
  20:132/q17/p1 It reassures the family that prayer will not cost them their livelihood. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says they should not abandon prayer for earning; Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ibn ʿĀshūr, and Islahi tie the assurance to freedom for the Hereafter and the Prophet's task. Qushayrī says the promise consoles the poor when provision is delayed.
  20:132/q17/p2 It reverses the tribute expected by earthly rulers and displays divine favor and independence. — reasons: Māwardī says God benefits every creature without receiving benefit. Jishumī contrasts worldly kings, Ibn ʿĀshūr taxes and tribute, and Asad a jealous deity; Rāzī says prayer benefits people alone. Study Quran compares the assurance with 51:57–58, where God asks no provision from people.
  20:132/q17/p3 It answers anxiety about where provision will come from. — reasons: Biqāʿī says the answer invokes God's boundless dominion and unexpected means; his next note says no provision concern rivals prayer.
  20:132/q17/p4 It explains that preaching needs no patronage from wealthy opponents. — reasons: Islahi says the Prophet and his companions depend on God's support rather than the opponents' wealth.
20:132/q18 How is 'We ask you no provision' placed in the sentence? [لَا نَسْـَٔلُكَ رِزْقًۭا · grammar]
  20:132/q18/p1 It is parenthetical between the prayer command and God's assurance of provision. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies this sentence-level placement.
20:132/q19 Whom does 'We provide for you' include? [نَّحْنُ نَرْزُقُكَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the singular pronoun only states the Prophet's direct address or also carries family, community, and universal provision.)
  20:132/q19/p1 The Prophet, with his family or all believers included in the promise. — reasons: Māwardī and Ṭabrisī extend divine provision to all creatures. Jishumī states provision to the Prophet. Biqāʿī includes the family; Islahi explains the singular pronoun by the Prophet's headship of the Muslim family, encompassing all Muslims, and compares a ruler's duty to provide for the community. He cites Abū Bakr and ʿUmar's concern for their people, including ʿUmar's fear of responsibility for a distant hungry dog.
  20:132/q19/p2 The Prophet is specifically addressed as the recipient of the earlier promised provision. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr links the singular promise to 20:131, where the Lord's provision is better and lasting.
20:132/q20 How is the fronted 'We' understood? [نَّحْنُ نَرْزُقُكَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether fronting conveys exclusivity or emphasis.)
  20:132/q20/p1 It restricts the provision to God. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports this as one force of fronting; Qushayrī says creatures have no causal power over provision.
  20:132/q20/p2 It adds emphasis to the divine promise. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī gives this as the other possibility.
20:132/q21 How is narzuquka read? [نَرْزُقُكَ · readings] (turns on: Whether the qāf is voiced with ḍamma, left still, or merged into kāf, and whether the word boundary permits assimilation.)
  20:132/q21/p1 The qāf has ḍamma and remains distinct. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān give this as the majority reading; al-Lawāmiʿ explains Abū ʿAmr's avoidance of qāf-to-kāf assimilation at a stopping point.
  20:132/q21/p2 The qāf is still. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya attributes this to a group.
  20:132/q21/p3 The qāf is assimilated into the following kāf. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī name Ibn Waththāb, Yaʿqūb, and a group; al-Lawāmiʿ says assimilating readers treat the ending as medial or avoid stopping. Samīn notes Ibn Waththāb usually assimilates only with a following plural mīm, citing 2:21.
20:132/q22 Does the assurance of provision license leaving work? [لَا نَسْـَٔلُكَ رِزْقًۭا نَّحْنُ نَرْزُقُكَ · law] (turns on: Whether divine provision replaces ordinary livelihood or only protects the priority of prayer.)
  20:132/q22/p1 No; lawful earning continues, and only work that obstructs required prayer must yield. — reasons: Rāzī cites 24:37, whose pious traders are not distracted from remembrance; al-Ālūsī says the general address permits leaving earning only when it prevents prayer and clarifies that persistence does not mean praying all day and night.
  20:132/q22/p2 Some infer that the command must be Prophet-specific to avoid authorizing everyone to abandon earning. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports and rejects this inference; his reply limits the effect of the general command to conflicts with prayer.
