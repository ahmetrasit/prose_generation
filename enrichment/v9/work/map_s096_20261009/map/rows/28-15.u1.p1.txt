28:15/q14 What does the Israelite's appeal mean? [فَٱسْتَغَٰثَهُ ٱلَّذِى مِن شِيعَتِهِۦ عَلَى ٱلَّذِى مِنْ عَدُوِّهِۦ · meaning]
  28:15/q14/p1 He asked Moses to rescue or support him against his opponent. — reasons: The verb derives from ghawth, help. Baydawi and Alusi explain ala through the sense of support or victory; Alusi cites the next day's appeal. Ibn Ashur infers the Israelite was overpowered.
  28:15/q14/p2 The expression also exposes a duty to help the oppressed. — reasons: Qurtubi calls this a duty in every revealed law; Ibn Ashur says coerced work was unjust.
28:15/q15 How is the appeal verb read? [فَٱسْتَغَٰثَهُ · readings] (turns on: Whether the transmitted assistance form is a valid reading and whether its contextual fit warrants preference.)
  28:15/q15/p1 The common reading is fastaghāthahu, 'he sought rescue'. — reasons: Al-Samin derives it from ghawth; Abu Hayyan calls it the majority reading.
  28:15/q15/p2 An assistance reading, istaʿānahu, replaces rescue with asking aid. — reasons: Attributed to Sibawayh, Ibn Miqsam and al-Zafarani. Ibn Jabbarah prefers it for context; Ibn Atiyya calls it a corruption or scribal error, challenged by Abu Hayyan and al-Samin on transmitted evidence. Al-Alusi questions Ibn Atiyya's attribution to al-Akhfash.
28:15/q16 What is omitted in 'the one from his party'? [ٱلَّذِى مِن شِيعَتِهِۦ · grammar]
  28:15/q16/p1 The relative clauses omit their subjects: 'the one who is from his party' and 'the one who is from his enemy'. — reasons: Al-Alusi makes the ellipsis explicit.
28:15/q17 What does wakz mean and where does it land? [فَوَكَزَهُۥ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the word denotes a fist blow or fingertip push and where synonymous or near-synonymous blows are placed.)
  28:15/q17/p1 A thrust, push, or strike with the hand, often a closed fist or gathered palm. — reasons: Mujahid, Tabari and many others specify gathered fingers; some put the blow at the chest, heart, torso, jaw or across the body. Lexicographers compare lakz, nakz, nahz and lahz; Ru'ba's and other cited poetry illustrate the word and related verbs. Abu Zayd's and Aisha's reports illustrate painful pushing.
  28:15/q17/p2 More narrowly, a push with the fingertips. — reasons: Abu Ubayda and al-Farra define it this way; Razi repeats it. Al-Samin says other authorities reverse the wakz/lakz distinction.
  28:15/q17/p3 The exact contrast with lakz varies: wakz at chest, heart or torso; lakz at back, jaw or palate. — reasons: Mawardi and Razi give chest versus back; Ibn Atiyya gives torso versus jaw. Qurtubi gives heart versus jaw and Abu Ubayda's chest sense; al-Samin records reversed assignments.
28:15/q18 What did Moses strike him with? [فَوَكَزَهُۥ · reports] (turns on: Whether the report follows Mujahid's hand account or Qatada's staff account; the lexical suitability of wakz is contested.)
  28:15/q18/p1 With his hand, palm or clenched fist. — reasons: Many sources attribute this to Mujahid; Moses' exceptional strength made one blow fatal. Variants describe a whole hand, gathered fist, or fist shape counted as seventy-three or eighty-three.
  28:15/q18/p2 With a staff or stick. — reasons: Qatada is the recurrent authority. Al-Mufaddal objects that wakz is not used of a staff; Al-Alusi notes Moses' famous staff came later from Shuayb and suggests this would have been another staff.
28:15/q19 What alternative words are read for wakz? [فَوَكَزَهُۥ · readings] (turns on: Which transmitted consonant is read and whether the verbs differ materially.)
  28:15/q19/p1 The ordinary text has fawakazahu. — reasons: Most lexical discussions gloss this form as a push or blow.
  28:15/q19/p2 Ibn Masud or Abdullah reads falakazahu. — reasons: The reports generally treat lakz as close in meaning to wakz, though some distinguish jaw/back from torso/chest.
  28:15/q19/p3 Abdullah or Ibn Masud also has fanakazahu. — reasons: Al-Farra says wakz, nakz and wahz share a meaning; other sources say nakz is pushing or striking, with a poem showing a snake's jab.
28:15/q20 What does 'he finished him' mean? [فَقَضَىٰ عَلَيْهِ · meaning]
  28:15/q20/p1 The man died: Moses' blow killed or finished him. — reasons: Qaḍā ʿalayhi also means finishing an affair; Jarir and al-Ashja are cited for the death idiom. Al-Mubarrad says his appointed time arrived; Baydawi compares 15:66. Bursevi's Mufradat explanation links death with completing one's allotted term.
28:15/q21 Who is the subject of 'finished him'? [فَقَضَىٰ عَلَيْهِ · grammar] (turns on: Which agent is supplied to the verb: Moses, God, or the fatal blow/death.)
  28:15/q21/p1 Moses is the apparent subject. — reasons: Al-Alusi calls this the obvious syntax. Ibn Ashur reports the view but says it is not evident.
  28:15/q21/p2 God is the subject, decreeing the Copt's death. — reasons: Ibn Ashur cites 34:14 for God as the one who decrees death; Abu Hayyan and Alusi list it.
  28:15/q21/p3 The implied blow or death itself is the subject. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and al-Samin list the blow; Ibn Ashur describes an omitted agent, death, in the fixed idiom.
28:15/q22 Did Moses intend to kill the man? [فَوَكَزَهُۥ فَقَضَىٰ عَلَيْهِ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether a forceful blow from Moses could reasonably be expected to kill, and whether he could have used a gentler means.)
  28:15/q22/p1 No; he intended a push or intervention and the blow unexpectedly caused death. — reasons: Qatada, Ibn Ishaq and others say this explicitly. His exceptional strength made one blow fatal; Qurtubi's Ka'b report gives Moses' age as twelve. Ibn Ashur says it struck a vital spot, while Tabatabai points to the absence of the explicit killing verb.
  28:15/q22/p2 The fatal outcome could have been foreseeable given Moses' strength; accident alone does not settle blame. — reasons: Razi rejects the inference of accidental death, saying a strong man's blow could certainly kill a weak man. He says Moses could have freed the Israelite another way even if the death was accidental.
28:15/q23 What does Moses mean by 'this is Satan's work'? [هَٰذَا مِنْ عَمَلِ ٱلشَّيْطَٰنِ · interpretation] (turns on: What 'this' points to and whether Satan's influence concerns anger, the fight, the victim, or partisan judgment.)
  28:15/q23/p1 Satan incited Moses' anger or harsh blow, leading to the death. — reasons: Moses had not been commanded to kill. Qushayri distinguishes Satan's prompting of severity from attributing the death itself to Satan. Al-Alusi prefers prompting or embellishment because it fits the ensuing description. Ibn Ashur allows 'this' to point to blow, death, or anger.
  28:15/q23/p2 'This' is the fight or hostility between the men, which Satan stirred. — reasons: Tabatabai says min may mean kind or origin; the fight brought Moses into peril. Al-Rida's report says Moses killed by God's command, while Satan's work denotes the men's fight.
  28:15/q23/p3 'This' is the slain man's conduct, which was Satanic and made him deserve death. — reasons: Al-Murtada and Razi transmit this reading; Razi also reports taking the man himself as belonging to Satan's faction.
  28:15/q23/p4 The deed was of the same kind as Satan's work, without asserting direct prompting. — reasons: Al-Alusi reports this alternative but prefers the direct prompting explanation.
  28:15/q23/p5 The error lay in tribal favoritism: Moses helped his kin without judging the dispute and killed an innocent man. — reasons: Asad reads verses 16–17 this way and cites Prophetic condemnation of fighting for one's group or helping it unjustly.
28:15/q24 How should Moses' deed and remorse be judged? [فَوَكَزَهُۥ فَقَضَىٰ عَلَيْهِ قَالَ هَٰذَا مِنْ عَمَلِ ٱلشَّيْطَٰنِ · theology] (turns on: Whether the man could lawfully be killed, Moses' intent and prophetic status, and whether remorse marks sin, a lesser choice, or nonculpable error.)
  28:15/q24/p1 He sinned by killing before permission or while under a protected status, and repented. — reasons: Al-Hasan says fighting unbelievers was barred then; Ibn Jurayj says a prophet may kill only by command. Kashshaf calls it a sin requiring repentance. Nasafi and Baydawi add that protected residence made an attack unlawful.
  28:15/q24/p2 His intervention was good defense of an oppressed person; the killing was accidental, not a culpable sin. — reasons: Tabrisi says defensive pain is good; Qushayri invokes divine decree to explain why one blow killed. Tabatabai calls the misguidance a harmful practical error, comparing Adam; Kuran Yolu says a slap rarely kills and Moses' guilt reflects the outcome.
  28:15/q24/p3 The killing may have been permitted, but delaying it or choosing a gentler rescue was preferable. — reasons: Al-Murtada and Razi describe forfeiting the reward of a recommended course; Razi says Moses could seek forgiveness for the lesser choice even if killing was lawful or accidental. Biqai says lack of specific command, not impermissibility, caused regret.
