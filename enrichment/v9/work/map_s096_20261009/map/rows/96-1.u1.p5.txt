96:1/q18 Why identify the Lord as Creator? [ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the description rules out idols, recalls divine favor, proves teaching, or serves cadence.)
  96:1/q18/p1 It distinguishes God from idols called lords, since they create nothing. — reasons: Al-Māwardī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, and Abū Ḥayyān note that Arabs called idols lords but conceded none of them created; the clause therefore removes ambiguity. BURSEVI (فتح الرحمن): Fath al-Rahman says the Creator attribute answers Arabs who called idols lords, since idols cannot share in creating. ALUSI (قيل): Another explanation says the creator description confirms that the intended Lord is God, since Arabs called idols lords but did not attribute creation to them. ALUSI-FULL: Who created recalls God's original favor and ability to teach reading; it may mean the one who creates or the one who created everything, and it rules out idols as true lords because Arabs did not ascribe creation to them. IBNASHUR: The relative clause signals why God's name should be invoked: He is Creator, whereas idols are not; polytheists themselves acknowledged that God created the heavens and earth, so creation is the clearest proof of uniqueness.; cites 31:25 IBNASHUR-FULL: Calling God 'the Creator' implies exclusive divinity: because He alone created, people should invoke Him rather than al-Lat or al-Uzza, though the polytheists acknowledged His creation of heaven and earth.; cites 31:25 BINTSHATI: The command is to recite in the name of the Creator, the rightful Lord rather than the idols the Arabs had fashioned and then worshiped. BINTSHATI: She centers Rabbika on the Prophet’s long search for guidance: the Creator revealed Himself as the Lord worthy of worship over created Arabian idols. BINTSHATI (بعض المفسرين): Al-Razi transmits an argument that naming God as Creator leads idolaters gradually to acknowledge Him: they know they did not create themselves and that their handmade idols did not create them. BINTSHATI: The context plainly asserts the Creator’s lordship; humans are singled out because they read, learn, bear obligations, and receive the revealed words. BINTSHATI (أبو حيان): Abu Hayyan says Rabbuka comforts Muhammad and identifies his exclusive Lord; the Creator attribute follows because Arabs called idols lords, though idols cannot create.
  96:1/q18/p2 Creation proves God's power to teach and nurture the Prophet. — reasons: Rāzī makes creation from nonexistence the ground of nurture; creation of the clot-grown human proves power to teach and care. BURSEVI: Calling God the Creator recalls the first blessing and proves that the One who gave humanity life and its perfections can teach reading; creation belongs solely to Him. ALUSI: The description recalls God's first favor to the Prophet and signals God's power to teach him to read in the gentlest way. ALUSI-FULL: Who created recalls God's original favor and ability to teach reading; it may mean the one who creates or the one who created everything, and it rules out idols as true lords because Arabs did not ascribe creation to them. ELMALILI: The attachment to “your Lord” explains the command through God's role as creator and caretaker, leaving no doubt that He can teach an unlettered recipient. KURANYOLU-TEFSIR: Creation is stressed because God made both human capacity for reading and the objects studied; denying God or gratitude while using these gifts is profound ingratitude.
  96:1/q18/p3 Visible creation offers proof of divine existence, wisdom, power, and unique lordship. — reasons: Jishumī says things are measured by divine wisdom; Rāzī distinguishes rational proof from creation and transmitted knowledge from the pen; al-Bayḍāwī says knowledge of God is the first obligation. BIQAI: Creation is named before command because it is perceptible, easier to conceive, and clearer evidence of God's existence, power, and wisdom; knowing God through His acts is the first obligation. BIQAI-FULL: Creation is placed before command because it is visible, easier to understand, and a clear proof of existence, power, and wisdom; knowing God through His acts is the first obligation. IBNASHUR: The relative clause signals why God's name should be invoked: He is Creator, whereas idols are not; polytheists themselves acknowledged that God created the heavens and earth, so creation is the clearest proof of uniqueness.; cites 31:25 IBNASHUR-FULL: Calling God 'the Creator' implies exclusive divinity: because He alone created, people should invoke Him rather than al-Lat or al-Uzza, though the polytheists acknowledged His creation of heaven and earth.; cites 31:25 IBNASHUR-FULL: The opening five verses gather divine attributes: lordship implies existence and oneness, creation and teaching indicate acts, and al-Akram entails perfection and freedom from deficiency.
  96:1/q18/p4 The ending khalaq suits the sūra's verse cadence. — reasons: BURHAN: The extra endings الأعلى in al-Aʿla and خلق in al-ʿAlaq suit each surah’s cadence, matching the surrounding verse endings.
96:1/q19 Why mention creation generally before creating humanity? [خَلَقَ · rhetoric]
  96:1/q19/p1 General creation comes first, then humanity is singled out for its remarkable origin and responsibility. — reasons: Al-Burhān says the general creative act is followed by the specific creation of a human from a clot. BURHAN: The command first states that God created generally, then singles out the human being's creation from a clot. ALUSI: The author raises, without yet answering in this passage, why humanity is singled out in the next verse if the creation description already has universal force.; cites 96:2 ALUSI-FULL: The preceding surah described humanity's best form; this surah explains its material origin in clinging blood and expands on its afterlife condition, linking the two surahs thematically.; cites 95:4 IBNASHUR: The surah directs him to reflect on God's creation, especially the astonishing human being formed from a clot, as a starting point for inquiry. BINTSHATI: The first verse states creation generally, while the next specifies the human, recipient of revelation and the one uniquely able to read, learn, and bear religious responsibility.
