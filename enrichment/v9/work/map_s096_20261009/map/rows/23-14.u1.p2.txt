23:14/q20 Why does the praise follow the creation account, and why name Allah? [فَتَبَارَكَ ٱللَّهُ · rhetoric] (turns on: The sources give the transition grounds in divine power and beneficence, human honor, humans' inability to praise during formation, and the majestic name.)
  23:14/q20/p1 The extraordinary formation displays God's power, wisdom and generosity and calls forth praise. — reasons: Al-Khaṭṭābī mentions subtle wisdom and mercy; al-Māwardī and Ibn Kathīr note favor, precise formation and resurrection power; al-Bayḍāwī, Ibn ʿĀshūr, Islahi and Kur'an Yolu connect praise with wondrous power and gratitude. Al-Ālūsī says fa introduces a concluding interjection; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī makes the closing praise universal by omitted complements.
  23:14/q20/p2 The praise marks humanity's special honor among created beings. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī notes that God does not praise Himself this way after enumerating heavens, Throne and other beings; he links 95:4 but says divine self-praise outranks praise of humanity. Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya and Mathnawī verses cite 17:70 and 95:4; Kur'an Yolu says the closing praise reflects high regard for humans.
  23:14/q20/p3 God praises Himself on behalf of humans who could not yet praise Him. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī and people of spiritual insight reported by Bursevī say humans were not able to thank Him as their stages were being described.
  23:14/q20/p4 The majestic name Allah gathers divine names and makes the audience feel awe. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says it gathers all beautiful names; al-Ālūsī says the shift to it marks these acts as divine prerogatives and invites glorification.
23:14/q21 How is aḥsan al-khāliqīn parsed? [أَحْسَنُ ٱلْخَٰلِقِينَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether a definite annexed superlative can qualify Allah, and how an elided comparison is supplied.)
  23:14/q21/p1 Its comparative measure, 'in creating or estimating,' is elided. — reasons: Al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn and Bursevī say khāliqīn makes the omission understood; al-Samīn compares 22:39. Al-Ālūsī likens it to the Prophetic saying that God is beautiful, with the act understood as beautiful.
  23:14/q21/p2 It is apposition to Allah. — reasons: Al-Nasafī allows apposition; Abū al-Baqāʾ denies an adjective because a comparative remains indefinite despite annexation; Bursevī adopts apposition. Al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī regard the apposition as possible but less preferred.
  23:14/q21/p3 It is predicate of an omitted huwa. — reasons: Al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn and Abū al-Baqāʾ list this parse; al-Samīn calls the ellipsis less likely.
  23:14/q21/p4 Aḥsanu is an adjective of Allah. — reasons: Al-Samīn prefers it because derived apposition is uncommon and argues the superlative annexation can be genuinely definite; al-Ālūsī prefers the same. Jishumī calls the phrase descriptive.
23:14/q22 What does khāliqīn mean and can beings besides God be called creators? [ٱلْخَٰلِقِينَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether khāliq describes craft, Jesus's permitted shaping, independent origination, or an intensifying superlative.)
  23:14/q22/p1 It means makers, shapers or estimators; humans can be called creators in this limited Arabic sense. — reasons: Al-Akhfash and al-Ṭabarī cite craft usage and Zuhayr's line about finishing an object; al-Wāḥidī explains measuring leather for flasks, skins or shoes; al-Aʿlam elaborates leatherwork. Al-Layth gives feminine khāliqāt for women makers. Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī and Kur'an Yolu distinguish manufacture from origination. Al-Ṭabrisī says exact true creation belongs to God (7:54); God creates without tools or limit according to Jishumī.
  23:14/q22/p2 The plural includes Jesus, who was allowed to create or shape. — reasons: Ibn Jurayj invokes Jesus and 3:49; al-Ṭabrisī distinguishes shaping clay from divine creation, and al-Burhān reports al-Qāḍī Abū Bakr's appeal to that sense. Al-Rāzī objects that the plural is broader than Jesus alone.
  23:14/q22/p3 Only God is properly a creator; others are called this only by qualification, belief or figurative extension. — reasons: Some reported by al-Qurṭubī deny the name to humans; al-Kaʿbī allows it only with qualification. Rāzī's fellow theologians cite 39:62 and compare 30:27, taking the plural as what people believe. Asad reports al-Ṭabarī's secondary usage; al-Ṭabrisī and al-Ālūsī reserve origination from nothing for God. Al-Rāzī rejects inferring human origination from the word.
  23:14/q22/p4 God's making surpasses statue-makers because He gives movement and spirit. — reasons: Muqātil compares inert statues; Yaḥyā cites image-makers who cannot breathe spirit and reports sayings warning that imitators must bring images to life or create even a fly, speck or mosquito. Al-Wāḥidī and Bursevī repeat the contrast.
  23:14/q22/p5 The plural denotes actual human creators of their own acts. — reasons: The Muʿtazila argue that the comparative presupposes other creators and humans originate chosen acts by estimation; Al-Asʾila al-Muqḥima poses the same objection. Bursevī answers with image-makers who cannot breathe spirit (5:110), and al-Ālūsī reports the Muʿtazilite sense of independent production.
  23:14/q22/p6 The superlative magnifies God's excellence without comparing Him with independent creators. — reasons: Islahi says Arabic afʿal with a plural can intensify rather than compare; he rejects altering creator to a lesser sense and says God fashioned people in the best mould. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ proposes aḥsan from khaliq meaning worthy rather than a straightforward comparison from khalaqa; she reports al-Rāghib's God-without-model versus human-with-model distinction.
23:14/q23 What does 'best' say about God's creation and human evil acts? [أَحْسَنُ · theology] (turns on: Whether goodness means flawless construction, moral goodness excluding evil acts, or the Creator's incomparable scope.)
  23:14/q23/p1 God's creating is unbounded, exact and free of defect. — reasons: Jishumī says God makes substances, accidents, life, death and agents without tools or limits; al-Ṭabrisī says His exact measure has no disparity; Elmalılı returns every created agency to Him.
  23:14/q23/p2 Best means precise, well-composed creation; this does not exclude God's creation of unbelief and sin. — reasons: Al-Rāzī answers the Muʿtazila that excellence can mean exact composition and that acts are good from God, who is subject to no higher command. Al-Ālūsī likewise says all is good insofar as it comes from God.
  23:14/q23/p3 Because all God's creation is good and wise, He does not create unbelief or sin; humans originate those acts. — reasons: The Muʿtazila infer this from the praise and from their claim of deliberate human origination.
23:14/q24 Who first uttered the closing phrase in the reports? [فَتَبَارَكَ ٱللَّهُ أَحْسَنُ ٱلْخَٰلِقِينَ · reports] (turns on: The named speaker and sequence vary, and the Ibn Abī Sarḥ and Muʿādh reports face chronological or transmission objections.)
  23:14/q24/p1 ʿUmar anticipated or concurred with the revealed ending. — reasons: Some reports have him speak as the Prophet paused, others after revelation began or before the matching ending descended; the Prophet is reported to say it was revealed that way. Al-Wāḥidī and al-Rāzī list it among four of ʿUmar's agreements, alongside the Station, veiling and admonition to wives. Al-Itqān treats Companion agreement as an occasion of revelation and reports a Prophetic saying that truth was put on ʿUmar's tongue and heart.
  23:14/q24/p2 The scribe ʿAbd Allāh ibn Abī Sarḥ anticipated a phrase and then claimed prophethood or apostatized. — reasons: Accounts vary whether he anticipated the closing praise or 'another creation,' and whether he later converted at the conquest or died an unbeliever. Al-Ṭabrisī says, if sound, coincidence is possible and Ibn Abī Sarḥ was self-deceived; al-Nasafī objects that a Medinan apostasy conflicts with the Meccan sūra. Al-Ālūsī considers a Medinan verse or dictation, noting implications for al-Suyūṭī's Meccan exceptions.
  23:14/q24/p3 Muʿādh ibn Jabal anticipated the ending. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Qurṭubī, al-Nasafī and Abū Ḥayyān report this variant. Zayd ibn Thābit's report has Muʿādh supply the words while the Prophet dictated, and the Prophet smile or laugh; Ibn Kathīr calls that report severely anomalous because the sūra is Meccan, but Zayd and Muʿādh belong to Medina.
  23:14/q24/p4 The accounts may reflect memorable anticipations of an ending cued by its preceding words. — reasons: Al-Burhān's tawshīḥ or al-muṭmiʿ has an opening that makes its close foreseeable, like the supports of a sash; al-Ālūsī says strong Qurʾānic composition often reveals the ending. This helps explain coincident speech without treating it as revelation from the speaker.
23:14/q25 Does anticipation of the verse's words challenge its inimitability? [فَتَبَارَكَ ٱللَّهُ أَحْسَنُ ٱلْخَٰلِقِينَ · theology] (turns on: Whether one predicted phrase is enough to rival the larger revealed discourse.)
  23:14/q25/p1 No: a coincident short phrase does not match the Qur'an's miraculous discourse. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says the utterance may fall below the amount where miraculous quality appears; Bursevī and al-Ālūsī compare the threshold of a complete shortest sūra. Al-Ṭabrisī says coincidence is possible and does not make the scribe a prophet. Al-Ālūsī says the ending depends on preceding composition.
  23:14/q25/p2 The scribe's anticipation seemed to him to undermine the Prophet's claim. — reasons: In al-Rāzī's version Ibn Abī Sarḥ objects that a human had uttered comparable words; al-Rāzī rejects the inference.
