B-ZILAL, d. 1386 · رسول الله [ ص ] | reports | The Prophet says prayer is invalid without reciting the Opening of the Book; the report is in both Sahih collections.
[QUTB-ZILAL:1:1/r3] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | The short surah gathers core Islamic creed, worldview, feelings, and guidance, explaining its repetition in every rakʿa and why prayer without it is invalid.
[QUTB-ZILAL:1:6/r1] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | The prayer asks God both to show the connected, straight road and to keep the worshippers upright on it; knowing and following it are gifts of His guidance and care.
[QUTB-ZILAL:1:6/r2] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | Guidance to the straight path is the believer's greatest and first request because it certainly secures happiness in both worlds and aligns human nature with God's law and creation.
[QUTB-ZILAL:1:7/r2] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · رسول الله | reports | Abu Hurayra reports in Sahih Muslim that God calls al-Fatiha a prayer divided between Himself and His servant: praise, mercy, and glorification are the Lord's; worship and aid are shared; guidance and the servant's request belong to the servant, who receives what he asks.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r4] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Asking for guidance expresses a desire for sound judgment and admits one's need for knowledge.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r1] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | After praise, the worshipers' confession of exclusive worship and help bridges praise to petition; trusting God's welcome and hoping for His favor, they ask for guidance.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r10] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | rejects | He rejects that distinction as, at most, a prevalent usage rather than proof that the verb changes meaning with its construction; both huda and hidaya are verbal nouns, while the source noun is hady.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r11] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Hadā necessarily takes one object; for a second, it is direct when it means showing or clarifying, and prepositional when it means general guidance or pointing, so construction signals the embedded sense.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r12] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The basic sense is showing someone the route to a destination; guidance meanings developed as widely used metaphors from this literal sense.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r13] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | The migration report says Abū Bakr hired a skilled route guide from Banū al-Dayl, illustrating the literal Arabic sense of hādī.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r14] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | In religious usage when ascribed to God, guidance is direction toward deeds pleasing to Him; its opposite, misguidance, is leading someone astray.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r15] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · الجمهور | prefers | The majority, including the Ashʿarites, define guidance as showing the way to an end whether or not arrival occurs; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls this correct.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r16] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · جماعة منهم الزمخشري | reports | A group including al-Zamakhsharī requires successful delivery in the definition, lest guidance fail to differ from misguidance; their dispute reflects different accounts of divine command and will versus creation.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r17] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | There is no dispute that guidance culminating in arrival is what religiously should be sought from both guide and guided, though a guide may err or the recipient may reject it.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r18] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · عبد الحكيم | reports | ʿAbd al-Ḥakīm chooses a shared sense covering both outcomes because the Qur'an uses guidance for each; absent contrary evidence, neither homonymy nor metaphor is assumed. | mentions 28:56, 41:17
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r19] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Guidance comprises four ordered kinds: innate powers for human existence, rational proofs distinguishing truth, revealed messengers and books for harder matters, and disclosure of higher truths.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r2] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The petition is the purpose of the preface, like the subject of a speech or the transition in a poem, fitting the Fātiḥa as a preamble to a book sent as guidance and mercy.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r20] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The first kind gives sensory and motor powers, from an infant's instinctive feeding and responses to danger through practical needs and the rational power that derives unknowns from knowns.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r21] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The second kind is theoretical knowledge supplied by proofs that distinguish truth from falsehood and correctness from error.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r22] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The third kind comes through messengers, revealed books, and measures of justice for matters beyond proof or too burdensome to settle by proof, as in God's description of the messengers. | mentions 21:73
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r23] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The highest kind unveils higher realities and meanings beyond the reach of rational minds through revelation, sound inspiration, or divine manifestations. | mentions 6:90
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r24] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | What a particular supplicant seeks depends on that person's or group's condition; as a prayer taught to all Quran-addressed believers, it requests kinds of guidance distributed according to each person's capacity.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r25] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Some guidance is sought for acquisition by those who lack it, while guidance already possessed, including much of the innate first kind, is sought for its continuance.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r26] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | A request form ordinarily asks that an act or abstention occur; asking only for continuance is figurative, as in the address to believers in 4:136. | mentions 4:136
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r27] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Asking for more guidance remains literal when some levels are attained and others lack; seeking increase also entails asking that existing guidance endure, making the request a kināya for continuance as well.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r28] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | For the Prophet, who had reached guidance's highest degree, the wording is figurative and an appropriation of the verse; the verse itself asks for complete guidance through further attainment and continuance.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r29] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Ṣirāṭ means a road; Arabic dialects pronounce it with ṣād or sīn, while the Hijaz dialect uses ṣād, an emphatic substitution easing the transition into emphatic ṭā.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r3] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · صاحب الكشاف | rejects | Al-Zamakhsharī treats the petition as an answer to an unstated question; Ibn ʿĀshūr prefers to read it as the natural aim of the preceding praise and confession.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r30] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · الجعبري | reports | Al-Jaʿbarī says Arabs change sīn to ṣād before several consonants; here ṣād matches ṭā in emphasis and elevation, avoiding a difficult movement from low articulation to high.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r31] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · حمزة | reports | Qays pronounces an intermediate sound between sīn and ṣād, called ishām, which Ḥamza reads in the transmission of Khalaf; al-Qurṭubī reports a pure zāy dialect among ʿUdhra, Kalb, and Banū al-Qayn, but says it is weak and unread.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r32] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · ابن كثير في رواية قنبل | reports | Most canonical readers use the eloquent ṣād form, while Ibn Kathīr in Qunbul's transmission reads sīn; Ibn ʿĀshūr prefers ṣād for its conformity to the muṣḥaf spelling and the eloquent dialect.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r33] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The Companions wrote ṣād to mark the more eloquent form in Quraysh usage; readers of sīn understood the written form as allowing known dialectal alternation, so the difference concerns pronunciation, not the word's lexical substance.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r34] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The spelling of al-muṣayṭir with ṣād likewise preserves a form whose origin is sīn, illustrating how the written ṣād need not rule out sīn pronunciation.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r35] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Al-ṣirāṭ is an Arabic word, although al-Naqqāsh and Ibn al-Jawzī report it as the Roman word for road; Abū Ḥātim is also cited, and al-Suyūṭī consequently listed it among loanwords.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r36] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The road is a metaphor for truth, through which a person reaches the success of God's approval that Islam seeks.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r37] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Mustaqīm is the active participle of istiqāma, the result of making something straight; it means having no bend or detour.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r38] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | A straight road is best because it reaches the destination most directly, without leading its traveller astray, causing hesitation, or producing confusion.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r39] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | By metaphor, the straight path is clear truth unmixed with false suspicion, like a road without branching side tracks.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r4] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The petition is separated from the preceding clauses because those clauses are declarative while this one is imperative.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r40] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · ابن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbās explains the straight path as the true religion and is also reported to call it the religion of Islam; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the two glosses explain one another.
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r41] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Ibn ʿAbbās's gloss does not mean asking for an obsolete religion: all divinely revealed religions were straight paths for their own communities, as Satan's words about God's path also show. | mentions 7:16
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r42] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The definite article evokes a mentally understood kind, requested through its unique instance: straightness is singular, while misguidance has many forms. | mentions 10:32, 5:100
[IBNASHUR:1:6/r43] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | One possible reading takes the path as Islam: God
