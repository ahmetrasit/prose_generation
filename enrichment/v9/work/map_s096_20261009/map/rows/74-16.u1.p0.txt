## THE CURRENT MAP
74:16/q01 What does kallā do here? [كَلَّآ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether kallā closes the preceding expectation or introduces the next clause.)
  74:16/q01/p1 It rebukes the man's expectation and denies him further increase. — reasons: Nahhas calls it restraint of greed; al-Wahidi al-Wajiz says it cuts off hope. Ibn al-Jawzi and the Ibn Abbas and Muqatil report in al-Wahidi al-Basit paraphrase it as God's 'I will not [increase him].' Al-Qurtubi allows it to close the preceding statement; Bursevi explicitly attaches it to what precedes. Al-Elmali describes kallā as a particle rejecting a claim, hope, or request. Ibn Ashur and others connect the denial to the man's ingratitude.
  74:16/q01/p2 It means 'indeed' and opens the following statement. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi transmits this as the alternative to taking kallā with the preceding hope.
74:16/q02 What further increase was the man hoping for? [كَلَّآ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether his hope concerned added worldly gifts or Paradise.)
  74:16/q02/p1 He expected more worldly gifts, wealth, or rank. — reasons: Muqatil says God had already expanded his wealth; al-Wahidi, al-Razi, Ibn Atiyya, and others identify his hope as more gifts. Abu Hayyan calls the desire greedy and worldly. Islahi says the affluent man's expectation of more was false, and his favors were unearned gifts testing gratitude.
  74:16/q02/p2 He hoped for admission to Paradise. — reasons: Al-Hasan and others, as reported by Abu Hayyan, say he claimed that if Muhammad spoke truthfully Paradise had been created for him alone.
74:16/q03 What happened to his fortunes after this verse? [كَلَّآ · reports]
  74:16/q03/p1 His fortunes declined continually until he died, with some reports specifying poverty. — reasons: Sa'id ibn Jubayr in Ibn Abi Hatim and al-Durr says his person, wealth, and children declined; al-Durr adds his identification as al-Walid ibn al-Mughira ibn Hisham al-Makhzumi, father of thirteen sons. Muqatil in al-Wahidi says he became poor and died poor; al-Razi reports the exegetes similarly. Al-Qurtubi, al-Kashshaf, al-Baydawi, Bursevi, and al-Alusi report continuing loss of wealth; al-Nasafi adds status. Al-Biqa'i treats the foretold decline as evidence of prophethood.
74:16/q04 Who is the man referred to by innahu? [إِنَّهُۥ · referent]
  74:16/q04/p1 He is al-Walid ibn al-Mughira. — reasons: Ibn Ishaq names him through Ibn Hisham; al-Tha'labi, al-Qurtubi, and the Study Quran use the same identification. Al-Jishumi reports Ibn Amir calling him al-Walid ibn al-Mughira al-Makhzumi, wealthy with sons and called al-Wahid among his people. Sa'id ibn Jubayr in al-Durr gives the longer name al-Walid ibn al-Mughira ibn Hisham al-Makhzumi and says he had thirteen sons.
74:16/q05 Why does the clause beginning innahu follow the rebuke? [إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ لِءَايَٰتِنَا عَنِيدًۭا · links]
  74:16/q05/p1 It explains why he is denied more: defiance of God's signs makes further gifts undeserved. — reasons: Al-Razi calls the clause an explanatory resumption answering 'why not increase him?'; al-Baydawi and al-Alusi also call it a resumed explanation. Al-Kashshaf, al-Nasafi, and Abu Hayyan say the ungrateful disbeliever does not deserve more from the Benefactor. Al-Qurtubi ties the refusal to unbelief in God's favors; al-Samin connects both the denial of wealth and rebuke of hope to hostility. Ibn Ashur says ingratitude became active opposition warranting loss of blessings. Al-Bursevi adds that defying clear Quranic signs after many gifts merits complete deprivation; the gifts had been gradual entrapment.
74:16/q06 What does kāna imply about his opposition? [كَانَ · rhetoric]
  74:16/q06/p1 His opposition was an established, persistent state. — reasons: Al-Razi says kāna shows that hostility had long been his settled condition. Asad takes the past form as implying permanently recurrent stubbornness and accordingly renders verses 18–25 in the present tense.
74:16/q07 What are 'Our signs' here? [لِءَايَٰتِنَا · referent] (turns on: Whether the signs are the Qur'an in the immediate context or the broader proofs, Prophet, truth, or blessings.)
  74:16/q07/p1 The Qur'an or its revealed verses. — reasons: Ibn Jubayr in al-Mawardi and Ibn al-Jawzi names the Qur'an; Muqatil in al-Wahidi and the verse glosses describes turning away from and disbelieving in it. Ibn Atiyya prefers Quranic verses because al-Walid subsequently called the Qur'an magic; Abu Hayyan prefers them because the context treats his speech about the Qur'an and allegation of magic. Islahi especially includes Quranic warnings of worldly and next-world punishment and God's rule over both realms. Al-Alusi and al-Elmali offer this alongside broader divine proofs.
  74:16/q07/p2 God's proofs more broadly, including revealed books, messengers, and signs of oneness. — reasons: Al-Tabari explicitly includes revealed books and messengers; al-Tabrisi calls them proofs and arguments recognized by the man. Al-Razi expands them to proofs of oneness, justice, power, prophethood, and resurrection. Al-Biqa'i stresses their guidance to oneness, and the Study Quran transmits the broad view attributed to Tabari. Al-Alusi and al-Elmali mention proofs of oneness as an alternative.
  74:16/q07/p3 Muhammad, the Messenger of God. — reasons: Al-Suddi gives this identification in al-Mawardi and Ibn al-Jawzi. Mujahid in al-Qurtubi speaks of obstinacy toward Muhammad and revelation; the Study Quran says that opposing the Prophet in al-Walid's story brings the cutting off of gifts.
  74:16/q07/p4 The truth. — reasons: Mujahid gives this gloss in al-Mawardi and Ibn al-Jawzi; al-Tabari says he turned away from the truth and transmits Mujahid's wording that he opposed and avoided it.
  74:16/q07/p5 God's blessings or favors. — reasons: Abu Hayyan raises blessings because the preceding verses list gifts, but prefers Quranic signs. Al-Alusi and al-Elmali include blessings with divine proofs as a possibility. The Study Quran reports blessings as a referent from Alusi and Ibn Kathir. Ibn Kathir and al-Qurtubi explain his hostile response as unbelief in God's favors.
74:16/q08 Why is lām used before 'Our signs'? [لِءَايَٰتِنَا · grammar] (turns on: Whether the added lām chiefly reinforces a weakened construction or emphasizes the action.)
  74:16/q08/p1 It reinforces the syntactic relation to a fronted object governed by a derived intensive form. — reasons: Al-Samarrai says ʿānada is directly transitive, so the lām is optional; no bāʾ is needed. He cites 7:154 for lām with a fronted object and 35:31 and 11:107 for lām after a deverbal governor. He says this verse has both conditions: a fronted object and ʿanīd, an intensive derivative of ʿānada. He also reports grammarians' explanation that lām strengthens a weakened syntactic link.
  74:16/q08/p2 The additional lām intensifies the event itself. — reasons: Al-Samarrai personally prefers this rhetorical account of the lām's emphasis.
74:16/q09 What does putting 'Our signs' before ʿanīd emphasize? [لِءَايَٰتِنَا عَنِيدًۭا · rhetoric]
  74:16/q09/p1 It singles out God's signs as the object of his hostility, exposing the depth of his loss. — reasons: Al-Razi says he did not oppose other signs but singled out divine proofs. Bursevi explicitly derives this restriction from fronting the signs. Al-Biqa'i says the signs guide to oneness while deceptive arguments for shirk did not meet the same opposition.
74:16/q10 What does ʿanīdan mean here? [عَنِيدًۭا · meaning]
  74:16/q10/p1 Stubbornly and deliberately opposed to truth or God's signs, rejecting or avoiding them. — reasons: Ibn Hisham adduces Ru'ba's line about striking the head of al-anad; al-Tabari compares an ʿanūd camel and cites a poem with al-ʿand; al-Mawardi cites al-Harith and supplies the line's continuation elsewhere. Al-Tabari derives the word from ʿānada and compares ʿām qābil; al-Mubarrad in al-Wahidi compares jālasa/jalīs and ḍājaʿa/ḍajīʿ; al-Razi compares jalīs, akīl, and ʿashīr. Al-Qurtubi says the root also describes a camel straying from its path. Mujahid emphasizes turning aside and avoiding truth; Ibn Abbas and Qatada emphasize denial and ingratitude. Al-Malawi in al-Biqa'i defines persistent crossing of bounds and knowing opposition, arising from pride, dry nature, bad character, or impaired reason. Bursevi says it is equivalent to muʿānd; Asad cites Lisan al-'Arab on rejection of what one knows to be true. Abu Hayyan refers to his earlier explanation at 14:15 without restating it.
74:16/q11 How did al-Walid's opposition show itself? [لِءَايَٰتِنَا عَنِيدًۭا · interpretation]
  74:16/q11/p1 He rejected the Qur'an and the proofs it brought, while recognizing the truth he opposed. — reasons: Muqatil and al-Wahidi say he turned away from the Qur'an and would not believe or obey. Ibn Abbas in al-Wahidi says he opposed everything Muhammad brought. Al-Tabrisi stresses that he recognized the proofs; al-Razi says he resisted proofs of oneness, justice, power, prophethood, and resurrection, knowing them inwardly while denying them verbally, which Razi calls the worst kind of unbelief. Ibn Kathir and Asad likewise stress knowing rejection; al-Biqa'i calls his obstinacy extreme and innate.
74:16/q12 What is reported about al-Walid's account of Muhammad? [* · reports]
  74:16/q12/p1 At pilgrimage time he advised Quraysh to call Muhammad a sorcerer who divided families. — reasons: Ibn Hisham reports al-Walid rejecting descriptions of soothsayer, madman, poet, and sorcerer as accurate before recommending 'sorcerer' for its effect on families. Al-Jishumi's account has him rejecting soothsayer, poet, and madman, then choosing 'magician' and saying the Qur'an separates families. The lists vary slightly, but the reported conclusion agrees.
74:16/q13 What does the account teach about blessings and ingratitude? [* · interpretation]
  74:16/q13/p1 Gifts call for gratitude; persistent defiance after receiving them worsens guilt and can bring their loss. — rea
