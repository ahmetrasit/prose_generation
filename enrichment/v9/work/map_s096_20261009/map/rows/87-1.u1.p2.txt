  87:1/q18/p1 The Prophet loved this sura, and it was called the best of the glorification suras. — reasons: Ali transmits the affection; Ibn Kathir says Ahmad alone transmits one route. Abu Tamim reports that Ubayy supplied its title after the Prophet forgot which glorification sura was best; al-Malawi attributes the love to abundant knowledge and blessings.
  87:1/q18/p2 Reciting the sura earns ten good deeds for each letter revealed to Abraham, Moses and Muhammad. — reasons: Jishumi attributes this promise to a report through Ubayy.
  87:1/q18/p3 The glorification has immense weight and earns forgiveness, Paradise, and Michael's intercession. — reasons: A report through Gabriel compares its weight to the Throne, Chair and mountains; Qurtubi gives no grading.
  87:1/q18/p4 Someone who knew the sura's merit would recite it sixteen times. — reasons: Razi transmits this prophetic saying without further grading in these notes.
  87:1/q18/p5 A reciter of the sura is credited as reciting the Quran and the scriptures of Moses and Abraham. — reasons: Study Quran reports this as a Shiite tradition from Ali and says its blessing warrants daily recitation.
87:1/q19 When did the Prophet recite or recommend this sura in prayer? [* · reports] (turns on: Which prayer and accompanying suras each transmitted report describes.)
  87:1/q19/p1 He recited al-Aʿla with al-Ghashiya on Eid and Friday, even when the two days coincided. — reasons: Reports come from al-Nu'man, Abu Utba, Ibn Abbas and Samura; Tirmidhi questions a link through Habib's father while other routes differ. Study Quran also mentions an evening-prayer recommendation.
  87:1/q19/p2 He recited it in the first unit of witr, followed by al-Kafirun and al-Ikhlas, sometimes with the two refuge suras. — reasons: Ubayy, Ibn Abbas, Ibn Abza, Aisha and others transmit the sequence; Aisha adds the refuge suras and al-Hakim grades her report sound.
  87:1/q19/p3 He used it in noon and sometimes afternoon prayer. — reasons: Anas reports both; Jabir b. Samura reports noon, and Imran says the Prophet noticed a man reciting it along with him at noon.
  87:1/q19/p4 He recommended it as a shorter recitation when Muʿadh prolonged congregational prayer. — reasons: Reports place the episode in Maghrib or night prayer and vary the accompanying short suras: al-Shams, al-Layl, al-'Alaq or al-Duha. One says a boy left to tend his camel; the Prophet warned against making prayer a trial.
  87:1/q19/p5 He recited it in the first unit of his last Maghrib prayer and al-Kafirun in the second. — reasons: Alusi reports this from Abd Allah b. al-Harith.
87:1/q20 What extra wording was rejected when reciting this sura? [* · reports]
  87:1/q20/p1 An improvised line about pregnancy and birth was rebuked as an addition to the Quran. — reasons: Razi recounts Aisha rebuking a Bedouin reciter and companions; Durr reports the Prophet telling Hadrami b. Amir that the Quranic text was sufficient and healing. The reports differ in speaker and response.
87:1/q21 How does the opening connect with the preceding sura and later verses? [* · links] (turns on: Different scopes of connection: the previous sura, the opening's foreshadowing, the whole sura's argument, and its historical setting.)
  87:1/q21/p1 It answers al-Tariq's themes of human creation, divine delay, and the seriousness of revelation with fuller praise of the Creator. — reasons: Abu Hayyan links 86:5 and 86:13 to 87:1 and 87:6. Biqai says the delay in 86:17 excludes haste, its denial of jest excludes false revelation, and this sura expands its creation themes. Alusi compares humans and vegetation in 86:12 and 87:2-4; Elmalili hears reassurance against deniers' plotting. Islahi sees the address turning from opponents to the Prophet.
  87:1/q21/p2 The opening prepares the promise of preserved recitation and guidance, then returns in purification, remembrance, and prayer. — reasons: Razi links the Prophet's anxious rehearsal to complete glorification. Ibn Ashur says verse 6 is foreshadowed. Biqai sees 87:14-15 and the ending reprise opening purification, removal of defects, positive virtues and prayer; Tabatabai says pure speech prepares the Prophet's proclamation.
  87:1/q21/p3 The sura's arc establishes God's oneness through creation, steadies the Prophet, contrasts hearers, and confirms earlier revelation. — reasons: Ibn Ashur names creation, sustenance, scripture, a gentle law, the righteous and wretched, and continuity with former scriptures. Qutb adds afterlife recompense and the ease of message, messenger and community; Islahi sees gradual maturation of creation and human capacities.
  87:1/q21/p4 The likely early setting of nearby praise and admonition is Mecca's Kaʿba cult. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum compares 73:2-5 and 74:2-3 and contrasts this with an already formed community's private devotions.
87:1/q22 Why does this sura open with an imperative of glorification? [سَبِّحِ · rhetoric] (turns on: The formal series of praise openings, the pedagogic order of transcendence, and the sura's consoling purpose.)
  87:1/q22/p1 It completes a Quranic sequence of tasbih openings: verbal noun, past, present, and imperative. — reasons: The author of al-Ajaib and Biqai identify 17:1, 57:1, 59:1, 61:1, 62:1 and 64:1 as the earlier forms; Burhan describes fourteen praise openings and Abu Shama calls 87:1 both praise and command. Samarrai surveys verbal forms and object/preposition patterns.
  87:1/q22/p2 The command starts with removing impossible notions before affirming attributes and teaching prophethood. — reasons: Biqai says clearing away defects precedes positive virtues, and theology proceeds through essence, attributes, self-subsistence and acts before prophethood; this counters imitation and denial and leads to obedience.
  87:1/q22/p3 It grants the Prophet permission to praise God and prepares reassurance about revelation. — reasons: Qutb calls the permission itself a favor; Ibn Ashur says the imperative foreshadows verse 6 and fits the sura's purpose of steadying the recipient of revelation.
87:1/q23 Why are the following descriptions coordinated or separated in praise? [ٱلْأَعْلَى · grammar]
  87:1/q23/p1 Distinct attributes may be independently coordinated; varied syntax expands and strengthens praise. — reasons: Burhan says conjunction signals independent adjectives, citing Zamakhshari and Abu al-Baqa; separation is stronger when meanings differ widely. It compares 68:10 and 57:3. Abu Hayyan and Samin discuss whether 87:2's 'who created' can attach across al-a'la.
87:1/q24 What theological claims are drawn from complete glorification? [* · theology] (turns on: Razi contrasts sovereign ownership with Mu'tazilite moral goodness and human welfare.)
  87:1/q24/p1 God's essence and eternal attributes are free of composition and deficiency; His acts are those of the absolute owner. — reasons: Razi says God is neither substance nor accident, His attributes are not originated or finite, and no creature may object to His decisions. Duties bring God no benefit and rest on sovereignty.
  87:1/q24/p2 All God's acts are good, and He neither does nor approves evil; duties serve people's interests. — reasons: Razi attributes these formulations to the Mu'tazilites, contrasting his ownership account.
  87:1/q24/p3 God must be affirmed above both defect and wrongdoing, including false speech, futile creation, and neglect of reckoning. — reasons: Biqai applies the sura's transcendence to resurrection, justice, non-imitability of revelation and divine patience; Jishumi says God's acts are wise and good.
87:1/q25 How is the verse understood inwardly? [* · inward] (turns on: Self-emptying through the Highest Name versus inward witnessing and remembrance.)
  87:1/q25/p1 The Highest Name encompasses God's essence and attributes; the soul's self-emptying lets divine perfections appear. — reasons: Qashani calls this the special praise of annihilation and says each thing glorifies through a particular divine name; Bursevi transmits this and adds each creature's own mode of praise.
  87:1/q25/p2 Glorification joins outward denial of rivals to inward remembrance and witnessing only God. — reasons: Tustari locates this in prayer; Qushayri describes knowing the names, swimming inwardly in exaltedness, and intimacy that resists disobedience.
87:1/q26 What earlier language or scripture does this wording evoke? [سَبِّحِ ٱسْمَ ٱلْأَعْلَى · meaning] (turns on: The proposed immediate language of influence, Hebrew or Syriac, and the biblical parallel.)
  87:1/q26/p1 Tasbih's religious praise sense may have entered Arabic from Hebrew. — reasons: Ibn Ashur proposes Hebrew borrowing and connects it to 2:30.
  87:1/q26/p2 The praise sense likely reflects Syriac shabbeh, itself translating Hebrew hillel and echoing psalmic hallelu-yah. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum says Arabic 'swim/glide' cannot explain the devotional sense, compares Syriac prayer terminology, psalmic praise of God's name, and the epithet Hebrew elyon; the closest biblical construction is YHWH elyon.
87:1/q27 What broader devotional or human purpose does the opening serve? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The spiritual effect of remembrance and the relation between voiced, enacted, and inward glorification.)
  87:1/q27/p1 Recognition and remembrance of the Most High give people a patron, exemplar, path, courage, and perseverance. — reasons: Biqai says God, Muhammad and Islam meet these needs, citing the Prophet's confession, and that mercy enables obedience. Islahi says sound knowledge of God strengthens faith and conduct; remembrance comforts the heart in 13:28 and protects against Satan.
  87:1/q27/p2 Praise of God's name is voiced publicly and privately, with fitting beliefs and acts, while inward thought glorifies God directly. — reasons: Ibn Ashur links the direct object to spoken declaration, the name in 56:74, and timely action in 33:42; naming
