34:24/q15 Which side is guided and which is astray? [لَعَلَىٰ هُدًى أَوْ فِى ضَلَٰلٍۢ مُّبِينٍۢ · interpretation] (turns on: The distinction between the verse's tactful surface alternative and the conclusion supplied by its prior proof.)
  34:24/q15/p1 Muhammad and the believers are guided; the idolaters or Meccan unbelievers are in manifest error. — reasons: Ikrima, Ziyad, Abu Ubayda, Muqatil, and many commentators state the allocation directly. Al-Wahidi says Abu Ishaq rejects this as a literal grammatical gloss but accepts it as the conclusion established by the proof. Qurtubi identifies associating partners with the provider as the error. Ibn Kathir says both camps cannot share guidance or error; al-Nazzam's paired syntax and ordered distribution also yield this result.
  34:24/q15/p2 The wording itself leaves the assignment open for hearers to resolve, though one party is guided and the other astray. — reasons: Zajjaj rejects a direct believer-guided gloss as literal Arabic; al-Mawardi reports Mujahid's refusal to name the parties directly. Al-Tabari, Qatada, al-Tha'labi, and Study Quran describe the shared two-way frame. Al-Wahidi distinguishes surface alternative from the known intended result.
34:24/q16 Why frame guidance and error as a balanced alternative? [وَإِنَّآ أَوْ إِيَّاكُمْ لَعَلَىٰ هُدًى أَوْ فِى ضَلَٰلٍۢ مُّبِينٍۢ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the balanced wording chiefly offers courteous deliberation, sharp mockery, or an instance of rhetorical amplification.)
  34:24/q16/p1 It is fair, gentle, indirect debate: a certain speaker lets opponents infer the rebuke themselves, reducing resistance and prompting reflection. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid's father relates the idiom to Muslims' weakness in Mecca. Ibn Qutayba and al-Farra compare 'one of us lies'; al-Farra adds softened curses and rebukes. Al-Tabari prefers gracious refutation. Abu al-Aswad's conditional poem voices known conviction without bluntness; Jarir, Hassan, and a Banū Asad poet offer further parallels. Al-Kashshaf, al-Razi, al-Baydawi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi, al-Alusi, Ibn Ashur, and Kuranyolu say fairness wins a hearing, avoids anger, and leads the interlocutor through accepted premises; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin call the staged argument istidraj. Al-Biqa'i cites 41:5 for their earlier refusal to consider proof; Study Quran calls it a gentle invitation. Al-Alusi rejects calling the expression dissimulation.
  34:24/q16/p2 The seemingly uncertain wording is sarcastic or mocking and makes an oblique accusation sting more. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi and others cite Abu al-Aswad's conditional poem: he knew love for the Prophet's family was right. Abu Hayyan reports that an indirect rebuke can be more cutting than a direct one.
  34:24/q16/p3 It exemplifies rhetorical hyperbole or amplified expression. — reasons: Al-Baqillani lists this verse among hyperbole examples; his surrounding discussion distinguishes pairing, verbal appropriateness, tasrif, implicit tadmin, and emphatic forms such as rahman, ghaffar, and qadīr. Al-Rummani explains intensified adjective patterns and says a disputant can speak in apparent uncertainty to strengthen a fair argument, comparing a conditional statement about a son.
34:24/q17 What do ala for guidance and fi for error picture? [لَعَلَىٰ هُدًى فِى ضَلَٰلٍۢ · rhetoric]
  34:24/q17/p1 Guidance is elevation, mastery, and clear sight, like riding a horse or standing at a beacon; error is immersion, darkness, confinement, and lost direction. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf starts the horse and darkness contrast; al-Razi adds an elevated lookout. Al-Baydawi gives minaret, horse, and pit; al-Biqa'i says the guided can avoid danger while one immersed in mistaken belief cannot see out. Abu Hayyan stresses firmness and mastery and notes the error preposition as 'an' in one report. Al-Alusi calls the imagery an implicit or derived metaphor; al-Samarrai cites 2:5, 27:79, 23:54, 9:45, 7:186, 19:75, 6:39, and 11:110 and adds that truth ascends toward God while error descends.
34:24/q18 Why is error called manifest while guidance is not? [هُدًى ضَلَٰلٍۢ مُّبِينٍۢ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether manifest distinguishes degrees of deviation or the contrast between outside observers and the deluded person.)
  34:24/q18/p1 Truth has one straight way, while error has many degrees, some more obvious than others. — reasons: Al-Razi explains the asymmetry this way.
  34:24/q18/p2 Manifest error is visible to observers even though one immersed in it cannot recognize it until stepping outside it. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i says the person then sees that his conduct lacked reason.
34:24/q19 What does mubin modify? [مُّبِينٍۢ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the adjective attaches only to its nearest noun or semantically to both alternatives.)
  34:24/q19/p1 It most naturally modifies error alone. — reasons: Al-Alusi calls this the apparent construction; Study Quran refers readers to its note on manifest error at 36:24.
  34:24/q19/p2 It can qualify both guidance and error despite the singular adjective and pronoun after aw. — reasons: Al-Alusi allows the wider scope.
34:24/q20 Why does guidance precede error? [هُدًى أَوْ فِى ضَلَٰلٍۢ · rhetoric]
  34:24/q20/p1 Guidance describes the believers named first as we, so its mention comes first. — reasons: Al-Razi ties predicate order to the earlier order of the two camps.
34:24/q21 What variant reading is reported for the alternatives? [أَوْ أَوْ · readings]
  34:24/q21/p1 Ubayy reads imma, or amma in the expanded report, for the second alternative marker. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf reports imma in place of aw; al-Alusi gives the wording 'inna aw iyyakum amma ala hudan aw fi dalalin mubin.'
34:24/q22 What did the Meccans propose before the challenge? [* · reports]
  34:24/q22/p1 They proposed comparing worldly livelihoods and charged Muhammad and his companions with error. — reasons: Muqatil reports their proposal and accusation.
34:24/q23 Who used the two-party formulation with polytheists? [وَإِنَّآ أَوْ إِيَّاكُمْ · reports]
  34:24/q23/p1 Muhammad's companions told the polytheists they were not on one course and one of the two parties was guided. — reasons: Qatada's report appears in al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir, and al-Durr.
34:24/q24 What change in judgment did Khalid and Amr report? [* · reports]
  34:24/q24/p1 They once marvelled that anyone entered Islam and later marvelled that anyone hesitated. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i reports this from Khalid ibn al-Walid and Amr ibn al-As.
34:24/q25 What does lawful sustenance do to spiritual life? [يَرْزُقُكُم · inward]
  34:24/q25/p1 Lawful food illuminates the heart and yields wisdom and spiritual growth, while unlawful food breeds heedlessness and obstructs accepted worship. — reasons: Bursevi cites hadith making lawful livelihood an obligation after basic duties, illumination and flowing wisdom after forty days of lawful eating, and an angelic call from Jerusalem saying prayers of one eating unlawful food are not accepted. Rumi's verses contrast light, knowledge, love, service, and resolve with envy and ignorance. Bursevi adds gnosis, unveilings, and spiritual stations.
34:24/q26 How does this verse connect to nearby challenges and parallel verses? [* · links] (turns on: Which surrounding argument or parallel passage is foregrounded.)
  34:24/q26/p1 It extends the surrounding case against powerless idols from harm and intercession to provision, while echoing questions in Yunus and al-Rad. — reasons: Al-Razi connects 34:22 and 34:24 as sole removal of harm and sole gift of benefit, citing 6:17 and 10:107. Al-Kashshaf cites the provider question and truth-versus-error at 10:31-32 and powerless patrons at 13:16. Ibn Ashur says the argument moves from idols' inability to help in this life and next to exclusive worship, and notes the opening has a precedent in al-Rad. Asad directs readers to his note on 10:31; Study Quran cites 35:3, 10:31, and 27:64.
  34:24/q26/p2 The challenge follows two verses on Iblis and belongs to a sequence of questions addressed to disbelievers. — reasons: Study Quran lists the sequence from 34:20-30, including this verse.
34:24/q27 Where was Surat Saba revealed? [* · sciences]
  34:24/q27/p1 It is Makkan. — reasons: Study Quran gives this in its sura introduction.
34:24/q28 Can Quranic wording bear a further possible sense beyond its primary construction? [* · interpretation]
  34:24/q28/p1 Yes; prophetic explanation can awaken a possible sense within the wording that accords with its aims. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says prophetic prostrations at prostration passages may exemplify this; if revealed, they more strongly establish divine intent for such possible senses.
34:24/q29 How does Abu al-Aswad's conditional poem parallel this verse? [* · rhetoric]
  34:24/q29/p1 The poet phrases his conviction about loving the Prophet's family or Ali as a condition, though he knows that love is guidance. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi says the poet was certain; al-Wahidi reports his verse on Ali, al-Tabrisi describes love of the Prophet's family as known guidance, and al-Alusi identifies Qushayr and Ali. The example supports the verse's apparent uncertainty without actual doubt.
