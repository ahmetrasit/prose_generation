[RAZI:1:7/r22] RAZI, d. 606 · أبو حنيفة | reports | Abū Ḥanīfa and his two companions treat the condition as incomplete until the whole sentence is finished, so one divorce takes effect when the sentence is complete.

[RAZI:1:7/r24] RAZI, d. 606 · author | reports | Scholars disagree whether kalima and kalām include meaningless speech: some say yes because it can be called unused or unintelligible speech, while others require meaningful expression.
[RAZI:1:7/r25] RAZI, d. 606 · author | reports | Scholars also dispute whether naturally meaningful sounds, such as pain cries, coughs, or bird calls, count as words; the issue affects whether hearing one triggers an oath.
[RAZI:1:7/r26] RAZI, d. 606 · ابن جني | reports | Ibn Jinnī says qawl literally applies to a sentence or a single word, kalām only to a complete sentence, and kalima only to an isolated word; a poem treating qāf as a qawl supports the first distinction.
[RAZI:1:7/r27] RAZI, d. 606 · ابن جني | reports | Ibn Jinnī says qawl can metaphorically mean a belief or opinion, as in attributing a doctrine to Abū Ḥanīfa or Mālik, because belief is apprehended through its expression.
[RAZI:1:7/r28] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Qāla can be used for nonverbal events, as when birds, eyes, or a full basin are said to speak; Razi also cites two Qurʾānic examples of such speech. | mentions 16:40, 41:11

[RAZI:1:7/r4] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | The inquiry can descend from words into speech and phonetics, respiratory and vocal anatomy, sensory qualities, philosophical categories, substances, accidents, existence, and knowledge.

[RAZI:1:7/r41] RAZI, d. 606 · المعتزلة | reports | The Muʿtazila require an informative word to contain at least two letters; they explain one-letter particles as shortened forms, but Razi notes that many useful particles remain counterexamples.
[RAZI:1:7/r42] RAZI, d. 606 · author | prefers | Razi's preferred definition is anything spoken that conveys something by convention; it includes simple and compound forms while excluding writing and gesture.
[RAZI:1:7/r43] RAZI, d. 606 · عباد | reports | ʿAbbād holds that words naturally signify their meanings; Razi rejects this because natural properties do not vary across places and times, and answers that conventional assignments need not be unmotivated.

[RAZI:1:7/r49] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | A simple word cannot explain its own meaning from scratch without circularity, since recognizing its convention requires prior awareness of the word and meaning; learned association resolves the problem.
[RAZI:1:7/r5] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | At the furthest epistemological stage, he asks how the unknown can be judged contrary to the known if its nature is unknown, since that judgment would make it known.
[RAZI:1:7/r50] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | The circularity problem does not apply to compounds: convention supplies the simple meanings, and reason combines them to understand the compound.
[RAZI:1:7/r51] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Words signify mental conceptions rather than external objects: changing one's perception of a distant object changes the name used, and contradictory propositions do not make the world both old and originated.

[RAZI:1:7/r53] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Concepts most necessary to communicate, such as commands, prohibitions, generality, and specification, are likeliest to receive words because need supplies strong motivation.
[RAZI:1:7/r54] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | A familiar word cannot name a hidden concept unknown to the public: motion names observable transition, not a subtle cause; likewise knowledge and power name the evident state, not its hidden ground.
