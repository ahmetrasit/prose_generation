20:69/q15 How is the hamza of إِنَّمَا read? [إِنَّمَا · readings] (turns on: The opened hamza makes the clause causal.)
  20:69/q15/p1 Opened hamza yields a causal clause: because their work is a trick. — reasons: Abu Ishaq allows it and Qurtubi reports it; Zajjaj says he does not know it as a reading here.
  20:69/q15/p2 Kasra introduces the explanation of their work. — reasons: Zajjaj says the known reading is stronger in sense.
20:69/q16 What does joined spelling of إِنَّمَا suggest? [إِنَّمَا · rhetoric]
  20:69/q16/p1 It may suit the magicians' completed deed. — reasons: Samarrai follows the mushaf and suggests a rhetorical pattern, comparing 8:41. Ibn Ashur says written joining does not reflect the relative distinction early scribes observed, comparing 2:173.
20:69/q17 How is كَيْدُ سَٰحِرٍ read and understood? [كَيْدُ سَٰحِرٍ · readings] (turns on: The variants differ in agent versus craft and nominative predicate versus accusative object.)
  20:69/q17/p1 Kaydu siḥr: nominative 'a plot of magic'. — reasons: Hamza, al-Kisai and Khalaf are named, with Kufans except Asim. It can mean possessor(s) of magic, magicians so steeped in it they are magic itself, or the kind of trick. Nahhas and Ibn al-Anbari compare cloth of silk; Alusi gives lām and min analyses of the explanatory genitive; Qiraat-ER makes the craft the scheming agent.
  20:69/q17/p2 Kaydu sāḥir: nominative 'a magician's plot'. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid names Ibn Kathir, Nafi, Abu Amr, Asim and Ibn Amir; others call it majority. Al-Farisi prefers attribution to the agent, supported by the following الساحر and al-A'mash; Abu Ubayda, Baghawi and Wahidi agree. Abu al-Baqa calls the annexation one of action to agent.
  20:69/q17/p3 Kayda siḥr or kayda sāḥir: accusative object of ṣanaʿū. — reasons: Tabari rejects an uninflected siḥr form for lack of authoritative support; Ibn Atiyya and Mujahid, Humayd and Zayd ibn Ali transmit accusative; Ibn al-Jawzi names Ibn Masud and Abu Imran. Zajjaj supplies the syntax.
20:69/q18 What does كَيْد mean here? [كَيْدُ · meaning]
  20:69/q18/p1 A magician's device, plot, or deceptive stratagem. — reasons: Muqatil contrasts it with Moses' act; Wahidi calls it an illusion without substance; Tabrisi gives device or stratagem; Qashani says deception and embellishment. Bursevi notes a scheme can be praiseworthy or blameworthy, though commonly blameworthy.
  20:69/q18/p2 A plot specifically of sorcery rather than another type. — reasons: Razi and Abu Hayyan say the modifier specifies its kind; Abu al-Baqa describes genus annexed to type.
20:69/q19 Why use singular indefinite سَٰحِرٍ for the group? [سَٰحِرٍ · rhetoric] (turns on: Generic class, belittlement, and avoidance of a specific person are proposed.)
  20:69/q19/p1 It denotes the class or one kind of magic, without stressing numbers. — reasons: Razi, Zamakhshari, Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan and Samin give generic force. Abu Hayyan says the group did one kind of magic. Kashshaf and Baydawi cite Umar and al-Ajjaj's line on worldly striving for indefinite annexation; Abu Hayyan calls the poetry necessity and questions the reported saying.
  20:69/q19/p2 It belittles the entire company's work. — reasons: Biqai says one or many amount to one insubstantial trick; Bursevi says the indefinite annexation diminishes it. Alusi objects that this implies ranks of magic and conflicts with 'great magic' at 7:116, despite apparent greatness and inner triviality being compatible.
  20:69/q19/p3 It avoids identifying a particular known sorcerer. — reasons: Alusi reports this proposal and says the context at 20:66 already identifies the magic's kind.
20:69/q20 Why is the later ٱلسَّاحِرُ definite? [ٱلسَّاحِرُ · rhetoric]
  20:69/q20/p1 It denotes the familiar generic class and resumes the earlier indefinite. — reasons: Razi says first mention denotes one kind and second extends futility to all kinds; Abu Hayyan compares the messenger sent to Pharaoh and Pharaoh's disobedience to the messenger in 43:46; Ibn Ashur calls it a familiar class.
20:69/q21 What does يُفْلِحُ mean? [يُفْلِحُ · meaning]
  20:69/q21/p1 To succeed, attain one's aim, or win. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya also gives remaining or surviving. Bursevi distinguishes worldly survival, wealth, power and honor from permanent afterlife life, wealth, honor and knowledge; his student-teacher story illustrates temporary prosperity amid ultimate loss.
  20:69/q21/p2 To remain or survive. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya offers this lexical nuance alongside attaining one's aim.
20:69/q22 What does 'the magician does not prosper' assert? [وَلَا يُفْلِحُ ٱلسَّاحِرُ · interpretation] (turns on: The interpretations differ between general failure and punitive failure; sources also discuss how far the denial extends.)
  20:69/q22/p1 No magician attains his aim through magic wherever he practices it. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and many commentators give the universal gloss. Tabrisi, Biqai, Tabatabai and Islahi ground it in unreality; Ibn Ashur limits denial to sorcery, not other trades, and says reflection exposes illusion. Razi raises whether the verse denies magic's good and evil effects; Asad cites Razi as including both and condemns every practice.
  20:69/q22/p2 It can mean a magician is killed wherever found. — reasons: Farra, Tabari, Zajjaj, Wahidi, Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan give this as the outcome of failure; jurists are said to share it. Jundub's Prophetic report is cited without grading by Wahidi and later sources; Ibn Kathir notes Companion and Prophetic transmissions.
20:69/q23 What does حَيْثُ أَتَىٰ mean? [حَيْثُ أَتَىٰ · meaning] (turns on: The place may be one where magic is practiced or where the sorcerer is found.)
  20:69/q23/p1 Wherever the magician is, goes, comes, or works magic. — reasons: Yahya preserves both 'is' and 'comes'; Baghawi says wherever he attempts his trick; Kashshaf compares coming, going and being sent. Qurtubi includes wherever he comes from on earth; Ibn Ashur universalizes every place attended with magic.
  20:69/q23/p2 Wherever he is found, he is unsafe and may be killed. — reasons: Jundub's reported tradition quotes the verse after an order to kill a caught sorcerer; Farra and jurists also take this sense.
20:69/q24 Is there a variant of حَيْثُ أَتَىٰ? [حَيْثُ أَتَىٰ · readings]
  20:69/q24/p1 أين أتى is transmitted instead. — reasons: Akhfash reports Ibn Masud and compares جئتك من أين لا تعلم and من حيث لا تعلم; Ibn Atiyya says the meanings are close. Tabari reports grammarians' discussion, including queries for water and pasture; Abu Hayyan and Alusi also list the variant.
20:69/q25 Why use أَتَىٰ? [أَتَىٰ · rhetoric]
  20:69/q25/p1 It evokes magicians brought from across Egypt and preserves verse endings. — reasons: Ibn Ashur compares kāna and ḥalla.
20:69/q26 How does the command connect to Moses' fear and the next verse? [* · links]
  20:69/q26/p1 It reassures Moses, explains the defeat of illusion, and leads to prostration. — reasons: Tabari supplies the omitted act of casting and swallowing. Sa'id, Suddi and Wahb recount prostration; Razi cites 20:70 to confirm the serpent, while Nasafi and Wahidi link swallowing to belief. Alusi says it completes the earlier prohibition of fear; a proposed inference about fear of public disbelief remains under consideration.
  20:69/q26/p2 It recalls the earlier question about Moses' staff. — reasons: Biqai and Alusi cite 20:17; Alusi says 7:117 gives the order by meaning. Ibn Ashur links the indirect wording to God's earlier question at 20:17 and to reassurance that the staff would again become miraculous.
20:69/q27 What legal or reported consequence is attached to the sorcerer? [وَلَا يُفْلِحُ ٱلسَّاحِرُ · law] (turns on: The issue is whether the failure formula itself supplies a killing rule.)
  20:69/q27/p1 A sorcerer caught or found should be killed. — reasons: Farra gives the exegesis, Zajjaj and Abu Ishaq attribute it to jurists, and Ibn Atiyya links punishment with failure. Jundub reports a Prophetic order followed by this verse in Wahidi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Itqan and Alusi; Wahidi and Alusi give no grading, while Ibn Kathir notes al-Tirmidhi's Companion and Prophetic transmissions.
  20:69/q27/p2 The verse states failure; the separate ruling is addressed elsewhere. — reasons: Qurtubi sends readers to al-Baqara for the legal ruling and meaning of magic; Tabari's primary gloss is universal failure before reporting the killing interpretation.
20:69/q28 What distinguishes Moses' sign from the magicians' work? [* · theology] (turns on: Sources appeal to visible miraculous reality or to prophets' wider character and impact.)
  20:69/q28/p1 The divine miracle is real and defeats illusory human magic. — reasons: Muqatil says Moses' act was not magic; Wahb's magicians know it exceeds their art; Qushayri says swallowing exposes their falsehood. Razi lists growth, organs, swallowing, and return as beyond craft; Ibn Kathir stresses public daylight proof. Tabatabai contrasts God's power with fabrication; Kuran Yolu says God nullified their skills. Razi argues that humans cannot instantly create a huge animal and shrink it; the public transformation thus shows a world-governing Creator.
