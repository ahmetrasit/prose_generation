LMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Ma aghna is either a rhetorical negative question or a direct negation, explaining his ruin: nothing in his wealth saved him or brought good.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5/r10] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Dahhâk | reports | Al-Dahhak reads kasb as Abu Lahab’s evil deed: plotting hostility against the Messenger.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5/r11] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Katâde | reports | Qatada reads kasb as deeds he mistakenly thought good, like the deeds made scattered dust in Furqan 25:23.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5/r12] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | reports | A report says Abu Lahab claimed he could ransom himself from his nephew’s message with his wealth and children.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5/r13] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | These interpretations do not conflict or restrict the meaning: kasb embraces all of them, and neither wealth nor any acquisition saved him.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Ma kasab can be a relative noun or verbal noun, and may likewise be read as a rhetorical question or negation.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | reports | One possible rendering asks what his wealth and earnings gained him: neither rescued him from disaster nor earned him any good.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | prefers | The reading that neither his property nor his earnings helped him better explains the ruin of both hands.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Wealth and earnings may mean capital and its profit, inherited property and self-earned income, or all old and new possessions and gains.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | More broadly, kasb includes every benefit or effort counted to his account, including children, social standing, customary good works, and his plots against the Prophet.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5/r7] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · İbn Abbas | reports | Ibn Abbas interprets ma kasab as Abu Lahab’s children.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | reports | A reported anecdote says Abu Lahab called his children an evil acquisition after one pushed him while he tried to stop their fight.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | reports | A Prophetic hadith says a man’s purest food comes from his own earnings and that his child is part of his earnings.
[QUTB-ZILAL:111:2/r1] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | Abu Lahab’s wealth and earnings could not benefit him, fend off ruin, or prevent his destruction.
[IBNASHUR:111:2/r1] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The verse begins a new statement shifting from insult and rebuke to announcing Abū Lahab’s hopeless ruin: his property and earnings will save him from nothing in the afterlife.
[IBNASHUR:111:2/r2] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The past “did not avail” makes his failure to be saved certain.
[IBNASHUR:111:2/r3] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | Mā is negative, though it may also be read as an interrogative expressing rebuke and denial.
[IBNASHUR:111:2/r4] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Māl means owned wealth, commonly camels among Arabs; ʿUmar’s saying and a line by Zuhayr attest that usage, while Medinans, Khaybar residents, and Bahrainis often use it for date palms.
[IBNASHUR:111:2/r5] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The phrase “what he earned” is a relative clause with an optionally omitted object pronoun, meaning what he amassed.
[IBNASHUR:111:2/r6] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | “What he earned” can mean assets besides livestock—currency, weapons, estates, goods, and food.
[IBNASHUR:111:2/r7] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Alternatively, “his wealth” includes everything, and “what he earned” specifies his own acquired wealth after inherited property: what he inherited from ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib versus what he himself acquired.
[IBNASHUR:111:2/r8] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · ابن مسعود | reports | Ibn Masʿūd reports Abū Lahab claimed he would ransom himself with his wealth and children if his nephew’s message proved true; the verse was revealed in response.
[IBNASHUR:111:2/r9] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · ابن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbās interprets “what he earned” as his children, since a child is his father’s earning.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p604/r1] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | ما كسب is a relative clause with an omitted object pronoun, meaning what Abu Lahab earned or amassed.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p604/r2] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | reports | One reading takes his wealth as movable assets such as money, weapons, estates, goods, and food, while what he earned is his personal acquisitions.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p604/r3] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | reports | Alternatively, his wealth includes everything; ما كسب then singles out what he personally gained in contrast to inherited property.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p604/r4] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · ابن مسعود | reports | He reports Abu Lahab saying he would ransom himself with wealth and children if his nephew's claim proved true; the verse answered that boast.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p604/r5] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · ابن عباس | reports | He identifies what Abu Lahab earned as his children, since a child is counted among a father's earnings.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p604/r6] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | The statement means nothing can spare him from Hell; revealed during Abu Lahab's lifetime, it foretold that he would die an unbeliever and thus testified to prophecy.
[TABATABAI:111:1-4/r8] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The first ma is negative; the second is relative, meaning his deeds or their effects, or a verbal noun meaning what he earned. Neither wealth nor work saved him from ruin.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1-2:kommentar/r1] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The sura develops from a curse against an enemy called ‘Father of Flame’; all verses except verse 5 refer back to him pronominally.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1-2:kommentar/r2] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The first, still unspecified threat says the accursed man’s accumulated wealth is useless; verse 3 clarifies the afterlife setting.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1-2:kommentar/r3] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The sura joins early Meccan prophetic criticism of wealth, a topos also important in the Hebrew Bible.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:2:anmerkung/r1] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The verse joins early Meccan warnings that earthly wealth is useless in the afterlife, especially 69:28, 92:11, and 104:1–3. | mentions 69:28, 92:11, 104:1, 104:2, 104:3
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:2:anmerkung/r10] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | rejects | The author warns against over-specifying the phrase through anecdotes rooted in early Islamic reception, as with interpretations of 93:3, 93:6–8, and 108:3. | mentions 93:3, 93:6, 93:7, 93:8, 108:3
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:2:anmerkung/r2] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The critique also belongs to broader Qur’anic criticism of possessions and a biblical topos, echoed in Psalm 39, 1 Timothy 6, and Luke 18/Mark 10. | mentions 100:8, 102:1, 102:2, 107:1
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:2:anmerkung/r3] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | reports | The particle mā can be read either as a question, asking what wealth helped him, or as a negation, saying it helped him nothing. | mentions 69:28, 92:11
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:2:anmerkung/r4] CORPUSCORANICUM · Bergsträßer | prefers | Bergsträßer favors the negative reading because the numerous parallel formulations make negation more likely here too. | mentions 69:28, 92:11
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:2:anmerkung/r5] CORPUSCORANICUM · Paret | prefers | Paret takes the perfect as resultative, giving the statement a present reference; the author follows this reading.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:2:anmerkung/r6] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The similar afterlife statement at 92:11 uses an imperfect instead of a perfect without an evident difference in meaning. | mentions 92:11
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:2:anmerkung/r7] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | reports | It is prima facie possible to treat mā kasab as synonymous with māluhū, meaning what Abū Lahab acquired, as 7:48 uses gathering in a similar context. | mentions 7:48
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:2:anmerkung/r8] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | rejects | The literal acquisition reading conflicts with the Qur’an’s dominant figurative use of kasaba as committing deeds, already attested in pre-Islamic poetry. | mentions 74:38
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:2:anmerkung/r9] CORPUSCORANICUM · Rubin | reports | Rubin identifies Abū Lahab’s deeds with his service to al-ʿUzzā and his protection of Muhammad, which he later renounced.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:3-5:kommentar/r7] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | rejects | The author rejects Rubin’s cult reading: the sura never accuses them of idolatry, verse 2 instead ties wrongdoing to wealth, and pagan ritual condemnation is not prominent in other early Meccan texts.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p511/r2] ISLAHI-TADABBUR · author | holds | The warning about wealth's false promise of immortality portrays Abū Lahab and his associates, who learned that wealth cannot protect them from God. | mentions 104:2, 104:3
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p511/r3] ISLAHI-TADABBUR · Some exegetes | reports | Some interpreters take what Abū Lahab earned to mean his sons; Islahi accepts that they ultimately failed to help him but calls this reading far-fetched.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p511/r4] ISLAHI-TADABBUR · Some others | reports | Other interpreters take the phrase to mean his illicit earnings; Islahi rejects this as linguistically unsuitable and redundant with the preceding clause.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p511/r5] ISLAHI-TADABBUR · author | prefers | The phrase instead refers to deeds he considered virtuous, but his evil disposition and polytheism deprived them of value before God.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p512/r1] ISLAHI-TADABBUR · author | holds | As treasurer of the Baytullāh, Abū Lahab performed welfare works for poor people and pilgrims as pretense to cover his misdeeds; these works had no divine value.
