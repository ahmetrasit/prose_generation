23:14/q27 When does an embryo count as an infant for the withdrawal or infanticide discussion? [ثُمَّ خَلَقْنَا ٱلنُّطْفَةَ عَلَقَةًۭ ثُمَّ أَنشَأْنَٰهُ خَلْقًا ءَاخَرَ · law] (turns on: The sources distinguish the completion of developmental stages from an earlier legally significant formed body.)
  23:14/q27/p1 Withdrawal is not minor infanticide before the embryo completes the seven stages. — reasons: ʿAlī's answer is reported by Ibn al-Jawzī and Ibn ʿĀshūr, with ʿUmar approving in the latter; Ibn ʿAbbās is also reported to reject calling withdrawal infanticide before those stages.
  23:14/q27/p2 A formed embryo has legal effect for the carrying slave woman. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī says that once bones and flesh are formed it is a human child and its mother becomes an umm walad; al-Qurṭubī sends fuller legal discussion of the stages to his commentary on 22:5.
23:14/q28 What does the sequence show about God's power and resurrection? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the stages are used chiefly as proof of resurrection, divine lordship and design, or human honor and duty.)
  23:14/q28/p1 Creation from a tiny beginning through elaborate stages proves divine power, wisdom, mercy and ability to recreate the dead. — reasons: Al-Māwardī says resurrection is easier than first making a human from nothing; Jishumī and al-Burhān frame the stages as an argument for the afterlife (75:40). Islahi relates 22:5 and 23:15–16, arguing that this ordered growth cannot be accidental matter and the One who made it can remake it. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says the prior creation account makes resurrection plain and needs less emphasis; for God neither act is harder.
  23:14/q28/p2 Gradual transformation reveals purposeful precision and diverse organs from homogeneous matter. — reasons: Bursevī answers the objection that instantaneous creation might show more power: staged formation better displays each organ's distinct shape and role. Al-Qushayrī lists varied structures from one drop; Kur'an Yolu recalls the soil-derived biological supply chain; Islahi says science uncovers hinted details, while al-Sāmarrāʾī says anatomy confirms the stages and some scientists converted.
  23:14/q28/p3 The creation is a sign of human honor and responsibility. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī links 95:4 and sees divine self-praise after human creation as unique; Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya and the Mathnawī cite 17:70 and 95:4. Elmalılı warns humans to seek felicity and the qualities their creation makes possible; Islahi recalls angels bowing to Adam and humans as vicegerents.
  23:14/q28/p4 The human account also teaches divine lordship, worship and gratitude. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī links the believers' virtues to God's sole lordship; Kur'an Yolu says these gifts call for gratitude; Islahi says reflection reveals a beneficent, peerless Creator rather than blind matter or a mere first cause.
23:14/q29 How does this passage connect to nearby verses and Sūrat al-Ḥajj? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the link emphasized is neighboring afterlife promises, the next death and resurrection verses, or parallel Qurʾānic creation passages.)
  23:14/q29/p1 It develops the argument from creation to death and resurrection, parallel to 22:5. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn ʿĀshūr, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī and Study Quran point readers to 22:5–6 for the stages; Islahi says the present passage elaborates that resurrection proof. Al-Rāzī cites 23:15 as support for life-stage interpretation, and al-Sāmarrāʾī contrasts emphasis on death and resurrection in 23:15–16.
  23:14/q29/p2 The account belongs to broader Qurʾānic descriptions of human development and other creatures. — reasons: Al-Khaṭṭābī recalls 86:6; Ibn ʿAṭiyya connects seven human stages with Ibn ʿAbbās's sevens and produce in 80:27. Al-Dāmghānī relates 2:259 and other creation verses; the Study Quran introduction recalls 22:5. Uzayr's reported discourse broadens to water, heavens, lands, animals and plants; Wahb speaks of Adam's clay, senses, spirit and four humors.
  23:14/q29/p3 The passage follows the believers' promised blessings by recalling human origins and God's power over their changes. — reasons: Jishumī gives three links: shift from promised afterlife blessings to original human blessings; creation in stages as a resurrection warrant; and worldly changing states set against eternal Firdaws. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī moves from believers' virtues to divine lordship; Islahi says deniers mock the promised afterlife.
  23:14/q29/p4 The perfected creation makes the following announcement of death surprising and therefore more emphatic. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī and Rūḥ al-Maʿānī say a listener might expect such a wondrous human to endure; this explains extra emphasis on mortality, whereas recreation is rationally easier after original creation.
  23:14/q29/p5 The wider sūra repeatedly shows wealthy and powerful people opposing prophets. — reasons: Study Quran notes a recurring pattern of opposition to prophetic warnings throughout the sūra and compares 7:59–62 and 7:136.
23:14/q30 Why does the account use past-tense verbs for an ongoing process? [خَلَقْنَا أَنشَأْنَٰهُ · rhetoric]
  23:14/q30/p1 The past tense can assert the certainty of the account while each human's creation continues. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu therefore translates the verbs in the present and notes that Qurʾānic past forms can also emphasize certain truth.
23:14/q31 How can the written skeleton support the bone readings? [عِظَٰمًۭا ٱلْعِظَٰمَ · readings]
  23:14/q31/p1 The same rasm accommodates singular and plural forms with or without an alif. — reasons: Al-Itqān includes this embryology phrase in its list of regular reading variants represented by one spelling, with parallels such as mālik/malik, plural forms with tāʾ, and other omitted or alternative letters. Its comparison with fākihūn illustrates absent alif despite a legitimate reading.
23:14/q32 What did Ibn ʿAbbās's argument from seven stages establish? [* · reports]
  23:14/q32/p1 He connected the seven human stages with other sevens when identifying the Night of Decree as the twenty-seventh night. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports ʿUmar praising Ibn ʿAbbās's insight; he says the seven human stages refer to this passage and the seven provisions to produce in 80:27.
23:14/q33 What do related reports say about creation of Adam and the wider world? [* · reports]
  23:14/q33/p1 God diversifies bodies, senses and provision through His creative command. — reasons: Wahb's account of Adam adds clay, water, senses and a spirit for moral discernment and planning; the four-humor account links bodily balance to health. ʿUzayr describes heavens and earth separated from water, night and day, diverse animals from water (24:45), varied plants from one earth and water, and limbs and nourishment in the womb.
23:14/q34 What inward meaning is drawn from the creation and final praise? [* · inward] (turns on: The inward readings range from divine names manifested in creation to personal spiritual realization.)
  23:14/q34/p1 The human is prepared for gnosis, love, servanthood and spiritual effacement. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī speaks of rare mature states and individual gifts of inspiration; al-Qāshānī interprets breathed divine spirit and image; Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya names the human a repository of gnosis and care. Al-Rāzī's gnostics contrast ʿUmar's good fortune and Ibn Abī Sarḥ's misery under 2:26.
  23:14/q34/p2 Creation manifests God's eternal name Creator while human spiritual capacity remains dependent on divine disclosure. — reasons: Al-Sulamī reports al-Ḥusayn's four quarters: divinity, traces of lordship, lights of governance and perception, and movement and rest; God formed Adam by power, descendants in stages, and reveals only what creatures can bear.
  23:14/q34/p3 The created human body and world invite contemplation of God's hidden essence and beautiful art. — reasons: Elmalılı quotes a poem on dust turned to beauty and the world as witness to divine existence.
23:14/q35 What linguistic or rhetorical parallels are cited around the verse? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: The notes use several literary, syntactic and lexical parallels to interpret the verse's conclusion and life theme.)
  23:14/q35/p1 The verse's ending is cued by its beginning, an example of tawshīḥ or al-muṭmiʿ. — reasons: Al-Burhān explains that the opening anticipates the end, comparing it with a sash; al-Itqān calls the conclusion apt and al-Ālūsī compares verses whose openings reveal their rhymes.

Al-Burhān reports a living person emerging from dead seed, with an alternative that animals are dead before given life.
