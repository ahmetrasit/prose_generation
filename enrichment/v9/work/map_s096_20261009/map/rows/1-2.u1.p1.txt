DURR, d. 911 (ابن عباس): Ibn Abbas explains al-hamd as gratitude, humble submission to God, and acknowledgment of His blessings, guidance, creation, and other favors. DURR, d. 911 (أبو عبد الرحمن الجبائي): Abu ʿAbd al-Rahman al-Jubbaʾi says prayer, fasting, and every good deed done for God count as gratitude, with al-hamd the best gratitude. ALUSI, d. 1270 (بعضهم): Other definitions reserve shukr for apparent blessings or, alternatively, for inward blessings. ALUSI, d. 1270 (صاحب التجريد): The author of al-Tajrīd adds a fourth form of gratitude: thanking God by God, since no one but God can give Him His due thanks. ALUSI, d. 1270 (author): People generally recognize three modes of shukr; it overlaps with ḥamd in some respects, but ḥamd is its strongest branch because it openly discloses the blessing. ALUSI, d. 1270 (ابن عمر): A report from Ibn ʿUmar calls ḥamd the head of gratitude and says a servant has not thanked God unless he praises Him; al-Ālūsī notes its chain is interrupted. ALUSI, d. 1270 (أنس): Al-Ālūsī cites a supporting report from Anas in which God tells Abraham that praise is the key to gratitude and gratitude ascends to the Lord of the Throne. ALUSI, d. 1270 (author): Calling ḥamd the head of gratitude suits its status as the most visible and principal form; this also explains why the Qurʾān opens with praise and begins the precious sūra with what is most precious. ALUSI, d. 1270 (author): Hamd is more elevated as worship than shukr because it need not be prompted by a benefit received, unlike gratitude for a gift. ALUSI, d. 1270 (author): An additional rationale is that shukr responds to finite gifts, while ḥamd can praise even for the unbounded benefit of averting harm, which is more urgent than gaining benefit. ALUSI, d. 1270 (author): In the common account, ḥamd has a limited means but a general object, whereas shukr has a general means but is directed specifically toward blessings. ALUSI, d. 1270 (داود): In a report, David asks how to thank God when gratitude itself is among His gifts; God answers that recognizing inability to thank Him is itself gratitude. ALUSI-FULL, d. 1270 (author): Hamd and gratitude overlap in part, but hamd is stronger because it openly discloses blessing while ingratitude conceals it; verbal praise is clearer than hidden belief or deeds that may be habitual. ALUSI-FULL, d. 1270 (ابن عمر): Ibn ʿUmar transmits the saying that praise is gratitude’s head and that no servant thanks God without praising Him; al-Ālūsī notes its interrupted chain but says a supporting report strengthens it. ALUSI-FULL, d. 1270 (author): Other rationales say praise is a fitting beginning for the Lord and that praise exceeds thanks because it need not arise from a benefit received by the speaker. ALUSI-FULL, d. 1270 (author): Praise can concern withholding as well as giving and is thus unbounded, while gratitude answers a finite gift; averting harm is more urgent than gaining benefit, favoring praise first. ALUSI-FULL, d. 1270 (author): Praise has a narrower occasion but a broader object than gratitude; its inclusion may signal a sacred allusion, and tasbīḥ is omitted because praise already includes both negative and positive perfections. ALUSI-FULL, d. 1270 (الإمام): An argument for rational gratitude before revelation says the verse establishes God's praiseworthiness through creation, mercy, and future judgment; al-Alusi replies that this proof itself is scriptural, not rational. ALUSI-FULL, d. 1270 (author): Al-Alusi says deriving the duty of gratitude from God's own revealed declaration only proves it through revelation, so it cannot establish a pre-revelation rational duty. ELMALILI, d. 1361 (Resûl-i Ekrem): The Prophet calls hamd the head of gratitude and says that whoever fails to praise God has not thanked Him. ELMALILI, d. 1361 (author): Hamd springs from justified hope or experienced enjoyment and leans toward the future, whereas shukr celebrates an accomplished gift and its received fulfillment. IBNASHUR, d. 1393 (author): Sibawayhi distinguishes supplicatory accusative sources from declarative ones such as hamdan and shukran, whose omitted verbs mean 'I praise' or 'I thank'. IBNASHUR, d. 1393 (author): Dhu al-Rumma's phrase 'we brought into being thanks for its Creator' confirms that Arabs described praise or thanks as something they create, treating it as performative expression. IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 (author): The sura also teaches gratitude by crowning supplication with praise of the giver of intellect and guidance; opening important speech with praise is therefore Qur'anic practice. IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 (سيبويه): Sibawayh explains conventional verbal nouns such as “beware” or “thanks” as accusatives governed by verbs suppressed from use, and distinguishes supplicatory formulas from other expressions. IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 (سيبويه): For hamdan or shukran, an understood verb such as “I praise” is omitted because the source itself stands in its place, as in the established supplicatory formulas. IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 (ذي الرمة): Dhu al-Rumma's line about a flame says that it produced gratitude to its Creator, showing Arabs could describe praise or thanks as something brought into being. SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT (author): Hamd is praise, not necessarily for a blessing and possibly for deeds; shukr responds only to blessings and is enacted through gratitude.
