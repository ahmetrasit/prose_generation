80:2/q11 Which leaders were present in the encounter? [جَآءَهُ · reports] (turns on: The different names and numbers supplied by occasion reports. Ibn al-Arabi objects to identifications involving al-Walid, Umayya or al-Abbas on a Medina chronology, whereas Alusi says Ibn Umm Maktum was Meccan and could meet them.)
  80:2/q11/p1 Quraysh leaders or a wealthy notable, without names. — reasons: Many accounts leave the leaders unnamed. Al-Dahhak speaks of one wealthy polytheist; Aisha's report through Ibn Kathir adds that the notables saw no fault in what the Prophet said. Bursevi says leaders' conversion might draw the public.
  80:2/q11/p2 Utba, Abu Jahl and al-Abbas. — reasons: Ibn Abbas's reports name this trio; Ibn Kathir questions the al-Awfi transmission of his report.
  80:2/q11/p3 Al-Abbas and Umayya ibn Khalaf. — reasons: Muqatil and al-Qushayri name these two.
  80:2/q11/p4 Umayya ibn Khalaf. — reasons: Qatada and Abu Malik name Umayya alone; Mujahid pairs him with Utba as the self-sufficient men.
  80:2/q11/p5 Ubayy ibn Khalaf. — reasons: Qatada's separate version and Anas's report name Ubayy.
  80:2/q11/p6 Utba and Shayba. — reasons: Aisha's report through Masruq names them; al-Hakim authenticated it.
  80:2/q11/p7 Utba, Abu Jahl, al-Abbas, Ubayy and Umayya. — reasons: Al-Baghawi and al-Majma as cited by al-Tabataba'i give this larger list.
  80:2/q11/p8 Utba and Umayya. — reasons: Mujahid identifies these as the self-sufficient men.
80:2/q12 Could al-Walid, Umayya or al-Abbas have met Ibn Umm Maktum at this event? [جَآءَهُ · reports] (turns on: Whether the encounter is located in Mecca or Medina.)
  80:2/q12/p1 No: a Medina location makes those Meccan figures impossible participants. — reasons: Ibn al-Arabi argues Ibn Umm Maktum was in Medina while al-Walid and Umayya were in Mecca, died unbelievers, and Umayya never came to Medina.
  80:2/q12/p2 Yes: Ibn Umm Maktum was Meccan and met the named Quraysh leaders. — reasons: Alusi directly corrects al-Qurtubi's chronology.
80:2/q13 Why did the Prophet turn away in the usual account? [جَآءَهُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the immediate concern was a lost opening for conversion, leaders' view of poor followers, or harm they might inflict on the visitor.)
  80:2/q13/p1 He was intent on converting Quraysh leaders, hoping their people would follow, and disliked losing the moment to an interruption. — reasons: Al-Farra, al-Zajjaj, al-Qurtubi, al-Biqai, Bursevi and Ibn Ashur stress the hoped-for conversion of leaders and followers. Al-Qurtubi notes Ibn Umm Maktum already believed; Ibn Ashur mentions fear that the gathering would disperse. Bursevi allows that the Prophet may simply not have heard the low voice.
  80:2/q13/p2 He feared leaders would disdain followers who were blind, poor or enslaved. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi, al-Baghawi, al-Qurtubi, Mujahid and al-Majma describe this concern. Islahi says the leaders already disparaged poor companions as evidence against prophethood; he denies that poverty or blindness itself caused the Prophet's displeasure.
  80:2/q13/p3 He may have wished to spare his companion insult from arrogant leaders. — reasons: Islahi offers this additional possible motive.
80:2/q14 Was the blind visitor discourteous? [جَآءَهُ ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ · interpretation] (turns on: What the visitor knew of the meeting and how to assess his persistence or passage past his guide.)
  80:2/q14/p1 No: he did not know the Prophet was occupied. — reasons: Jishumi says he would not have persisted had he known. Al-Qurtubi says interruption would be poor manners only with knowledge of the meeting and hope for conversion. Bursevi and the occasion reports describe his inability to see the situation.
  80:2/q14/p2 He showed some roughness or deserved to be ignored for discourtesy. — reasons: Ibn Zayd's guide report says he pressed ahead; al-Qurtubi calls that rough. Al-Tabrisi reports a view that God nevertheless rebuked the response lest he think poverty caused rejection and status won the leaders' welcome.
80:2/q15 How did the Prophet treat Ibn Umm Maktum afterward? [جَآءَهُ ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ · reports] (turns on: Different reports describe immediate pursuit, later personal welcome, and a lasting change in public conduct.)
  80:2/q15/p1 He honored him, welcomed him as the man whose encounter brought divine reproof, and asked what he needed. — reasons: Numerous accounts report later solicitude. Al-Thawri adds that he spread his cloak. Ibn Abbas reports repeated offers of help. Al-Sadiq's alternative account says the Prophet welcomed him saying God would never reprove him on his account; the kindness made Ibn Umm Maktum withdraw out of bashfulness, as al-Tabataba'i explains.
  80:2/q15/p2 He followed the man and sent someone to find him. — reasons: Al-Qushayri reports this from a tafsir account.
  80:2/q15/p3 He never again frowned at or turned away from a poor person. — reasons: Al-Qushayri reports this; al-Hakam says he was thereafter never seen courting a rich person or turning from a poor one.
80:2/q16 How often did Ibn Umm Maktum govern or lead in Medina for the Prophet? [ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ · reports] (turns on: The different reported counts, and whether the count concerns governing as deputy or leading prayers.)
  80:2/q16/p1 He deputized for the Prophet twice. — reasons: Qatada, al-Dahhak, al-Tha'labi, al-Qushayri, al-Baghawi, al-Thawri, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, al-Biqai and others report twice during campaigns; Kuran Yolu says he led prayers twice.
  80:2/q16/p2 He deputized three times. — reasons: Bursevi records this alternative number.
  80:2/q16/p3 He led prayers in Medina thirteen times. — reasons: Alusi attributes this count to Ibn Abd al-Barr, alongside a general report of deputizing.
80:2/q17 What do later reports say of Ibn Umm Maktum's role at al-Qadisiyya and death? [ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ · reports] (turns on: Conflicting reports about whether he died in the campaign or returned to Medina.)
  80:2/q17/p1 Anas saw him at al-Qadisiyya in armor with a black banner. — reasons: Multiple transmitters repeat Anas's eyewitness report; al-Qurtubi adds that he was mounted. Bursevi says the battle saw Umar's forces defeat the Persians and take spoils.
  80:2/q17/p2 He died as a martyr at al-Qadisiyya during the conquest of al-Madain. — reasons: Alusi reports this version in the period of Umar.
  80:2/q17/p3 He returned to Medina and died there. — reasons: Alusi records this alternative.
80:2/q18 What do reports say about his calls to prayer and attending prayer? [ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ · reports]
  80:2/q18/p1 He was the Prophet's second caller to prayer and gave the dawn call after Bilal's night call. — reasons: Bursevi calls him the second muezzin and quotes the Prophet's instruction to eat and drink until his call. Ibn Umar's report adds that, being blind, he waited for others to tell him dawn had appeared.
  80:2/q18/p2 Despite blindness and difficulty reaching the mosque, he was told to attend on hearing the call. — reasons: Abu Umama reports his age, frailty, blindness and unsuitable guide, yet no exemption; Kab ibn Ujra's report says he lacked a guide and should answer God's caller.
80:2/q19 How should the frown be judged? [* · theology] (turns on: Prophetic conduct and culpability: unseen discourtesy, a new prohibition, prophetic standards, rational moral judgment, or excess zeal in preaching.)
  80:2/q19/p1 It was not sinful but occasioned instruction toward the highest manners. — reasons: Jishumi and al-Tabrisi argue a blind man could not see the frown, so it did not hurt him; God still trains the Prophet to prioritize the believer and avoid alienation or a repeated habit. Al-Qurtubi says attending the poor believer was better, though outreach to the rich had a purpose.
  80:2/q19/p2 It was permissible before this newly revealed prohibition, though it could become sinful afterward. — reasons: Al-Jubba'i gives the principle that later prohibition does not retroactively make an earlier act sinful.
  80:2/q19/p3 Even a minor discourtesy became a major sin when committed by a prophet. — reasons: Asad says prophetic status explains the severity of the divine rebuke.
