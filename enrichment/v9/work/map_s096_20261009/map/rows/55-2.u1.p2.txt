55:2/q22 How should Qur’anic commentary be compiled and transmitted? [* · sciences]
  55:2/q22/p1 Use reliable chains, careful selection and organized explanation that serves readers. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabi criticizes chainless notebook copying and mixed sound and poor material; teachers pass learning to students, and without chains anyone could claim anything. He says excessive reports and disputes discourage seekers, while early commentators lacked law and resolution of difficulties. He gathered over one hundred tested or heard books and nearly three hundred teachers, organizing, clarifying, shortening or expanding material to meet demand. Al-Wahidi reports complaints about huge low-benefit works and records his own recitation and book studies with al-Busti, al-Hiri, al-Farisi and al-Thaʿlabi, including Ibn Mihran, al-Zajjaj and over five hundred parts of al-Thaʿlabi’s work. Al-Jishumi honors early foundations and later refinement and says his work balances length and brevity for beginners and advanced readers.
55:2/q23 Who is remembered for knowing and teaching Qur’anic interpretation? [* · reports]
  55:2/q23/p1 Early interpreters pursued verse meanings intensely and were recognized for distinct expertise. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya recounts Ali praising Jabir for interpreting 28:85 and Masruq traveling through Basra to Syria for a verse’s interpreter. Al-Suyuti lists ten prominent Companion exegetes: the four caliphs, Ibn Masud, Ibn Abbas, Ubayy, Zayd, Abu Musa and Ibn al-Zubayr; Ali has most reports among caliphs, possibly because the others died earlier. Ali claimed knowledge of each verse’s time and place and an understanding heart; Ibn Masud is praised for Qur’an and Sunna, said Ali knew the seven readings’ outward and inward, and said he himself knew each verse’s audience and place. Prophetic prayers and Ibn Umar’s report honor Ibn Abbas’s wisdom and interpretation.
55:2/q24 How are transmitted Qur’anic readings authenticated? [* · readings] (turns on: Whether tawatur belongs to the written consonantal text, each vocalization, or specialist transmission.)
  55:2/q24/p1 The Uthmanic consonantal text is mass transmitted, but individual compatible vocalizations and named chains need not each be mutawatir. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says variants outside the codex, such as Ibn Masud’s, fall outside acceptance; Companion-era use without objection made accepted choices collectively widespread, while individual chains remain singular. Ibn al-Arabi, Ibn Abd al-Salam al-Tunisi, Ibn Idris and al-Abyari deny that each reading is mutawatir, and Ibn Ashur endorses this: chains show named readers’ differences, while wording is independently established. He rejects the claim that methods and recensions are all mutawatir, though Nafi has two Companion transmitters. The ten traditions trace to eight Companions; Ibn Arafa and Ibn Lab debated this.
  55:2/q24/p2 Compatible readings are mutawatir because they follow the mass-transmitted script. — reasons: Ibn al-Arabi gives this basis; Imam al-Haramayn and Ibn Salama say the readings are mutawatir, though al-Abyari rejects Imam al-Haramayn.
  55:2/q24/p3 Readings are mutawatir among recitation specialists, though not among Muslims generally. — reasons: Al-Maziri’s middle position.
55:2/q25 How should grammar be weighed against transmitted readings? [* · readings] (turns on: Whether grammatical school preference can override transmitted reading.)
  55:2/q25/p1 Sound transmitted readings must be accepted even when grammarians disfavor their syntax. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says most parses are mutawatir but allows different parses with the same sense, such as wa-lata hina and hatta yaqula. He rejects Basran or Kufan norms as exhaustive tests of Arabic and says less preferred grammar can preserve pronunciation and yield distinct rhetorical effects. Al-Zamakhshari rejected Ibn Amir’s passive zuyyina with nominative qatlu, accusative awladahum and genitive shuraka’ihim; Abu Ali al-Farisi instead defended readings grammatically in al-Hujja.
  55:2/q25/p2 Some readings are altered or condemned for doctrinal reasons. — reasons: Ibn Ashur reports some Muʿtazilites taking Allah accusative in ‘God spoke to Moses’ to avoid divine speech, and condemns a Rafidi dual ‘misleaders’ aimed at Abu Bakr and Umar.
  55:2/q25/p3 Al-Zamakhshari judged several transmitted readings grammatically weak. — reasons: Ibn Ashur cites his criticism of Ibn Amir’s passive zuyyina construction as an example of the rejected approach.
55:2/q26 How is the Qur’an described as enduring guidance? [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · interpretation]
  55:2/q26/p1 It gives life to hearts and has been protected from alteration while superseding earlier religions. — reasons: Al-Zarkashi says it is called a spirit because it brings lasting life to the heart, enables people and invites reflection (40:15). Al-Jishumi says it was revealed without addition or omission and made to supersede other religions.
55:2/q27 What kinds of knowledge and argument does the Qur’an convey? [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · interpretation]
  55:2/q27/p1 It gives clear proofs in familiar Arabic while allowing deeper rational and rhetorical insight. — reasons: Al-Burhan says it contains every kind of proof, demonstration, division and definition; 14:4 explains why messengers address their people’s language. Obscure disputation can show inability to establish a point plainly. Its arguments address ordinary hearers while specialists discern subtleties, using reason, hearing, reflection and memory (2:164). Change of states proves the world’s origination; Abraham’s setting stars, moon and sun extend proof from the lesser bodies to all corporeal things (6:76–78).
55:2/q28 What do Qur’anic parables teach? [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · interpretation]
  55:2/q28/p1 They make meanings memorable, prompt reflection and convey ethical and legal guidance. — reasons: Al-Suyuti cites 39:27 and 29:43 and reports a prophetic fivefold division: lawful, unlawful, clear, ambiguous and parables, each with its response. Al-Mawardi says grasping the represented meaning is essential, comparing neglected representation to an unbridled horse. Al-Shafiʿi requires jurists to know parables of obedience and disobedience. Ibn Abd al-Salam says praise, blame, reward and wasted deeds can indicate rulings and that praise alongside permission can mark recommendation or obligation. Al-Suyuti lists exhortation, deterrence, sensory clarification, proof, reward and punishment; al-Zarkashi adds eloquence and al-Zamakhshari says analogy makes conjectural ideas visible with fitting comparisons.
55:2/q29 What does the adjoining word bayān mean? [* · meaning]
  55:2/q29/p1 It is evidence leading to knowledge or clarification that distinguishes one meaning from another. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi offers these two descriptions; al-Jurjani adds that speech presents knowledge with its qualities and states, while the best speech depends on arrangement rather than isolated words.
55:2/q30 Whom may al-insān in the next verse designate? [* · referent]
  55:2/q30/p1 It may mean humanity as a kind, Adam, or Muhammad. — reasons: Al-Nasafi reports the three alternatives.
55:2/q31 How is the sura’s opening counted and classified? [* · sciences]
  55:2/q31/p1 Most verse counters count the opening as one verse, and al-Zarkashi classifies this sura among declarative openings. — reasons: Ibn Ashur gives the verse count; al-Zarkashi lists al-Rahman with al-Anbiya and al-Qamar among twenty-three declarative sura openings.
55:2/q32 What other reading or expression in this sura is noted? [* · readings]
  55:2/q32/p1 ʿAmr ibn ʿUbayd read the rose comparison in 55:37 in the nominative, picturing a rose-colored sky emerging. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari classifies the expression as tajrid.
55:2/q33 How can one Qur’anic statement hold two aspects? [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · interpretation]
  55:2/q33/p1 An act may be affirmed of a human in one respect and denied in another because God causes its effect. — reasons: Al-Zarkashi illustrates with 8:17: the Prophet threw, but the resulting effect was not his own act.
