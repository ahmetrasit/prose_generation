[TAB:1:1/r30] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | The preposition bāʾ requires a governing verb, omitted here; the listener supplies it from the act underway, just as an answer like “food” can omit an already evident verb.
[TAB:1:1/r31] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Before reciting, standing, sitting, or another action, the phrase means beginning that act by naming and remembering God.
[TAB:1:1/r32] TAB, d. 310 · عبد الله بن عباس | reports | In Ibn ʿAbbās’s report, Gabriel glosses the basmala as reading, standing, and sitting in remembrance of God.
[TAB:1:1/r33] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Al-Ṭabarī rejects construing the phrase as “by God” in every act: it means beginning with God’s naming and remembrance, not claiming that an action is performed by God.
[TAB:1:1/r34] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | The scholarly consensus that saying “by God” at slaughter does not fulfill the prescribed “in the name of God” supports his distinction between the formula and “by God.”
[TAB:1:1/r35] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Here “name” stands for “naming”: Arabic often uses a verbal noun in a form different from its regular pattern, as in karāmah for ikrām and kalām for taklīm.
[TAB:1:1/r36] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Poetic examples use ʿaṭāʾ for iʿṭāʾ, ṭawl for iṭāla, and muṣāba for iṣāba, supporting the unusual verbal-noun form in the basmala.
[TAB:1:1/r37] TAB, d. 310 · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | rejects | A report has Jesus explain the letters of “bism” as God’s glory, splendor, and kingdom; al-Ṭabarī suspects a transmitter’s error and thinks separate alphabet letters may have been run together, since the gloss makes no sense in the full basmala.
[TAB:1:1/r38] TAB, d. 310 · مقدم في العلم بلغة العرب | rejects | An unnamed authority in Arabic usage reads Lubayd’s “then the name of peace be upon you” as simply “then peace be upon you”; al-Ṭabarī rejects that reading as linguistically unsound.
[TAB:1:1/r39] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | If “name” could simply mean its referent, Arabic would also permit “I ate the name of honey”; the unanimous rejection of such usage undermines that reading of Lubayd.
[TAB:1:1/r4] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | It is the Mother of the Qurʾān because it precedes the other sūras in reading and writing, while they follow it.
[TAB:1:1/r40] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Al-Ṭabarī’s likelier reading of Lubayd is an exhortation to keep God’s name and remembrance after a delayed exhortative word, while leaving off remembering and grieving for the dead.
[TAB:1:1/r41] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | A second possible reading of Lubayd invokes God’s name for protection over the two people.
[TAB:1:1/r42] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | To support the delayed exhortation reading, al-Ṭabarī cites Arabic word order that places the thing urged before the delayed “take it” command.
[TAB:1:1/r43] TAB, d. 310 · عبد الله بن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbās explains Allah as the one worshipped by every thing and served by all creation.
[TAB:1:1/r44] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Al-Ṭabarī derives Allah from al-ilāh by inference rather than direct attestation; ilāh means the worshipped one, supported by Arab use of taʾallaha for worship and seeking God.
[TAB:1:1/r45] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Ruʾba’s “min taʾalluhī” means devotion and seeking God through worship, showing the root’s sense.
[TAB:1:1/r46] TAB, d. 310 · ابن عباس ومجاهد | reports | Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿAbd Allāh, and Mujāhid read “wa-ilāhataka” at 7:127 as “your worship”; Pharaoh was worshipped rather than worshipping. | mentions 7:127
[TAB:1:1/r47] TAB, d. 310 · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | A report attributed to the Prophet has Jesus gloss Allah as “the god of gods,” which al-Ṭabarī uses in explaining how God is worshipped by the servant and worships the servant in the stated idiom.
[TAB:1:1/r48] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Allah’s form comes from al-ilāh: the initial hamza is dropped, then the two lām sounds meet and assimilate into one doubled lām.
[TAB:1:1/r49] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | The contraction is comparable to “lākinn huwa Allāh rabbī” from “lākinn anā huwa Allāh rabbī,” and to the poem’s “lākinn iyyāki” from “lākinn anā iyyāki.”
[TAB:1:1/r5] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Arab usage calls a comprehensive or leading thing with dependent followers an “umm”; examples include the membrane around the brain and an army’s gathering banner.
[TAB:1:1/r50] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | “Al-Raḥmān” follows faʿlān from raḥima, like ghaḍbān, sakrān, and ʿaṭshān from their verbs.
[TAB:1:1/r51] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | “Raḥīm” follows faʿīl as a form of praise; Arabic uses faʿīl for praise or blame even when the verb’s vowel differs, as in ʿālim/ʿalīm and qādir/qadīr.
[TAB:1:1/r52] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | In Arabic, al-Raḥmān is farther from the ordinary faʿila yafʿalu pattern than al-Raḥīm, and that greater departure intensifies praise.
[TAB:1:1/r53] TAB, d. 310 · العرزمي | reports | Al-ʿArzami says al-Raḥmān extends mercy to all creation, while al-Raḥīm is mercy toward believers.
[TAB:1:1/r54] TAB, d. 310 · عيسى ابن مريم | reports | A report says Jesus distinguished al-Raḥmān as mercy in this world and the next, and al-Raḥīm as mercy in the next world.
[TAB:1:1/r55] TAB, d. 310 · author | prefers | Al-Ṭabarī accepts both transmitted distinctions: al-Raḥmān denotes God’s mercy to all creation, while al-Raḥīm denotes mercy specified for some, whether in this life, the next, or both.
[TAB:1:1/r56] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | God’s universal worldly mercy includes provision, rain-bearing clouds, plants, bodily and mental health, and countless other blessings shared by believers and unbelievers. | mentions 14:34
[TAB:1:1/r57] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | In the hereafter, universal mercy means God’s just judgment: He wrongs no one even by an atom’s weight and repays each soul for what it earned. | mentions 4:40
[TAB:1:1/r58] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | God’s special mercy to believers in this life is the gracious aid that enables obedience, faith, following revelation, and avoiding sin, which He withholds from those He leaves astray. | mentions 33:43
[TAB:1:1/r59] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | In the next life, special mercy to believers is the lasting bliss, triumph, and honor prepared for them rather than unbelievers.
[TAB:1:1/r6] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Dhu al-Rumma’s banner poem uses “umm” for the standard around which companions gather for travel and battle.
[TAB:1:1/r60] TAB, d. 310 · عبد الله بن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbās glosses al-Raḥmān as tender toward those God wills to show mercy, and al-Raḥīm as gentle toward those He wills to treat gently, but severe toward those He wills to chastise.
[TAB:1:1/r61] TAB, d. 310 · author | prefers | Al-Ṭabarī prefers the Prophet-attributed and al-ʿArzami distinctions to Ibn ʿAbbās’s account, while acknowledging that Ibn ʿAbbās also gives the words distinguishable meanings.
[TAB:1:1/r62] TAB, d. 310 · عطاء الخراساني | reports | ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī says God’s name was al-Raḥmān, then paired it with al-Raḥīm after the impostor Musaylima appropriated al-Raḥmān.
[TAB:1:1/r63] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Al-Ṭabarī considers ʿAṭāʾ’s explanation plausible: God uniquely combines the two names to distinguish His name, even if their individual meanings differ or overlap.
[TAB:1:1/r64] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Al-Ṭabarī rejects the claim that Arabs did not know “al-Raḥmān”: denial by polytheists does not prove ignorance, just as people who know Muhammad can still reject him. | mentions 25:60, 2:146
[TAB:1:1/r65] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Pre-Islamic verses by an unnamed poet and Salāma ibn Jandal use “al-Raḥmān,” demonstrating that the name was known among Arabs.
[TAB:1:1/r66] TAB, d. 310 · بعض من ضعفت معرفته بتأويل أهل التأويل | reports | An unnamed, weakly informed interpreter equates al-Raḥmān with “possessor of mercy” and al-Raḥīm with “merciful,” then inconsistently treats the pair as equivalent forms like nadmān and nadīm.
[TAB:1:1/r67] TAB, d. 310 · author | rejects | Al-Ṭabarī objects that “possessor of mercy” asserts an established attribute, whereas “merciful” can describe past, present, or future action; the terms therefore do not simply mean the same thing.
[TAB:1:1/r68] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Arabic normally names the subject first and follows it with attributes, so the sequence places the referent before its descriptions.
[TAB:1:1/r69] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | The order puts Allah first because worship belongs to Him alone, al-Raḥmān next because it is also reserved for Him, and al-Raḥīm last because creatures may share some quality of mercy. | mentions 27:60, 27:61, 27:62, 27:63, 27:64, 17:110
