## THE CURRENT MAP
76:26/q01 What does 'from the night' specify? [وَمِنَ ٱلَّيْلِ · interpretation]
  76:26/q01/p1 A portion of the night is set aside for worship and prayer. — reasons: Al-Biqai says the remainder is for sleep and rest. Al-Baydawi and Alusi specify prayer; Elmalili explicitly glosses min as partitive. Al-Nahhas supplies an understood 'from the night' with the command.
76:26/q02 What does prostrating to Him at night mean? [فَٱسْجُدْ لَهُۥ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the prostration command identifies the two prescribed evening prayers or night prayer more generally.)
  76:26/q02/p1 It refers specifically to the Maghrib and Isha prayers. — reasons: Al-Biqai calls prostration the prayer's most excellent act; Alusi and Elmalili take the part to name the whole prayer. Al-Suyuti's Itqan illustrates this figure with prayer's standing, recitation, bowing, and prostration standing for prayer, alongside examples involving face and hands (55:27, 2:144, 88:8, 88:3, 2:286, 2:184, 17:78, 2:43, 5:95). Al-Baydawi and Bursevi phrase the identification tentatively. Muqatil names Isha before sleep, and al-Mawardi calls them sunset and late-night prayers.
  76:26/q02/p2 It directs prayer at night generally, whether obligatory or voluntary. — reasons: Ibn Ashur locates this prayer when the Prophet is free from public preaching and cites 73:2, 73:20. Al-Tabari explains it as praying and glorifying at night; Ibn Abbas likewise says prayer and glorification. Elmalili says prostration explains remembrance as prayer by naming a part for the whole. Ibn Kathir compares the night-vigil commands in 17:79 and 73:1-4.
76:26/q03 What force does the fa in 'fa-sjud' have? [فَٱسْجُدْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the emphatic fa has an implied conditional clause or is grammatically extra.)
  76:26/q03/p1 It answers an implied condition: whatever happens, prostrate to Him. — reasons: Bursevi gives the conditional paraphrase. Al-Samarrai reports grammarians' conditional analysis and says it stresses prostration and its rank.
  76:26/q03/p2 It is an extra particle for emphasis. — reasons: Al-Samarrai reports this grammatical analysis and accepts that, on either account, the fa magnifies prostration.
76:26/q04 How is the lam in 'lahu' understood? [لَهُۥ · grammar]
  76:26/q04/p1 Lam can convey the sense of 'on' or 'against'. — reasons: Al-Suyuti cites falling on chins (17:107), lying on one's side (39:29), and a curse upon people (12:103) as comparisons for lam's range; the note does not explicitly demand that sense here.
76:26/q05 Why is the night phrase put before the command to prostrate? [وَمِنَ ٱلَّيْلِ فَٱسْجُدْ لَهُۥ · rhetoric]
  76:26/q05/p1 It highlights the greater effort, sincerity, and merit of worship at night. — reasons: Al-Baydawi and Bursevi link night worship to difficulty and sincerity away from display. Al-Biqai adds fewer sensory distractions, less showing off, and deeper humility and delight. Alusi likewise stresses effort and sincerity. Al-Samarrai connects the burden of leaving sleep with prostration's stature and reward, citing 51:17-18, 32:17, 17:79; he says the preceding waw and fronting preserve force that ordinary word order would lose, comparing 106:1-3 and 74:3. He contrasts al-Muzzammil's earlier ordinary order (73:2-4) with al-Insan's later fronting as the prophetic mission grew more burdensome.
76:26/q06 What does 'glorify Him' mean here? [وَسَبِّحْهُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether tasbih here is specified by the prayer context or retains its broader sense of remembrance and worship.)
  76:26/q06/p1 It means prayer, particularly voluntary night prayer or tahajjud. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and Sufyan say every Quranic occurrence of glorification means prayer. Muqatil and al-Kalbi specify voluntary prayer after obligatory prayers; Ibn Habib and Abd al-Malik ibn Habib likewise specify voluntary night worship. Al-Razi's reported reading contrasts the five daily prayers with the night vigil. Al-Biqai, al-Nasafi, al-Baydawi, and Study Quran call it tahajjud or vigil; Study Quran cites 11:114, 20:130, 21:20, 25:63-64, 50:40, 52:49, 73:1-2, 6, 20. Al-Tabari and Ibn Abbas also say prayer and glorification. Ibn Ashur reasons from its coordination with night prostration to voluntary devotion, comparing 52:48.
  76:26/q06/p2 It can include unrestricted remembrance and worship, in or out of prayer. — reasons: Alusi derives tasbih from declaring God free of imperfection and allows verbal or bodily worship generally. Qurtubi says it may be unrestricted remembrance inside or outside prayer. Ibn Ashur includes verbal praise, good speech, and reflection on God's attributes. Kuran Yolu's preferred broad reading includes glorification, supplication, repentance, and seeking forgiveness alongside prayer.
76:26/q07 Why is the night vigil called glorification? [وَسَبِّحْهُ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the word evokes the worshipper's effort and love or marks the command's relative legal rank; Alusi disputes the latter inference.)
  76:26/q07/p1 The struggle to leave sleep expresses God's transcendence and the worshipper's love. — reasons: Al-Biqai says rising despite sleep recalls God's majesty and transcendence, while leaving beloved rest displays profound love for Him.
  76:26/q07/p2 Calling it tasbih signals a rank below the preceding prostration instruction. — reasons: Bursevi combines this word choice with the later placement of the time phrase as evidence for a lower rank. Alusi questions whether those features establish that this command is not obligatory.
76:26/q08 How long is the night glorification? [لَيْلًا طَوِيلًا · interpretation] (turns on: How strictly 'a long night' fixes a share of the night, or whether it covers all waking time.)
  76:26/q08/p1 It occupies most of the night, leaving only a little. — reasons: Al-Tabari cites 73:2; Ibn Ashur says the long night restricts the command and cites 73:2, 4.
  76:26/q08/p2 It is a substantial variable portion, such as two thirds, half, or a third. — reasons: Al-Nasafi gives two thirds, half, or one third. Bursevi gives the same alternatives for a vigil he calls obligatory for the Prophet. Al-Biqai permits half, more, or less. Muqatil simply calls it a long night of prayer; al-Baydawi a substantial portion.
  76:26/q08/p3 Devotion extends through every time one is awake at night. — reasons: Asad gives this gloss without a numerical fraction.
76:26/q09 How do 'a night' and 'long' work grammatically? [لَيْلًا طَوِيلًا · grammar]
  76:26/q09/p1 Laylan is an adverb of time and tawilan its adjective; 'long' asks for prolonged worship. — reasons: Bursevi says the adjective does not exclude shorter night prayer by distinguishing long nights from short nights.
76:26/q10 What is the legal force of the prolonged night worship command? [وَسَبِّحْهُ لَيْلًا طَوِيلًا · law] (turns on: Whether the imperative imposed an obligation, whether 73:20 or the five prayers ended it, and whether the obligation applied only to the Prophet.)
  76:26/q10/p1 It was obligatory initially, then abrogated by the five prescribed prayers. — reasons: Al-Razi's reported view reads repeated imperatives as obligation and cites 73:20 for later abrogation. Ibn Zayd and others are reported by Qurtubi and Alusi to connect abrogation with the five prayers; al-Nahhas also records abrogation as one view.
  76:26/q10/p2 It remains obligatory for the Prophet, while voluntary for his community. — reasons: Muqatil distinguishes obligatory night vigil for the Prophet from extra tahajjud. Ata says night prayer was obligatory for Muhammad. Ibn al-Jawzi explicitly contrasts his obligation with the community's voluntary practice. Bursevi says the Prophet's required vigil could last two thirds, half, or one third. Alusi reports a specifically prophetic obligation; Kuran Yolu reports the same contrast from Razi. Al-Nahhas and Qurtubi list specificity to Muhammad among the views.
  76:26/q10/p3 It remains in force as a recommendation or voluntary devotion. — reasons: Al-Razi reports this as an un-abrogated voluntary ruling. Alusi reports a generally applicable recommendation. Al-Nahhas and Qurtubi include recommendation among alternatives. Al-Wahidi prefers voluntary prayer because Muhammad was not required to pray the whole night. Qurtubi calls voluntary night worship sound; al-Mawardi and Ibn Habib also identify it as voluntary. Alusi challenges Bursevi's inference from tasbih and delayed time phrase to non-obligation, without thereby rejecting recommendation itself.
76:26/q11 How should the sounds of 'wa-sabbihhu' be articulated? [وَسَبِّحْهُ · readings]
  76:26/q11/p1 The hāʾ must remain hāʾ and the preceding ḥāʾ must be clear; assimilating them or turning hāʾ into ḥāʾ is impermissible. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari says the throat ḥāʾ needs distinct articulation before a matching or neighboring letter, especially when vowelless; some speakers shift weak hāʾ toward ḥāʾ, making a doubled sound, but he reports unanimous prohibition. He also calls for strong voiced articulation of a letter when vowelless before certain voiceless letters lest it merge with shin, especially when doubled; and for a thin ḥāʾ near emphatic consonants.
76:26/q12 What additional articulation rules appear in the recitation discussion? [وَسَبِّحْهُ · readings] (turns on: These are distinct consonant cautions in the cited excerpt; the notes do not tie each to a separate word of this verse.)
  76:26/q12/p1 Khāʾ and other emphatics retain emphasis, strongest with fatha, then damma, then kasra. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari reports Ibn al-Tahhan's vowel-based ranking of emphasis.
  76:26/q12/p2 A dāl substituted for tāʾ must remain distinct. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari warns that the tongue may revert to the original tāʾ.
  76:26/q12/p3 Vowelless dhāl remains clear before nūn and thin near emphatics. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari warns against changing it to ẓāʾ; he notes Nabatean dāl and non-Arab zāy substitutions.
