 أهل الإلحاد الطاعنون في القرآن | reports | Critics ask why al-Fātiḥa has seven verses when, they argue, the meaning of its remaining verses is already contained in “Master of Judgment Day” and “You alone we worship and ask for help.”
[TAB:1:7/r55] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Earlier scriptures each contain some meanings found together in the Qur'an: the Torah gives admonitions and detail, the Psalms praise and glorify, and the Gospel admonishes and reminds; the Qur'an gathers these and adds more.
[TAB:1:7/r56] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Al-Ṭabarī says the prior books lack a miracle certifying their recipients, whereas the Qur'an's unmatched composition defeats all attempts to produce even a short surah and proves Muhammad's prophethood.
[TAB:1:7/r57] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | The Qur'an's varied contents—encouragement, warning, commands, prohibitions, stories, argument, and parables—also make its extended expression purposeful.
[TAB:1:7/r58] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Al-Fātiḥa's inimitable arrangement proves prophethood, while its praise and glorification direct people to recognize God's greatness, sovereignty, power, and dominion and to thank Him for His favors.
[TAB:1:7/r59] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | The account of those favored through knowledge of God and His enabling teaches that every religious and worldly blessing comes from Him, directing requests to God rather than idols or rivals.
[TAB:1:7/r60] TAB, d. 310 · author | holds | Recounting punishments for disobedience warns people away from sin and from incurring God's anger, whose consequences lead to destruction.
[TAB:1:7/r61] TAB, d. 310 · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Abū Hurayra reports that the Prophet said God answers the worshipper's recitation: He calls al-ḥamdu praise, al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm commendation, Mālik Yawm al-Dīn glorification, and the rest of the surah the servant's share.
[TAB:1:7/r62] TAB, d. 310 · أبو هريرة | reports | A second Abū Hurayra version gives the same recitation exchange but is not attributed to the Prophet.
[TAB:1:7/r63] TAB, d. 310 · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh reports the divine saying that the prayer is divided between God and His servant, with the servant receiving what he asks; the first three praises are God's, and what follows is for the servant.
[TAB-FULL:v1p111#3/r1] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · author | holds | God taught Muhammad to put His beautiful names before every action, establishing a practice for people opening speech, letters, books, and requests.
[TAB-FULL:v1p111#3/r2] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · author | holds | The prefixed bāʾ requires a governing verb omitted from the wording; the listener recovers it from the immediately accompanying action or speech.
[TAB-FULL:v1p111#3/r3] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · author | holds | At Qurʾān recitation the implicit verb is read, while in other acts it is rise, sit, or the relevant action, all begun with God’s name.
[TAB-FULL:v1p113/r1] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · عبد الله بن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbās reports Gabriel first taught seeking refuge and then instructed Muhammad to read, rise, and sit in remembrance of his Lord.
[TAB-FULL:v1p113/r2] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · author | holds | The phrase means beginning by naming and remembering God, rather than claiming that the act itself is performed through God’s power.
[TAB-FULL:v1p113/r3] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · author | holds | Arabic can use a verbal noun in a form normally associated with a noun; thus bismillah can express beginning with the act of naming.
[TAB-FULL:v1p115/r1] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · author | holds | Poetic usage confirms verbal nouns can take other noun forms: ʿaṭāʾika means iʿṭāʾika, ṭūl means iṭāla, and muṣāb means iṣāba.
[TAB-FULL:v1p115/r2] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · author | holds | Accordingly, the name stands for naming: the phrase means that one begins an action or recitation by naming God.
[TAB-FULL:v1p116/r1] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · عبد الله بن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbās reports Gabriel’s instruction that Muhammad read and undertake actions in remembrance of God his Lord.
[TAB-FULL:v1p116/r2] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · author | prefers | Ṭabarī adopts the interpretation that recitation starts with God’s naming and remembrance, using His beautiful names and exalted attributes.
[TAB-FULL:v1p116/r3] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · author | rejects | The wording does not mean simply “by God”; the consensus on slaughter treats saying bi-llāh without bismillah as omitting the prescribed invocation.
[TAB-FULL:v1p116/r4] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · author | holds | The present question concerns whether the attached “name” is a noun or a verbal noun meaning naming; the broader identity of name and named is outside this discussion.
[TAB-FULL:v1p118/r1] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · author | rejects | Ṭabarī rejects taking Labīd’s “name of peace” simply as “peace”; that reading would license impossible Arabic such as eating the name of honey.
[TAB-FULL:v1p118/r2] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · author | reports | One possible reading makes al-Salām a divine name: “adhere to God’s name” after a year, with the command for adherence delayed in the wording; Ṭabarī finds this likelier.
[TAB-FULL:v1p118/r3] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · author | reports | A second possible reading is a protective invocation: may God’s name be upon the two addressed people against harm.
[TAB-FULL:v1p118/r4] TAB-FULL, d. 310 · author | prefers | Of the two defensible readings of Labīd, the first—taking al-Salām as God’s name and an exhortation to keep His remembrance—is more apt.
