  20:132/q23/p1 Prayer and devotion open provision or relieve want, sometimes unexpectedly. — reasons: Qushayrī calls prayer a door to provision. Ibn Kathīr cites 65:2–3 and 51:56–58; a divine report via Abū Hurayra promises inner richness and relief from poverty for devotion, with the opposite for neglect. Biqāʿī records al-Tirmidhī's ḥasan gharīb grading of that report. Reports from Ibn Masʿūd and Zayd ibn Thābit contrast focus on the Hereafter, order and inner wealth with scattered worldly cares. Wahb says earlier believers often found relief through prayer. Islahi says only experience conveys prayer's guarantee and cites devout lives. Nasafī adds the maxim that whoever occupies himself with God's work receives God's care.
  20:132/q23/p2 Provision is allotted by God, unaffected by greater or lesser pursuit. — reasons: Biqāʿī says neither diligence nor neglect changes each person's eternally allotted share; trusting God yields contentment, while reliance on one's own effort yields toil.
  20:132/q23/p3 The elect are nourished by witnessing the Provider, beyond material provision. — reasons: Qushayrī distinguishes provisions that strengthen souls from witnessing the Provider that strengthens hearts; ordinary people live by provisions, the elect by witnessing God. Realizing the divine 'We' erases distinctions between kinds of provision; prayer heals and nourishes the heart especially when bodily sustenance is delayed.
  20:132/q23/p4 God's provision includes higher knowledge and freedom from dependence on people. — reasons: Qāshānī contrasts lower-world perfections and sensory knowledge with spiritual knowledge and holy realities. Bursevī says inward forms of prayer free their practitioners from dependence on people, cites 93:8, and quotes the Prophet's saying that his Lord feeds and gives him drink at night.
20:132/q24 What does al-ʿāqibah mean here? [وَٱلْعَٰقِبَةُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the word itself denotes only a good end or can denote any consequence, with the context fixing its value.)
  20:132/q24/p1 The praiseworthy final outcome. — reasons: Jishumī, Baghawī, Ṭabrisī, Abū Ḥayyān, and Bursevī give the favorable sense; Bursevī says an unqualified ʿāqibah denotes the good reward, Paradise. Qurṭubī says others have outcomes too, but a blameworthy one is rhetorically treated as nonexistent.
  20:132/q24/p2 Whatever follows an affair, good or bad, though common usage favors good. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr gives the broad lexical sense, then says piety's result is good.
20:132/q25 What is the good outcome promised to piety? [وَٱلْعَٰقِبَةُ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the stated end is specifically Paradise, the wider favorable consequence of piety, an inward transformation, or an image of piety's sole value.)
  20:132/q25/p1 Paradise, the final reward of the God-fearing. — reasons: Muqātil, Yaḥyā, Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Suddī in Wāḥidī, Wāḥidī's Wajīz, Qurṭubī, Bursevī, Durr, and Study Quran identify Paradise. Yaḥyā cites 43:35; Qurṭubī says a blameworthy outcome counts as no true outcome. Al-Ālūsī reports al-Suddī's narrower identification while allowing a broader good end.
  20:132/q25/p2 A praiseworthy end or ultimate success for the God-fearing, in this life or the next. — reasons: Ṭabarī speaks of the final outcome of deeds, not the heedless; Ibn ʿAṭiyya names victory here and mercy hereafter; Ibn Kathīr includes both worlds, ending in Paradise. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the Hereafter claim is absolute and worldly good possible. Islahi treats the verse as glad tidings of ultimate success after worldly sustenance; al-Ālūsī says the end is broader than Paradise. Ibn Kathīr cites an authentic dream report: dates from Ibn Ṭāb foretold believers' good worldly outcome, elevation, and sound religion.
  20:132/q25/p3 The true end is inward detachment or disciplined self and limbs. — reasons: Sulamī reports Abū ʿUthmān: restraint from all that knowledge does not permit; Bursevī repeats this. Qāshānī reads the worthy end as shedding bodily garments and psychic dispositions.
  20:132/q25/p4 It is like a market where only piety has value. — reasons: Jishumī reports this image as another explanation of why the outcome belongs to piety.
20:132/q26 Who are the people of piety in the ending? [لِلتَّقْوَىٰ · referent] (turns on: Whether the expression is read through its people or emphasizes piety as the named possessor of the outcome.)
  20:132/q26/p1 Those who fear God and obey, including believers who affirm and follow the Prophet. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās in Wāḥidī, Baghawī, and Ṭabrisī specifies affirmation, following, and fear of God. Wāḥidī's Wajīz and Bursevī name the Prophet and believers. Ṭabarī contrasts them with those who neither fear punishment nor hope for reward; Asad glosses taqwā as God-consciousness.
  20:132/q26/p2 The abstract piety stands for the pious person or people. — reasons: Al-Akhfash gives 'people of piety'; Qushayrī notes naming a person by the verbal noun, and Samīn and al-Ālūsī compare 7:128. Nasafī says two annexed nouns are elided; al-Ālūsī adds that omission highlights piety itself.
20:132/q27 How does the grammar of li-l-taqwā assign the outcome? [لِلتَّقْوَىٰ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the construction is explained through an omitted possessor or the lām's assertion of possession.)
  20:132/q27/p1 An understood 'people of' makes the outcome belong to the pious. — reasons: Al-Akhfash, Qushayrī, Samīn, Nasafī, and al-Ālūsī explain the abstract noun for its bearers; Nasafī posits two omitted annexed nouns, while al-Ālūsī says omitting the noun foregrounds piety. Samīn and al-Ālūsī cite 7:128.
  20:132/q27/p2 The lām marks possession of the good outcome. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says it confirms the intended favorable consequence; the claim is absolute for the Hereafter and possible for worldly good.
20:132/q28 How does the verse connect with the previous warning about worldly adornment? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the link is practical redirection from wealth, honor in the ordered commands, or evidence for revelation timing.)
  20:132/q28/p1 Prayer and divine provision redirect the Prophet and family from others' wealth. — reasons: Al-Wāsiṭī says the warning consoles the poor; Abū Ḥayyān places the family command after timed praise and the warning against unbelievers' wealth. Biqāʿī says zakāt is omitted because the previous verse already discourages attachment. Ibn ʿĀshūr sees ahl answering the preceding mention of spouses, so family finds enjoyment in prayer; Islahi says no patronage from opponents is needed.
  20:132/q28/p2 The prior praise command and this prayer command mark the Prophet's elevated rank. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reads the sequence from glorification to prayer to his direct perseverance as an honor.
  20:132/q28/p3 The sequence dates the verse early in the Meccan period. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī combines earlier daily prayer and patience commands with the warning against unbelievers' goods to infer early Meccan revelation.
20:132/q29 How does the ending connect to the prayer command and other verses? [وَٱلْعَٰقِبَةُ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ · links] (turns on: Which Qurʾānic context is used to explain the final statement.)
  20:132/q29/p1 It completes the prayer counsel by stating that piety yields the good end. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the sentence is coordinated with God's not asking provision and explains persistence in prayer; it forms a universal proverb. Study Quran points to 7:128, 11:49, and 28:83; Yaḥyā cites 43:35.
  20:132/q29/p2 It echoes 22:37: what reaches God is piety, not sacrificial meat or blood. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī makes this connection while stressing God's independence from provision.
20:132/q30 How does this passage continue the earlier call to patience? [* · links] (turns on: The connection can be the continuing patience theme or the earlier command's prayer practice.)
  20:132/q30/p1 The following objection of the opponents continues the call to endure their speech. — reasons: Rāzī links the next account to 20:130, after the counsel on patience and prayer.
  20:132/q30/p2 The family command extends the earlier daily prayer and glorification instruction. — reasons: Islahi identifies the prayer of 20:130 with the vigilant prayer here. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says hardship reports support a broad understanding of that glorification as prayer.
20:132/q31 What event is reported around the revelation of these verses? [* · reports]
  20:132/q31/p1 A Jewish lender demanded collateral when the Prophet sought a loan; the Prophet offered armor and these verses were revealed. — reasons: Abū Rāfiʿ's report in Ṭabarī includes a guest and links 15:87 with 20:131–132; Wāḥidī's Wajīz says the lender's demand saddened the Prophet.
20:132/q32 What is said about the revelation time of this verse? [* · sciences]
  20:132/q32/p1 It is Meccan like the rest of the sūra, probably early Meccan. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī finds no authority excluding it as Medinan; he infers an early date from daily prayers, patience, and the warning against unbelievers' wealth. Ibn Masʿūd calls Sūrat Ṭā Hā one of the earliest old sūras. He notes the tension between this timing and the apparent Medinan setting of door-visit reports.
20:132/q33 What lexical information is supplied about ahl beyond the referent? [أَهْلَكَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the lexical note gives the ordinary and extended sense of ahl or only associated root vocabulary.)
  20:132/q33/p1 Ahl ordinarily denotes blood relatives, with broader figurative uses shown by context. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī defines the ordinary kinship bond and says context permits extension; Islahi argues for the wider Arabic use and cites Abū Bakr's expression and 19:55.
  20:132/q33/p2 The lexical excursus also notes ihāla for melted fat and istaʾhala for eating it. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī records these cognate words without making them the verse's referent.
