53:39/q10 How does the verse connect to its surroundings and earlier scriptures? [* · links] (turns on: Which nearby or intertextual frame explains the personal-accountability statement.)
  53:39/q10/p1 It belongs to the teachings in Abraham's and Moses's scrolls. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj, al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Nasafī, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī and Study Quran connect it to 53:36–37; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī counts seventeen verses. Islāḥī contrasts their teachings and sacrifices with inherited claims.
  53:39/q10/p2 It completes the rule that no soul bears another's burden. — reasons: Al-Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Ālūsī and Ibn ʿĀshūr say another's good cannot simply transfer any more than their sins; Ibn ʿĀshūr says it blocks an inference that only sins are personal. Al-Rāzī links Abraham's failed prayer for his father with both rules; he says the no-burden rule is the most apparent scroll content, though the scrolls hold more.
  53:39/q10/p3 It is compared with verses about varied effort, individual earnings and enduring traces. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī cites 92:4; Elmalılı cites 2:286 and 52:21. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī connects 36:12, 4:9 and 8:37 to traces of acts and descendants; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ uses an echo of this verse.
  53:39/q10/p4 It rebukes claims to ancestral rank without personal effort. — reasons: Islāḥī says people boasting of Abraham or Moses while withholding charity should heed these prophets' teaching.
53:39/q11 What continuing benefit do cited reports describe? [* · reports] (turns on: Which report and kind of continuing credit is cited.)
  53:39/q11/p1 After death, three sources continue: charity, beneficial teaching or practice, and a righteous child's prayer. — reasons: A Prophetic report is cited by al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr and Study Quran; Imam al-Ṣādiq names guided practice. Ibn Kathīr treats these as effects of one's own work, citing 36:12.
  53:39/q11/p2 A sick believer still receives credit for habitual deeds performed while healthy. — reasons: Imam al-Ṣādiq reports the Prophet's account of God's instruction to the recording angel during illness.
  53:39/q11/p3 The Prophet recited these verses, said the return-to-God formula, and became subdued. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās reports the practice.
53:39/q12 Can worship or gifts be performed for the deceased? [* · law] (turns on: Whether the act discharges a duty, gifts reward, or is bodily or financial.)
  53:39/q12/p1 Proxy devotional acts are barred unless specific evidence permits them. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī's school and al-Ṭabrisī name Hajj as an exception. Al-Qurṭubī reports agreement that no one prays for another. Ibn ʿĀshūr says an act cannot discharge another's personal duty, while assuming financial liability for accidental killing is prescribed support.
  53:39/q12/p2 Bodily acts differ from financial acts, and gifting reward is prayer for grace. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān reports the bodily/financial distinction; Ibn al-Humām attributes one to Mālik and al-Shāfiʿī. Al-Khafājī locates dispute in discharge of another's bodily duty during or after life, with or without permission; feeding and asking God to convey reward are different.
  53:39/q12/p3 Both bodily and financial good can benefit the deceased. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya invokes Saʿd's charity for his mother; al-Qurṭubī reports ʿĀʾisha's retreat and manumission for her brother and the Prophet's permission of charity, water being best. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī separates gifting reward from proxy performance.
  53:39/q12/p4 An heir's proxy fasting belonged to early Islam and was abrogated. — reasons: Al-Ṭaḥāwī preserves proxy Hajj but not fasting.
53:39/q13 May pilgrimage be performed for another? [* · law] (turns on: Whether Hajj is delegable and what conditions apply.)
  53:39/q13/p1 Proxy Hajj is allowed for a dead or unable person under varying conditions. — reasons: The Prophet authorizes it for a deceased father and likens a dead sister's vow to paying God's debt. Al-Shāfiʿī permits it; the Ḥanafīs require incapacity lasting to death, with recovery requiring personal Hajj; Aḥmad does not. Ibn Ḥabīb permits a son without bequest and an elderly person's proxy; Ibn al-Qaṣṣār allows it for dead but not incapacitated living persons; Ibn ʿArafa bought it for a ruler under a non-Mālikī school.
  53:39/q13/p2 Mālik generally rejects it, except when a will stipulates it. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī and Ibn ʿĀshūr report the limitation. Ibn al-Faras counts bequeathed obligatory Hajj as the deceased's striving; al-Bājī says Mālik enforced a bequest and required the hired proxy to have done Hajj first.
  53:39/q13/p3 Some authorities dislike or forbid proxy Hajj. — reasons: Ibn al-Jallāb dislikes hiring for an incapable person but does not void the agreement. Some Mālikī books report a different Abū Ḥanīfa position; one Shāfiʿī opinion forbids it citing this verse.
  53:39/q13/p4 The Prophet affirmed a child's Hajj and the mother's separate reward. — reasons: Al-Baghawī and Bursevī transmit the question and answer.
53:39/q14 Does gifted Qur'an recitation or voluntary worship reach the dead? [* · law] (turns on: Whether recitation is transferable and how intention or payment affects it.)
  53:39/q14/p1 Recitation's reward does not reach the deceased. — reasons: Al-Shāfiʿī and followers cite the verse, absence of Prophetic recommendation or Companion precedent, and the rule that worship requires text rather than analogy. Al-Nawawī calls it the well-known Shāfiʿī view; the Itqān author's school agrees. Another Shāfiʿī view excludes voluntary prayer and other voluntary acts.
  53:39/q14/p2 Recitation and voluntary rewards can reach the deceased. — reasons: The other three imams, Aḥmad and some Shāfiʿīs are reported in favor; Ibn al-Faras reports Mālik's school permits it and Ibn ʿĀshūr rejects an unsupported blanket ban attributed to Mālik. Al-Biqāʿī includes recitation among beneficial ties. Ibn ʿĀshūr compares ʿĀʾisha's protective recitation over the Prophet and cites payment for al-Fātiḥa as healing.
  53:39/q14/p3 Ask God after recitation to convey its reward; paid recitation apparently yields none. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī favors supplication after reciting, though some require initial intention. Payment for teaching is lawful. Ibn ʿĀshūr says hired Hajj is possible through its expense, but prayer and fasting apparently cannot be hired.
53:39/q15 How is striving understood on the spiritual path? [* · inward] (turns on: Whether the spiritual fruit is earned, divinely granted, or beyond ordinary striving.)
  53:39/q15/p1 Effort belongs to wayfaring; realization depends on divine carrying or attraction. — reasons: Al-Naṣrābādhī contrasts the paths of wayfaring and Reality, seeing the kingdom and seeing the King, with poems about the rare traveler and the beloved. Bursevī cites Jāmī: years of exertion cannot arrive without divine attraction. He says effort and attraction meet in God and many paths converge.
  53:39/q15/p2 Striving, its visibility, recompense, annihilation, and renewed life mark successive stations. — reasons: Al-Warrāq maps vv. 39–43 to beginner, middle, end, loss of individual will, and second arising. Bursevī adds witnessing, subsistence, bodily nourishment and delayed lawful provision.
  53:39/q15/p3 Pure deeds earn Paradise, while vision and nearness are gifts to the spirit. — reasons: Al-Baqlī says deeds must lack ostentation; Paradise levels are earned, but unveiling, witness and intimacy come by God's grace.
  53:39/q15/p4 Reward and punishment follow settled dispositions; spiritual returns differ. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī says repeated acts shape dispositions, though divine decree governs immediate fortunes; he names bodily resurrection, return to primordial heart and return to gifted Real existence after annihilation, only the first universal.
  53:39/q15/p5 Self-knowledge and restraint of desire safeguard effort. — reasons: Muḥammad ibn ʿĪsā al-Hāshimī says most striving follows desires.
53:39/q16 What rhetorical principles do the cited notes invoke? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether rhetorical force comes from contextual syntax or studied literary arrangement.)
  53:39/q16/p1 Meaning and arrangement, learned through poetry and grammar, ground rhetorical merit. — reasons: Al-Jurjānī says poetry, advanced grammar and taste allow one to understand Qur'anic inimitability; rhetorical causes should be investigated. Al-Jāḥiẓ warns against claiming nothing remains to learn. Tamīm, ʿAdī, Bashshār and al-Buḥturī liken verse making to intellectual craft and measured arrangement, not pleasant sounds or ease of letters. A group dismisses poetry and advanced grammar; al-Jurjānī condemns that stance.
  53:39/q16/p2 Context decides whether near-negation means near failure or no approach to occurrence. — reasons: Al-Jurjānī says 2:71's mā kādū yafʿalū implies eventual action, unlike the poem's lam yarahā wa-lam yakad; the conditional idhā gives past lam yakad future force.
