2:31/q26 What claim is ‘if you are truthful’ testing? [إِن كُنتُمْ صَٰدِقِينَ · interpretation] (turns on: Sources connect it to several distinct prior or implied angelic claims, or simply to accuracy in naming.)
  2:31/q26/p1 Their assumption that God would create none more knowledgeable, honored or excellent than themselves. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan and Qatāda's accounts say they privately boasted of prior worship and precedence; Adam's lesson and their admission correct the assumption.
  2:31/q26/p2 Their forecast that the earthly successor would corrupt and shed blood. — reasons: Reports from Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Masʿūd and Companions connect it to 2:30; inability to know visible names exposes uncertainty about future unseen conduct.
  2:31/q26/p3 Their implicit claim that they deserved vicegerency because of purity, worship or sinlessness. — reasons: Al-Māwardī mentions their own glorification against another creature's disobedience; al-Bayḍāwī and Asad take purity as the implied claim, and Elmalılı makes governance the test.
  2:31/q26/p4 Their ability to give a correct answer about the names, without a specific earlier boast. — reasons: Al-Akhfash calls it exposure of ignorance; al-Naqqāsh says the accuracy condition excludes speculative ijtihād, comparing the mistaken guess in 2:259. Abū Ḥayyān says truthful means correct rather than merely non-lying.
  2:31/q26/p5 Their implied judgment that appointing Adam was unfitting for God's wisdom. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī allows truthfulness of an implication in a question; Ibn ʿĀshūr considers whether the angels' words were an objection or a submissive request for explanation.
2:31/q27 What does Adam's lesson show about knowledge and its possessors? [وَعَلَّمَ ءَادَمَ ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ · interpretation] (turns on: How the lesson establishes Adam's rank and the value of knowledge relative to worship, authority, and mere labels.)
  2:31/q27/p1 Divinely taught knowledge gives Adam public honor, priority over angels and fitness for vicegerency. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī contrasts secret teaching with public display; al-Baghawī uses it for prophetic rank over angels. Al-Rāzī argues knowledge, rather than strength or wealth, explains prostration and leadership. Ibn ʿĀshūr connects naming to practical analysis, justice and human governance. Ibn ʿĀshūr links Adam's demonstrated distinction to his fitness as steward and to what the angels did not know.
  2:31/q27/p2 Knowledge is more exalted than uncomprehending worship and demands humility, practice and fear of God. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī contrasts angelic praise with knowing; al-Rāzī adduces 35:28, 39:9, 58:11, 96:1-5, 20:114 and many reports, distinctions among kinds of scholars, and warnings about hoarding, vanity and knowledge without action. Bursevī traces knowledge to love and obedience.
2:31/q28 Was Adam's knowledge a miracle proving he was already a prophet? [وَعَلَّمَ ءَادَمَ · theology] (turns on: Whether the knowledge was extraordinary proof of commission, and how angels could verify it.)
  2:31/q28/p1 Yes: the extraordinary command of all names was Adam's prophetic miracle. — reasons: Al-Jishumī says God perfected Adam's mind, commissioned him and made the naming a miracle; Muʿtazilites suggest Eve or angels as recipients. Some propose angel groups knew different languages or God gave prior evidence so they could verify Adam.
  2:31/q28/p2 The knowledge does not itself prove prophethood: divine teaching can be ordinary, or the act a saintly wonder or precursor. — reasons: Al-Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān say learning a language when taught is ordinary and ask how ignorant angels verified it; al-Rāzī allows a saintly wonder or pre-prophetic sign.
  2:31/q28/p3 Adam was not yet a prophet because his later lapse preceded election or there was no suitable addressee. — reasons: Opponents cite 2:37, 20:122 and 6:124, the concern about major sin after prophethood, and the lack of a plausible audience; al-Rāzī finds the audience objection weak.
2:31/q29 How could the angels recognize that Adam named things correctly? [أَنۢبِـُٔونِى بِأَسْمَآءِ · interpretation] (turns on: The angels' own partial knowledge, prior divine evidence, information given before Adam, or a miracle.)
  2:31/q29/p1 Different angelic groups knew different names or languages; Adam united the whole. — reasons: Al-Jishumī and al-Rāzī say each group could verify its portion although no group could supply all.
  2:31/q29/p2 God informed the angels beforehand or gave evidence of Adam's truthfulness. — reasons: Al-Jishumī reports advance divine information or a separate miracle; al-Rāzī says prior proof let them trust Adam's recitation.
  2:31/q29/p3 Their verification remains a dilemma if they lacked all mappings, yet knowing them all would erase Adam's advantage. — reasons: Al-Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān frame the dilemma as a challenge to treating naming as a straightforward prophetic miracle.
  2:31/q29/p4 The angels already knew some relevant information through earlier commission. — reasons: Al-Jishumī gives this as a reason they could recognize Adam's answers.
2:31/q30 How does this scene connect to the vicegerency dialogue and surrounding verses? [* · links] (turns on: Explanations differ about narrative chronology and whether the lesson completes the response of 2:30.)
  2:31/q30/p1 Adam's teaching gives the concrete answer to the angels' concern in 2:30. — reasons: Al-Rāzī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ibn Kathīr, al-Ālūsī and others say God moves from general assurance to displayed knowledge. Ibn Kathīr says the teaching is narrated before prostration to answer the question despite later chronology. Ibn ʿĀshūr says naming Adam after the dialogue identifies him as the steward; the next verse moves from a general statement to concise detailed proof of his fitness and of what the angels did not know. He illustrates this general-to-detail movement with al-Nābigha's poem, whose later lines clarify an earlier allusion.
  2:31/q30/p2 The clause coordinates directly with prior divine speech or with an implied act of creating or appointing Adam. — reasons: Al-Samīn gives independent or genitive coordination; Abū Ḥayyān supplies 'He made a vicegerent' and rejects direct coordination with the earlier saying; al-Ālūsī reports both parses.
  2:31/q30/p3 The discourse follows actual creation of Adam and the breathing of spirit. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī infers Adam's presence from the dialogue's opening; Biqāʿī prefers prostration after his demonstrated knowledge, whereas Ibn Kathīr places the teaching after prostration in chronology.
  2:31/q30/p4 The scene's wording prepares subsequent angelic submission and the contrast with Iblis. — reasons: Biqāʿī says demonstrated knowledge precedes their prostration; al-Ḥaralī links their admission with Iblis's refusal and Adam's later repentance.
  2:31/q30/p5 The sura's emphasis on Adam's honor shapes this telling compared with al-Aʿrāf. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī connects al-Baqara's preceding grant of earth and divine knowledge to Adam's fitness; al-Aʿrāf begins at creation and formation as a continuation.
2:31/q31 What inward reading is given to Adam's names? [ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ · inward] (turns on: Whether the names are divine secrets, manifestations or a comprehensive human capacity.)
  2:31/q31/p1 Adam is a comprehensive locus for divine names and the realities of creation, exceeding each angel's limited rank. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports the people of realization: each existent thing points to divine activity without incarnation or union. Ibn ʿArabī compares angels to specialized human faculties and Adam to the synthesis of all divine names.
  2:31/q31/p2 God disclosed a hidden divine name through which Adam knew all names. — reasons: Al-Jurayrī gives this report; al-Qushayrī says Adam was taught divine names privately and then demonstrated creaturely names publicly.
  2:31/q31/p3 Knowledge was held in Adam but he forgot the entrusted covenant when left to himself. — reasons: Al-Sulamī and al-Qurṭubī's Sufi report connects the taught names with forgetting in 20:115.
  2:31/q31/p4 The angels' limited knowledge shows every knowing and happiness is granted by God. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī describes faculties receiving Adam's announcement; al-Ḥaralī says the angels' admission returns knowledge to its source.
2:31/q32 What are words and how do they denote things? [ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ · meaning] (turns on: Conventional signification versus natural correspondence.)
  2:31/q32/p1 Uttered words convey meanings by convention, not by an essential natural link. — reasons: Al-Rāzī defines words as utterances and notes that meanings change across places and times; gesture could establish first conventions. Al-Jurjānī says names identify already known referents, while sentence composition, not isolated words, conveys relations. Al-Zajjājī says names generally suit meanings but proper labels merely identify.
  2:31/q32/p2 Particular sounds have special affinities with their meanings. — reasons: ʿAbbād argues arbitrary assignment lacks a determinant; al-Rāzī counters with examples of undetermined creation times and personal names, while conceding sound imitation in qaṭā, laqlaq, khadm and qadm.
  2:31/q32/p3 A metaphor claims a meaning of its target rather than transferring the word's name. — reasons: Al-Jurjānī says calling a man lion communicates courage by inference, as in metonymy.
