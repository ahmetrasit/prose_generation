[IBNASHUR:1:5/r24] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | rejects | The author rejects Fakhr’s dismissal of reward-and-fear worship as a low, fallen grade: Islam itself teaches it, most believers practice it, and it is the aim of obligation; higher stations are rare and even the devout still hope and fear. | mentions 35:28, 17:57
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r25] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Asked why he exerted himself in worship after God had forgiven his past and future sins, the Prophet replied, ‘Should I not be a grateful servant?’; the author sees this as the highest station without hope of more reward.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r26] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Worship’s purpose is human perfection: God made the world display His attributes, endowed people with spirit and intellect to ascend, guided them through revelation, and prescribed repeated worship to recall judgment, discipline the self, sustain social order, and develop individual and communal virtues. | mentions 51:56
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r27] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · ابن سينا | reports | Ibn Sina explains that human interdependence requires exchange, justice, and a divinely prescribed law; a lawgiver and just recompense require knowledge of God, preserved by repeated worship.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r28] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Worship is driven by reverence, either from love or bare fear; love is more important because it pleases the worshipper, and it entails fearing the beloved’s displeasure.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r29] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · محمود الوراق أو منصور الفقيه | reports | A couplet attributed uncertainly to Mahmud al-Warraq or Mansur al-Faqih argues that claiming to love God while disobeying Him is strange, since a true lover obeys the beloved.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r3] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · حسان بن ثابت | reports | Abu al-Faraj al-Isfahani transmits Hassan ibn Thabit’s account: at al-Nuʿman’s gathering, a man’s rajaz outside the tent prompted al-Nuʿman to recognize al-Nabigha and admit him; no grading is given.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r30] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Qur’an 3:31 links following the Prophet’s law with God’s love, supporting the claim that love of God leads to obedience and the wish to be loved in return. | mentions 3:31
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r31] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · المعري | reports | Al-Maʿarri’s couplet presents Ahriman as evil and darkness, born from a malign thought that crossed Yazdan, the deity of good, unawares.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r32] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Most nations’ worship, including worship prescribed in revealed laws, is founded on love of God; polytheists likewise love their idols as they love God, as 2:165 says. | mentions 2:165
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r33] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Some peoples worshipped from fear alone to avert harm: examples include some devil worshippers and Manichaeans, who worshipped Ahriman as lord of evil and harm.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r34] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Fronting the object in ‘You alone we worship’ gives true restriction: the believers taught this praise worship none but God.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r35] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · ابن الحاجب | reports | Ibn al-Hajib argues that fronting the object conveys emphasis but not restriction, and reads 1:5 as emphasis rather than exclusivity; he cites the wording of 39:2 against relying on 39:66. | mentions 39:66, 39:2
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r36] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | rejects | The author rejects Ibn al-Hajib’s argument from 39:2: expressions cannot be treated as identical across contexts, and the rebuttal is established in works on semantics. | mentions 39:2
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r37] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The clause ‘and You we ask for help’ is coordinated with worship; it is not separated by the repetition common in supplicatory enumeration, emphasizing that both acts matter under this restriction.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r38] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Seeking help is requesting assistance; assistance makes a difficult act easier through tools, bodily participation, guidance or teaching, counsel, or money, as illustrated by al-Hariri’s ‘my character is a fine helper.’
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r39] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · الحريري | reports | Al-Hariri writes in the Maqāma, ‘My character is a fine helper,’ using ʿawn in the ordinary sense of aid.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r4] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · الجمهور من أئمة البلاغة | reports | Most rhetoricians define iltifāt as switching the mode used for the same referent after first expressing it through one of speech, absence, or address.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r40] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Seeking God’s help means exerting one’s own capacity while asking God to facilitate what a person cannot accomplish alone; this is the religious sense of divine assistance.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r41] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · العلماء | reports | Scholars define necessary aid as creating what completes an act: the agent’s capacity, awareness of the act, its material, and its tool; together these are ability or sound means and tools, enabling moral obligation.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r42] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Nonessential aid provides something that eases an act, such as arranging a mount for someone able to walk; together with necessary aid it completes tawfīq, creating capacity and the impulse to obey.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r43] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · الراغب | reports | Al-Raghib calls the second, easing kind of aid tawfīq; the author says this does not conflict with scholars’ definition because it follows the necessary aid that first makes tawfīq possible.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r44] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The omitted object of ‘we seek help’ is deliberately general: it includes important acts, especially receiving religion and overcoming the self’s resistance to virtue, as indicated by the sūrah’s opening and the placement after worship.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r45] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The complement governed by ‘seek help’ is omitted; its normal construction would name the object after ʿalā, and omission makes the request general while reserving it to God out of reverence.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r46] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Knowledge, guidance, revelation, and sciences are also requested aids from God: 90:8–10 points to perception, speech and instruction, and revealed law. | mentions 90:8, 90:9, 90:10
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r47] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Fronting the object in seeking help gives an assertive, rhetorical restriction, since ordinary mutual assistance exists; the restriction means reliance on God for grave matters beyond human help. | mentions 5:2
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r48] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The two restrictions indirectly rebuke polytheists: some worshipped other gods while forgetting God, including Sabaʾ and groups among Persians, Egyptians, and peoples of Sudan.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r49] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Many Arabs combined worship of God with idols or celestial bodies; named tribes worshipped the sun, moon, Sirius, Aldebaran, or Pleiades, treating these beings as intermediaries and thus as sources of help.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r5] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · السكاكي | reports | Al-Sakkaki defines iltifāt as choosing a mode of speech, address, or absence other than the mode most fitting at that point.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r50] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · السلكوتي | reports | Al-Salkuti notes that true restriction cannot directly refute an opposing belief, since its literal meaning is true; the rebuke of polytheists therefore works by implication (taʿrīḍ).
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r51] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · الشيخ الجد | reports | In his note on the Ibn ʿAbbas hadith, the author’s grandfather says its lack of an explicit restrictive form hints that asking belongs only to the all-powerful, all-knowing God; rhetoricians can omit restriction when exclusivity is intrinsic.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r52] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | The Prophet teaches Ibn ʿAbbas: ‘If you ask, ask God; if you seek help, seek God’; the wording has no explicit object-fronting restriction.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r53] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The hadith teaches an already established Muslim, so it can assume exclusivity; this sūrah opens revelation and legislation and rebukes shirk, making explicit restriction apt, while its conditional wording also implies disavowal of divination at idols.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r54] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The plural ‘we’ in worship and seeking help includes the reciter with the community of believers; it displays their collective strength to polytheists and makes their shared praise more eloquent than an individual’s testimony.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r55] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The plural worshippers’ testimony itself praises God: the speaker moving from praise to intimate address takes every chance to show that many recognize the praised one’s merit.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r56] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Al-Nabigha’s elegy lists Ghassan, Turks, and other peoples hoping for al-Nuʿman’s return to imply his greatness and the size of his realm, like the communal praise here.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r57] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Worship precedes seeking help because it draws the servant near God, is the worshipper’s own commitment before a request for aid, is presupposed by divine assistance, and is itself among the acts aided.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r58] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The order also preserves the sūrah’s cadence, whose endings use the same or a nearby consonant of the tongue’s articulation.
[IBNASHUR:1:5/r59] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Repeating ‘You’ before seeking help marks a different restriction from worship: people may assist one another, as 5:2 commands, but only God is relied on for grave matters and counts as true aid. | mentions 5:2
