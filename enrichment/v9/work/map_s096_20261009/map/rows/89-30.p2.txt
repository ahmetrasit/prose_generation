[ALUSI:89:29/r10] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | Another analysis takes nafs as the soul and rabb as its owner, the body: the soul returns to its former body, then enters the servants and the abode of reward.
[ALUSI:89:29/r12] ALUSI, d. 1270 · ابن زيد وجماعة | reports | Ibn Zayd and a group say the address comes at death, with the soul understood as the spirit returning to the sacred realm and then joining the close servants.
[ALUSI:89:29/r15] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | On the death-time reading, the soul is pleased with its bliss and pleasing to God; entry among the close servants follows immediately, while entry into the eternal Garden follows at resurrection, though certainty permits the future event to follow by fa.
[ALUSI:89:29/r16] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | Other proposed sequencing allows both commands without delay: entry among the servants may mean joining righteous souls of one’s kind or their bodies, and Garden entry may be temporary enjoyment before the Hour.
[ALUSI:89:29/r17] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | The temporary-Garden proposal cites a hadith that believers’ souls are in birds’ crops in Paradise and an athar that a believer receives half his promised Garden at death; al-Ālūsī calls the mirror-and-radiance explanation of their gathering rhetorical speculation.
[ALUSI:89:29/r18] ALUSI, d. 1270 · أبو صالح | reports | Abū Ṣāliḥ says the return is at death, meaning departure from worldly life, while the two entry commands are spoken at resurrection.
[ALUSI:89:29/r19] ALUSI, d. 1270 · محمد بن كعب القرظي | reports | Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb al-Quraẓī places the address after death but before resurrection: the believer sees his Garden home, returns to the body, and awaits questioning by Munkar and Nakīr; no grading is stated.
[ALUSI:89:29/r2] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Entering God’s Garden is the bodily happiness promised to the tranquil soul, prepared for such souls.
[ALUSI:89:29/r20] ALUSI, d. 1270 · زيد بن أسلم | reports | Zayd ibn Aslam says the soul is given glad tidings of Paradise at three stages: death, resurrection, and the day of gathering.
[ALUSI:89:29/r21] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | rejects | An inward reading applies the commands throughout earthly life: turn to God in every matter, join the sincere servants through good works, and enter the Garden potentially; al-Ālūsī says this is not a sound interpretation.
[ALUSI:89:29/r22] ALUSI, d. 1270 · ابن عباس | reports | A report through Juwaybir from al-Ḍaḥḥāk identifies the occasion with ʿUthmān’s purchase of Biʾr Rūma for public water; al-Ālūsī treats such identifications as examples, not limits on the verse.
[ALUSI:89:29/r23] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | Other reported occasions identify the tranquil soul with Ḥamza or with Khubayb ibn ʿAdī, whose face was miraculously turned toward the qibla after his captors faced it toward Medina; these too are examples.
[ALUSI:89:29/r24] ALUSI, d. 1270 · ابن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbās is also reported to identify the soul as the Prophet; al-Ālūsī likewise treats this as an illustrative identification rather than restricting the verse’s general meaning.
[ALUSI:89:29/r25] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | Some readings imply that souls existed before bodies in the realm of dominion; al-Ālūsī notes that most theologians, like Plato and his followers, instead hold that souls are created when bodies become ready for them.
[ALUSI:89:29/r27] ALUSI, d. 1270 · السادة الصوفية | reports | Sufi authors see three stations in the passage—tranquil, content, and approved—and explain each in their works.
[ALUSI:89:29/r3] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | The first command signifies spiritual happiness through companionship with the righteous; the second signifies bodily happiness, and the superior spiritual reward is mentioned first, with the Garden completing it.
[ALUSI:89:29/r4] ALUSI, d. 1270 · أبو حيان | reports | Abu Hayyan explains that dukhūl takes fī with a figurative or nonliteral domain, but usually takes a real container directly.
[ALUSI:89:29/r5] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | One reading takes ‘return to your Lord’s promise’ as the promised Garden and company of the righteous, spoken after the reckoning; the objection that this implies prior residence is answered by a potential existence in Adam’s loins while he was in the Garden.
[ALUSI:89:29/r6] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | The proposed prior existence is compared with reports from ʿAlī and Ibn ʿAbbās that maʿād in 28:85 means Paradise rather than Mecca; this works only if Adam’s Garden was the eschatological Garden, a strongly disputed identification discussed by Ibn al-Qayyim. | mentions 28:85
[ALUSI:89:29/r7] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | Another interpretation makes ‘return to your Lord’s command’ mean return to one of His affairs: His treatment of her without distress, placement in an honored station, or something similar.
[ALUSI:89:29/r8] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | A further view understands the return as return to God’s general favor, from the barzakh after death or from the period after resurrection but before judgment.
