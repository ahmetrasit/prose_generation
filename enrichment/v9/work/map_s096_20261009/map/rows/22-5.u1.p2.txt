22:5/q29 Are Qurʾān readers or believers spared this decline? [أَرْذَلِ ٱلْعُمُرِ · interpretation] (turns on: Reports invoking 95:5–6 against Rāzī's reading of that passage.)
  22:5/q29/p1 Qurʾān readers, or people strengthened by faith, may be spared decrepit loss. — reasons: ʿIkrima and Ibn ʿAbbās cite the exception in 95:5–6; ʿAbd al-Malik says reciters retain intellect longest; Study Quran reports a faith version.
  22:5/q29/p2 The exception in 95:5–6 concerns believers' lasting reward, not exemption from senility. — reasons: Rāzī rejects a literal exemption from old age, reading the return to the lowest state as blame or punishment countered by reward.
22:5/q30 What inward meanings are given to bodily decline and earth's revival? [أَرْذَلِ ٱلْعُمُرِ ٱلْأَرْضَ هَامِدَةًۭ · inward] (turns on: Different spiritual analogies.)
  22:5/q30/p1 The worst age images spiritual stagnation, veiling, desire, self-reliance, or estrangement from God. — reasons: Qushayrī records distinct glosses: stagnation after striving, veiling after witnessing, returning to desires, disobedience, abasement, adversaries, ignorance of one's worth, self-reliance, and illusion of independence. Baqlī adds interruption after communion.
  22:5/q30/p2 The lifeless earth images an ignorant soul revived by knowledge and spiritual virtues. — reasons: Wāsiṭī says God revives humans here by knowledge and after death by spirits; Qāshānī interprets water as knowledge descending from the spirit.
  22:5/q30/p3 Created beings emerge from the wombs of nonexistence at a divinely appointed term. — reasons: The Najmī inward reading transfers gestation imagery to all creation; Bursevī connects it to eternal divine will and gradual perfection.
22:5/q31 Why does the verse turn from human development to the earth? [وَتَرَى ٱلْأَرْضَ · links] (turns on: How the second sign relates to the first and why the address shifts.)
  22:5/q31/p1 Reviving visible dead land supplies another analogy for bodily resurrection. — reasons: Qushayrī invokes spring; Ibn ʿĀshūr contrasts unseen origin with visible growth; Abū Ḥayyān says the second proof is directly observable; several link it to the coming Hour in 22:6–7.
  22:5/q31/p2 The singular 'you see' invites any observer to inspect an outward sign. — reasons: Wāḥidī says the shift can mark a separate passage still linked by resurrection; Biqāʿī and Ālūsī address each suitable observer.
22:5/q32 What water is sent down on the earth? [ٱلْمَآءَ · referent] (turns on: Rain alone or rain and other irrigation.)
  22:5/q32/p1 Rain is the immediate referent. — reasons: Ṭabrisī states this; Ālūsī prefers rain because the text says it is sent down; Biqāʿī stresses its descent by divine power.
  22:5/q32/p2 Water also includes rivers, springs, and irrigation. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān broadens the referent; Ālūsī reports that possibility.
22:5/q33 What does hāmidah describe? [هَامِدَةًۭ · meaning] (turns on: Dry barren land, worn-out land, dust, or literally extinguished ash.)
  22:5/q33/p1 Dry, lifeless, barren earth with no vegetation. — reasons: Lexicographers compare an extinguished fire; al-Layth adds hard crust and Shammar settled ash. Ibn ʿAbbās mentions lost moisture; al-Aʿshā's worn-clothes verse attests the root.
  22:5/q33/p2 It means dusty, gray, or crumbling land. — reasons: Qatāda, Ibn Kathīr and al-Itqān specify the earth's dusty or broken appearance.
  22:5/q33/p3 It depicts the earth literally as extinguished ash from an earlier burning state. — reasons: Elmalılı prefers this over summer-dried soil and invokes contemporary theories of earth as hardened deposited ash; Kur'an Yolu reports his claim.
22:5/q34 What moves when the watered earth اهتزت? [ٱهْتَزَّتْ · interpretation] (turns on: The land's soil, its plants by metonymy, or an inverted event order.)
  22:5/q34/p1 The earth's soil stirs, loosens, or rises as vegetation sprouts. — reasons: Muqātil compares serpent motion in 28:31; Qurṭubī explains plant pressure metaphorically; Ibn ʿĀshūr sees upward movement; Elmalılı adds vibrating particles.
  22:5/q34/p2 The plants move or sway; 'its vegetation' is understood. — reasons: Mubarrad supplies the omitted plant noun; Māwardī cites a poem about a leafy branch; Ṭabarī reports this despite the verse grammatically naming earth; Qurṭubī argues the later verb favors earth as subject.
  22:5/q34/p3 The sense is 'swelled, then stirred' despite the verse's order. — reasons: Abū ʿUbayda and Yaḥyā propose semantic inversion; Māwardī says that analysis follows the swelling-as-emergence view.
22:5/q35 What does ربت mean? [رَبَتْ · meaning] (turns on: Increase of earth and vegetation or swelling as plants emerge.)
  22:5/q35/p1 The earth swells, rises, and increases its vegetation. — reasons: The root r-b-w means increase; Qurṭubī unifies these senses, while Ṭabrisī and Ibn ʿĀshūr connect swelling to emerging plants. Qatāda says rain is visible in the swelling.
22:5/q36 How is وربت read? [رَبَتْ · readings] (turns on: Unhamzated increase versus hamzated elevation, with differing judgments on the latter.)
  22:5/q36/p1 The majority read unhamzated rabat from r-b-w, 'increased or swelled.' — reasons: Farrāʾ and Ṭabarī reject the hamzated form as mistaken or ill-fitting; Ibn ʿAṭiyya deems it weak; other sources transmit both.
  22:5/q36/p2 Abū Jaʿfar and others read hamzated rabaʾat, 'rose to prominence.' — reasons: Farrāʾ links it to a lookout but objects; Thaʿlabī cites 41:39; Qurṭubī, al-Samīn, Abū Ḥayyān, and Ibn ʿĀshūr cite the scout word and poetry.
22:5/q37 What does the earth bring forth? [وَأَنۢبَتَتْ مِن كُلِّ زَوْجٍۭ بَهِيجٍۢ · interpretation]
  22:5/q37/p1 Vegetation of many beautiful kinds and colors. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr adds varied tastes, scents, forms, and uses; Biqāʿī says paired kinds complement one another; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the beauty also grants human delight.
22:5/q38 What does zawj mean here? [زَوْجٍۭ · meaning] (turns on: Species, color, or literal paired plants.)
  22:5/q38/p1 Each kind or species of plant. — reasons: Ibn Fāris allows 'variety'; Ibn ʿĀshūr derives this from the basic meaning of a paired male or female member; Biqāʿī describes complementary kinds.
  22:5/q38/p2 Every plant color. — reasons: Māwardī gives green, red, and yellow; Qurṭubī allows color as a gloss.
  22:5/q38/p3 A real pair, as opposed to a single plant. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾ offers this alternate reading and notes experimental confirmation of plant pairing.
22:5/q39 What does bahīj mean? [بَهِيجٍۢ · meaning]
  22:5/q39/p1 Beautiful, fresh, bright, and pleasing to sight or senses. — reasons: Qatāda gives beautiful; Mubarrad connects 27:60; Abū Zayd analyzes the adjective; al-Layth cites meadow blossoms and poetry; Ibn Qutayba notes its participial form.
22:5/q40 How do this verse's signs establish resurrection? [* · theology] (turns on: Initial creation, repeated transformations, revived earth, and further divine attributes.)
  22:5/q40/p1 The One who first creates humans and revives barren earth can restore dead bodies. — reasons: Ṭabarī, Rāzī, Zamakhsharī and many others argue from initial creation to restoration; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says reason establishes possibility and revelation confirms occurrence; Islāḥī adds divine purpose and accountability.
  22:5/q40/p2 The signs also establish God's knowledge, free will, and reality against naturalistic or reductive accounts. — reasons: Rāzī stresses knowledge and power, rejects reducing the resurrected person to a heart constituent; Qurṭubī says angels are only means and God alone creates; Ṭabāṭabāʾ reasons from divine truth to a lasting afterlife.
22:5/q41 What legal consequences can miscarriage or newborn signs of life have? [مُّخَلَّقَةٍۢ غَيْرِ مُخَلَّقَةٍۢ · law] (turns on: Whether form, a recognized clot/morsel, ensoulment, cry, or another sign controls distinct rulings.)
  22:5/q41/p1 A recognized clot or morsel can count as delivery, ending the waiting period and sometimes establishing umm walad status. — reasons: Mālik and Awzāʿī accept an identifiable morsel; Qāḍī Ismāʿīl cites 65:4 and argues pregnancy existed; Qurṭubī prefers ending ʿiddah when a settled clot or morsel is expelled.
  22:5/q41/p2 Visible human formation is required for umm walad status and, in Ibn al-ʿArabī's view, other consequences. — reasons: Shāfiʿī and Abū Ḥanīfa accept even one discernible body part; Qurtubī reports agreement when a child is fully formed. Ibn al-ʿArabī rejects consequences before formation.
  22:5/q41/p3 Prayer for a miscarriage requires live birth evidenced by a cry. — reasons: Qurṭubī reports the agreement on praying for a crying newborn and the view of Mālik, Abū Ḥanīfa, and Shāfiʿī against prayer without a cry.
  22:5/q41/p4 Prayer may be offered for miscarriage without a cry, or after ensoulment. — reasons: Ibn ʿUmar, Ibn al-Musayyib, and Ibn Sīrīn are named; al-Mughīra orders washing, naming and shrouding; Ibn al-ʿArabī restricts his case to a visibly formed fetus.
  22:5/q41/p5 Inheritance and full blood money follow any certain sign of life, including movement or breathing. — reasons: Thawrī, Awzāʿī, and Shāfiʿī accept multiple signs; Khaṭṭābī prefers the jurists' view; Mālik requires a cry, granting only ghurra otherwise.
