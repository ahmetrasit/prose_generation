## THE CURRENT MAP
27:8/q01 What does the pronoun in 'came to it' refer to? [جَآءَهَا · referent] (turns on: The nearest named antecedent is the fire; another reading supplies the tree of the scene.)
  27:8/q01/p1 The fire Moses had seen or thought he saw. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī prefer the earlier explicit fire; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the feminine pronoun follows Moses' description of the appearance as fire.
  27:8/q01/p2 The tree bearing the light. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān reports a green samur or ʿalayq tree which did not burn and seemed to recede; Ālūsī finds this antecedent implausible.
27:8/q02 What was the apparent fire Moses approached? [ٱلنَّارِ · referent] (turns on: Whether nār names Moses' initial impression, a physical veil, or spiritual light.)
  27:8/q02/p1 Divine light rather than ordinary fire. — reasons: Moses mistook light for fire, say Mawardī, the majority he reports, Ibn ʿAbbās and others, and many later sources. Qatāda calls it God's light; Muqātil the Lord's light. Ibn ʿĀshūr says only Moses saw this otherworldly light and his wife may have doubted him. The Study Quran notes Arabic nār can stand for nūr.
  27:8/q02/p2 A real fire, or fire containing light. — reasons: Saʿīd ibn Jubayr lists God's veils of might, dominion, authority, fire, light, cloud and water, identifying this as the fire of the call. Baghawī and Biqāʿī connect it to the prophetic veil report; Mawardī records fire with light.
  27:8/q02/p3 Prophetic spiritual illumination. — reasons: Asad reads it as the light God bestows on prophets and, alternatively, as God's own light at the core of illumination. The Turkish commentary reports his metaphorical reading.
27:8/q03 What happened at the tree when Moses approached? [جَآءَهَا ٱلنَّارِ · reports]
  27:8/q03/p1 A luminous fire or light appeared in a green tree without consuming it and drew Moses near. — reasons: Wahb describes a green ʿalayq branch whose flame grew as its greenery increased; Moses tried to take a branch, recoiled when the flame leaned toward him, then heard the call. Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls the tree samur and says the light seemed to recede. Ibn Kathīr says its light reached heaven; Biqāʿī describes Moses following it into a ravine.
  27:8/q03/p2 Moses' sheep repeatedly fled before he approached God's light. — reasons: Abū Ṣakhr gives the repeated flight of the sheep as the lead up to the encounter.
27:8/q04 Why does this account say jāʾahā rather than atāhā? [جَآءَهَا · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the verbs imply differing difficulty in the narrated settings.)
  27:8/q04/p1 Jāʾa fits the more arduous scene, whereas atā suggests easier arrival. — reasons: Al-Rāghib distinguishes easy atyan from broader majīʾ. Al-Sāmarrāʾī cites derivatives implying ease and 23:27, 50:19, 18:71, 18:74 for harder jāʾa. Naml's firmer promise, shihāb qabas, and mission to Pharaoh's dispersed people make its scene harder than Qaṣaṣ or Ṭā Hā; frequency of the verbs in the sūras also fits.
  27:8/q04/p2 The parallel accounts use different verbs for Moses' arrival. — reasons: Al-Burhān contrasts Ṭā Hā's atāhā; al-Sāmarrāʾī contrasts Qaṣaṣ's atāhā and notes the broader comparison of parallel narratives.
27:8/q05 Who was called, and why is the caller left unnamed here? [نُودِىَ · rhetoric] (turns on: How the passive verb is supplied and whether its lack of an explicit caller serves suspense.)
  27:8/q05/p1 Moses was called by God; the passive first magnifies the call and creates suspense. — reasons: Biqāʿī says the unannounced caller creates suspense and the explanation signals the event's greatness; Iṣlāḥī says the passive magnifies the event. Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī prefer Moses as the passive subject. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says Moses sought directions; Qurṭubī compares the right side of the mountain in 19:52.
  27:8/q05/p2 The passive subject is the phrase an būrika or an implicit 'calling'. — reasons: Zajjāj and Naḥḥās allow the whole phrase to be nominative passive subject; Samīn and Abū Ḥayyān also report an implied call explained by the following words. Al-Farrāʾ makes the case of an depend on whether Moses is supplied.
27:8/q06 From where and in what manner did Moses hear the call? [نُودِىَ · reports] (turns on: The reported source of the sound and the nature of divine speech.)
  27:8/q06/p1 God spoke and Moses heard speech from the direction of the tree, mountain, or fire. — reasons: Wahb says the call came from above; al-Suddī says God's speech came from heaven at the tree. Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Baghawī and Bursevī describe an audible call from the tree or mountain; Ibn ʿAbbās says it came from the light.
  27:8/q06/p2 God created speech in the tree, and Moses heard it from there. — reasons: Al-Naqqāsh and al-Jubbāʾī give created speech in the tree as the explanation; al-Jubbāʾī places the tree in the fire and angels around it.
  27:8/q06/p3 The call came from every direction and reached Moses through all his senses. — reasons: Biqāʿī reports this account without further proof.
  27:8/q06/p4 Moses heard God's letters and sound directly behind the fire's veil. — reasons: Ālūsī says divine wisdom allowed disclosure in the desired fire form while divine might remains unlimited; he rejects the claim that al-Ashʿarī said Moses actually heard inner speech.
  27:8/q06/p5 Divine speech may be heard without ordinary letters or sound, even though the ordinary voice is also a reported account. — reasons: Rāzī reports Sunni explanations: necessary knowledge on hearing an extraordinary attribute or a voice from the tree. Ālūsī cites the sacred hadith and 42:11 for extraordinary hearing; he says al-Māturīdī permits hearing nonsound exceptionally and Ibn al-Humām's impossibility concerns ordinary experience.
27:8/q07 How could Moses know the caller was God? [نُودِىَ · theology] (turns on: Which sign can rule out a deceptive creature as caller.)
  27:8/q07/p1 A miraculous green tree burning without being consumed established the caller's identity. — reasons: Rāzī prefers this accompanying miracle. He rejects proofs from a human's inability to call from fire, since Satan could enter it, and from the voice's magnitude, since angelic or demonic capacities are unknown. The Transoxanian imams raise the epistemic question.
  27:8/q07/p2 The extraordinary character of divine speech gave Moses necessary knowledge. — reasons: Rāzī reports this Sunni explanation: speech unlike letters or sounds is necessarily recognized as God's attribute.
  27:8/q07/p3 No human can call from the fire or tree. — reasons: Rāzī reports and rejects this first Transoxanian proof because Satan could enter either and call.
  27:8/q07/p4 The voice's magnitude proved its divine origin. — reasons: Rāzī rejects this second proof because the capacities of angels and devils are unknown.
27:8/q08 How is an in the call parsed? [أَنۢ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the clause explains a speech act, nominalizes a call with omitted bi, or contracts emphatic anna; the last hinges on the separator rule for supplication.)
  27:8/q08/p1 It is explanatory: the call conveys speech, then its content follows. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī, Rāzī, Nasafī, grammarians, Ibn ʿĀshūr, and Ālūsī use the speech sense in nūdiya; Ālūsī prefers it. Al-Raḍī says this is necessary if būrika is a prayer, but Ālūsī rejects the claimed consensus. Biqāʿī says it directs the listener to the clarification.
  27:8/q08/p2 It is a verbal noun particle, with an omitted preposition after nūdiya. — reasons: Zajjāj and Naḥḥās describe the resulting accusative construction; al-Farrāʾ relates case to whether Moses is implicit. Abū Ḥayyān, Samīn, Bursevī and Ālūsī give the omitted preposition analysis. Al-Kashf objects that an optative verbal noun would not announce good news, an objection Ālūsī questions.
  27:8/q08/p3 It is lightened from anna, with an implied pronoun of the affair. — reasons: Bayḍāwī and Nasafī say prayer permits the absent separator; Abū Ḥayyān replies to Zamakhsharī that qad is not used with a supplication. Samīn notes the difficulty of a prayer as its predicate. Ālūsī reports rule exceptions and disputes al-Raḍī's consensus.
27:8/q09 How is the nūn in an būrika pronounced? [أَنۢ بُورِكَ · readings]
  27:8/q09/p1 Before bāʾ the silent nūn becomes a concealed mīm with nasalization, without assimilation. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī says there is no disagreement here; al-Dānī's reported dispute concerns original silent mīm before bāʾ. The sound matches the hidden mīm in yaʿtaṣim bi-llāh.
27:8/q10 What does būrika mean? [بُورِكَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the verb describes abundant good, sanctity, or voices a prayer of blessing.)
  27:8/q10/p1 To be blessed with established and increasing good. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ defines baraka as growing good established in something; Biqāʿī includes increase, purity and all good. Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls it abundant good; Naṣafī gives goodness and blessing; Ālūsī reads the verb as a blessing predicate.
  27:8/q10/p2 To be sanctified, purified, or made holy. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Suddī give sanctification; Ibn ʿĀshūr links it to the sacred valley and removal of sandals in 20:12. Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān and Study Quran include purity alongside goodness.
  27:8/q10/p3 It is an optative greeting: may the recipient be blessed. — reasons: Bursevī reports al-Kāshifī's gloss and some commentators' greeting interpretation; Biqāʿī gives supplication for the seeker of the fire.
27:8/q11 What Arabic constructions govern the blessing formula? [بُورِكَ · grammar]
  27:8/q11/p1 Bāraka can take a direct object or fī, ʿalā, or li; the passive may omit an understood preposition. — reasons: Ṭabarī cites bārakaka Allāh and bāraka fīka; al-Kisāʾī, al-Farrāʾ, Thaʿlabī, Wāḥidī, Qurṭubī, Ibn al-Jawzī, Samīn and Ibn ʿĀshūr attest variants. Anonymous poems and one by ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr supply examples. Samīn identifies man fī al-nār as būrika's passive subject.
