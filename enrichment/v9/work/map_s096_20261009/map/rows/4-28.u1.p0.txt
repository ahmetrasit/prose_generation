## THE CURRENT MAP
4:28/q01 What does God intend to lighten for people? [يُرِيدُ ٱللَّهُ أَن يُخَفِّفَ عَنكُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the immediate marriage concession exhausts the wording or exemplifies a general principle; other readings identify repentance, temporary marriage, inward relief, or guidance as the intended relief.)
  4:28/q01/p1 He eases religious duties and burdens generally, with marriage to enslaved women among the concessions. — reasons: Mujahid includes every concession (MUJAHID, TAB-FULL, IBNABIHATIM). Ibn Abbas and others compare this community with the Children of Israel (WAHIDI-BASIT, RAZI-FULL); Razi cites 7:157, 2:185, 22:78. Baghawi cites 7:157 and the Prophet's description of an easy religion. Ibn Kathir includes commands, prohibitions and decreed circumstances. Biqai mentions lifted earlier restrictions. Ibn Ashur describes a law that weighs benefit and harm, cites 22:78, 2:185, 7:157 and reports on easy religion, easy instruction to envoys, good news, and the rebuke of prolonged prayer. Study Quran adds eased obligations (8:66), Ramadan nights (2:187), the five prayers after the Night Journey, and earlier communities' self-imposed obligations (7:157, 3:93). Elmalili relates the prayer of 2:286 and 7:157, 2:185, 22:78. Tabatabai cites 2:286 and 22:78. Asad calls guidance a reconciliation of bodily and spiritual impulses.
  4:28/q01/p2 He permits men who cannot afford free wives to marry enslaved women when needed, relieving sexual pressure and avoiding fornication. — reasons: Muqatil and Tabari explicitly cite inability to afford a free wife and fear of fornication. Ibn Zayd says without necessity the original rule remains (TAB-FULL). Ibn Atiyya treats this as the immediate context; Alusi supports it from context. Tabatabai objects that slave marriage already existed before Islam, though disliked; he says the passage instead removes aversion to enslaved women's equal humanity and companionship.
  4:28/q01/p3 He lightens people's burden by accepting repentance and enabling them to repent. — reasons: Tabrisi and Alusi report this as another interpretation of the phrase.
  4:28/q01/p4 He legislates temporary marriage to relieve sexual pressure. — reasons: Tabatabai says it avoids ordinary marriage burdens such as dowry and maintenance, addresses the believing community specifically, and follows the easing of desire through marriage and ownership of female slaves. He argues slave marriage predated Islam and was already available, although disliked.
  4:28/q01/p5 He lightens sins through repeated litanies and their lights entering the heart. — reasons: Qushayri reports this inward explanation.
  4:28/q01/p6 He lightens spiritual struggle through the lights of witnessing entering the heart. — reasons: Qushayri reports this inward explanation.
  4:28/q01/p7 He lightens service through the sweetness of obedience. — reasons: Qushayri reports this inward explanation.
  4:28/q01/p8 He lightens the burden of the trust by carrying it for people. — reasons: Qushayri reports this inward explanation; Sulami reports a gloss of lightening the immense trust people assumed in ignorance.
  4:28/q01/p9 He prescribes servanthood while knowing people's weakness and ignorance. — reasons: Sulami reports this gloss.
  4:28/q01/p10 He gives guidance that reconciles bodily and spiritual impulses so both elements of human nature can flourish. — reasons: Asad describes this as the way God lightens the human burden.
4:28/q02 What does takhfīf mean here? [يُخَفِّفَ · meaning]
  4:28/q02/p1 It means making a burden easier or gentler, including reducing or removing a legal duty. — reasons: Damghani gives ease or gentleness; Jishumi compares easing to reducing weight; Ibn al-Jawzi specifies easing or removing part of a duty.
4:28/q03 What omitted object is understood after 'lighten'? [يُخَفِّفَ عَنكُمْ · grammar] (turns on: Which unspoken thing the transitive verb 'lighten' governs.)
  4:28/q03/p1 The omitted object is a burden or duty, including concessions such as slave marriage and removal of older restrictions. — reasons: Abu Hayyan lists concessions and prior burdens among proposed supplements; Samīn supplies a burden of reasoning and dispelling confusion. Burhan illustrates the clause as 'lighten [the burden] for you.'
  4:28/q03/p2 The omitted object is clarified rules. — reasons: Abu Hayyan lists this among the proposed senses.
  4:28/q03/p3 The omitted object is reward for obligations. — reasons: Abu Hayyan lists this among the proposed senses.
  4:28/q03/p4 The omitted object is sin, including what people commit ignorantly. — reasons: Abu Hayyan mentions pardon of ignorant sins; Samīn reports the sin of what people commit.
4:28/q04 What syntactic role does 'an yukhaffifa' have after 'God wants'? [يُرِيدُ ٱللَّهُ أَن يُخَفِّفَ · grammar]
  4:28/q04/p1 The an-clause is the accusative object or complement of yurīdu. — reasons: Qurtubi calls it an accusative complement; Burhan says an with its clause occupies the object position.
4:28/q05 How does 'God wants to lighten for you' connect syntactically with the preceding clause? [يُرِيدُ ٱللَّهُ أَن يُخَفِّفَ عَنكُمْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether a circumstantial clause can attach across the intervening coordinated sentence with an overt divine name rather than a pronoun.)
  4:28/q05/p1 It is an independent sentence with no syntactic role in the preceding sentence. — reasons: Abu Hayyan parallels the promise that God wants ease. He and Samīn object that a coordinated sentence intervenes before the proposed circumstantial governor and an overt noun replaces the pronoun expected in such a clause. Alusi likewise calls it independent.
  4:28/q05/p2 It is circumstantial under the earlier 'God wants to turn to you.' — reasons: Samīn reports this alternative; Abu Hayyan and Samīn challenge the separation by an unrelated coordinated sentence and the overt subject in place of a pronoun.
4:28/q06 What is meant by 'the human' in the statement of weakness? [ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ · referent] (turns on: Whether the marriage context limits the generic word 'human' or supplies one example of it.)
  4:28/q06/p1 Humanity generally is meant, although weakness concerning women is one included instance. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya extends the immediate marriage case to general human susceptibility; Ibn Ashur calls the phrase a generalization, includes Tawus's specific example, and says it does not exhaust the verse. Kuran Yolu describes vulnerabilities alongside will and power as part of humanity's test.
  4:28/q06/p2 The relevant human weakness is especially that of men in relation to women and marriage. — reasons: Tawus and other commentators focus on the immediate marriage ruling; Ibn Ashur accepts this as an instance but says it is not exhaustive.
4:28/q07 What weakness was the human created with? [وَخُلِقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ ضَعِيفًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether weakness is chiefly sexual impatience, wider moral vulnerability, material origin or bodily frailty, dependence, impaired judgment, or an inward capacity.)
  4:28/q07/p1 People have little patience to resist sexual desire, especially for women. — reasons: Muqatil and Tabari connect this with the slave-marriage concession preventing fornication; Salama singles out intercourse itself as the weakest moment, and Waki says reason departs around women. Tawus says no sphere makes a person weaker. Saʿid ibn al-Musayyib fears temptation even at eighty with failing sight, and ʿUbada fears Satan if alone with an unlawful woman despite wealth and frailty (Thalabi, Qurtubi); Alusi reports a saying that even a noble man is overcome by women. Tabatabai describes implanted sexual powers continually pressing toward their objects; Kuran Yolu treats sexual desire as a vulnerability directed into lawful satisfaction.
  4:28/q07/p2 People are broadly vulnerable to desire, appetite, passion, anger, fear and grief, and struggle with demanding obedience. — reasons: Zajjaj, Ibn Kaysan, Nahhas and Qurtubi mention desire and appetite, with Ibn Kaysan adding fear and grief; Kashshaf and Nasafi add hardship of worship. Al-Qadi (in Razi) stresses strong motives toward pleasure rather than weak bodily capacity; Razi accepts that account while disputing its Muʿtazilite implication. Alusi reports these variants; Bursevi adds unreliable control of appetites and powers. Kuran Yolu adds love of wealth, warning of its corruption while encouraging lawful earning.
  4:28/q07/p3 Human weakness lies in being created from lowly fluid or another frail beginning. — reasons: Al-Hasan cites 30:54; Ibn al-Jawzi also identifies daʿf in 30:54 as semen. Raghib relates weakness to humanity's beginning and end. Alusi rejects the weak-substance explanation as poorly fitted to the marriage context.
  4:28/q07/p4 People are weak in judgment and reason unless God grants certainty and light. — reasons: Sulami says strength comes through certainty, not the self; Alusi reports that divine light alone discloses hidden wisdom, but calls this poorly supported by the marriage context.
  4:28/q07/p5 Weakness means poverty, need and dependence on God and one another. — reasons: Qushayri treats need as an excuse. Biqai says people cannot abstain from marriage and other appetites or act without divine support. Raghib relates weakness to comparison with the celestial realm, the human apart from God's aid, or mutual dependence; he balances this with human intellect, vicegerency, nearness to God, and God's favor in 17:70.
  4:28/q07/p6 It is physical frailty, shown by pain from even a small event. — reasons: Alusi reports this from al-Hasan but rejects it as ill suited to the marriage context.
  4:28/q07/p7 People cannot endure a moment's separation from God; this weakness reflects divine and human mutual love. — reasons: Bursevi praises this spiritual weakness as a mark peculiar to humans.
