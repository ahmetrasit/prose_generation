44:49/q10 What does the taunt teach about power, disbelief and punishment? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The explanations emphasize failed worldly strength, resurrection denial, just reversal, or an inward account of pain formerly hidden by heedlessness.)
  44:49/q10/p1 Worldly power, self-exaltation and supposed invulnerability collapse under God's punishment. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says the sinner trusted power apart from God and must see what became of him. Al-Biqāʿī contrasts the boast with the warnings he denied; Bursevī says Abū Jahl's denial of divine power amid an oath by God shows unbelief's confusion and ego, like demanding stones from heaven. Kur'an Yolu says worldly power left such people helpless. The Study Quran links punishment to disbelief and actions; Ṭabāṭabāʾī notes their mistaken expectation of honor and a good outcome with God (41:50).
  44:49/q10/p2 Arrogant denial or doubt of the Hereafter and accountability brings the torment they questioned. — reasons: Asad connects the taunt with disbelief in life after death and accountability, citing 96:6. Iṣlāḥī says disbelief in resurrection caused Quraysh's rebellion and heedlessness, and that the sinners taste the torment they doubted. The Study Quran notes parallel chastisements for denial in 32:20, 34:42, 52:13–16 and 83:17; on the fatḥa reading it links doubt causally to haughtiness.
  44:49/q10/p3 The scene displays just recompense and the reversal of the sinner's former condition. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says the passage uses intellectual and historical evidence for reward and punishment and depicts different fates for believers and disbelievers with complete justice. Al-Qāshānī reads a corrupted primordial nature as reversing bodily pleasure, might and psychic dignity into spiritual pain, humiliation and abasement.
  44:49/q10/p4 Worldly heedlessness prevented him from sensing pain already caused by his injustice. — reasons: Bursevī says death removes the veil and reveals punishment that was present but unfelt in life.
44:49/q11 How does this scene connect with the surrounding account of judgment? [* · links]
  44:49/q11/p1 It follows arguments for judgment and depicts the disbelievers' fate alongside the believers' different fate. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says the section first gives intellectual and natural arguments for reward and punishment, identifies Quraysh's denial of the afterlife as the root of rebellion, then portrays each group's fate under complete justice. The Study Quran compares other depictions of the damned being told to taste punishment (32:20, 34:42, 52:13–16, 83:17).
