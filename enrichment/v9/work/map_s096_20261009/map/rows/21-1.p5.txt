[TABATABAI:21:1-15#2/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Analyzed by its purpose of revealing hidden mental meanings, speech can be treated as real; this approach also applies to conventional ideas such as prostration, obedience, sovereignty, and the Book.
[TABATABAI:21:1-15#2/r2] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Speech is what reveals what is hidden in the mind; every effect reveals its cause's latent perfection, and essential attributes reveal an object's hidden essence.
[TABATABAI:21:1-15#2/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · الفلاسفة | reports | Philosophers call God's essential attributes—knowledge, power, and life—His speech, and also call the world His speech because it reveals Him.
[TABATABAI:21:1-15#2/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Under this analysis, speech's temporal status follows its mode of being: divine knowledge is eternal, created Zayd is an originated sign, and revelation is originated as imparted but eternal as divine knowledge.
[TABATABAI:21:1-15#2/r5] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The recited Qur'an as conventional, meaningful verses is neither eternal nor originated in itself, though its sounds originate; its true meanings as known by God are eternal with His knowledge.
[TABATABAI:21:1-15#2/r6] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | rejects | The created-versus-eternal debate is unproductive: eternal recited sounds are impossible, while eternal divine knowledge needs no separate Qur'an attribute beyond knowledge.
[TABATABAI:21:1-15#2/r7] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | rejects | If every analysis that reduces to a divine attribute counted as a separate essential attribute, the list would be limitless; scripture's use of “speech” refers plainly to the first, recited sense.
[TABATABAI:21:1-15/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Successive reciters produce distinct sound tokens resembling the original utterance; they are not numerically the same sounds except by convention and loose usage.
[TABATABAI:21:1-15/r2] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Sounds count as speech only when conventionally made signs for mental meanings; they count as one utterance only by treating their intended purpose as one.
[TABATABAI:21:1-15/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Speech qua speech is a conventional construct without external existence; only the sounds exist, and calling the construct actual is metaphorical.
[TABATABAI:21:1-15/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Because speech as a convention is not an external reality, it has neither temporal origination nor endurance nor eternity, which apply to externally existing things.
[TABATABAI:21:1-15/r5] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Eternal speech made of ordered letters is impossible: succession entails that a later letter follows and is preceded, contradicting eternity.
[TABATABAI:21:1-15/r6] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Speech in its proper conventional sense is neither a real action nor an essential attribute of the speaker; only its external sounds are acts, and “speaking” is attributed by extension.
[TABATABAI:21:1-15/r7] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The terms speaking and causing speech are extended from the speaker's production of sounds, as with conventional relations such as reverence, insult, buying, and selling.
[TABATABAI:21:1/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The sūra treats prophethood on the foundations of monotheism and resurrection: it opens with approaching judgment and heedlessness, then answers mockery through earlier prophets, their stories, judgment, and the victory of the pious.
[TABATABAI:21:1/r10] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | If the passage aimed to describe the Hour's terrors and punishment, it would more fittingly say “Day of Reckoning” or explicitly refer to its time.
[TABATABAI:21:1/r2] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The sūra moves from accusations that the Prophet is a magician, dreamer, fabricator, or poet to the shared qualities and message of earlier prophets, then to judgment and the pious inheriting the earth.
[TABATABAI:21:1/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Its principal emphasis is warning and threat rather than promise and glad tidings; its Meccan origin is undisputed and confirmed by its flow.
[TABATABAI:21:1/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Iqtaraba and qaruba both mean “draw near,” but the expanded form is stronger and conveys greater attention to the nearness.
[TABATABAI:21:1/r5] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Qaruba and iqtaraba take min or ilā; min measures nearness from the object, while ilā measures it from the subject, as min marks origin and ilā endpoint.
[TABATABAI:21:1/r6] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The lām means “toward” (ilā), not “from” (min): the verse pictures the reckoning itself approaching people who are heedless.
[TABATABAI:21:1/r7] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | prefers | The reckoning is God's actual accounting of people's deeds on Judgment Day, not a metonym for its time or an omitted word meaning “time of reckoning.”
[TABATABAI:21:1/r8] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · بعضهم | reports | Some insist that the verse means the time of reckoning; another explanation says nearness properly belongs to time and only indirectly to events occurring in it, which the author rejects here.
[TABATABAI:21:1/r9] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The warning recalls accountability for deeds: wisdom requires a divine reminder, and people must heed it seriously rather than play or let their hearts be distracted.

[ASAD-NOTES:21:intro/r1] ASAD-NOTES, d. 1412 · author | holds | According to the Itqan, this Meccan surah belongs to the final group of Meccan revelations; its title reflects its repeated references to earlier prophets.
