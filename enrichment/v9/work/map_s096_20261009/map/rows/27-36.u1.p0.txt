## THE CURRENT MAP
27:36/q01 Who or what came to Solomon? [جَآءَ سُلَيْمَٰنَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the omitted subject is supplied from the messenger, the sent object, or the queen's action; the following return command and the earlier sending supply contextual evidence.)
  27:36/q01/p1 The queen's messenger or delegation came bearing her gift. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ, al-Ṭabarī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ibn ʿĀshūr and Iṣlāḥī explain that a grammatically singular messenger can represent a group. Al-Zajjāj, al-Wāḥidī and al-Ālūsī cite the following singular command to return; al-Nasafī and al-Itqān name the envoy Mundhir. Ibn ʿĀshūr notes that a royal gift would travel in a caravan.
  27:36/q01/p2 The queen's gift or wealth itself came to Solomon. — reasons: Yazīd ibn Rūmān says the gifts arrived. Al-Zajjāj, al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī allow the gift as the implicit subject; al-Ālūsī prefers understanding that she sent it and it arrived.
  27:36/q01/p3 The queen's act of sending is the implied subject. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī mentions this alternative but prefers that the gift reached Solomon.
27:36/q02 Why is 'came' singular, and what plural form is reported? [جَآءَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether agreement follows a singular collective messenger or expresses the multiple envoys explicitly.)
  27:36/q02/p1 The singular agrees with a messenger, even if he represents a delegation. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ says a single envoy, reportedly a woman, can be described by the queen's plural; al-Ṭabarī says Arabic can use plural for an unspecified individual; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān explain that rasūl spans singular and plural and gender. Al-Shanqīṭī is not cited; Iṣlāḥī takes the delegation as a whole. Al-Kisāʾī treats the agent as recoverable from context; al-Zarkashī says it is implicit, not grammatically deleted.
  27:36/q02/p2 A reported reading has 'they came,' agreeing explicitly with the messengers. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ and al-Ṭabarī attribute jāʾū to ʿAbd Allāh; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Samīn also report plural fa-irjiʿū. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī mention the variant.
27:36/q03 When did Solomon speak these words? [فَلَمَّا جَآءَ قَالَ · reports] (turns on: Whether the narrative sequence is immediate or compresses the intervening embassy tests.)
  27:36/q03/p1 He spoke when the envoys arrived. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī prefers the apparent sequence and suggests eagerness to guide them. Al-Elmalılı summarizes immediate refusal.
  27:36/q03/p2 He spoke after the presentation, puzzles, and tests. — reasons: Bursevī says the surface sequence compresses events; al-Ālūsī reports an alternative based on Wahb and other story accounts.
27:36/q04 Whom does Solomon's plural address include? [أَتُمِدُّونَنِ أَنتُم · referent] (turns on: Whether the sender is included in speech to her representatives; al-Ālūsī cites the later singular command, while al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites who sent the gift.)
  27:36/q04/p1 It addresses the envoys present and, through them, the queen and her people. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Biqāʿī allow the sender to stand within the address; Bursevī uses masculine plural for present envoy over absent queen. Al-Ālūsī prefers extending the rebuke to the sender in light of the singular return command. Ibn ʿĀshūr says an envoy transmits speech to the sender. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī argues the envoys did not themselves send the gift.
  27:36/q04/p2 It addresses the envoy and companions or delegation present. — reasons: Al-Nasafī says the address is to the envoys; al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī allow an envoy and party. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī objects that the envoys did not send the gift.
27:36/q05 What does 'are you supplying me with wealth?' mean? [أَتُمِدُّونَنِ بِمَالٍۢ · interpretation] (turns on: The surface question states financial support; the bribery reading uses the preceding demand for submission and the following threat to explain its purpose.)
  27:36/q05/p1 Solomon reproaches their attempt to add wealth to his resources, since he needs none of it. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ's imdād is additional supply; Abū Zayd distinguishes supplying people from other senses of extension; Bursevī says the verb takes its second object with bi. Al-Māwardī, al-Ṭabrisī and al-Qurṭubī point to Solomon's visible riches. Al-Rāzī, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn stress the interrogative's rejection and disdain.
  27:36/q05/p2 The gift is an attempted bribe to make Solomon tolerate the queen's rule and unbelief. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr says Solomon wanted Islam or force, not treasures; Ibn ʿĀshūr ties the bribe to the queen's unanswered demand for submission in 27:31. Iṣlāḥī says accepting it would concede rule to disbelief. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī connects the subsequent threat to their refusal.
27:36/q06 How is أَتُمِدُّونَنِ read and formed? [أَتُمِدُّونَنِ · readings] (turns on: Whether the two underlying nūns remain separate, assimilate, or one drops, and whether the first-person yāʾ is pronounced in connection and pause. Codex spelling and Arabic contraction are cited.)
  27:36/q06/p1 The two nūns assimilate into one doubled nūn, generally with yāʾ. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās allows the resulting consonant cluster; al-Fārisī identifies indicative and protective nūns; Ibn al-Jazarī says all codices still write two and assimilators lengthen the preceding alif and wāw. Sources vary on whether Ḥamza pauses with yāʾ; al-Samīn reports pause omission, while Ibn al-Jazarī reports retention.
  27:36/q06/p2 Two distinct nūns are read without yāʾ in connection or pause. — reasons: ʿĀṣim, Ibn ʿĀmir and al-Kisāʾī are repeatedly named. Al-Farrāʾ prefers the consonantal text where Arabic usage supports it; al-Jishumī says the kasra indicates the omitted yāʾ. Abū ʿUbayd prefers the two-nūn spelling of all codices, regardless of yāʾ.
  27:36/q06/p3 Two distinct nūns are read, with yāʾ in both connection and pause. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr and some Makkan/Sahl traditions retain yāʾ throughout; the two nūns are the indicative and protective nūns. Al-Fārisī says the protective nūn need not assimilate. Readings accounts vary in naming transmitters.
  27:36/q06/p4 Two distinct nūns are read, with yāʾ in connection but omitted at pause. — reasons: Basran and Medinan traditions, including Nāfiʿ and Abū ʿAmr, use this form. Al-Fārisī explains retention in connected speech because it is not a pause; al-Farrāʾ notes that the written text can omit a pronounced yāʾ.
  27:36/q06/p5 One light nūn is read with yāʾ, in both connection and pause. — reasons: Al-Musayyibī transmits this from Nāfiʿ. Al-Fārisī and al-Wāḥidī take it as loss of the protective nūn; Ibn al-Anbārī gives contraction parallels and says yāʾ is kept at pause, while al-Ālūsī also records an alternative analysis deleting the first nūn.
27:36/q07 What was the queen's gift? [بِمَالٍۢ بِهَدِيَّتِكُمْ · reports] (turns on: The reports list different contents; al-Zajjāj's inference from māl favors monetary value.)
  27:36/q07/p1 It included money, gold, or silver. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj infers money from Solomon's word māl; Ibn ʿĀshūr says gold and wealth seem likely. The paving accounts describe the envoys' precious bricks.
  27:36/q07/p2 It included a box and puzzle objects: pearl, bead, staff, and a vessel. — reasons: Gabriel revealed a sealed box containing an unpierced pearl and crooked-holed carnelian bead, according to al-Thaʿlabī. Al-Māwardī adds the Himyarite royal staff and the request for water from neither sky nor earth. Al-Ālūsī recalls these reports.
  27:36/q07/p3 It included male and female attendants, horses, and other worldly goods. — reasons: Wahb ibn Munabbih, Yazīd ibn Rūmān and Ibn Lahiʿa specify attendants, Arabian or fine-bred horses; Ibn Lahiʿa gives one hundred horses. Ibn ʿAbbās says the attendants' sex was disguised.
27:36/q08 Why is the offered wealth called 'māl' without specification? [بِمَالٍۢ · rhetoric]
  27:36/q08/p1 Its indefinite form belittles the offer against Solomon's God-given resources. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī explicitly draw this from the indefinite; Bursevī adds that māl is passing acquisition, also called ʿaraḍ, citing al-Mufradāt's saying that wealth moves from house to house.
27:36/q09 What Arabic sound rules bear on the alif of māl? [بِمَالٍۢ · readings] (turns on: Sībawayh's examples weigh kasra, yāʾ, emphatic articulation, distance, case ending and pause; these are general phonetic analogies, not a reported variant of this verse.)
  27:36/q09/p1 The alif can incline toward imāla where an underlying yāʾ or kasra favors it. — reasons: Sībawayh lists māl with nāb and bāʿ as alifs standing where yāʾ could stand; some imale māl in genitive or all cases by analogy with Mālik. Stable forms like ʿimād and ʿābid differ from case-varying māl. Rāʾ with kasra after an alif can favor imāla even against an earlier emphatic; imāla of kāfir is more common than qādir, which Hudbah's verse attests. He notes variation in kāfir, manābir, fār, ḥimār, dinānīr, ʿimrān, ʿafran, and related forms.
  27:36/q09/p2 Emphatic consonants, proximity, word form, and pause can block or weaken imāla. — reasons: Sībawayh says seven emphatics raise the tongue and block imāla adjacent to or after alif, even across intervening letters; ṣaqt for sabaqt illustrates articulatory accommodation. Earlier kasra can counter a more distant emphatic, with qifāf versus miṣbāḥ and rare ʿirqan/ḍīqan analogies. Particles usually resist imāla unless named; ba, ta and vocative yā are special. Geminate fāʿil, mafāʿil and hollow forms resist it. Rāʾ acts like a doubled sound and blocks or permits inclination according to case, distance and neighboring qāf; at pause the lost kasra no longer inclines dāʿ. Fatḥ is favored in fāriq and firāsh.
