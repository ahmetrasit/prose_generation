## THE CURRENT MAP
44:49/q01 What does the command to taste convey? [ذُقْ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the command is explained by its object, its humiliating force, or Bursevī's account of newly perceived prior pain.)
  44:49/q01/p1 Experience the punishment of Hell. — reasons: Muqātil calls it torment; al-Baghawī, Ibn al-Jawzī, Bursevī and the Study Quran say punishment. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls tasting a metaphor for experiencing; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says it conveys full perception of pain and pleasure. The Study Quran links other punishment-as-tasting verses (3:106, 3:181, 4:56, 6:30, 7:39, 8:35, 8:50, 10:52, 22:22, 32:14, 32:20, 34:42, 46:34). Ṭabrisī reports Muqātil's scene: the keeper lifts the sinner over the fire, his head parts from his brain, and extremely hot water is poured in; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the address occurs amid successive punishments. Iṣlāḥī says they taste the torment they had questioned.
  44:49/q01/p2 The imperative insults, threatens, and humiliates the condemned. — reasons: Muqātil calls it mockery and humiliation; Bursevī says the punishment is degrading; Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies a humiliating imperative. Al-Burhān calls it denial and rebuke, and al-Biqāʿī says it recalls the messengers' worldly warning of the sinner's lowliness. Al-Itqān lists threat, insult, humiliation through transformation, and challenge exposing inability among imperative functions; it also uses this verse as sarcastic address.
  44:49/q01/p3 He begins to feel the pain of injustice that he failed to sense during life. — reasons: Bursevī says heedlessness and veiling numbed him to punishment already present in this world; death awakens perception of it.
44:49/q02 Who speaks the taunt? [ذُقْ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْكَرِيمُ · referent] (turns on: Whether the utterance has an identified divine or angelic speaker, is implicit quoted speech, or is the situation expressed as speech.)
  44:49/q02/p1 An angel or the guardians of Hell speak it. — reasons: Muqātil names the keeper; al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, Iṣlāḥī and the Study Quran identify an angel or wardens. The Study Quran says they execute God's command.
  44:49/q02/p2 God directly addresses the disbelievers. — reasons: The Study Quran reports this interpretation.
  44:49/q02/p3 The situation and punishment themselves express the taunt, with no identified speaker. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī reports this reading as an alternative to speech by the wardens.
  44:49/q02/p4 It is a quoted utterance introduced by an omitted verb of saying, with the speaker left implicit. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr supplies an ellipsis such as 'say to him' or 'it is said to him'.
44:49/q03 How is إِنَّكَ read and construed? [إِنَّكَ · readings] (turns on: The vowel on the hamza determines an independent assertion and pause or a subordinate causal construction; commentators note their meanings can converge.)
  44:49/q03/p1 Most readers use kasra, innaka, as a new independent clause: 'indeed you are'. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj, al-Fārisī, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Samīn, Ibn al-Jazarī's Nashr and Ibn ʿĀshūr contrast it with al-Kisāʾī's fatḥa. Al-Naḥḥās says the clause begins a sentence and prefers the supported majority reading; al-Qurṭubī says one pauses after 'taste'. Al-Fārisī and Abū ʿAlī (via Ibn al-Jawzī) say the independent assertion echoes Abū Jahl's own claim; al-Samīn says it still conveys a cause. The Study Quran renders it 'surely you'.
  44:49/q03/p2 Al-Kisāʾī reads fatḥa, annaka, making the clause causal: 'because you are'; other witnesses also transmit it. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj, al-Fārisī, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Samīn, al-Nashr, al-Biqāʿī, al-Ālūsī, Ibn ʿĀshūr and Qiraat-ER note the variant. Al-Qurṭubī reports al-Ḥasan from ʿAlī; al-Ālūsī names al-Ḥasan ibn ʿAlī. Al-Fārisī expands it as 'taste because you are'; al-Bayḍāwī also allows attachment to an omitted 'punishment'; Ibn ʿĀshūr supplies an implied lām of purpose, and Qiraat-ER notes a subordinating or object-like link. Al-Qurṭubī says no pause after 'taste'. Al-Biqāʿī says speech and enacted punishment meet the sinner's former self-praise; the Study Quran links doubt to haughtiness. Al-Naḥḥās objects that this boast is less than the disbelief the construction would imply.
44:49/q04 What does the detached pronoun أَنتَ do? [أَنتَ · grammar]
  44:49/q04/p1 It singles out and emphatically reinforces the addressee. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says it isolates the supposedly mighty sinner against those who exposed his worthlessness. Ibn ʿĀshūr says a detached nominative emphasizes the attached pronoun in innaka and that such emphasis requires this nominative form; he says the emphasis heightens sarcasm.
44:49/q05 What does ٱلْعَزِيزُ denote? [ٱلْعَزِيزُ · meaning]
  44:49/q05/p1 A mighty, powerful, protected person who prevails. — reasons: Muqātil and al-Dāmghānī give 'impregnable' or mighty; al-Dāmghānī cites parallel descriptions of strength in 4:158, 48:7, 63:8, 4:139 and 35:10. Al-Biqāʿī specifies one who overcomes and is never overcome. The Abu Jahl reports frame it as power and protection in Mecca's valley.
44:49/q06 What does ٱلْكَرِيمُ denote here? [ٱلْكَرِيمُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether karīm describes the usual honorable trait or the condemned man's inflated self-description.)
  44:49/q06/p1 Noble or generous, with dignity and proud self-regard. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī combines generosity, dignity of soul and proud refusal. The reports of Abū Jahl's boast use nobility, honor or generosity as near wordings; al-Wāḥidī al-Wajīz has 'generous'.
  44:49/q06/p2 Self-honoring, arrogant or noble only in one's own estimation. — reasons: Muqātil gives conceited or self-honoring; al-Dāmghānī says it is the condemned man's own claim. Ibn al-Jawzī's Nuzha reports an exegetical sense 'arrogant' or 'self-important', used mockingly; Qatāda and Ibn ʿAbbās give 'in his own eyes'.
44:49/q07 Why is the condemned called 'the mighty, the noble'? [ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْكَرِيمُ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the titles repeat the sinner's self-praise, invert their surface meaning, or acknowledge real but useless social status.)
  44:49/q07/p1 The titles throw his former boast or self-image back at him in scorn. — reasons: Muqātil, al-Majāz, Qatāda and many later commentators say Abū Jahl styled himself the mightiest and noblest in Mecca; the keepers repeat his claim. Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatāda and Abū ʿAlī say the titles are true only in his own eyes or claim. Al-Fārisī and al-Wāḥidī cite Zahrat al-Yaman's self-praise and Jarīr's retort; al-Fārisī and al-Wāḥidī compare the alleged partners in 28:62. Ṣāḥib al-Naẓm compares the unbelievers' mocking address in 15:6. Al-Samīn also cites Jarīr. Al-Biqāʿī says repeating what the sinner and followers believed compounds the rebuke; al-Burhān calls it taḥakkum and speech framed by the addressee's belief. Ṭabāṭabāʾī recalls his expected good standing with God (41:50); the Study Quran says the speech ridicules worldly self-image.
  44:49/q07/p2 The apparent praise is sarcastic reversal: he is now lowly, weak and humiliated. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba records the opposite-sense gloss; Ibn Abī Ḥātim transmits Ibn ʿAbbās's 'neither mighty nor noble'; Qatāda and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr similarly negate the titles and stress insult. Al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabrisī, al-Burhān, Ibn ʿĀshūr and others call it ironic antithesis or opposite-sense metaphor. Al-Qurṭubī compares the ironic 'forbearing, right-minded' in 11:87; al-Itqān compares opposite-sense glad tidings and similar taunts, while al-Sāmarrāʾī cites 'glad tidings' of punishment in 4:138. Al-Burhān and al-Sāmarrāʾī say the surrounding punishment disambiguates praise as condemnation. Al-Biqāʿī points to the sinner's present ruin; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the mockery adds to punishment.
  44:49/q07/p3 He really had worldly standing among his people or family, but it cannot help him now. — reasons: Ibn ʿĪsā, al-Ṭabrisī, al-Māwardī as cited by Ibn al-Jawzī, and al-Ālūsī give this reading. Al-Rāzī adds that he relied on power apart from God and is made to see its failure. Bursevī includes the person's standing among his people alongside self-estimation.
44:49/q08 Whom does the address target? [ذُقْ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْكَرِيمُ · referent] (turns on: Whether the Abu Jahl occasion limits the address or exemplifies a wider class.)
  44:49/q08/p1 Abū Jahl is the specific addressee or occasion. — reasons: Muqātil, Qatāda, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Dāmghānī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Burhān, al-Durr, Bursevī, al-Ālūsī and the Study Quran identify him. Al-Burhān cites this as specific address meant for its specific addressee. His Quraysh leadership and boasts identify him in the reports.
  44:49/q08/p2 The address applies to the punished disbeliever or every sinner who makes the same claim. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī expressly extends it beyond Abū Jahl to every such sinner at the Resurrection. Bursevī says the verse threatens Abu Jahl and his likes; Ṭabāṭabāʾī, Iṣlāḥī and the Study Quran describe sinners, disbelievers or Hell's inhabitants more generally.
44:49/q09 What was reported to have happened with Abū Jahl and this verse? [* · reports] (turns on: The reports differ in the occasion and timing of the boast: a worldly boast or prophetic warning before Badr, a naming episode before the Zaqqum verses, or a claim after the order to drag him into Hell.)
  44:49/q09/p1 He boasted that none in Mecca's valley or between its two mountains was mightier or nobler; the verse answered his claim. — reasons: Muqātil says he called himself Quraysh's mightiest and noblest; Qatāda, Ibn ʿAbbās and many exegetes vary the boast as the strongest, most honored, generous, or most protected in the valley. Al-Thaʿlabī says the phrase sharply affirms his own claim in derision. Al-Durr reports Qatāda's question of how Muḥammad could threaten such a man.
