[IBNASHUR:1:2/r48] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The lam in li-llah may mean figurative exclusive possession or strengthen the verbal noun's relation as a weak, derived construction; exclusivity is also conveyed by the two definite parts.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r49] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | One group treats the sentence as a plain report that praise belongs to God, objecting that this leaves the speaker not actually praising Him.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r50] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | Defenders of a purely declarative analysis answer that reporting praise recognizes God's beautiful attributes, that the speaker is included in the general report, or that inherited practice entails following it.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r51] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | A response says literal report may serve its implied praise, as saying 'long sword-strap' to imply tall stature.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r52] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | Another group says the wording remains a report but its intended purpose is performative praise, like declarative wording for grief in 3:36; report form permits permanence, universality, exclusivity, and emphasis. | mentions 3:36
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r53] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · فريق ثان | reports | A contrasting position calls the sentence a pure performative, shifted from reporting into praise like contracts and verbs of praise or blame.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r54] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | prefers | Ibn Ashur's preferred account is a report used to perform praise: the intention is performative, while report form supplies duration, universality, exclusivity, and emphasis unavailable to direct performative wording.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r55] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Dhu al-Rumma's phrase 'we brought into being thanks for its Creator' confirms that Arabs described praise or thanks as something they create, treating it as performative expression.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r56] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Allah is the proper name of the necessarily existent essence, worthy of every praise.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r57] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | prefers | The name derives from al-ilah, definite form of ilah, a generic word for deity; ilah is linked to worship or states entailing humble reverence and follows a passive fa'al pattern like kitab.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r58] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Ibn Ashur thinks the name was established before Arab idolatry and originally signified the unique deity; it became a proper name through exclusive reference, not common usage.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r59] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Arabs do not appear to have used definite singular al-ilah for an idol: they used a possessive construction, another title, or the plural alihah.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r60] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The name's origin recalls God specifically as deity and signals that He alone deserves worship because He created all.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r61] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Frequent use led speakers to drop the hamza from al-ilah, as in al-nas from al-unas; Ba'ith ibn Hurayth preserves al-ilah and Ubayd ibn al-Abras preserves al-unas with hamza.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r62] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Proper-name formation can alter a word: Ibn Jinni's example Shums ibn Malik changes the usual vowel, and Zamakhshari cites fixed-case Abdullah; Allah's naming has no parallel among personal names.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r63] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Calling Allah with ya while retaining the definite article shows that speakers treat the article as part of the name, unlike ordinary nouns entered by al.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r64] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · صاحب الكشاف | reports | Al-Zamakhshari uses al-Ba'ith's 'ma'adh al-ilah' as evidence that Allah originated from al-ilah; Ibn Ashur says this is persuasive because ma'adh conventionally takes Allah as its complement.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r65] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · الجوهري عن سيبويه | reports | Another proposed derivation takes Allah from laah, a verbal noun meaning to be hidden; the article was added to suggest the original sense, as in fadl and majd used as names.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r66] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | A second proposal derives the name from wilah, 'overwhelmed and bewildered'; initial waw became hamza after kasra, then the article was added and hamza dropped.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r67] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | A further etymology borrows Syriac laaha as a divine name, Arabized by removing its final alif and adding the article.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r68] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · الزجاج ونسب إلى الخليل وسيبويه | reports | Another group says the name was coined directly for God without derivation from alaha; one rationale is that Arabs named everything and would not leave God unnamed.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r69] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · صاحب الكشاف | reports | Al-Zamakhshari says Arabs pronounce Allah's lam emphatically except where the preceding sound requires kasra, treating inherited consensus as evidence against speculative explanations.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r70] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · جمع من العلماء | reports | A group of scholars, cited by al-Tilimsani, says discussion of Allah's name should be avoided out of reverence and because it requires scriptural permission.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r71] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Rabb is an attribute of Allah: after praise is attached to His essence to show inherent entitlement, the attribute adds entitlement through His qualities; this is clearer than saying 'praise belongs to Lord of the worlds.' | mentions 83:6
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r72] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The four descriptions—Lord of the worlds, Merciful, Compassionate, King of Judgment Day—show each divine name's meaning merits praise and distinguishes God from alleged deities. | mentions 1:3, 1:4
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r73] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Rabb may be a verbal noun or adjective on fa'l from rabba-yarubbu, 'to nurture and govern,' or from 'to own'; a verbal noun is intensive.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r74] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Tarbiya means gradually bringing something to completion; the adjective pattern fa'l from fa'ala yaf'ulu is rare, as in namma-yunimmu, nammun.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r75] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | prefers | Ibn Ashur prefers 'nurturer and governor' over 'owner' because it fits God's management of creation toward perfection and avoids redundancy with 'King of Judgment Day'; the latter can specify the afterlife. | mentions 1:4
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r76] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · صاحب الكشاف | reports | Al-Zamakhshari limits rabb to 'master and owner,' its frequent Arabic sense, while allowing verbal-noun or adjective analysis; Ibn Ashur says context can select a less frequent established sense.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r77] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · الحارث بن حلزة | reports | Al-Harith ibn Hilliza calls Amr ibn Hind al-rabb in a poem, evidence that Arabs applied the title to a human ruler.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r78] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · النابغة | reports | Al-Nabigha calls al-Nu'man ibn al-Harith rabb and uses rabb again of al-Nu'man when describing God's favor to him.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r79] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · غاوي بن ظالم أو عباس بن مرداس | reports | A pre-Islamic poet applies rabb to an idol, ridiculing a deity with a fox urinating on its head; al-Uzza was also called al-Rabba and the plural is ارباب.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r8] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The many requirements for successful guidance and reception gather around these six foundations of spiritual preparation.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r80] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · صاحب الكشاف ومن تابعه | rejects | Al-Zamakhshari and followers claim rabb was used for others only with a qualifier; Ibn Ashur rejects this on unqualified poetic usage and the plural أرباب.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r81] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The Quran uses rabb for the Egyptian official in 12:23 and for multiple false gods in 12:39, confirming that Arabic applies the term to beings other than God. | mentions 12:23, 12:39
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r82] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | A hadith discourages calling a human master 'my rabb' as etiquette to prevent implication of worship; 'master' is preferred, compounds for a house or animal are allowed, and unrestricted use for a king is more disliked.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r83] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Al-alamin is the plural of alam; few nouns on fa'il take this plural, with yasm (jasmine) the other example, whose plurals occur in al-A'sha's verse.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r84] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | An alam is a genus among existing things; the word may derive from knowledge or sign because each genus is distinguishable and serves as a sign by which it is known.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r85] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The fa'il pattern usually marks an instrument; worlds are thus imaginatively instruments for knowing the Maker or truths, a subtle coinage by the Arabs.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r86] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The plural uses the rational masculine form even though some worlds are nonrational, by giving precedence to rational beings.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r87] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · التفتزاني | reports | Al-Taftazani says alam names knowledgeable beings or each genus through which the Creator is known; singular alam normally needs a qualifying genitive, while 'the entire cosmos' is later theological terminology.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r88] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | In this rhetorical context the definite article is exhaustive: there is no external or mental antecedent and no suitable literal or generic reading, so all genera are included.
[IBNASHUR:1:2/r89] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Exhausting all genera entails their individuals, since lordship over abstract essences makes no sense; the plural with the definite article explicitly signals comprehensive scope.
