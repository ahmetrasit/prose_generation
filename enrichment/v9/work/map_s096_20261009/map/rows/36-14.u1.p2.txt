36:14/q17 What is reported about the envoys' encounter with Ḥabīb? [* · reports] (turns on: Reports differ over how many believed and over the later fate of Habib and the envoys.)
  36:14/q17/p1 The envoys met Ḥabīb the shepherd or carpenter, cured his sick son and others, and he believed. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari says they met the carpenter tending goats near the city; Habib requested a sign, and the sick, blind and leprous, including a son ill for two years, were healed. Al-Qurtubi calls Habib the shepherd of a few sheep and identifies him with the man from the far end of town; his son is described as ill or possessed. Al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi and al-Tabataba'i recount the son's healing; al-Nasafi says he rose after their touch. News of many healings reached the king.
  36:14/q17/p2 Only Ḥabīb al-Najjār believed; he left his work, later urged the townspeople to follow the messengers, and was killed. — reasons: Muqatil calls him an Israelite and former polytheist. Ibn Atiyya says a running believer was killed while the people persisted; Sa'id ibn Jubayr says Habib had concealed his faith, all three envoys were killed, and Habib was then killed in a well at al-Rass. Ibn Ishaq's version has Habib run from the city's far gate when the king and people resolve to kill the envoys. Kuranyolu treats his likely murder as part of the warning.
36:14/q18 How did the envoys and their helper present signs to the king? [* · reports] (turns on: The reported sign narratives differ in the person revived and in what the signs establish about the envoys' status.)
  36:14/q18/p1 They healed a blind boy and revived a child dead seven days, exposing the idols' powerlessness. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari describes Simon's disguise and court friendship, his challenge to the king's idol, the blind boy's missing eyes restored through prayer and two clay pellets, and revival after a seven-day death. The revived boy warned of seven valleys of Fire and said a handsome youth interceded for the three in heaven. Al-Qurtubi says the envoys prayed openly while Simon prayed secretly and the boy testified to God, Jesus and the envoys, naming Simon best; al-Tabrisi, al-Baydawi and al-Nasafi tell related versions. Al-Baydawi and al-Nasafi say the king admitted his idols neither hear, see, harm nor benefit. Abu Hayyan cites healing the blind and lepers and raising the dead as signs of clear delivery. Al-Biqa'i says messengers are accompanied by credible signs and cites Tufayl ibn Amr's requested light first on his forehead, then his whip.
  36:14/q18/p2 Shamʿūn revived a girl from her grave before the people. — reasons: Muqatil says he struck the grave with his foot, called her by God's permission, and she emerged testifying that the emissaries were sent. She warned that acceptance brings forgiveness and refusal punishment, predicted disbelief, then returned to her grave at her request.
  36:14/q18/p3 Miracle reports may be Jesus's confirming wonder through the apostles, rather than proof they were prophets. — reasons: Al-Alusi records this answer to the claim that only prophets properly have miracles; the apostles may have spoken as direct envoys or representatives.
36:14/q19 How did the king and townspeople finally respond? [* · reports] (turns on: Conflicting narratives of the king's conversion and whether any townspeople survived as believers.)
  36:14/q19/p1 The king and some people believed; Gabriel's cry killed those who refused. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari and al-Baydawi say Simon's argument moved the king; al-Qurtubi says the revived boy's testimony led Simon to identify himself and the king and many believed. Al-Qushayri says the king believed but his people did not. Al-Nasafi includes Simon and the messengers among those who believed. Al-Tabataba'i's al-Ayyashi variant says the revived man was the king's son and king and people believed, though the destruction of unbelievers is recounted by other versions.
  36:14/q19/p2 The king remained an unbeliever and joined his people in persecuting the envoys; the unbelievers were destroyed. — reasons: Wahb and Ka'b say the king and people continued torture and killing. Bursevi prefers this account: if king or subjects had believed they would have aided the envoys as Habib did, especially with clear proof and a population that follows its king. Ibn Ishaq also says the king resolved to kill them; Ibn Atiyya says the townspeople persisted and a heavenly cry silenced them.
36:14/q20 How did the envoys understand the townspeople's language? [* · reports] (turns on: Different reported means of overcoming the language barrier.)
  36:14/q20/p1 God put them to sleep, angels carried them to Antioch, and they awoke speaking its language. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi links this wonder to God's strengthening Jesus with the Holy Spirit in 2:87 and 2:253.
  36:14/q20/p2 An angel gave them luminous wine enabling comprehension of the local language. — reasons: Bursevi reports this from Kashf al-Asrar, alongside the claim that Jesus was told to dispatch apostles to cities before his ascension.
36:14/q21 Who spoke the declaration 'we are sent to you'? [فَقَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّآ إِلَيْكُم مُّرْسَلُونَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the plural speech is simultaneous or a narrative summary of successive declarations.)
  36:14/q21/p1 All three messengers addressed the people together, or one spoke with the others' agreement. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi, al-Baghawi, al-Nasafi and Bursevi take the plural as the three. Al-Alusi allows plural attribution when one speaks for a concurring group. Bursevi says denial of the two amounts to denial of the third because their message is one. Islahi allows collective embassy language despite different ranks, making Moses primary, Aaron assisting and the believer preaching Moses's message.
  36:14/q21/p2 The first two spoke before the third arrived, and the third spoke after them. — reasons: Yahya ibn Sallam reports this speaking sequence, although the verse gives a collective declaration after mentioning the reinforcement.
36:14/q22 What commission did they declare to the people? [إِنَّآ إِلَيْكُم مُّرْسَلُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: The breadth of mursalūn as 'messengers' or 'message bearers' and the reported content of their call.)
  36:14/q22/p1 They were sent to call the people to worship the one God, abandon idols, and accept guidance and faith. — reasons: Al-Tabari says they renounced idols; Abu al-Aliya says worship God without partner. Al-Zamakhshari and al-Qurtubi report their replies to the king that God created him and his idols, does as He wills, rules as He chooses, and heals by His permission. Ibn Atiyya says the call was to guidance and faith; al-Biqa'i says the two announced faith in God. Al-Zajjaj and al-Qurtubi say their duty was clear delivery, not control of belief; Abu Hayyan says they entrust their truth to God. Muqatil's revived girl warned of forgiveness for acceptance and punishment for rejection.
  36:14/q22/p2 The line can also be rendered 'we are message bearers unto you.' — reasons: Study Quran notes this translation for the declaration here and in 36:16.
36:14/q23 Why is this first declaration emphasized with innā, and how does it differ from the later claim? [إِنَّآ إِلَيْكُم مُّرْسَلُونَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the first utterance is truly initial information or already answers a request or denial.)
  36:14/q23/p1 The first claim begins with innā; after stronger denial, the later assertion adds lām and, in the fuller exchange, an oath. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari and Abu Hayyan say the first is initial information and the later lām responds to denial. Al-Samin says plain denial receives inna whereas emphatic denial receives inna and lām; he distinguishes initial, requestive and denial contexts, and reports al-Kindi's point that the forms have distinct meanings, recalling his failed attempt to imitate the Quran at al-Ma'ida. Al-Suyuti lists other Quranic emphasis particles and says emphasis rises with doubt or denial; for Jesus's envoys, the later oath and lām follow stronger rejection. Bursevi says prior rejection explains the group's emphasis.
  36:14/q23/p2 Calling the first speech merely 'initial' misclassifies a context in which the audience already seeks or denies proof. — reasons: Al-Samin rejects al-Shaykh's initial-versus-denial account for confusing requestive and initial contexts; he still holds that inna and the later lām mark different degrees of denial.
36:14/q24 What does this account teach about successive messengers and evidence? [* · theology]
  36:14/q24/p1 A messenger can be supported by another; simultaneous messengers may share a law, while successive arrivals require renewed miracle and law. — reasons: Al-Jishumi draws the permissibility of reinforcing one messenger with another and distinguishes shared versus successive commissions. Al-Biqa'i says God sends no messenger without signs that can bring belief, directly or through another; he cites Tufayl ibn Amr's light as an example of a requested sign.
36:14/q25 What agency rule is drawn from God's attribution of the dispatch? [أَرْسَلْنَآ · law]
  36:14/q25/p1 An authorized subagent is the principal's agent: the principal may remove him but the intervening agent may not. — reasons: Al-Razi and Bursevi apply this rule to Jesus's sending under God's command.
