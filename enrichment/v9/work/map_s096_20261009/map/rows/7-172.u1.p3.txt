7:172/q32 How do interpreters reconcile or challenge the literal particle account? [* · theology] (turns on: Physical feasibility, human identity and memory, report authority, and whether the verse speaks of Adam's back or successive human backs.)
  7:172/q32/p1 Literal particles can be rational and addressable through divine power, even in tiny or non-bodily form. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj cites ant speech and mountain praise; Razi suggests indivisible living particles or immaterial rational souls. Defenders distinguish forgetting the scene from retaining knowledge.
  7:172/q32/p2 The account is impossible as a history of fully formed future persons in Adam's physical back. — reasons: Mu'tazilites cite lack of space, required bodily structure, accountability regress, Qur'anic creation from fluid and two lives/two deaths; Tabatabai adds soul-body identity, practical reason's dependence on social life, and the uselessness of a duplicated world.
  7:172/q32/p3 The extraction report can be true, yet need not be the verse's own explanation. — reasons: Ibn al-Ikhshid says it inspires gratitude and fitra; Ibn Atiyya separates earlier soul creation in reports from later covenant-taking in the verse. Ibn Ashur says reports add predestination, not the explicit wording.
  7:172/q32/p4 The verse and hadith state two compatible extractions, one from Adam and one through later backs. — reasons: Razi says neither excludes the other; Ibn Ashur reasons from direct children to their father; Tabatabai says the verse itself already implies both and criticizes Razi's division.
  7:172/q32/p5 The prophetic answer about the primordial event redirects a question about a present rational covenant. — reasons: Al-Shirazi proposes this as reconciliation; Alusi calls it good but requires figurative language for eternity because created events cannot be eternal.
7:172/q33 What spiritual or inward meanings are read in the covenant? [* · inward] (turns on: Whether inward response is direct differentiated experience, divine action through effaced selves, or a metaphysical account of fixed forms.)
  7:172/q33/p1 The elect recognized and loved God in a distinct spiritual manner, while other souls met Him through different degrees of veiling. — reasons: Al-Kharraz contrasts believers' tranquility with unbelievers' overwhelming encounter; Najmiyya distinguishes forerunners, right and left; Sufi reports make the covenant distinguish spiritual ranks.
  7:172/q33/p2 Their answer occurred through divine power while individual selves were absent, hidden, or annihilated. — reasons: Yusuf, Ibn Bannan and Ali ibn Abd al-Rahim say they existed in God's power before ordinary witnessing; al-Husayn says God answered as the hearers vanished; others speak of surrender and effaced self.
  7:172/q33/p3 The taking manifests God's majesty and self-sufficiency and establishes proof without divine need. — reasons: Al-Nasrabadi distinguishes earlier from later taking through lineages and calls creation forms moved by divine power; al-Jariri says knowledge bestowed by God enables response.
  7:172/q33/p4 The dialogue is an echo of created existence; God's question leaves room for later partners. — reasons: Ibn Arabi says God omitted 'alone' because of natural desire; Alusi objects that reports and leading exegetes affirm exclusive oneness.
  7:172/q33/p5 Pre-eternal intelligible forms or fixed entities answer through primordial capacity rather than later tongues. — reasons: Al-Khalakhali distinguishes subsistence from existence; Alusi accepts that distinction. Another scholar describes the exchange as divine manifestation and potential readiness.
7:172/q34 How does this verse connect with its surrounding passage and other covenants? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the local context primarily widens Sinai's covenant, turns to Arab idolaters, or remains within the surrounding Jewish narratives.)
  7:172/q34/p1 It generalizes the nearby Sinai covenant to all humanity, combining scriptural and rational proof. — reasons: Jishumi, Tabarsi and Alusi connect 7:171 and 2:63 with the universal pledge; Alusi calls the wording's shift a thematic digression but prefers a universal audience.
  7:172/q34/p2 It returns from earlier narratives to a universal proof of monotheism and a warning to Arab or Quraysh idolaters. — reasons: Razi sees it after Moses' story; Ibn Ashur notes Israelites were not idolaters and connects the ancestral-shirk excuse to the sura's opening. Islahi says earlier nations prepare the final warning and later Israelite example.
  7:172/q34/p3 It concerns Jewish ancestors and their descendants within the surrounding Jewish narratives. — reasons: Zamakhshari takes ancestors as Jews who claimed Ezra was God's son; Abu Hayyan lists neighbouring Jewish episodes at 7:163–175. Alusi rejects limiting the verse to them.
  7:172/q34/p4 It links the first covenant with fitra, prophetic warnings and later failures to keep covenant. — reasons: Reports cite 30:30, 53:56, 7:102, 5:7 and 3:83; Burhan compares another self-witnessing exchange in al-An'am.
7:172/q35 What spiritual orders and conduct does Sahl connect with humanity's offspring? [ذُرِّيَّتَهُمْ · inward] (turns on: The different spiritual ranks and the effects of their witnessing.)
  7:172/q35/p1 Sahl distinguishes three orders and connects the seeking and chosen to Adam's and Muhammad's lights. — reasons: He describes pre-creation light from Muhammad, a million years' worship and witnessing, and the mercy and forward light of those spiritually near; 57:12 is cited.
  7:172/q35/p2 Witnessing faith and divine observation protects the elect from Iblis and shapes their response to trials. — reasons: Sahl connects felicity to faith, obedience, asceticism and adab; he prescribes austere food, clothing, mosque dwelling, remembrance, reflection and patience. Missing congregational prayer or pilgrimage is a sign of misery.
  7:172/q35/p3 Muhammad received every station of service granted to humanity and a special form of divine sustenance. — reasons: Sahl says the Prophet's feeding and drinking was a divine privilege akin to nourishment, not ordinary food, which he would have shared with family and the Suffa.
7:172/q36 Which further historical details appear in covenant reports? [* · reports] (turns on: Reports add distinct details to the core acknowledgment: decrees, Jesus' pre-birth spirit, mixed groups, and a shadow-world test.)
  7:172/q36/p1 All future persons' names, deeds, lifespans, provisions and afflictions were written or fixed. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and al-Suddi report the fixed decree, with a sealed pen; one report says Eve was formed from Adam's rib during the account.
  7:172/q36/p2 Jesus' spirit was among those covenanted and was later sent to Mary. — reasons: Ubay's version adds that he entered Mary's mouth.
  7:172/q36/p3 The primordial groups were mixed after being distinguished. — reasons: A report from Abu Umama says both hands were right, both groups affirmed, and God mixed them; its continuation connects eventual deeds with their homes.
  7:172/q36/p4 Some descendants underwent a shadow-world trial of obedience. — reasons: Abu Ja'far says Adam's clay was mixed with sweet and bitter water; entry into fire is read by Tabatabai as a metaphor for servitude.
7:172/q37 What is reported about the verse's revelation setting? [* · sciences]
  7:172/q37/p1 The sura is Meccan, though a view makes the passage through this covenant verse Medinan. — reasons: Qatada excepts the town verse; another report extends the Medinan exception through this verse. Ibn Hajar and Suyuti discuss the convention for exceptions.
7:172/q38 What does 'we' in testimony imply about created speakers? [شَهِدْنَآ · grammar] (turns on: Whether first-person plural is necessarily majestic or can include a real collective.)
  7:172/q38/p1 A single created speaker should not use the plural for self-exaltation. — reasons: Al-Mubarrad and al-Hariri reserve majestic plural for God; a proposed account says God's plural can reflect acts through creatures.
  7:172/q38/p2 A scholar can say 'we' when referring to the school collectively. — reasons: Burhan offers this inclusive use, without self-exaltation.
7:172/q39 Can the testimony in this verse ground legal acknowledgment? [شَهِدْنَآ · law]
  7:172/q39/p1 It can be cited as a basis for acknowledgment; actual debt answers depend on wording or custom. — reasons: Shurayh invokes a relative's testimony; Suyuti treats the verse as a basis for legal confession. Jurists differ whether balā and naʿam to a creditor's negative question establish a debt.
