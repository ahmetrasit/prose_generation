38:7/q08 What force does in have in 'in hādhā illā ikhtilāq'? [إِنْ هَٰذَآ إِلَّا ٱخْتِلَٰقٌ · grammar]
  38:7/q08/p1 It is negative, equivalent to mā: 'this is only fabrication.' — reasons: Bursevī explicitly identifies in as a negator; the following illā restricts the predicate to fabrication.
38:7/q09 What does ikhtilāq mean in their accusation? [ٱخْتِلَٰقٌ · meaning]
  38:7/q09/p1 A lie or invented falsehood, alleged to have been made up by Muhammad himself. — reasons: Al-Walīd makes the personal accusation; al-Ṭabarī's Ibn ʿAbbās says false conjecture, while Mujāhid, Qatāda, al-Suddī and Ibn Zayd give lie or something made up. Al-Naḥḥās says khalaqa/ikhtalaqa can mean originating without a model; al-Dāmghānī illustrates fabricated speech with 26:137 and 29:17. Ibn al-Jawzī cites 26:137; al-Qurṭubī also notes literal creation without a prior model. Al-Rāghib says khalaqa of speech means a lie, which led some to avoid calling the Qur'an created. Ibn ʿĀshūr adds that the claim lacks plausible grounds. Study Quran lists other accusations against Noah, Moses and Muhammad, and Qur'anic answers in 21:5, 25:4, 32:3, 34:8, 34:43, 42:24, 46:8, 23:38, 28:36, 10:37–38, 11:13, 11:35 and 12:11.
38:7/q10 What occasion is reported for these verses? [* · reports] (turns on: The reports give different triggers: Abū Ṭālib's illness and the leaders' appeal, or their agitation after ʿUmar's conversion.)
  38:7/q10/p1 During Abū Ṭālib's illness, Quraysh leaders sought to stop Muhammad's criticism of their gods, and rejected his request for the declaration of God's oneness. — reasons: Al-Suddī names Abū Jahl, al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil, al-Aswad ibn al-Muṭṭalib and al-Aswad ibn ʿAbd Yaghūth, and says the elders feared Arab reproach if they attacked after Abū Ṭālib's death. Muhammad promised a word yielding Arab allegiance and rule over non-Arabs; the leaders rejected 'There is no god but God' and left angry. Ibn ʿAbbās's report in al-Wāḥidī and al-Biqāʿī gives the illness and the one word; al-Tirmidhī graded it ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ. Study Quran's IK/Q/R/Ṭ report says Arab allegiance and non-Arab tribute and that the chiefs scoffed they would say ten declarations; its IJ report connects the event to 38:1–7. Ibn al-Jawzī also identifies the opening through 38:7 with this episode.
  38:7/q10/p2 After ʿUmar's conversion, Quraysh sought a compromise over gods and idols; the Prophet called them to one God and they refused. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī transmits this account from the commentators as another setting in which the verses followed.
38:7/q11 What happened when Muhammad urged Abū Ṭālib himself to profess God's oneness? [* · reports]
  38:7/q11/p1 Abū Ṭālib refused from fear that Arabs would say he yielded at death and remained with his forefathers' religion; 28:56 followed. — reasons: Al-Suddī reports the deathbed appeal and refusal, then the revelation 'You do not guide whom you love' (28:56). Ibn ʿĀshūr recalls the chiefs' question whether Abū Ṭālib would leave ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib's religion.
38:7/q12 Were people between prophets excused for lacking knowledge of monotheism? [* · theology]
  38:7/q12/p1 No; the earlier divine message was available and people could be held responsible. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says reports about the introduction of idols and Abraham's actual practice refute blanket ignorance in the interval, while 17:15 bears on the issue. He cites 16:36: each community received a messenger calling it to worship God and avoid idols, with some guided and others astray. In Anas's report a man is told both his father and the Prophet's father are in Hell. Al-Biqāʿī also argues reflection can reveal monotheism.
38:7/q13 What does their statement reveal about their motive? [* · interpretation]
  38:7/q13/p1 It reveals envy and resistance to Muhammad's revelation. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās calls the speakers envious. Al-Thaʿlabī connects the fabrication charge with their question why the Qur'an came to Muhammad alone and the answer that they doubt revelation. Iṣlāḥī portrays their leaders as proud defenders of idols and inherited religion, unwilling to accept a messenger from among themselves.
38:7/q14 How does this rejection fit the opening of Sūrat Ṣād? [* · links]
  38:7/q14/p1 The opening presents proud Quraysh rejecting the reminder of tawḥīd as earlier nations did, until belief comes too late. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says Ṣād, like preceding sūrahs in its group, is founded on tawḥīd; 38:1–11 show a reminder opposed through arrogance and polytheism and parallel prior nations. The leaders call the local messenger a sorcerer and liar, rallying defense of idols. Study Quran notes a further discussion of notables' rejection of revelation at 34:34.
38:7/q15 Can the Qur'an have been fabricated by alteration or loss? [ٱخْتِلَٰقٌ · theology]
  38:7/q15/p1 No; divine preservation and public transmission make alteration or concealment untenable. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī cites God's guarantee, including 41:42, and multitudes across places and circumstances who memorized the Qur'an for recitation, law, exegesis, eloquence and scrutiny. A changed word of Imruʾ al-Qays's less consequential poetry would be disowned; change in a text essential to prayer and law would be still more readily caught.
38:7/q16 What in the Qur'an supports its divine source against the charge of invention? [ٱخْتِلَٰقٌ · theology] (turns on: Whether Qur'anic form belongs to a known speech form, and whether its reports could have come from ordinary learning.)
  38:7/q16/p1 Its distinctive order and reports of unseen earlier events support Muhammad's prophethood. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī says reports of unseen earlier events are evidence because Muhammad neither learned them from scripture nor witnessed them; he cites 29:48, 28:44, 28:46 and 11:49. Its style differs from ordinary prophetic speech, as two men's sermons differ, and falls outside familiar patterns. He says one must rule out poetry, sajʿ and metrical speech, which takes reflective, acquired discernment. Disagreement among believers about the route of proof does not nullify proof, just as arguments for the world's origination differ.
  38:7/q16/p2 It is poetry, according to some Quraysh opponents, or contains poetry, according to some heretics. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī reports both claims as forms he argues must be excluded to establish the Qur'an's exceptional order.
  38:7/q16/p3 It is rhymed prose, albeit more eloquent than ordinary sajʿ. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī reports this description from some Muslims while arguing that its order lies outside familiar speech forms.
  38:7/q16/p4 It is metrical speech, hence within familiar discourse. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī reports the claim as another form to be excluded in his proof.
38:7/q17 Which passage was the last revelation? [* · sciences] (turns on: Whether 'last' means the final revelation absolutely or the latest each authority personally heard.)
  38:7/q17/p1 Sūrat al-Naṣr. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī reports Ibn ʿAbbās's identification, 110:1.
  38:7/q17/p2 Sūrat al-Māʾida. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī reports ʿĀʾisha's identification, 5:1.
  38:7/q17/p3 Sūrat Barāʾa. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī reports al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib's identification, 9:1.
  38:7/q17/p4 The verse of return to God, 2:281. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī reports Saʿīd ibn Jubayr's identification.
  38:7/q17/p5 The instruction to say 'God is sufficient for me,' 9:129. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī reports al-Suddī's identification.
  38:7/q17/p6 The reports can differ because each reporter identified the last passage he heard personally. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī says disagreement over placement or last hearing does not entail disagreement about the Qur'an's divine source; he compares the reports for 110:1, 5:1, 9:1, 2:281 and 9:129.
38:7/q18 How should apparent foreign words in the Qur'an be understood? [ٱلْمِلَّةِ ٱلْءَاخِرَةِ · meaning] (turns on: Whether a shared word arose independently in languages or entered Arabic and became naturalized before revelation.)
  38:7/q18/p1 Parallel forms across languages can arise through coincidence. — reasons: Al-Zarkashī reports al-Ṭabarī that Arabic, Persian and Ethiopian sometimes share words by chance; Ibn Fāris transmits Abū ʿUbayd's version of that account.
  38:7/q18/p2 Words of foreign origin can be adopted, altered and made Arabic through Arab usage. — reasons: Al-Zarkashī reports Ibn ʿAṭiyya that trade and travel brought terms into Arabic, where their sounds changed and poetry and conversation naturalized them before their Qur'anic use. Ignorance of a term, as Ibn ʿAbbās's ignorance of fāṭir, does not make its current use non-Arabic.
38:7/q19 What function are the disconnected letters said to serve? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the letters work as attention signals or numerical signs; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ traces the latter reports to a disputed narrative setting.)
  38:7/q19/p1 They catch listeners' attention and prompt them to listen to the revelation that follows. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports that unfamiliar letters are not mistaken for a complete statement. She reports al-Juwaynī's endorsement: the Qur'an's important meanings require alert hearing, and these sounds suit revelation better than ordinary interjections.
  38:7/q19/p2 They encode abjad numbers from which durations can be calculated. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports calculations for the Muslim community, earlier peoples or the world; she tentatively traces such reports to Ibn Isḥāq's story of the Jewish Ḥuyayy ibn Akhṭab amid disputes and plotting.
38:7/q20 What broader knowledge is said to have been derived from the Qur'an? [* · sciences]
  38:7/q20/p1 People derived timekeeping, literary sciences and inward terminology
