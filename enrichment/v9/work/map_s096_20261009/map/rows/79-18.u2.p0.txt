## THE CURRENT MAP
79:18/q01 What does “هل لك” convey in Moses’ address? [هَل لَّكَ · meaning]
  79:18/q01/p1 It asks whether Pharaoh has a desire, need, or inclination toward the offered purification, in an established idiom of invitation. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī and al-Bursevī gloss the desire or need; al-Elmalılı adds intention. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it a concise fixed invitation, like asking a guest to stay, and cites Kaʿb’s هل لك فيما and Bujayr’s هل لك في التي. Al-Rāzī compares هل لك في. Al-Samīn gives هل لك في الخير as an idiom for رغبة. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī takes the question as asking inclination.
79:18/q02 How is “هل لك إلى أن تزكى” grammatically understood? [هَل لَّكَ إِلَىٰٓ أَن تَزَكَّىٰ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the unusual إلى is explained by an omitted noun, an implicit verb of inviting, an expanded sense of the preposition, or figurative usage.)
  79:18/q02/p1 An implicit nominal such as need or desire supplies the omitted subject of the question. — reasons: Sībawayh says هل لك في ذلك, like لا عليك and من له في ذلك, omits understood حاجة through frequent use. Al-Fārisī supplies حاجة أو إربة; al-Wāḥidī gives the same and cites an anonymous poetic line. Al-Rāzī reports al-Wāḥidī and cites هل لكم فيها إلي. Al-Samīn describes لك as predicate of an omitted subject, with إلى أن attached to it, and cites the same poetic pattern. Al-Bursevī and Ibn ʿĀshūr also identify an implicit subject, Ibn ʿĀshūr suggesting رغبة or ميل; al-Elmalılı similarly notes implied subject. Al-Ālūsī locates إلى with the omitted predicate and cites poetry, while recording others' omitted رغبة.
  79:18/q02/p2 The implicit noun can specifically be a way or access to purification. — reasons: Al-Rāzī allows an omitted سبيل, as does al-Samīn's paraphrase هل لك سبيل إلى التزكية; Abū Ḥayyān lists a way alongside desire and need.
  79:18/q02/p3 The unusual “إلى” reflects an implied invitation, so the construction includes the force of ‘I call you to.’ — reasons: Abū al-Baqāʾ explains إلى through an intended أدعوك; al-Samīn says this does not change parsing. Al-Burhān treats it as تضمين and says the prepositional phrase is governed by لك. Al-Itqān explains verbal تضمين as one verb carrying another's meaning and unusual preposition; it favors expanding the verb over the preposition because verb examples are more numerous, and applies أدعوك here. Ibn ʿĀshūr reports Ibn Jinnī's shared-meaning account via al-Ṭībī, comparing رفث إلى at 2:187; al-Ālūsī allows attachment to the utterance's meaning or an implicit expression.
  79:18/q02/p4 “إلى” is used with the sense of the more usual “في.” — reasons: Al-Burhān reports this explanation alongside the implicit-call reading. Al-Itqān explicitly glosses إلى as في here, ‘are you willing in this matter?’ Its discussion says some linguists and grammarians favor expanding the preposition's sense.
  79:18/q02/p5 The preposition signals a figurative invitation rather than a syntactic inclusion. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr reports this alternative to Ibn Jinnī's تضمين; al-Bursevī also allows هل لك as figurative for ‘shall I attract and invite you?’ Al-Itqān says تضمين is figurative because a word cannot literally carry both meanings simultaneously.
  79:18/q02/p6 The object of “إلى” is elided and understood as desire, need, or a way toward something. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān explicitly proposes an omitted object of إلى and cites an unnamed poet; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī also says its object is elided, yielding inclination to purification.
79:18/q03 Why does Moses frame the offer as a question? [هَل لَّكَ · rhetoric]
  79:18/q03/p1 The question offers purification gently and tactfully, drawing Pharaoh toward acceptance. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān calls it an invitation any reasonable person would accept. Al-Bursevī says the tactful, unthreatening offer could draw Pharaoh from obstinacy, avoiding accusations of polytheism, ignorance, or disbelief. Al-Ālūsī says it lowers Pharaoh from arrogance. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ ties its tact to 20:44. Ibn ʿĀshūr describes an encouraging offer. Al-Elmalılı says beginning with desire recognizes inclination and intention as prerequisites for voluntary acts.
79:18/q04 What purification does “تزكى” invite Pharaoh to seek? [تَزَكَّىٰ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether purification is the initial move to faith, its declaration, good conduct, comprehensive moral growth, repentance from tyranny, inward self-effacement, or charity; the charity gloss is disputed in Pharaoh's unbelieving context.)
  79:18/q04/p1 To leave unbelief or shirk and embrace faith, Islam, and God's oneness. — reasons: Muqātil includes repairing corruption and accepting God's oneness. Al-Ṭabarī says pure belief; Ibn Zayd links this sense throughout the Qur'an to Islam, citing 20:76 and 80:3, 7. Al-Fārisī and al-Wāḥidī cite 91:9 and 18:74 for purification from shirk, al-Fārisī also 80:7. Al-Māwardī transmits Qatāda. Al-Baghawī says both readings indicate escape from shirk, Islam, and uprightness. Al-Rāzī says the invitation includes monotheism and religious law. Others mention rebellion, tyranny, obedience, or freeing Israel as extensions of the change.
  79:18/q04/p2 To cleanse deficiencies and vices and acquire virtues: a full moral purification that includes faith. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya says restricting the sense to conversion or the declaration is too narrow. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Biqāʿī agree; al-Biqāʿī specifies outward and inward vices, with faith and freeing Israel as the lowest beginning. Al-Bursevī adds the opacity of human and natural conditions. Al-Elmalılı adds growth in Islamic qualities; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ adds growth of the soul's virtues. Al-Rāzī speaks of every defect and what is unfitting.
  79:18/q04/p3 To profess that there is no deity but God. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī reports ʿIkrima. Al-Wāḥidī reports Ibn ʿAbbās, and also Muqātil's related formulation of affirming divine oneness. Al-Baghawī reports Ibn ʿAbbās. Al-Qurṭubī transmits it through al-Ḍaḥḥāk from Ibn ʿAbbās. Al-Qushayrī reports a promise attached to the declaration: an imperishable kingdom, ageless youth, four hundred years of comfort, then paradise. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the formula is contained in a broader purification.
  79:18/q04/p4 To do good. — reasons: Al-Māwardī and al-Wāḥidī both report this gloss from al-Kalbī.
  79:18/q04/p5 To repent of tyranny and return to God. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports this reading, with ensuing faith-driven fear motivating obedience and restraining sin.
  79:18/q04/p6 In an inward reading, to annihilate selfhood. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī says guidance to fear is knowledge of the unity of the Essence, which softens and dissolves selfhood.
  79:18/q04/p7 To give alms. — reasons: Abū ʿAmr's explanation derives this from assimilated تتزكى. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Khālawayh reject applying it here because Moses first called an unbeliever to belief, not charity; Ibn Khālawayh cites the wording زكية in 18:74. Al-Qurṭubī reports Abū ʿAmr's preference for the light reading and belief sense.
79:18/q05 What lexical sense and form underlie “تزكى”? [تَزَكَّىٰ · meaning]
  79:18/q05/p1 It draws on purity and growth in goodness; the reflexive form conveys seeking or working to become pure. — reasons: Al-Jishumī says zakāh is growth in good, zākī grows in good, and tazakkī seeks that status. Al-Rāzī takes zakī as free of defects, citing 18:74 and 91:9. Ibn ʿĀshūr says zakāt is increase, conventionally inward good without a contextual clue, citing 91:9–10; he says the reflexive form implies self-training. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says zakā is growth in goodness and blessing. Al-Elmalılı joins cleansing and flourishing.
79:18/q06 How is “تزكى” read and formed? [تَزَكَّىٰ · readings] (turns on: How the original two tāʾs of تتزكى are reduced, which readers transmit each form, and whether the stronger form carries a distinct sense.)
  79:18/q06/p1 With a doubled zāy, assimilating a tāʾ from original تتزكى. — reasons: Reports assign it variously to Nāfiʿ and Ibn Kathīr; the Hijaz readers and Yaʿqūb; Abū Jaʿfar, Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr, and Yaʿqūb; and in variant transmissions Abū ʿAmr. Al-Ṭabarī's summary differs, naming Medinans generally as doubled. Al-Fārisī cites proximity of tāʾ and zāy; al-Samīn compares assimilated تصدى at 80:6; Qirāʾāt ER says the cluster contracts while preserving root ز ك و and Form V. Al-Biqāʿī hears more complete purification in the heavier form. Abū ʿAmr's charity gloss for it is contested in the interpretation question.
  79:18/q06/p2 With a light zāy, dropping one tāʾ from original تتزكى. — reasons: Reports assign it to the other readers, with different accounts of Abū ʿAmr; al-Naḥḥās expressly assigns a light reading to Medinans and Abū ʿAmr, while al-Ṭabarī reports Kufans and Basrans generally light. Al-Fārisī finds deletion and lightening more natural; al-Ṭabarī calls it more eloquent. Al-Baghawī and al-Naḥḥās say both readings share the sense of purification. Al-Samīn says grammarians dispute which of two initial letters is omitted and compares 97:4. Al-Biqāʿī says lightening suggests an easier, lesser first degree. Al-Elmalılı describes the underlying تتزكّى, though calls it an imperfect in tafʿīl.
