## THE CURRENT MAP
20:50/q01 Who answers Pharaoh, and why is the reply singular? [قَالَ · referent]
  20:50/q01/p1 Moses alone answers. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī say Pharaoh addressed Moses specifically; al-Qurṭubī cites the singular verb. Ibn ʿAṭiyya adds that Moses claims attributes Pharaoh cannot share. Al-Rāzī offers two explanations for singling him out after addressing both brothers: Moses is the principal prophet and Aaron his minister, or Pharaoh aimed at Moses' stutter and Aaron's eloquence.
20:50/q02 Who is included in 'our Lord'? [رَبُّنَا · referent] (turns on: Whether the plural names the two envoys or the full creation.)
  20:50/q02/p1 The pronoun includes Moses and Aaron. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī gives this as one scope of the plural.
  20:50/q02/p2 The pronoun includes all creatures, including Pharaoh. — reasons: Bursevī expressly includes Moses, Aaron, Pharaoh and others; al-Ālūsī attributes the universal scope to some investigators.
20:50/q03 How does 'our Lord, the one who...' function grammatically? [رَبُّنَا ٱلَّذِىٓ · grammar] (turns on: Whether an unstated pronoun precedes 'our Lord'.)
  20:50/q03/p1 'Our Lord' is subject and the relative clause is its predicate. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī gives this as the first parse.
  20:50/q03/p2 'Our Lord' is the predicate of an omitted 'He is,' and the relative clause modifies it. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī gives this alternative parse.
20:50/q04 What does God give each thing as its 'creation'? [أَعْطَىٰ كُلَّ شَىْءٍ خَلْقَهُۥ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether khalq is form, counterpart, useful endowment, provision, or existence, and how the objects of 'gave' are construed.)
  20:50/q04/p1 He gives each being its distinctive, fitting form and constitution. — reasons: Mujāhid distinguishes human and animal kinds and links measured creation to 25:2; al-Zajjāj and Abū Isḥāq stress the most useful form; Ibn ʿAbbās says God perfects what He creates. Al-Biqāʿī includes shape, size, color, temperament, benefits and effects; Asad includes inner nature and outer form. Ibn ʿĀshūr includes genera, species, classes and individuals. Al-Māwardī and al-Qurṭubī cite poetry using khalq for form. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr adds fitting mating and that creatures do not control their own creation, provision or mating. Al-Qurṭubī says the wording embraces all reported interpretations.
  20:50/q04/p2 He gives each creature a corresponding mate of its own kind. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ pairs human male with woman, sheep with sheep, and bull with cow; al-Kalbī gives man/woman and camel/she-camel; al-Thaʿlabī and Saʿīd give horse/mare and donkey/female. Al-Ṭabarī says unlike species are not paired and construes an omitted 'like' by analogy with calling a person a lion. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī's reported view cites 51:49. Ibn Qutayba includes sexed form within fitting form. Al-Ālūsī treats pairing as an example, questioning a literal universal.
  20:50/q04/p3 He gives each thing what suits, repairs, perfects or benefits it. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan and Qatāda describe fitness and well-being; al-Ḍaḥḥāk illustrates with useful organs. Al-Wāḥidī says its form suits its purpose. Al-Ālūsī says individuals remain complete in their rank despite defects, invoking 32:7 and 67:3 and Sunni teaching that wise creation is grace, not obligation.
  20:50/q04/p4 He equips creatures with the faculties and organs proper to their functions. — reasons: Al-Ḍaḥḥāk's examples are hands for grasping, feet for walking, tongues for speech, eyes for sight and ears for hearing. Al-Wāḥidī understands 'creation' as the things created in bodily organs; al-Qushayrī places the created faculties in limbs. Al-Rāzī adds the coordinated powers of a body.
  20:50/q04/p5 He gives every creature everything it needs or can use. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī and later grammarians connect this to reversing the two objects of 'gave'; al-Bayḍāwī says the advanced object is the point being explained. Al-Jubbāʾī includes worldly blessings and religious guidance; Abū Muslim includes what people possess. Ibn ʿĀshūr compares 6:99. Al-Ālūsī finds the usual object order clearer; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects narrowing 'everything' to blessings and 'creation' to creatures.
  20:50/q04/p6 He gives each creature inspired knowledge or craft. — reasons: Al-Māwardī and al-Qurṭubī report this further explanation.
  20:50/q04/p7 He gives each thing existence itself or its created reality. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr takes khalq as bringing into existence; al-Samīn reports a gloss that God originated each thing. A reported account in al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī makes existence accord with disposition; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects it as contextually remote.
  20:50/q04/p8 He gives each creature its soul. — reasons: The report from Ibn ʿAbbās places the soul before guidance to mate, food, drink and dwelling.
20:50/q05 How are the two objects of 'gave' parsed? [أَعْطَىٰ كُلَّ شَىْءٍ خَلْقَهُۥ · grammar] (turns on: Which noun receives the gift and which names the gift.)
  20:50/q05/p1 'Every thing' is the first object and 'its creation' the second. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī treats khalq as a passive participle with a pronoun returning to every thing; Ibn ʿĀshūr takes khalq as origination. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Nasafī and al-Samīn also connect this order to fitted form, and al-Samīn allows a mate. Al-Ṭabarī construes an omitted 'like' in the mate explanation.
  20:50/q05/p2 'Its creation' is the first object and 'every thing' the second, fronted. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī and others read the gift as everything creatures need or use; al-Bayḍāwī says the second object is advanced for emphasis. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the syntax allows it and cites 6:99. Al-Ālūsī reports al-Jubbāʾī's version but finds the usual parsing clearer.
  20:50/q05/p3 Khalq is a verbal noun: each thing receives the act of creation suited to its readiness. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports this proposed construction and judges it weak.
20:50/q06 How is khalqahu read, and what follows from the reading? [خَلْقَهُۥ · readings] (turns on: Whether the lām is quiescent or open, and whether the resulting clause leaves an object implicit.)
  20:50/q06/p1 With a still lām, khalqahu is a noun meaning 'its creation.' — reasons: Al-Jishumī says the readers otherwise have sukūn; al-Ṭabrisī says the other readers pronounce it so and explains the verse as each animal's own form.
  20:50/q06/p2 With an open lām, khalaqahu is a past verb, 'he created.' — reasons: Nusayr/Naṣr transmits al-Kisāʾī; reports also name ʿUmar, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Aʿmash, Ibn al-Sumayfaʿ, ʿAbd Allāh, al-Ḥasan, Abū Nuhayk, Ibn Abī Isḥāq, Ibn Nūḥ, Salām and Companions. The clause modifies 'all' or 'thing'; the second object of 'gave' is omitted, whether a creature's completion, benefit, needs, or God's bounty generally. Al-Kashf favors the absolute omission, while al-Ālūsī hesitates and prefers another analysis.
20:50/q07 Does 'every thing' cover individuals or kinds? [كُلَّ شَىْءٍ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether incidental defects and the past tense limit a universal statement.)
  20:50/q07/p1 It covers every individual in its fitting rank. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī rejects restricting it because incidental defects do not undo each thing's completeness in its own rank; he cites 32:7 and 67:3 and says past tense does not establish a restriction. Ibn ʿĀshūr allows distributive generality: each receives its own creation as each rider mounts their own animal. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī argues unqualified wording extends guidance to everything.
  20:50/q07/p2 It denotes kinds, or conventional generality limited to suitable recipients. — reasons: Some in al-Ālūsī invoke defective individuals; al-Ālūsī counters that even if many individuals do not yet exist, already existing kinds do not settle the past-tense objection. Ibn ʿĀshūr permits conventional generality; the animal-mate explanation likewise takes 'all' as abundance.
20:50/q08 To what does God guide creatures? [هَدَىٰ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether guidance is general practical direction, mating instinct, the universal movement toward an end, religious guidance, destiny, or a later faculty.)
  20:50/q08/p1 He directs each creature toward its needs, welfare and livelihood. — reasons: Mujāhid, Qatāda and Yahyā speak of learned benefit and taking what is needed; al-Suddī and Muqātil specify pasture and food, with grain and meat differing by animal. Ibn Qutayba includes avoiding danger; Ibn ʿAbbās lists food, drink, dwelling, birth and mates. Al-Rāzī adds refining materials, preparing medicines and food, bees' hexagonal cells, and offspring finding mothers' breasts. Al-Ḥasan connects guidance to what makes a thing sound; Abū Isḥāq and al-Zajjāj include mating within the broader welfare sense. Al-Qurṭubī says general wording encompasses the explanations, and Ibn Qutayba says their senses relate.
  20:50/q08/p2 He inspires creatures to find and approach their mates. — reasons: Al-Kalbī, al-Farrāʾ and Ibn Qutayba stress instinctive intercourse; al-Zajjāj says males approach females without instruction. Ibn ʿAbbās adds affection, joined hearts and marriage; Saʿīd ibn Jubayr glosses knowledge of how a male approaches a female. This is included in the broader welfare reading according to Abū Isḥāq and al-Zajjāj.
  20:50/q08/p3 He guides beings to their created ends and fullest attainable development. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī distinguishes instinct from choice and preservation from perfection; al-Burūsawī links equipment to purpose. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī defines guidance as showing or conveying a thing to its goal: innate powers produce its effects, as an embryo becomes a human; interacting systems are governance from another view. Al-Ālūsī reports natural powers in nonanimals and choice in animals, but objects that 'creation' already includes powers and this makes 'guided' metaphorical.
