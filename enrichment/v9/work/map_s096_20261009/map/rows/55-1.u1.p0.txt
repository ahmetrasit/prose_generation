## THE CURRENT MAP
55:1/q01 What does al-Raḥmān mean? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ · meaning] (turns on: The notes emphasize respectively the breadth of mercy, its first creative act, or what mercy means when attributed to God.)
  55:1/q01/p1 The One whose mercy is abundant, complete, boundless, and encompasses all beings. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj glosses it as abundant mercy and says earlier scriptures use the name. Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it an intensive form derived from mercy. Ṭabrisī says its mercy encompasses everything; Bursawī distinguishes general worldly provision from forgiveness for believers in the next life, illustrating common bounty with a Persian poem about God's table shared by friend and enemy. Elmalılı says mercy and beneficence are boundless and continuous. Study Quran gives 'Infinitely Good' and 'Loving' as renderings.
  55:1/q01/p2 The One whose foundational mercy gives beings existence and their first perfections. — reasons: Al-Rāzī distinguishes prior mercy in creation from later mercy in provision, understanding, and aid. Qāshānī describes the initial gift of entities and their first perfections. Study Quran says existence becomes manifest through this Name as universal mercy before creation.
  55:1/q01/p3 Mercy in God means willing good or bestowing favor, rather than creaturely affection. — reasons: Bursawī derives the creaturely sense from affection or spiritual inclination, analogized to the womb bending over what it carries. He reports al-Ghazālī's account of God's care through creation, guidance to faith and happiness, joy in the afterlife, and the vision of His face.
55:1/q02 How does al-Raḥmān relate to al-Raḥīm? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ · theology] (turns on: The distinction is explained either by the sequence and scope of divine acts or by the relation of names to the Essence and acts.)
  55:1/q02/p1 Al-Raḥmān denotes the prior mercy of creation; al-Raḥīm denotes later mercy through sustaining and helping. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says only God creates, whereas people can imitate later mercy by feeding, clothing, and helping. Study Quran contrasts the universal, unqualified mercy of al-Raḥmān with the more particular mercy of al-Raḥīm, which can describe humans.
  55:1/q02/p2 Al-Raḥmān relates to the divine Essence; al-Raḥīm refers to mercy extended through God's acts. — reasons: Study Quran states the distinction twice and cites 1:3; Bursawī calls al-Raḥmān the name of the Essence encompassing the names and attributes. Ibn Burjān, reported by al-Biqāʿī, calls al-Raḥmān the manifest aspect of Allah and the hidden aspect of al-Rabb, with these names disclosing and veiling the Essence.
55:1/q03 Is al-Raḥmān a name reserved for God, and is it a proper name? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ · theology] (turns on: Whether the term is a proper name, a name-like epithet, or a meaningful attribute; al-Rāzī tests the proper-name claim against Arabic morphology and permissible naming.)
  55:1/q03/p1 It is a divine name or name-like epithet that cannot rightly be given to anyone else. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj and al-Ḥasan with Quṭrub state its exclusivity; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the attribute belongs uniquely to God. Ṭabrisī contrasts raḥim and raḥīm, which may describe humans. Al-Rāzī calls it an epithet made name-like by frequent use and grounds exclusivity in God's sole power to create; calling the Yamāmī man al-Raḥmān is linguistically possible but transgressive and based on false belief. Study Quran grounds exclusivity in universal creation through the Name and says 17:110 equates it with Allah.
  55:1/q03/p2 Al-Raḥmān may be analyzed as a proper name, as Allah is by some, allowing direct invocation. — reasons: Al-Rāzī reports appeal to 17:110 and to the vocative 'O All-Merciful.' On a proper-name analysis, he says God's reservation of a name makes its application to idols transgressive even if language permits it.
  55:1/q03/p3 Treating al-Raḥmān as a proper name is weak; its divine restriction follows the meaning of a name or attribute. — reasons: Al-Rāzī objects especially to the vocative. He argues that Allah with its article is unlikely to be a primary proper name, since its initial hamza drops as hamzat waṣl; Ilāh or Lāh can instead be the original proper name with an added article, or mean 'the worshipped,' a meaning belonging to God alone. He contrasts names such as al-Karīm and al-Wadūd, usable of people, with al-Khāliq, al-Qadīm, and 'the worshipped,' which are not.
55:1/q04 Can al-Raḥmān be formed from letters at other sūrah openings? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ · interpretation]
  55:1/q04/p1 Yes: the initials alif-lām-rāʾ, ḥā-mīm, and nūn combine to make this divine name. — reasons: Al-Māwardī attributes the account to Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and Ibn ʿAbbās; al-Qurṭubī attributes it to Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and ʿĀmir al-Shaʿbī. The named transmitters vary.
55:1/q05 How is the opening name construed grammatically? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the following verb is the predicate and whether al-Raḥmān is counted as a separate verse.)
  55:1/q05/p1 Al-Raḥmān is the subject and 'taught the Qur'an' is its predicate, possibly followed by further predicates. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās says the predicate comes in the following construction. Al-Zamakhsharī makes subsequent verbal sentences successive predicates without conjunctions, each listing a favor and rebuking deniers; his analogy lists favors to Zayd before asking what kindness he could deny. Al-Rāzī calls the first analysis sounder. Abū Ḥayyān calls it the apparent parse. Al-Samīn ties this parse to not counting the name separately; Bursawī and Elmalılı say it begins the sentence.
  55:1/q05/p2 Al-Raḥmān is the predicate of an omitted subject: 'He/God is al-Raḥmān.' — reasons: Al-Rāzī calls this weaker because it depends on the standalone-verse reading. Al-Samīn and Ibn ʿĀshūr connect it to counting the word as a verse; Ṭabrisī says an elliptical 'God, the Merciful' preserves the verse-ending pattern. Elmalılı allows it because the word is a separate verse.
  55:1/q05/p3 Al-Raḥmān is a subject with an omitted predicate, such as 'Al-Raḥmān is our Lord.' — reasons: Al-Samīn offers this for the standalone-verse count; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the missing predicate is supplied by context. Abū Ḥayyān also reports an omitted-predicate parse.
55:1/q06 Why does the sūrah open with this divine name? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ · rhetoric] (turns on: The opening is explained through the sūrah's theme of mercy or as an answer to denial of the divine name.)
  55:1/q06/p1 The name announces mercy as the source of the worldly, otherworldly, bodily, and spiritual gifts counted in the sūrah. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī points to worldly and otherworldly blessings. Bursawī adds bodily and spiritual gifts and attributes them to the name encompassing all names. Al-Biqāʿī says it signals immense Qur'anic beneficence beyond enumeration (14:34); Kur'an Yolu calls religion the greatest gift to rational beings and the Qur'an its highest human gift (1:1). Ibn ʿĀshūr connects these favors with the Prophet as mercy to all worlds (21:107). Qāshānī says the broad initial Name grounds the attributes that follow.
  55:1/q06/p2 It names the deity whom the Meccans denied, making His subsequent works identify Him and rebuke them. — reasons: Muqātil and al-Wāḥidī say listing works leads people to recognize and worship Him alone after the denial in 25:60. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the bare name may expose their error like isolated opening letters, an analysis he calls strongest for such letters. He observes that no other sūrah opens directly with a divine name. Al-Sāmarrāʾī cites this opening as an example of compact wording that foregrounds the main purpose and a general class rather than individuating through repeated relatives and locatives (compare 2:255).
55:1/q07 What occasion is reported for the opening revelation? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ · reports] (turns on: The reports connect it either to Meccan denial of the name, a claim of human instruction, or refusal of the basmala at Ḥudaybiyya.)
  55:1/q07/p1 Meccans rejected or questioned the name al-Raḥmān, prompting God to identify Himself through His works. — reasons: Muqātil, reported by al-Wāḥidī, Ibn al-Jawzī, and Abū Ḥayyān, connects refusal to prostrate in 25:60 with the revelation; al-Wāḥidī says 17:110 occasioned a claim that only Musaylima of Yamāma bore the name. Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī, Ṭabrisī, Ibn ʿĀshūr, and Study Quran report the question 'What/who is the Merciful?' or denial after hearing it invoked. Ṭabrisī also cites 17:110; Study Quran cites 13:30 for their disbelief and says some asked about the Prophet's frequent invocation. Ibn ʿĀshūr conditionally places the sūrah after al-Furqān if this is its occasion.
  55:1/q07/p2 It answers Meccans who claimed that a human taught Muhammad. — reasons: Al-Baghawī reports this alternative occasion.
  55:1/q07/p3 It was revealed at the Ḥudaybiyya treaty when Suhayl ibn ʿAmr and others refused the basmala. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān reports a Medinan occasion; Ibn ʿĀshūr reports it from Ibn ʿAbbās as the basis for a Medinan classification.
55:1/q08 What does the opening's link to teaching the Qur'an convey? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ · interpretation]
  55:1/q08/p1 Teaching and revealing the Qur'an are acts of mercy and the foremost divine favor. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī calls religion foremost and the Qur'an the summit, proof, and standard of heavenly books. Abū Ḥayyān says it heals hearts; Ibn Kathīr adds that God eases memorization and understanding for those shown mercy. Al-Biqāʿī says it clarifies divine pleasure and prohibitions and leads to the promised abode, citing 2:31; Bursawī calls it the basis of happiness, cites sayings about Qur'an-bearers and the best people learning and teaching it, and says it contains the realities of earlier scriptures. Ibn ʿĀshūr reports al-Kashshāf's order of religious favor before creation and expression. Iṣlāḥī says mercy supplied revelation as education in place of demanded punishment; Kur'an Yolu calls the Qur'an religion's highest gift.
