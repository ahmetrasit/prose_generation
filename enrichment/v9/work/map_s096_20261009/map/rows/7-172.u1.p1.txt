7:172/q12 Why use these words and this order for the taking? [أَخَذَ مِن ظُهُورِهِمْ ذُرِّيَّتَهُمْ · rhetoric]
  7:172/q12/p1 The wording dignifies and strengthens the covenant and gives prominence to the descendants. — reasons: Alusi says 'took' signals selection and covenantal gravity; Biqai sees fathers' strong backs and human capacity; Alusi says delaying the object creates anticipation and keeps its pronoun after its antecedent.
7:172/q13 How did God make people witness against themselves? [وَأَشْهَدَهُمْ عَلَىٰٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether witnessing is personal verbal confession, reciprocal testimony, innate rational evidence, or immediate experience in a prior realm.)
  7:172/q13/p1 He made each confess personally that He alone is Lord. — reasons: Muqatil, Ibn Bahr and Bursevi stress self-testimony; Tabatabai says God showed people the reality of their dependent selves. The preposition can mean witness against oneself.
  7:172/q13/p2 Some descendants testified to the acknowledgment of others. — reasons: Tabari prefers this apparent meaning if the disputed Prophetic wording is unsound; Ibn Abbas and Ubayy are also cited. A pause at bala is then generally unsuitable.
  7:172/q13/p3 God established innate knowledge and rational signs, so their selves testify by their condition. — reasons: Zamakhshari, Nasafi and others treat the encounter as representation; Ibn Kathir compares circumstantial witness in 6:130, 9:17, 100:7 and 14:34. Biqai includes miracles and messengers among the proofs.
  7:172/q13/p4 God made direct witnessing of His lordship possible in a prior realm. — reasons: Tabatabai reads the knowledge as immediate experience, with the self, tongue and heart aligned; some Imami reports say knowledge was fixed even when the scene was forgotten.
7:172/q14 What does 'Am I not your Lord?' assert? [أَلَسْتُ بِرَبِّكُمْ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the confirmation is a figurative depiction of evidence or an actual communication in an unseen mode.)
  7:172/q14/p1 It is a confirmatory question demanding acknowledgment of God's exclusive lordship. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj and Thalabi call it confirmation; Bursevi and Alusi explain Lord as unrestricted master and nurturer. Ibn Ashur compares 46:34; Bint al-Shati notes interrogative laysa with prefixed bā emphatically affirms.
  7:172/q14/p2 The question is genuine creative communication, even if no ordinary voice or bodily organ was involved. — reasons: Tabatabai defines speech as conveying an intended meaning; God created a form able to answer.
7:172/q15 What did the descendants' answer affirm, and how was it given? [قَالُوا۟ بَلَىٰ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the primordial response was equally sincere in all or already differentiated the blessed and the wretched.)
  7:172/q15/p1 They acknowledged God alone as Lord and their own servanthood, worship and obedience. — reasons: Some reports include the prohibition of shirk, a pledge to obey, and God's provision; Islahi stresses lordship and providence rather than bare deity.
  7:172/q15/p2 The blessed answered freely and sincerely, while the wretched answered reluctantly or deceptively. — reasons: Al-Suddi and others connect this with willing and unwilling submission in 3:83; Bursevi's Najmiyya places the forerunners, right and left in different spiritual modes.
  7:172/q15/p3 All answered truly or had a universal capacity to acknowledge God. — reasons: Study Quran reports rejection of reluctant testimony and prefers the verse's shared possibility of recognition; Tabatabai says heart and tongue were one in the prior realm.
7:172/q16 Why did they answer with 'balā', and what would 'naʿam' mean? [بَلَىٰ · grammar] (turns on: Whether response follows the question's negative form, its affirmative intent, or ordinary usage allowing an ambiguous naʿam.)
  7:172/q16/p1 Balā cancels the negative wording and unambiguously affirms God is Lord; naʿam would confirm the negation and mean disbelief. — reasons: Al-Farra and reports from Ibn Abbas stress the contrast; Burhan and Itqan give the general rule for negative or confirmatory interrogatives.
  7:172/q16/p2 Naʿam could affirm the intended affirmative sense of a confirmatory question, though balā follows its negative form. — reasons: Al-Suhayli and others object to declaring naʿam disbelief; Samīn cites poetic use. Burhan distinguishes genuine inquiry from confirmation.
  7:172/q16/p3 Naʿam is ambiguous after a confirmatory negative question; balā is the safe explicit answer. — reasons: Al-Saffar, Ibn Hisham and Alusi distinguish affirmation of spoken negation from intended affirmation; Alusi prefers that Ibn Abbas meant insufficiency, not unbelief.
  7:172/q16/p4 The alif of balā is original, added after bal, or a feminine ending. — reasons: Itqan and Alusi list the three morphological accounts; the feminine analysis appeals to imāla.
7:172/q17 Who says 'we have witnessed'? [شَهِدْنَآ · referent] (turns on: Where the descendants' quoted speech ends, whether an unspoken command to angels is supplied, and how the purpose clause attaches.)
  7:172/q17/p1 The descendants themselves say it, confirming their own acknowledgment or witnessing one another. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and Ubayy are cited; Tabarsi calls this apparent because angels have not been mentioned. Ibn Ashur calls it reinforcement of balā.
  7:172/q17/p2 The angels say it after being commanded to witness. — reasons: Al-Kalbi supplies an omitted divine command; on this account a pause at balā is apt. Tabarsi objects that angels are not named.
  7:172/q17/p3 God, or God together with the angels, declares His witness. — reasons: Al-Suddi names God and angels; some give God alone. Tabari conditionally favors a raised report but doubts its wording because reliable routes stop at Ibn Amr.
  7:172/q17/p4 It begins a new divine report, 'We testify,' anticipating Judgment-day speech. — reasons: The author of the Nazm ends the covenant dialogue at balā and makes the next clause God's present witnessing; al-Jurjani invokes a poetic present use of shahida.
  7:172/q17/p5 Other witnesses include Adam or the seven heavens and earth. — reasons: Ubayy's version has God call them as witnesses; Study Quran notes variants naming Adam, heaven, earth and God.
7:172/q18 Where may one pause around 'balā' and 'we have witnessed'? [بَلَىٰ شَهِدْنَآ · grammar] (turns on: Identification of the speaker of shahidnā and the resulting syntactic attachment of an taqūlū.)
  7:172/q18/p1 Pause after balā because the descendants' speech ends there. — reasons: This follows readings that make angels or God speak shahidnā; Wāḥidī, Qurtubi and Ibn al-Jawzi explain the division. Burhan notes a general preference for stopping at balā.
  7:172/q18/p2 Do not pause after balā because shahidnā is the descendants' continued speech. — reasons: Wāḥidī says the purpose clause then attaches to 'He made them testify'; Tabari's mutual-witness reading needs the confession to continue.
7:172/q19 How does 'lest you say' attach grammatically? [أَن تَقُولُوا۟ · grammar] (turns on: The speaker of shahidnā and whether the suppressed element is a negation, fear or aversion, or an implied remembering command.)
  7:172/q19/p1 It gives the purpose of witnessing or taking the covenant: lest people later plead ignorance. — reasons: Kufans supply omitted lā, compared with 16:15 and al-Qutami's poem; Basrans supply a noun or verb expressing aversion or fear, compared with 4:176. Ibn Ashur supplies a causal lām and implied negation.
  7:172/q19/p2 It attaches specifically to 'He made them testify,' when descendants speak shahidnā. — reasons: Abu Bakr calls this clearer; Wāḥidī says attaching to the descendants' 'we testified' would make them the excuse makers, though Samīn allows one group to testify about another.
  7:172/q19/p3 It depends on an implied command to remember, mention, or explain the covenant. — reasons: Alusi finds this elegant, while calling attachment to witnessing the apparent syntax.
7:172/q20 How is 'that you say' read? [أَن تَقُولُوا۟ · readings] (turns on: Whether the clause continues third-person narrative or turns to those addressed in the covenant dialogue.)
  7:172/q20/p1 Most readers use second-person taqūlū, directly addressing the covenant people. — reasons: The direct divine question motivates the shift. Makkī prefers it for sound meaning and majority use; Ibn Ashur calls it change of addressee rather than iltifāt.
  7:172/q20/p2 Abu Amr and other reported readers use third-person yaqūlū, continuing narration about descendants. — reasons: Abu Ali cites preceding third-person wording and their response; Ibn Abbas, Sa'id ibn Jubayr and others are additionally named. Both verbs in 7:172–173 usually agree in person.
7:172/q21 What ignorance is forestalled by 'we were heedless of this'? [إِنَّا كُنَّا عَنْ هَٰذَا غَٰفِلِينَ · referent] (turns on: Whether 'this' points to a remembered primordial event or to recognized evidence of the Creator.)
