 letter names behave when made into proper names? [ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ · grammar] (turns on: Masculine and feminine usage, inflection, shortening and lengthening vary by form.)
  2:31/q35/p1 Letter and particle names vary in gender and take special extensions or doubling when made names. — reasons: Sībawayh reports Yūnus and poetic masculine/feminine agreement for Kāf and Mīm; vowel choice, declension, doubled weak endings, and extensions of Lā, Fī, Dhā, Mā, Zāy and spelled letters preserve usable forms. He distinguishes paused spelling sounds from inflected lexical names and gives examples from al-Khalīl and poetry.
  2:31/q35/p2 A masculine bearer of a feminine name is usually diptote, but common or morphologically masculine forms may decline. — reasons: Sībawayh lists ʿAnāq, ʿAqrab, feminine collectives and numerals as diptote; Dh irāʿ and wind adjectives can decline, with Kurāʿ debated. Poets attest adjectival wind usage; he contrasts ʿAnūq, Nisāʾ, and forms usable of both sexes.
  2:31/q35/p3 Species epithets name a kind, while personal names single out known individuals. — reasons: Sībawayh contrasts Abū al-Ḥārith and Thaʿāla with Zayd, explains why wild animals seldom have individual names and domesticated animals do, and compares definite species compounds with indefinite Ibn Labūn and Ibn Makhāḍ. Poetry and Abū ʿAmr's analysis bear on article use and declension.
2:31/q36 How are weak endings and demonstratives pronounced in pause? [هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ · grammar] (turns on: Sībawayh records varying pause hāʾ usage.)
  2:31/q36/p1 Pause hāʾ clarifies weak or clipped endings in selected forms, including hāʾulāʾ. — reasons: Sībawayh discusses pronoun yāʾ, Hī and Hū, clipped interrogative mā, vocatives, and hāʾulāʾ and hahunā, contrasting fully inflected endings and examples where hāʾ is omitted; speaker variation and possible confusion with possessive hāʾ explain limits.
2:31/q37 How do Arabic verbs govern objects and complements? [وَعَلَّمَ · grammar] (turns on: Transitivity, order and adverbial complements.)
  2:31/q37/p1 A transitive verb governs an object; verbal nouns, times and places may also function as governed complements. — reasons: Sībawayh describes active and passive participles, verbal nouns, fronted objects, and event types; temporal complements are closer to verbs than places because verbs encode time. He calls direct ḏahabtu al-Shām anomalous and cites Saʿīda ibn Juʾayya's poetry.
2:31/q38 How do inflected and fixed Arabic endings differ? [ءَادَمَ هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ · grammar] (turns on: Sībawayh separates changing case or mood endings from built forms.)
  2:31/q38/p1 Nouns and imperfect verbs have changing case or mood endings; built forms retain fixed endings. — reasons: Sībawayh gives eight paired pronunciations and details noun cases, imperfect moods, past and imperative forms, dual and plural markers, diptotes, gender, number and definiteness. Qur'an 16:124 exemplifies an imperfect with lām; adjective and broken-plural patterns govern diptote behavior.
2:31/q39 What is knowledge, as invoked by Adam's lesson? [وَعَلَّمَ · theology] (turns on: Theological, Muʿtazilite and philosophical definitions differ; al-Rāzī argues for its self-evidence.)
  2:31/q39/p1 Knowledge is what makes a knower know, or true apprehension or disclosure of the known. — reasons: Al-Ashʿarī, al-Qāḍī Abū Bakr, al-Isfarāyīnī, Ibn Fūrak and al-Qaffāl offer formulations. Al-Rāzī tests each for circularity, exclusion of divine or non-action knowledge, and obscurity.
  2:31/q39/p2 Knowledge is certain belief producing tranquility of soul. — reasons: The Muʿtazila give this account; Rāzī's associates object that belief is distinct from knowledge and divine knowledge has no soul's tranquility. The Muʿtazila answer with a common element of belief.
  2:31/q39/p3 Knowledge is a form or image in the soul corresponding to the object. — reasons: Philosophers and al-Ghazālī compare cognition to a mirror image. Rāzī objects from absent objects, abstract relations, nonexistents, perception's location, heat and the philosophers' own account of mirrors.
  2:31/q39/p4 Knowledge is too immediately evident to define by something clearer. — reasons: Al-Rāzī argues everyone directly knows their existence and place; knowing a relation requires knowing its terms. Failed definitions may reflect this extreme obviousness.
  2:31/q39/p5 Belief varies from certain true understanding to false certainty, doubt, expectation and illusion. — reasons: Al-Juwaynī divides immediate, inferred and imitative certainty and uncertain belief; al-Rāzī says flaws remain to be discussed.
2:31/q40 How do Arabic terms for knowing differ? [وَعَلَّمَ ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ · meaning] (turns on: Different terms describe perception, memory, reasoning, conjecture, skill and wisdom.)
  2:31/q40/p1 Terms such as perception, awareness, conception, preservation, remembrance and understanding describe stages or modes of knowing. — reasons: Al-Rāzī distinguishes idrāk, shuʿūr, taṣawwur, ḥifẓ, tadhakkur and dhikr, raising a paradox about commanded remembrance and answering from introspective capacity. He further defines fahm, fiqh, ʿaql, dirāya and ḥikma, distinguishing divine from human usage.
  2:31/q40/p2 Knowledge may be certain, conjectural, intuitive, experienced or inferred by signs. — reasons: Al-Rāzī surveys yaqīn, thought, intuition, quick intelligence, first principles, deliberation, practical discernment, experience, opinion and physiognomy; he distinguishes praiseworthy evidence-based conjecture from blameworthy weak conjecture and sensory image from sleeping apparition.
  2:31/q40/p3 Maʿrifa is particular knowledge, conceptual grasp, or recognition after earlier perception. — reasons: Al-Rāzī reports three analyses and says grasp of God's essence is beyond people; one may assent to the Necessary Being without such maʿrifa.
2:31/q41 What examples are given of knowledge's practical power? [وَعَلَّمَ ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ · reports] (turns on: The sources offer legal, rhetorical and advisory cases rather than a single event.)
  2:31/q41/p1 Legal learning and verbal ingenuity resolve difficult disputes or avert danger. — reasons: Al-Rāzī recounts Abū Yūsuf on theft and oaths, Yaḥyā ibn Yaʿmar on maternal descent, Abū Ḥanīfa on representation, vows, theft and marriage, al-Shāfiʿī on consensus, and poets and courtiers escaping punishment through wording. His reports also mention ingenious answers about a ruler's fear and learning.
  2:31/q41/p2 Knowledge and action improve souls, while ignorance and love of the world obstruct them. — reasons: Al-Rāzī compares learning with life, repentance and light, cites the ant's knowledgeable leadership and a trained dog's purified catch, and says the heart rules by knowledge. His verse and reports urge study, reflection, defense of truth and righteous practice.
2:31/q42 What reports about Adam's creation and the surrounding events are cited? [ءَادَمَ · reports] (turns on: Reports differ in details of earth gathering, body formation and sequence.)
  2:31/q42/p1 God formed Adam from mixed earth, producing human variety. — reasons: Reports from Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Suddī and the Prophet describe varied earth and the Angel of Death's handful; al-Qurṭubī says al-Tirmidhī grades the Prophetic report ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ. Bursevī adds Earth refusing Gabriel, Michael and Isrāfīl, weeping springs, forty cubits and return of bodies to their soil.
  2:31/q42/p2 Adam's clay body was formed, lay for a period, then received the spirit; Iblis refused prostration. — reasons: Al-Suddī's account gives sticky clay, forty years, Iblis testing the hollow body, Adam sneezing and praising God, haste for fruit, and all angels but Iblis prostrating. Biqāʿī debates whether creation and prostration precede the naming demonstration.
  2:31/q42/p3 A report places soil through Adam across Saturday to Friday, but its attribution and chronology are disputed. — reasons: Biqāʿī quotes Abū Hurayra's schedule; al-Mizzī notes a view that it came from Kaʿb. Al-Ṭabarī resolves a tension by saying days were named before their spans and Saturday's soil preceded Saturday's created reality.
  2:31/q42/p4 Some angels who questioned Adam repented, and some reports add punishment or the Hārūt and Mārūt trial. — reasons: Al-Rāzī records Ibn ʿAbbās's troops-of-Iblis narrative, private angelic boasting, a burning-fire report, and the story in which Hārūt and Mārūt's censure of humans led to testing with desire and wine.
2:31/q43 What parallel account of Adam and naming is reported from the Torah? [ءَادَمَ ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ · reports] (turns on: The Torah narrative differs in chronology and episodes.)
  2:31/q43/p1 The quoted Torah has Adam naming living creatures after their creation and before the woman is made from his rib. — reasons: Biqāʿī recounts heaven and earth, vegetation, lights, animals and humanity, then Eden, rivers and the forbidden tree; the serpent's deception, expulsion, cherub and Adam's 930 years follow. He explains cherub and compares textual versions.
