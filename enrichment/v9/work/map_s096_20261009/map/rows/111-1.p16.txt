[TABATABAI:111:1-4/r5] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Abu Lahab, the Prophet's uncle, was persistently hostile and abusive; after the first public invitation he told the Prophet to perish, and this sura answered him.
[TABATABAI:111:1-4/r6] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · بعضهم | reports | Some say Abu Lahab was his given name despite its form as a kunya; other reported names are Abd al-Uzza and Abd Manaf.
[TABATABAI:111:1-4/r7] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | prefers | The kunya is preferable to his personal name because Abu Lahab mocks him as bound to Hell's flames; Abd al-Uzza was avoided because al-Uzza is an idol's name.
[TABATABAI:111:1-5/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · موسى بن جعفر عليه السلام | reports | When the sura was revealed, Umm Jamil approached angrily with a stone; the Prophet told Abu Bakr she could not see him, and said God had placed a barrier between them.
[TABATABAI:111:1-5/r2] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Comparable versions of the Umm Jamil account occur through several Sunni chains.
[TABATABAI:111:5/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · مجمع البيان | reports | Al-Majma' asks whether Abu Lahab was still obligated to believe and could have done so without disproving the threat; it answers that faith remained obligatory and the threat was conditional on disbelief.
[TABATABAI:111:5/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | rejects | The author says God's decisive decree does not cancel choice: He wills human acts as voluntary, so Abu Lahab could choose faith and escape the punishment decreed for his unbelief.
[TABATABAI:111:5/r5] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The same pattern applies to declarations that Quraysh disbelievers will not believe and to verses about hearts being sealed. | mentions 2:6, 36:7
[TABATABAI:111:5/r6] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · ابن عباس | reports | After the warning to nearest kin, the Prophet asked Quraysh whether they would believe a warning of imminent attack; when they affirmed, he warned them of punishment and Abu Lahab cursed him, prompting the sura.
[TABATABAI:111:5/r7] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · ابن عباس | reports | Al-Tabataba'i says Sa'id ibn Jubayr also transmits the Ibn Abbas account in the sura commentary, without linking it to the nearest-kin verse.
[TABATABAI:111:5/r8] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · طارق المحاربي | reports | At the Dhul-Majaz market, he saw Muhammad inviting people to profess God's unity while Abu Lahab followed, bleeding his legs and calling him a liar.
[ASAD-NOTES:111:1#n1/r1] ASAD-NOTES, d. 1412 · author | holds | Abu Lahab’s real name was Abd al-Uzza.
[ASAD-NOTES:111:1/r1] ASAD-NOTES, d. 1412 · Baghawi; Muqatil; Zamakhshari; Razi | reports | Abu Lahab’s nickname meant “He of the Flame” and referred to his handsome, glowing face; because it predated Islam, Asad says it was not pejorative. | mentions 17:13
[ASAD-NOTES:111:intro/r1] ASAD-NOTES, d. 1412 · author | holds | This very early Meccan surah, sixth in revelation order, takes its name from its final word and addresses Abu Lahab’s hostility, rooted in arrogance, wealth, and resistance to human equality and judgment by merit; Ibn Zayd is cited by Tabari.
[BINTSHATI-IJAZ:p442/r1] BINTSHATI-IJAZ, d. 1419 · author | holds | The form tabbat is unique in the Quran; the root appears in 111:1 and 40:37, exhausting the Quran’s use of this root. | mentions 40:37
[BINTSHATI-IJAZ:p442/r2] BINTSHATI-IJAZ, d. 1419 · ابن الأثير | reports | Ibn al-Athīr explains tabban laka in the report about Abū Lahab as a curse of destruction, with an unspoken verb understood.
[BINTSHATI-IJAZ:p442/r3] BINTSHATI-IJAZ, d. 1419 · ابن السكيت | reports | Ibn al-Sikkīt places the word among curses invoking affliction and grave misfortune.
[BINTSHATI-IJAZ:p442/r4] BINTSHATI-IJAZ, d. 1419 · author | holds | Tattbīb is glossed as loss, but that is approximate: tab in Arabic chiefly means destruction and is not used for material commercial loss, unlike khusr, which extends to worldly and religious loss. | mentions 40:37
[BINTSHATI-IJAZ:p50/r1] BINTSHATI-IJAZ, d. 1419 · الباقلاني | reports | Al-Bāqillānī rejects the claim that 111:1 is poetry: a brief coincidence with a poetic meter does not make prose poetic, which requires balanced parts and deliberate metrical construction.
[BINTSHATI-IJAZ:p50/r2] BINTSHATI-IJAZ, d. 1419 · الجاحظ | reports | Al-Jāḥiẓ argues that ordinary speech, such as asking someone to close a door or calling out to buy eggplant, can accidentally scan; only intentional poetic craft counts as poetry.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1-2:kommentar/r1] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The sura develops from a curse against an enemy called ‘Father of Flame’; all verses except verse 5 refer back to him pronominally.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1:anmerkung/r1] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The construction ʾabū plus genitive marks a person by a defining trait, as in “father of guests”; plural aṣḥāb plus genitive works similarly, for example in 85:4. | mentions 85:4
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1:anmerkung/r10] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | prefers | The reported reading wa-qad tabba by Ibn Masʿūd, al-Aʿmaš, and Ubayy b. Kaʿb clarifies the ambiguous text secondarily; the received wa-tabba is the more difficult reading and should stand.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1:anmerkung/r11] CORPUSCORANICUM · Fischer | reports | Fischer reads the second tabba as a past assertion that doom has already struck, intensifying the preceding curse; Paret instead favors emphatic repetition, comparable to qutila in 74:19–20. | mentions 74:19, 74:20
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1:anmerkung/r2] CORPUSCORANICUM · Horovitz | holds | Horovitz suggests “Father of Flame” is not an actual name but a figurative kunya coined by the sura to mark its bearer as hellbound.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1:anmerkung/r3] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | If the figure is Muhammad’s uncle ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā, his “Father of Flame” epithet likely arose after this sura; the phrase should be translated to retain its link to the fire imagery in verse 3. | mentions 111:3
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1:anmerkung/r4] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The sura may instead describe a generic archetypal damned man of flames, like the wretched one burning in 92:15; identifying him with ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā may be a later anecdotal development in early Quran reception. | mentions 92:15
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1:anmerkung/r5] CORPUSCORANICUM · Rubin | holds | Rubin takes yad as merit, translating the line as Abū Lahab’s merits being lost, based on a report that ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā earned merit through a vow to tend al-ʿUzzā’s shrine.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1:anmerkung/r6] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | prefers | Although yad can mean merit, Fischer’s synecdoche of Abū Lahab’s two hands for his whole person is more likely, since the dual fits poorly with an abstract meaning.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1:anmerkung/r7] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The objection that repeating tabba makes the hands and the person distinct fails: the final phrase most likely emphatically repeats the curse.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1:anmerkung/r8] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | Classical exegetes gloss tabba as either losing or perishing; Rubin sees “lose” as original and “perish” as secondary overlay, but that depends on his reading yad as merit.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:1:anmerkung/r9] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | With Fischer’s synecdoche, both glosses are possible nuances rather than competing meanings.
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:3-5:kommentar/r2] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | Following Neuwirth’s observation, the pointed name Abū Lahab is only fully articulated by verse 3’s nār ḏāt lahab; the fire scene retrospectively motivates ‘Father of Flame.’
[CORPUSCORANICUM:111:3-5:kommentar/r3] CORPUSCORANICUM · author | holds | The text first leaves Abū Lahab enigmatic, then motivates the name through the hellfire description, creating rhetorical tension.

[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p504/r2] ISLAHI-TADABBUR · Imām Farāhī | reports | Farāhī says al-Naṣr, al-Ikhlāṣ, and the two refuge sūrahs form a harmonious sequence, so al-Lahab's placement between them must have a purpose.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p505/r1] ISLAHI-TADABBUR · author | holds | The conquest sermon maps its three clauses to al-Kāfirūn, al-Naṣr, and al-Lahab: God's oneness, fulfilled promise, and defeat of enemies. | mentions 17:81
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p505/r2] ISLAHI-TADABBUR · author | rejects | Most exegetes call al-Lahab Makkan because it answered Abū Lahab's insult at Ṣafā, but Islahi says this dating argument is unsound.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p506/r1] ISLAHI-TADABBUR · Exegetes | reports | Exegetes report that at Ṣafā the Prophet warned his gathered Quraysh relatives of punishment and Abū Lahab retorted with a curse; they say this occasioned the sūrah.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p506/r2] ISLAHI-TADABBUR · author | rejects | Islahi accepts that the incident occurred but says it is improbable that the sūrah answered Abū Lahab and his wife's misconduct.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p507/r1] ISLAHI-TADABBUR · author | holds | The revealed phrase is no tit-for-tat curse; Islahi says it carries no ring of condemnation.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p507/r2] ISLAHI-TADABBUR · author | holds | The opening predicts the end of Abū Lahab's political dominance, the defeat of his allies, and the loss of his pomp and wealth.
[ISLAHI-TADABBUR:vol-9-english:p507/r3] ISLAHI-TADABBUR · author | holds | The opening is a prediction rather than a statement of a present fact.
