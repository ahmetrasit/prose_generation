[ELMALILI:111:1/r24] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The predicted earthly death came about about fifteen years after the revelation, but the verse means enduring bodily and spiritual loss through afterlife punishment too.
[ELMALILI:111:1/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | “May his hand wither” first suggests paralysis, but figuratively asks that he be bankrupt, powerless, and frustrated in every effort.
[ELMALILI:111:1/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | prefers | “May he be ruined” or “ruined he was” better captures tabāb; Abu Lahab’s later confirmed ruin makes the declarative sense especially apt.
[ELMALILI:111:1/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The surah’s placement after the Prophet’s victory and Abu Lahab’s death signals fulfilled foretelling: his ruin only deepened, while the wording still condemns him.
[ELMALILI:111:1/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | His two hands stand for every means he tried to use, for or against, in worldly and religious affairs; all his efforts against the Prophet and Islam turned against him.
[ELMALILI:111:1/r7] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Abu Lahab is singled out between the surahs about the Prophet and God despite other unnamed enemies; this marks him out among the criminals, echoing Yasin.
[ELMALILI:111:1/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | His special distinction is more lamentable because he was the Prophet’s paternal uncle yet opposed him; the contrast exalts the Prophet and divine truth above lineage.
[ELMALILI:111:1/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The epithet “Abu Lahab,” literally father of flame, represents fire-spewing enemies of Islam, so his ruin exemplifies theirs and points to God completing His light.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5#2/r15] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The opening prediction was fulfilled: because Abu Lahab did not believe in God’s oneness, he could not benefit from the Prophet’s intercession; status and wealth did not rescue him.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5#2/r16] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | He did not repent of saying “tabban lak”; he died in ruin, and the author seeks refuge from an evil ending.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5#2/r17] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | After contrasting the good and ruined endings, the text points to monotheism and sincerity, then refuge in God, as the path to a good end.
[ELMALILI:111:2-5#2/r18] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The conclusion prepares for Ikhlas by saying that knowing God through pure monotheism and seeking His protection is the remedy for ruin.
[ELMALILI:v1p89/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Fuzûlî | reports | Fuzuli curses scribes who ruin fine writing; his Arabic stanza opens with a deliberate echo of the 'tabbat yada' wording.

[QUTB-ZILAL:111:1/r11] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | The surah answers the public war Abu Lahab and his wife waged; God undertakes the battle on behalf of His Messenger.
[QUTB-ZILAL:111:1/r12] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | Tabb means ruin, destruction, and severance; the first occurrence is a supplication, the second confirms that it has come to pass.
[QUTB-ZILAL:111:1/r13] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | In a single short opening verse the curse is issued and fulfilled, ending the conflict and drawing its curtain.
[QUTB-ZILAL:111:1/r2] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | Abu Lahab was Muhammad’s uncle, ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā son of ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib; his bright face gave him the nickname Abu Lahab.
[QUTB-ZILAL:111:1/r3] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | Umm Jamil, Arwa daughter of Harb ibn Umayya and Abu Sufyan’s sister, supported her husband’s sustained campaign against Muhammad and the message.
[QUTB-ZILAL:111:1/r4] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · ربيعة بن عباد الديلي | reports | Rabiʿa recalls accompanying his father as a youth, seeing Muhammad preach to tribes while Abu Lahab followed, warning them against abandoning al-Lat and al-ʿUzza for his alleged innovation.
[QUTB-ZILAL:111:1/r5] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | reports | Ibn ʿAbbas reports Muhammad warned Quraysh from al-Baṭḥāʾ; Abu Lahab retorted, “Was that why you gathered us?”, after which the surah was revealed.
[QUTB-ZILAL:111:1/r6] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | reports | A variant says Abu Lahab shook his hands and cursed Muhammad for the rest of the day, again prompting revelation of the surah.
[QUTB-ZILAL:111:1/r7] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | When Banu Hashim agreed under Abu Talib to protect Muhammad out of clan solidarity, Abu Lahab defected to Quraysh and joined their boycott document.
[QUTB-ZILAL:111:1/r8] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | Abu Lahab had betrothed his sons to Muhammad’s daughters before the mission, then ordered them to divorce the women to burden Muhammad.
[QUTB-ZILAL:111:1/r9] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | reports | It is reported that Umm Jamil carried thorns and put them in Muhammad’s path.

[IBNASHUR:111:1/r13] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The sura rebukes and threatens Abu Lahab for his insult, and threatens his wife for supporting him and hating the Prophet.
[IBNASHUR:111:1/r14] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Opening with destruction signals the sura's reproach and threat, a fitting opening for a satire, like a poem that begins by cursing its target.

[IBNASHUR:111:1/r17] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · أبو تمام | reports | Abu Tammam begins a satire with fire, disgrace, harm, and destruction, another opening that forecasts denunciation.
[IBNASHUR:111:1/r18] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · أبو بكر بن الخازن | reports | Abu Bakr ibn al-Khazin adapted that kind of forecast in the opening of a congratulatory poem on a birth.
[IBNASHUR:111:1/r19] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Tabb means loss and destruction; the wording is both a prayer and a rebuke, returning Abu Lahab's own insult against Muhammad as fitting recompense.
[IBNASHUR:111:1/r2] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Some codices and tafsirs name it Surat al-Masad; al-Itqan mentions only this and Surat Tabbat.

[IBNASHUR:111:1/r22] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The hands are named because they caused the Prophet's harm; this resembles a curse wishing stones in the mouth of someone who says something offensive.

[IBNASHUR:111:1/r25] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Abu Lahab was ʿAbd al-ʿUzza ibn ʿAbd al-Muttalib, the Prophet's uncle, and was also known as Abu ʿUtba after his son.
[IBNASHUR:111:1/r26] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | One reported explanation says he was called Abu Lahab in pre-Islamic times for his handsome, radiant face; Tariq's report and a similar report from Rabiʿa ibn ʿAbbad al-Dayli indicate the nickname was well known.

[IBNASHUR:111:1/r29] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The kunya also permits a pointed implication that he belongs to Hell, since lahab is the flame of a fire once its smoke has dissipated.
[IBNASHUR:111:1/r3] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | A number of exegetes call it Surat Abi Lahab, understood as the sura mentioning Abu Lahab.
