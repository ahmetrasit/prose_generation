28:15/q25 Who does 'he' refer to, and what does the Satan description say? [إِنَّهُۥ عَدُوٌّۭ مُّضِلٌّۭ مُّبِينٌۭ · referent]
  28:15/q25/p1 The pronoun is Satan, the clear enemy and misleader of Adam's descendants. — reasons: Tabari says Satan beautifies ugly acts; Ibn Ashur says the omitted object of enmity is Adam and his offspring. Samarai distinguishes Satan's abiding and active misleading from God's abiding and renewed guidance.
28:15/q26 How are 'enemy, misleader, manifest' parsed? [عَدُوٌّۭ مُّضِلٌّۭ مُّبِينٌۭ · grammar] (turns on: Whether mubīn specifies Satan's enmity, his misguidance, or both.)
  28:15/q26/p1 They can be successive predicates or adjectives; mubīn describes manifest enmity. — reasons: Nahhas lists both parses; Qurtubi calls the enemy description a second predicate. Al-Alusi treats mubīn as a second adjective of enemy and derives it from intransitive abāna.
  28:15/q26/p2 Mubīn modifies 'misleader' or both 'enemy' and 'misleader'. — reasons: Ibn Ashur takes it with misleading; Al-Alusi reports a shared-adjective or implicit-wording analysis.
28:15/q27 What theological implication is drawn from Satan's role? [مِنْ عَمَلِ ٱلشَّيْطَٰنِ مُّضِلٌّۭ · theology] (turns on: Whether divine causation is compatible with calling the event Satan's work.)
  28:15/q27/p1 The killing and misguidance are not created by God; Satan's censure as misleader excludes God's causing misguidance. — reasons: Jishumi argues against determinists, saying attribution and blame would be incoherent if God created the deed or misleading. Razi reports the Mu'tazilite argument with parallels from Joseph, Moses' companion, and Adam's parents.
  28:15/q27/p2 God creates causes when He wills an outcome, including the one fatal blow. — reasons: Qushayri says many blows may fail to kill, while this one fulfilled the decreed death.
28:15/q28 How does this incident connect to the wider Moses narrative? [* · links] (turns on: How the event functions as the narrative hinge from maturation in Egypt to accusation and migration.)
  28:15/q28/p1 The killing begins the chain leading to Moses' departure for Midian and later prophetic calling. — reasons: Biqai sees the transition from preparation to migration; Ibn Kathir says it fulfilled the divine decree; Tabatabai situates the episode after maturity. Ibn Ashur says the empty hour explains lack of witnesses and prepares for the accusation in verse 19.
  28:15/q28/p2 Moses' palace lay outside or at the edge of the city, illuminating the later messenger from its far side. — reasons: Bursevi and Tabatabai link the entry route to 28:20.
  28:15/q28/p3 The next day's appeal and accusation show how the killing became known. — reasons: Sa'id ibn Jubayr's report says another opponent recognized Moses as yesterday's killer, leading to flight; Ibn Ashur links the accusation to 28:19.
  28:15/q28/p4 Moses' present act followed his maturation and developing Israelite identity. — reasons: Ibn Abbas says his standing curtailed Egyptian oppression. Ibn Ashur suggests his mother disclosed his origins, explaining solidarity with Israelites; Razi says wisdom and knowledge preceded this killing.
28:15/q29 What inward reading is given to the city, combat, and killing? [* · inward] (turns on: Whether the spiritual killing signifies overcoming passion or an excessive countermeasure.)
  28:15/q29/p1 The city is the body; quiet psychic powers let the heart aid intellect against passion. — reasons: Qashani maps the two men to intellect and passion, and Moses' intervention to practical wisdom and angelic aid overcoming satanic fancy.
  28:15/q29/p2 The killing warns against treating one extreme vice with its opposite rather than with merciful justice. — reasons: Qashani lists greed checked by lethargy, miserliness by extravagance, and wastefulness by stinginess.
28:15/q30 What surrounding report details explain Moses' situation and reaction? [* · reports]
  28:15/q30/p1 Moses' anger at the Copt's assault led to the fatal blow, witnessed only by God and the Israelite. — reasons: Ibn Abbas gives this detail; Ibn Ashur cites Exodus saying Moses looked around and buried the man in sand.
  28:15/q30/p2 Moses was called Pharaoh's son. — reasons: Ibn Ashur reports this as one account of his status.
  28:15/q30/p3 Wahb reports God pardoned Moses because the slain man had never acknowledged God as creator or provider. — reasons: The report says even one acknowledgment would have brought punishment.
28:15/q31 How does Moses' subsequent language relate to this incident? [* · links]
  28:15/q31/p1 His plea after the death echoes Adam's self-wronging. — reasons: Abu Hayyan notes that the bāʾ in the later phrase may mark an oath or depend on an omitted plea for protection through God's favor; he cites 7:23.
28:15/q32 How does fāʾ express the sequence from blow to death? [فَوَكَزَهُۥ فَقَضَىٰ عَلَيْهِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether fāʾ conveys causal succession or a looser discourse order.)
  28:15/q32/p1 Fāʾ marks causal result: the blow caused death. — reasons: Burhan says fāʾ can combine coordination and causation; Itqan compares Adam receiving words and repenting. Burhan allows wāw too against Ibn Jinni's stricter claim; an objection concerns whether learning must follow teaching, with a reply that learning needs intervening conditions.
  28:15/q32/p2 Fāʾ may mark discourse or semantic order, not necessarily chronological sequence. — reasons: Itqan reports al-Farra's denial of ordering and a counterreading of an example as intention of destruction before arrival.
28:15/q33 What semantic range of ʿalā is discussed? [عَلَىٰ حِينِ غَفْلَةٍۢ · meaning]
  28:15/q33/p1 It can mark elevation, accompaniment, origin, causation, or time; here the temporal sense is relevant. — reasons: Itqan cites separate Quranic examples for the non-temporal senses and 2:102 for the temporal one.
28:15/q34 What related lexical examples are supplied outside this verse? [* · meaning]
  28:15/q34/p1 Maʿīn may mean visible or flowing water. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba derives it from ʿayn; Ibn Faris cites water flowing and a horse running far.
28:15/q35 What is reported about the revelation of Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ? [* · sciences]
  28:15/q35/p1 All of al-Qaṣaṣ is Meccan. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi reports Hasan, Ata and Ikrima for this view.
28:15/q36 What reading authority is ascribed to Nāfiʿ? [* · readings]
  28:15/q36/p1 His Medinan reading was selected by agreement among leading readers and became the community's authoritative practice. — reasons: Nāfiʿ describes choosing readings agreed by two of five. Ibn Wahb and Malik call it Sunnah; Ibn Mujahid reports public and specialist convergence, and Layth dates his leadership to 110 AH. An unnamed report places it already by 100 AH; a disciple says his reading saved assembling one independently. His final admonition echoes 8:1.
28:15/q37 What pronunciation of ṣād and neighboring consonants does Sībawayh describe? [* · readings]
  28:15/q37/p1 Before dhāl, unvowelled ṣād approaches or becomes zāy, while moving ṣād is best kept clear. — reasons: Sībawayh says emphatic ṣād can approximate the nearest voiced zāy but full conversion is also spoken; moving ṣād cannot be replaced. Sīn in the same place shifts to zāy, and shīn or jīm may approximate it, with examples.
28:15/q38 How are certain defective nouns and vocatives formed? [* · grammar] (turns on: The distinct morphological analogies for dual formation and vocative shortening.)
  28:15/q38/p1 Defective four-letter nouns form duals and plurals with yāʾ, whether their alif replaces a root or is augmentative. — reasons: Sībawayh explains both by analogy with corresponding verbs, including wāw-root forms and examples ḥublā and maʿzā.
  28:15/q38/p2 Quadriliteral-like wāw and yāʾ behave as added, radical-like letters and remain under vocative shortening. — reasons: Sībawayh cites qanūr and habīj, their capacity for further augmentations, and avoidance of deleting two letters; Manṣūr may shorten to Manṣ.
28:15/q39 How do commands, supplications and topicalization affect case and fāʾ? [* · grammar] (turns on: Whether preposed nouns are command objects, topics, or resumptions of implied prior discourse.)
  28:15/q39/p1 An object before a command is normally accusative under an overt or implied verb. — reasons: Sībawayh covers direct and prepositional objects, amā clauses, and third-person commands; commands require a verb more strongly than interrogatives. A nominative topic is possible with an understood context, but 'Zayd fa-ḍribhu' cannot be an ordinary subject; its object pronoun must remain.
  28:15/q39/p2 A relative or generalized topic with conditional force can take fāʾ in its predicate. — reasons: Sībawayh gives all-man and relative-clause examples and cites 2:274. He distinguishes this from a simple Zayd topic.
  28:15/q39/p3 Revelatory law verses begin with raised nouns under an implied context, though accusative readings exist. — reasons: Sībawayh analyzes 24:2, 5:38 and 4:16 through an antecedent topic about ordinances and notes some readers' accusative forms.
