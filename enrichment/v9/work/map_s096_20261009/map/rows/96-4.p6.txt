[IBNASHUR:96:1/r7] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · عائشة، أبو موسى الأشعري | reports | Sound reports from Aisha establish that these first five verses were first, delivered to Muhammad in Hira on Ramadan 17, year 40 after the Elephant; Abu Musa and most exegetes agree.
[IBNASHUR:96:4/r1] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | A qalam is a piece of reed trimmed smooth and slit lengthwise at its tip; when dipped in ink it writes on paper and similar surfaces. Ibn Ashur refers to 3:44. | mentions 3:44
[IBNASHUR:96:5/r3] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The two teachings point to three routes of knowledge: written records and reading books, oral instruction and dictation, and discoveries or inventions produced by reasoning; writing lets learning pass across distance and generations.
[IBNASHUR:96:5/r6] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The verses signal God's concern that the revealed Qur'an be written down; Muhammad therefore appointed scribes for revelation from the beginning of his mission.
[IBNASHUR:96:5/r7] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The command to read followed by teaching through the pen hints that Muhammad remained unlettered, a feature completing the Qur'an's miraculous character, as 29:48 says. | mentions 29:48
[IBNASHUR:96:5/r8] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | These are the final verses of the five that were first revealed to Muhammad in the cave of Hira.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p435/r3] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · عائشة | reports | Aisha narrates in the two Sahihs that Gabriel came to Muhammad in Hira, commanded him three times, embraced him forcefully each time, then recited the opening through 'what he did not know.'
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p435/r4] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | Aisha's narration is directly from the Prophet; her account that he returned trembling with the passage means he retained and recited what had been dictated.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p435/r5] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | Khadija's request that Waraqa hear him further indicates Muhammad recited the revealed passage immediately; Gabriel's repeated command repeated God's wording to accustom him to reading.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p439/r2] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | The clause is a new explanatory statement: Rabbuka is subject, with either 'who taught by the pen' or 'taught humanity what it did not know' as predicate.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p439/r3] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | Without Aisha's revelation report, the statement answers Muhammad's presumed question about reading without literacy: the one who taught writing can teach what he does not know.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p439/r4] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | With the hadith, the passage answers his 'I cannot read': God can teach by dictation, instruction, or inspiration, just as He taught Adam the names without literacy.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p439/r5] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | Repeating 'your Lord' instead of using a pronoun stresses the care already implied in 'recite in your Lord's name' and marks reading as a special gift of lordship; it also leads into al-Akram.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p439/r6] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | Al-Akram intensifies generosity rather than comparing God with another; generosity is giving benefit, and God's favors begin with creation and its sustaining provision.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p439/r7] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | The opening five verses gather divine attributes: lordship implies existence and oneness, creation and teaching indicate acts, and al-Akram entails perfection and freedom from deficiency.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p440/r1] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | The two objects of 'taught by the pen' are omitted, understood as teaching writers or teaching people to write; Arabs prized literacy and associated it with scriptural communities.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p440/r2] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · أبو حية النميري | reports | Abu Hayya's line about a Jew's hand supports the association of writing with Jews and skilled script.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p440/r3] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | reports | A second poem boasts of learning writing though its speaker says he is no writer; writing first appeared among Arabs in Anbar, then reached Hijaz through Harb ibn Umayya, who learned from Aslam ibn Sidra, taught by Muramir ibn Murra; Himyar earlier used Musnad.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p440/r4] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | Placing 'who taught by the pen' between subject and predicate reassures the Prophet that the one who taught writing can teach him recitation though he cannot write.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p440/r5] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | The pen is a sharpened reed used to draw ink on paper; the discussion also recalls pens cast to decide who would care for Mary. | mentions 3:44
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p441/r5] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | The initial command and reference to pen teaching imply that Muhammad remained unlettered, an attribute completing the Qur'an's miracle, as another verse states. | mentions 29:48
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p441/r6] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | These are the final five verses of the passage first revealed at Hira; the following discussion on verses 6–10 begins a new subject.
[TABATABAI:96:1-7/r10] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The ba is causal: God taught reading or writing, with the pen serving as the means; the clause may be circumstantial or a fresh statement.
[BINTSHATI:v2p13/r2] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | She presents the surah’s opening as the first revelation, noting that Ibn Ishaq and al-Tabari report no disagreement about that account.
[BINTSHATI:v2p13/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · عائشة أم المؤمنين | reports | Aisha recounts Muhammad’s true dreams, retreat to Hira, Gabriel’s command and three embraces, and the recitation of the opening five verses, which remained fixed in his heart; no grading is stated.
[BINTSHATI:v2p13/r4] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · عائشة أم المؤمنين | reports | Aisha continues that Muhammad saw Gabriel in the sky, returned to Khadija, and was reassured because his character included kinship, truthfulness, trustworthiness, hospitality, and helping in adversity; no grading is stated.
[BINTSHATI:v2p14/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الفخر الرازي | reports | Al-Razi also transmits that only al-Alaq’s first five verses came at the start; the remainder came after public opposition and were joined to the surah by God’s command.
[BINTSHATI:v2p14/r4] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · أبو حيان | reports | Al-Bahr al-Muhit says the surah is Meccan and its opening was the first revelation at Hira, as established in Bukhari and elsewhere; it judges the claims that al-Muddaththir or the Fatiha came first unsound.
[BINTSHATI:v2p15/r1] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | The reports in al-Tabari and the Prophetic biography stop the first-revelation account at verse five, supporting the view that the opening alone was first revealed.
[BINTSHATI:v2p22/r2] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | Knowledge is apprehending a thing as it truly is, and its opposite is ignorance; she had surveyed Quranic uses of knowledge in her commentary on al-Takathur. | mentions 102:3
[BINTSHATI:v2p22/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | The pen is the instrument of writing, as in the opening oath of Surat al-Qalam. | mentions 68:1
[BINTSHATI:v2p22/r4] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الطبري | reports | Al-Tabari interprets teaching by the pen as teaching humans writing they had not known.
[BINTSHATI:v2p22/r5] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الزمخشري | reports | Al-Zamakhshari likewise takes it as writing, which records sciences, wisdom, ancient reports, and revealed books, enabling religion and worldly life to function; Abu Hayyan quotes him.
[BINTSHATI:v2p22/r6] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الفخر الرازي | reports | Al-Razi discusses the honor of the pen and the wisdom in its creation; Ibn Qayyim devotes a chapter to the pen’s rank and benefits.
[BINTSHATI:v2p23/r1] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | reports | Continuing the preceding discussion of Ibn Qayyim, the author lists his twelve pens, from the primordial decree and revelation through juristic, medical, political, accounting, judicial, testimonial, dream, historical, linguistic, and polemical writing.
[BINTSHATI:v2p23/r2] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الفخر الرازي | reports | Al-Razi reads a symbolic sequence: creation from a clot signals rational proofs of divine power and wisdom, while writing by the pen signals revealed evidence, law, and prophethood.
[BINTSHATI:v2p23/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · النيسابوري | reports | Al-Nishaburi offers an interpretation like al-Razi’s symbolic contrast between rational evidence and knowledge of revelation.
[BINTSHATI:v2p23/r4] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | rejects | She sets aside symbolic readings and takes the pen as the writing tool that records, preserves, and transmits knowledge across time, place, and generations.
[BINTSHATI:v2p23/r5] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | The Quran draws an unlettered Prophet and people’s attention to the pen as a Creator’s sign and to acquired knowledge as a human distinction; revelation connects knowledge with Adam’s honor and stewardship. | mentions 2:30
[BINTSHATI:v2p24/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · ابن عباس | reports | Ibn Abbas is reported to gloss the verse as teaching writing by the pen; Abu Hayyan judges this to be an explanatory reading.
