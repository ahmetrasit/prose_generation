5:27/q11 What does qarrabā mean here? [قَرَّبَا · meaning] (turns on: Whether it simply means drawing near or the established act of offering worship.)
  5:27/q11/p1 Each brought a devotional offering to seek God's nearness. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī excludes literal movement in place; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls qarrabā a lexicalized sacrificial verb derived from qurbān. The Study Quran notes it can describe charity. Al-Aṣmaʿī cites transitive taqarraba bi- to mean qarraba.
  5:27/q11/p2 A proposed reflexive derivation means they offered it and thereby drew near. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī's analysis treats taqarraba bi- as the response to qarraba; Abū Ḥayyān objects that both have the same agent and are not passive counterparts. Al-Samīn finds the derivation remote but challenges the Shaykh's rule.
5:27/q12 What is a qurbān? [قُرْبَانًۭا · meaning]
  5:27/q12/p1 Anything offered to draw near to God, including sacrifice, charity, prayer, or another gift. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba and later lexicographers derive it from nearness; al-Māwardī calls it a pious act seeking mercy. Jishumī and al-Ṭabrisī compare royal courtiers. Ibn ʿĀshūr includes prayer. Kuranyolu distinguishes the broad lexical sense from the technical timed slaughter. The Study Quran stresses nearness as sacrifice's aim.
5:27/q13 Why is qurbān singular though two brothers offered? [قَرَّبَا قُرْبَانًۭا · grammar] (turns on: Whether the singular is a distributive, a collective noun, or an uninflected verbal noun.)
  5:27/q13/p1 The noun began as a verbal noun or collective, so it need not be dual. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī compares furqān and ʿudwān; al-Wāḥidī says verbal nouns are not dualized. Al-Samīn says it later named the offering itself and that singular form supports this history; al-Ṭabrisī allows generic or verbal-noun analysis.
  5:27/q13/p2 Each brother offered one offering; the singular is distributive. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī says the dual verb entails two offerings; Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī compares 24:4's distributive eighty lashes; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the noun is generic.
5:27/q14 What legal status do later Islamic animal sacrifices have? [قُرْبَانًۭا · law] (turns on: The dispute concerns the Eid sacrifice's obligation versus confirmed Sunnah.)
  5:27/q14/p1 The Eid sacrifice is obligatory for those meeting the conditions, in Abū Ḥanīfa's view. — reasons: Kuranyolu says this view invokes hadith and 108:2; its legal indication is probabilistic but sufficient for obligation. The rite was instituted in 2 AH by Qur'an, Sunnah and consensus; 37:107 supports legitimacy.
  5:27/q14/p2 The Eid sacrifice is a confirmed Sunnah in the majority view. — reasons: Kuranyolu says the hadith's “if you wish” clause allows choice; the Prophet's constant practice and reports of merit establish the Sunnah. Vowed sacrifices are obligatory, while others can be voluntary.
5:27/q15 What wider history of sacrifice is drawn from qurbān? [قُرْبَانًۭا · interpretation]
  5:27/q15/p1 Offerings across religions vary; revealed religions include animal sacrifice. — reasons: Kuranyolu lists plants, grain, money, animals and even human sacrifice across religions, cites 22:34–37 for animal sacrifice in revealed laws, contrasts Christian claims about Jesus with the Qur'an's rejection of human sacrifice and the ram given after Abraham's test. The Study Quran notes Hajj and Eid sacrifices and charitable distribution.
5:27/q16 What corresponds to the brothers' offering for this community? [قُرْبَانًۭا ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether prayer alone or the wider field of righteous action is intended.)
  5:27/q16/p1 Prayer is the God-fearing person's offering. — reasons: ʿAdī b. Thābit and others give this identification; al-Qurṭubī limits it to one form of worship. Abū al-Dardāʾ and Ibn ʿUmar say certainty of one accepted prayer or prostration would outweigh the world.
  5:27/q16/p2 Prayer, charity, fasting, and other righteous deeds perform the role of an offering. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī compares earlier sacrifice to charity and zakat, but says Muslims cannot immediately know acceptance. The Study Quran cites prayer, fasting and obligatory alms; its 22:36–37 comparison says reverence matters more than flesh. Al-Sulamī privileges divinely promised accepted deeds and prostration, citing 96:19. Al-Qurṭubī cites the hadith on obligatory and voluntary deeds drawing a servant near.
5:27/q17 What did each brother offer, and whose was accepted? [قَرَّبَا قُرْبَانًۭا فَتُقُبِّلَ مِنْ أَحَدِهِمَا وَلَمْ يُتَقَبَّلْ مِنَ ٱلْءَاخَرِ · reports] (turns on: Species and produce vary; one transmission reverses which brother was accepted.)
  5:27/q17/p1 Abel the shepherd offered a choice sheep or ram; Cain the farmer offered inferior grain or produce; Abel's was accepted. — reasons: Some add milk and butter, a white horned ram, a fat lamb/ewe, or Cain's darnel and eaten good kernel. Ibn ʿAbbās and the majority attest the sheep and crop contrast. The Qur'an leaves the brothers unnamed, a detail al-Biqāʿī and Ibn ʿĀshūr find unnecessary. Ibn ʿĀshūr notes the Torah's crops and firstborn flock.
  5:27/q17/p2 The Qur'an establishes only one accepted and one rejected offering; their details are secondary. — reasons: The Study Quran says it does not state why one was chosen; al-Biqāʿī treats the objects and identification as immaterial to the passage's purpose. Ibn ʿĀshūr says distribution of offerings is unknown.
  5:27/q17/p3 Abel offered a fat camel rather than a sheep; Cain brought poor wheat. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī reports the camel variant; Bursawī allows camel or ram.
  5:27/q17/p4 Cain may have offered a cow instead of grain. — reasons: The alternative is preserved in the earlier-scripture report cited by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr.
  5:27/q17/p5 A report reverses acceptance, making Cain's produce accepted. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr says Ibn Jarīr's attribution of this to Mujāhid is likely unpreserved; ʿAbd al-Razzāq's Qatāda report likewise says fire consumed Qābīl's offering while leaving the other.
5:27/q18 How was acceptance of an offering known? [فَتُقُبِّلَ مِنْ أَحَدِهِمَا وَلَمْ يُتَقَبَّلْ مِنَ ٱلْءَاخَرِ · reports] (turns on: Whether fire consumed the accepted or rejected offering, or acceptance was known another way.)
  5:27/q18/p1 Heavenly fire consumed or lifted the accepted offering and left the rejected one. — reasons: Many describe white, sometimes smokeless fire; the untouched offering was left for birds and beasts. Al-Rāzī prefers this by majority testimony; 3:183 attests an Israelite fire-sign. Al-Ṭabarī and others say there were no poor recipients, though Ibn Kathīr calls the rite an offering rather than charity.
  5:27/q18/p2 God revealed the verdict to Adam, or the brothers knew it by an unspecified sign. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr suggests revelation to Adam; al-Biqāʿī allows another indication besides fire; Ṭabāṭabāʾī notes the dialogue proves both knew but the verse does not say how.
  5:27/q18/p3 Fire consumed the rejected offering; the accepted one was lifted to heaven. — reasons: Mujāhid's reversal is reported by al-Ṭabrisī, al-Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān; al-Ṭabrisī finds the other sign more apparent, al-Rāzī follows the majority. ʿAbd al-Razzāq's report also has Qābīl's consumed.
5:27/q19 Why was one offering accepted and the other refused? [فَتُقُبِّلَ مِنْ أَحَدِهِمَا وَلَمْ يُتَقَبَّلْ مِنَ ٱلْءَاخَرِ · interpretation] (turns on: Sources distinguish material quality, inward piety or sincerity, resentment of God's decree, and unstated divine judgment.)
  5:27/q19/p1 Abel was pious and sincere, giving his best; Cain lacked piety or sincerity and gave his worst. — reasons: The best/worst contrast accompanies willingness versus reluctance in ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAmr; Ismāʿīl b. Rāfiʿ says Abel's heart was pure. Al-Bayḍāwī and Bursawī add Cain's resentment of God's judgment. Al-Qurṭubī calls Abel a believer and Cain an unbeliever; Ibn ʿĀshūr says Cain was sinful but rejects unbelief because he made an offering. Ibn Kathīr limits piety to the deed.
  5:27/q19/p2 Abel's greater piety or prayer, independently of the offering's material, explains acceptance. — reasons: Al-Māwardī records piety glossed as prayer against the alternative that material quality decided it; Ibn al-Jawzī similarly separates piety from best/worst property. 22:37 and the Prophetic gesture to the heart stress internal piety.
  5:27/q19/p3 The superior property given by Abel and inferior property given by Cain explains the outcome. — reasons: Ibn ʿUmar and most exegetes are named by al-Māwardī; Ibn al-Jawzī and al-Rāzī present this as an alternative explanation.
  5:27/q19/p4 The Qur'an leaves God's reason unstated. — reasons: The Study Quran explicitly observes this; al-Biqāʿī says only the evident verdict matters.
5:27/q20 What became of Abel's accepted animal? [فَتُقُبِّلَ مِنْ أَحَدِهِمَا · reports] (turns on: Accounts vary on the identity of Abraham's intended sacrificial son and on the animal's sojourn.)
  5:27/q20/p1 It grazed or remained in Paradise and became the ram ransoming Abraham's son. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās specifies forty years in Paradise; Ibn Kathīr grades his chain good and finds the identification fitting. Ismāʿīl b. Rāfiʿ says the beloved lamb was kept there. Saʿīd b. Jubayr and others report it was later sent down. Some reports merely say “Abraham's son” or “the one to be sacrificed.”
  5:27/q20/p2 The ram ransomed Ishmael. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī explicitly names Ishmael; Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports the same and later rejects these and similar reports as weak or corrupted.
  5:27/q20/p3 The ram ransomed Isaac. — reasons: Al-Māwardī attributes this identification to al-Ṭabarī.
5:27/q21 Why are acceptance and the accepted brother left unnamed? [فَتُقُبِّلَ مِنْ أَحَدِهِمَا وَلَمْ يُتَقَبَّلْ مِنَ ٱلْءَاخَرِ · rhetoric]
  5:27/q21/p1 The passive and anonymity focus on the rejection and moral point
