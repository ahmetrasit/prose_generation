## THE CURRENT MAP
55:2/q01 Who is the teacher named by this clause? [عَلَّمَ · referent]
  55:2/q01/p1 God, the All-Merciful, is the teacher of the Qur’an. — reasons: Muqatil and Qushayri identify Him as the one denied by disbelievers; Qushayri reports that the Real alone acquainted servants with it. Al-Wahidi says God identifies Himself through His works to establish His oneness. Bursevi attributes teaching ultimately to God even where teachers and memorizers mediate, comparing Adam’s names, David’s armor, Jesus’s learning and al-Khidr’s hidden knowledge (2:31; 21:80; 3:48; 18:65).
55:2/q02 What does ʿallama mean here? [عَلَّمَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the verb denotes instruction and enabling learning or is derived from ʿalāma, a sign.)
  55:2/q02/p1 God instructed in the Qur’an, imparting its wording and meanings. — reasons: Ibn Ashur distinguishes doubled ʿallama, instruction, from form IV aʿlama, merely informing; Maʿn ibn Aws’s archery line and 5:110 and 36:69 illustrate teaching a discipline. Al-Alusi includes reliable understanding, layered insights and cosmic events. Elmalılı uses Turkish ta‘lim etti to retain this sense alongside the sign interpretation.
  55:2/q02/p2 God made the Qur’an easy to learn, remember and recite. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj and transmitters give this gloss; Qurtubi, al-Alusi and Study Quran cite the repeated refrain in 54:17, 22, 32, 40. Al-Imam permits a figurative sense of enabling knowledge; al-Kashifi says God eases Muhammad’s learning so he can teach others. Other definitions take teaching as preparing or awakening the mind to conceive meanings.
  55:2/q02/p3 God made the Qur’an a sign or miracle for reflection and proof of prophethood. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari reports this alternative; Qurtubi says a sign concerning what people worship. Al-Samin derives the verb from sign, as do al-Alusi, Elmalılı and Kuranyolu; they relate it to the preceding surah’s moon miracle (54:1). Abu Hayyan calls it remote because the clauses enumerate favors, and al-Alusi calls the derivation far-fetched despite its cited parallels. Elmalılı’s translation deliberately keeps the possibility open.
55:2/q03 Who received the Qur’an taught here? [عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · referent] (turns on: The unnamed first recipient may be inferred from the next verse, prophetic transmission, angelic descent, or an inward account of spirits.)
  55:2/q03/p1 Humanity or the community is the intended learner. — reasons: Al-Samin prefers humanity because the teaching is general and 55:3 names the human; al-Alusi finds this clearest in context. Ibn Atiyya says people learn and memorizers and understanders gain special merit. Al-Wahidi’s alternative says this community memorized it. Al-Wahidi, Qushayri, Ibn ʿAta and Bursevi describe Muhammad passing it to his community; Qushayri compares Adam’s taught names (2:33), prayer’s required Fatiha and worshippers’ intimate address to God. Al-Tabrisi says the teaching supplies needed religious wisdom; Study Quran says human beings were created for knowledge.
  55:2/q03/p2 Muhammad is the immediate learner, then he conveys or teaches the Qur’an to others. — reasons: Al-Kalbi and Ibn al-Saib specify Muhammad and onward teaching; Qurtubi says he conveyed it to everyone. Al-Wahidi and Ibn Ashur tie God’s instruction of Muhammad to denial of a human teacher. Bursevi specifies Gabriel as the outward intermediary, and elsewhere includes divine mysteries (4:113). Al-Sulami and Qushayri compare Muhammad’s instruction with Adam’s names and 4:113. Al-Samin and al-Alusi list Muhammad as a possible omitted object.
  55:2/q03/p3 Gabriel or the angels are learners. — reasons: Al-Razi says angels could receive it before human creation, citing the preserved book touched by the purified and its descent from the Lord (56:77–80). Abu Hayyan and al-Samin list Gabriel; al-Alusi adds the nearest angels and notes that this entails the Qur’an being God’s speech.
  55:2/q03/p4 Spirits received it directly before embodiment, while bodies or souls later learn through them or intermediaries. — reasons: Al-Sulami reports both bodies following spirits and al-Husayn’s distinction between directly instructed spirits and mediately instructed souls. Qushayri says children learn before hearing a person and likens this prior love to a poem.
55:2/q04 How is the object structure of ʿallama parsed? [عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · grammar] (turns on: Which derivation and argument structure ʿallama has, and which of its two objects is unstated.)
  55:2/q04/p1 The verb takes two objects; Qur’an is the second, and the first, the learner, is omitted. — reasons: The Master of Composition compares ordinary ‘Zayd fed food.’ Abu Hayyan identifies the missing object as first; al-Samin calls this stronger, and Ibn Ashur analyzes the doubled form. Al-Alusi and Elmalılı explain that omission leaves the benefit general, allowing Gabriel, Muhammad or humanity as learner. Al-Alusi says the Imam may merely have demanded another object beside Qur’an, so his supposed error is uncertain.
  55:2/q04/p2 An alternative parse describes the second object as omitted. — reasons: Al-Razi is attributed this analysis by Abu Hayyan; al-Alusi reports the Imam similarly, though he leaves open that the Imam intended an additional object rather than a true reversal.
  55:2/q04/p3 On the sign derivation, the verb needs no omitted learner. — reasons: Elmalılı makes this grammatical consequence explicit; al-Samin gives the sign derivation.
55:2/q05 How do the opening clauses attach to al-Raḥmān? [عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the clauses are coordinated predicates or one begins an independent construction.)
  55:2/q05/p1 ʿAllama al-Qur’an and the following clauses are successive predicates of al-Raḥmān. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari takes the opening name as subject and the verbs as predicates; Abu Hayyan calls it the apparent analysis. Al-Samin says the clauses appear predicative and prefers this over Abu al-Baqa’s independent-clause proposal.
  55:2/q05/p2 The opening name has an omitted predicate or the teaching begins a fresh thought. — reasons: Abu Hayyan acknowledges a parse supplying an omitted predicate; Study Quran says the teaching clause may begin a new thought.
  55:2/q05/p3 ‘Created man’ is a fresh clause, with ‘taught him’ possibly a prospective circumstance with implied qad. — reasons: Al-Samin reports Abu al-Baqa’s parse but calls it unobvious, saying al-Zamakhshari gives only the successive-predicate parse.
55:2/q06 Why are the opening favor clauses unjoined? [عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · rhetoric]
  55:2/q06/p1 Each clause stands independently in a close enumeration of blessings and rebukes deniers. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari illustrates the rebuke with a beneficiary helped through poverty, lowliness and scarcity who is then asked which kindness he denies; conjunctions resume when clauses are more naturally paired. Al-Samin stresses the list’s close connection.
55:2/q07 What does the past tense in ‘taught’ convey? [عَلَّمَ · rhetoric]
  55:2/q07/p1 It expresses God’s care and invitation. — reasons: Al-Sulami attributes this reading to al-Wasiti.
55:2/q08 Why is Qur’an teaching named before human creation? [عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · rhetoric] (turns on: The sources explain priority by rank and purpose, celestial chronology, or pre-eternal inward teaching.)
  55:2/q08/p1 It is the supreme religious blessing and is the purpose for which humans were created. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari says the purpose precedes creation in presentation; al-Razi says expression and creation explain the means of teaching. Al-Samin calls teaching the cause of creation despite later timing. Abu Hayyan calls it religion’s foundation and salvation’s path. Al-Samarrai cites 51:56, worship as the purpose of humans and jinn, and says order may follow purpose, merit and context. Al-Baydawi calls Qur’an the foundation of religion and law; Ibn Ashur says religion reforms this life and saves in the next.
  55:2/q08/p2 Qur’an teaching belongs to the celestial order and preceded humanity’s creation. — reasons: Al-Razi pairs teaching the Qur’an above with teaching expression below, naming the celestial first; his angelic reading invokes 56:77–80. Al-Samarrai says Qur’an, as God’s speech and knowledge, existed before humanity, so it precedes in existence as well as purpose.
  55:2/q08/p3 Pre-eternal preparedness or teaching precedes embodied human creation, while expression follows it. — reasons: Ibn ʿAta says teaching was known eternally and appeared at creation; Bursevi’s unnamed authority contrasts the unified Qur’anic origin with differentiated Furqan (25:1) and says expression depends on embodiment. Bursevi explains the stated order from that distinction.
55:2/q09 What is the Qur’an designated here? [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · referent] (turns on: Whether ‘Qur’an’ names the recited revelation or an inward unified human capacity.)
  55:2/q09/p1 The verbal revelation to Muhammad, recited in worship and inimitable even at a single sura. — reasons: Ibn Ashur gives this definition and says the named Qur’an signals Islam and the favors of prior religions (6:92). Al-Zamakhshari calls it the summit and standard of heavenly books, and al-Biqaʿi calls it written and recited, gathering good and distinguishing confusion.
  55:2/q09/p2 In an inward reading, it is complete human preparedness or Qur’anic intellect, containing realities, qualities and rulings. — reasons: Al-Qashani contrasts its unified origin with its final differentiation as Furqan (25:1); Bursevi reports a related account of pre-eternal readiness.
55:2/q10 How is ‘Qur’an’ read? [ٱلْقُرْءَانَ · readings]
  55:2/q10/p1 Ibn Kathir and others omit the hamza in Qurān, with no change of meaning. — reasons: Qiraat-ER says the pronunciation is smoother and reflects Hijazi hamza softening.
