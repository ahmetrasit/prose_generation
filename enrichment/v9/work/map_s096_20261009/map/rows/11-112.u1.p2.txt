11:112/q24 What part of Hūd was said to have made him gray, and why? [فَٱسْتَقِمْ · reports] (turns on: Whether the report points to the command's difficulty, previous nations' fates, the community's risk, or shared eschatological material.)
  11:112/q24/p1 The command to stand firm as ordered, because fulfilling it is especially demanding. — reasons: A dream in Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Rāzī, Qurṭubī, and Qāshānī has the Prophet identify this verse rather than the destroyed peoples' stories; Qāshānī says an ʿārif saw the dream, while Qurṭubī names Abū ʿAlī al-Sarrī in al-Sulamī's account. Ibn ʿĀshūr and Kuryolu likewise cite the identification; Bayḍāwī links Hūd's effect to the difficulty of uprightness. Al-Shihāb says the verse summarizes Hūd's guidance and raises fear of falling short of its demands without impugning infallibility.
  11:112/q24/p2 Fear that earlier peoples' punishment might overtake his community. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls this the well-known explanation; al-Ālūsī's expanded report includes what befell earlier nations, while Ṭabāṭabāʾ says the grave address after their destruction heightens the warning.
  11:112/q24/p3 Concern for his community because their steadfastness is vulnerable. — reasons: Elmalılı connects the singular positive command and plural prohibitions to risk among the community and says this rhetorical feature aged the Prophet.
  11:112/q24/p4 The Resurrection scenes common to the named suras may explain the effect. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾ proposes this because al-Wāqiʿa has no equivalent command to stand firm; al-Kashf as reported by Ālūsī likewise questions singling out uprightness when the sister suras do not mention it.
11:112/q25 What other reports illustrate steadfastness and moderation? [فَٱسْتَقِمْ وَلَا تَطْغَوْا۟ · reports] (turns on: The reports illustrate distinct settings and outcomes rather than competing accounts of one event.)
  11:112/q25/p1 A report from ʿUmar says extreme prayer and fasting do not attain steadfastness if people prefer two over one. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī reports it under the Prophet's authority through ʿUmar; the note does not explain the final phrase further.
  11:112/q25/p2 The Prophet taught that religion is easy and urged moderate practice without overburdening oneself. — reasons: Abū Hurayra's report in Baghawī adds rejoicing and seeking help at times of day and night.
  11:112/q25/p3 Earlier believers held firm under torture, and the Prophet promised Islam's eventual completion. — reasons: Khabbāb's report in Biqāʿī describes saws and iron combs, safety on the road from Ṣanʿāʾ to Ḥaḍramawt, and the Prophet's rebuke of impatience.
11:112/q26 What readings and analyses of lammā in the neighboring verse are reported? [* · readings] (turns on: The vocalization of in and kull, the doubled mīm, whether lammā is a noun, particle, contraction, or ellipsis, and the limits of Arabic usage.)
  11:112/q26/p1 The transmitted tanwīn reading can treat lammā as a verbal noun or adjective describing comprehensive fulfillment. — reasons: Abū ʿAlī compares aklan lammā; al-Ṭabrisī says an adjectival analysis needs kull annexed to an indefinite noun and has no governing element for a circumstantial reading. Ibn Jinnī likens emphatic fulfillment to qiyāman; a proposal derives it from gathering on the pattern faʿlā, like tatrā, with al-Zuhrī's tanwīn supporting it.
  11:112/q26/p2 Lammā may contract li-man mā, dropping one of three mīms. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ cites a poem for this.
  11:112/q26/p3 Lammā may mean illā, an exception particle. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj proposes it; al-Farrāʾ and Abū ʿAlī object that this use belongs to oaths. Al-Ṭabrisī reports a related reading wa-in kulla illā la-yuwaffiyannahum, allowing illā as extra with lightened in, and explains a poem's extra illā as saying time confuses its people and frustrates petitioners.
  11:112/q26/p4 Its doubled form may be an emphatically doubled light form. — reasons: Al-Māzinī proposes this; al-Zajjāj objects that Arabic only weakens a doubled form under poetic necessity, rather than doubling a light form. Al-Kisāʾī says he cannot explain the doubling, and Abū ʿAlī accepts the difficulty.
  11:112/q26/p5 An omitted phrase may explain it: each will be repaid for what they did. — reasons: ʿAlī ibn Abī al-Ṭayyib supplies wa-in kullan lammā ʿamilū la-yuwaffiyannahum rabbuka aʿmālahum and cites poetry illustrating ellipsis.
  11:112/q26/p6 With light in and nominative kull, it can be an adjective of indefinite kull or amount to each certainly being repaid. — reasons: Abū ʿAlī says this would ease the analysis, parallel to 43:35; al-Ṭabrisī says a definite annexation blocks it. Al-Ṭabrisī rejects proposals using inserted wa-in hum illā or explanatory kullan, and says a doubled mīm with either form of in yields none of the known senses of lam, ḥīn, or illā.
11:112/q27 What other neighboring word forms and meanings are noted? [* · meaning] (turns on: The notes concern separate neighboring words and give no dispute beyond dialect variation.)
  11:112/q27/p1 The readings of zulan are dialect variants, and the word is accusative as a time adverb. — reasons: Jishumī says the common zulafan has ḍamma on zāy and fatḥa on lām, while Abū Jaʿfar, Ibn Muḥayṣin, and Mujāhid vocalize it differently; he also gives its adverbial case.
  11:112/q27/p2 Mirya is doubt despite clear evidence; a share is an allotted portion; disagreement can be contradictory or validly varied. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī illustrates valid difference by judgments about facing the qibla and includes istiqāma as staying straight and transgression as excess in corruption.
  11:112/q27/p3 Readers vary the kāf vowel in tarkunū, with rounded kāf a dialect form for settling or being content. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports this alongside the final verb's readings.
11:112/q28 How does Abū ʿAmr pronounce tāʾ in wa-man tāba maʿak and comparable phrases? [وَمَن تَابَ مَعَكَ · readings] (turns on: Whether consonants are adjacent in connected speech and how the transmitted routes treat the following thāʾ.)
  11:112/q28/p1 He pronounces the tāʾ clearly in wa-man tāba maʿak. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī contrasts it with his assimilation of wa-man tāba min baʿdi ẓulmihi in al-Māʾida, arguing that immediate adjacency determines assimilation; he lists ten following letters and illustrates with bil-bayyināt thumma.
  11:112/q28/p2 Assimilation is transmitted in al-zakāt thumma and al-tawrāt thumma. — reasons: Ibn Ḥabash transmits this through al-Dūrī and al-Sūsī, and al-Dānī read both routes thus. Ibn Mujāhid prefers clear pronunciation because fatḥa after a quiescent consonant is light; his students transmit that too.
  11:112/q28/p3 Clear pronunciation is preferable in those two thumma phrases. — reasons: Ibn Mujāhid cites ease after a quiescent consonant; his pupils transmit it. The rival assimilated routes are reported by Ibn Ḥabash and al-Dānī.
  11:112/q28/p4 Assimilating the pronoun tāʾ in wa-idhā raʾayta thumma raʾayta is exceptional and rejected. — reasons: Ibn Shanabūdh alone transmits it; Ibn al-Jazarī rejects it as contrary to Abū ʿAmr's principles and favors clear pronunciation.
11:112/q29 How are quiescent hamzas like the one in fastāqim eased? [فَٱسْتَقِمْ · readings] (turns on: Whether connected quiescent word-initial hamza is treated like other medial hamzas and what the transmission records.)
  11:112/q29/p1 They are replaced by the homorganic long vowel and count as medial when joined to the preceding word. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī gives wāw after ḍamma, yāʾ after kasra, and alif after fatḥa; Abū ʿAmr, Abū Jaʿfar, and Warsh are reported to ease these cases without disagreement. He rejects Maghrebi distinctions that treat a word-initial hamza differently, since a quiescent hamza here occurs only in connected speech. He also rejects Ibn al-Bādhish's attribution of full articulation to Ibn Ghalbūn, his father, and Ibn Sahl, citing al-Tadhkira for agreed replacement.
  11:112/q29/p2 Full pronunciation is attributed to some transmitters. — reasons: Ibn al-Bādhish attributes it to Ibn Ghalbūn, his father, and Ibn Sahl; Ibn al-Jazarī rejects that attribution against al-Tadhkira.
11:112/q30 What happens to the hāʾ after easing hamza in cited forms? [* · readings] (turns on: Whether the incidental yāʾ after hamza easing affects the preceding hāʾ.)
  11:112/q30/p1 Some give the hāʾ a kasra by analogy with forms followed by yāʾ. — reasons: Abū Bakr ibn Mujāhid, Abū al-Ṭayyib ibn Ghalbūn, and his son apply this to anbihim and nabbiʾhum, comparing fīhim and yuʾtīhim.
  11:112/q30/p2 Most retain its ḍamma because the resulting yāʾ is incidental. — reasons: Ibn Mihrān, Makkī, al-Mahdawī, Ibn Sufyān, and most readers take this line; Ibn Ghalbūn accepts either pronunciation.
