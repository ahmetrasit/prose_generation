## THE CURRENT MAP
53:34/q01 What did he give a little of? [وَأَعْطَىٰ قَلِيلًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the small gift is the reported payment or ordinary wealth, verbal approval, initial faith or listening, temporary obedience, or a broader spiritual contribution; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the money reading needs a report.)
  53:34/q01/p1 Part of the money or property promised to someone who offered to bear his punishment; he withheld the balance. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says miserliness kept back the remainder; al-Thaʿlabī and others identify the payee as a guarantor. Al-Kāshifī attributes withholding to ignorance and stinginess. Al-Rāzī says the pledge was not legally owed, but promises are customarily kept and payment cannot transfer sins. Reports differ on whether al-Walīd had followed, accepted, or merely approached Islam; Study Quran's report reverses the proposed religious exchange, saying he offered a follower money to return to Quraysh religion.
  53:34/q01/p2 A small amount of wealth or charity, followed by withholding or curtailed spending. — reasons: Al-Ḍaḥḥāk calls it wealth; Ibn ʿAbbās says most was withheld. The ʿUthmān reports describe charity curtailed after a camel and gear were given to his foster brother, then resumed. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reads giving as spending. Bursevī says the property was meant as ransom from punishment. Al-Ālūsī allows either 'only a small amount' or 'only a little.'
  53:34/q01/p3 A little good by speech: praise of the Qurʾān or verbal assent, then silence or inward refusal. — reasons: Muqātil speaks of good offered with the tongue; al-Wāḥidī's ʿAṭāʾ and Muqātil specify praise of the Qurʾān, and al-Māwardī contrasts tongue with heart. Reports about Abū Jahl and al-ʿĀṣ likewise describe partial verbal agreement without faith.
  53:34/q01/p4 A brief act of listening to revelation or approach toward faith, then withdrawal. — reasons: Mujāhid describes giving of oneself through listening or enjoyment; Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān describes a little movement toward faith. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the latter follows the wording directly, whereas identifying money requires a report. Ibn ʿĀshūr records an inclination toward Islam stopped like digging at rock.
  53:34/q01/p5 A short period of obedience or good conduct, followed by disobedience or cessation. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās says he obeyed briefly and then disobeyed; Ibn Kathīr attributes the obedience reading also to Mujāhid, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, ʿIkrima, Qatāda and others. Study Quran warns that stopping good because past deeds seem sufficient can make earlier deeds fruitless.
  53:34/q01/p6 A little of what was due for the afterlife, then abandonment of revelation for worldly life. — reasons: Al-Rāzī expressly prefers this broader reading to limiting the words to al-Walīd's payment.
  53:34/q01/p7 A small investment of life's capital in the spiritual path, later spent on worldly pleasure. — reasons: Bursevī says the seeker tires, returns to the sensual ego, and thereby shows lack of readiness for union.
53:34/q02 How does ‘gave’ govern the following question? [وَأَعْطَىٰ · grammar]
  53:34/q02/p1 Its first object is omitted; the question ‘Does he have knowledge of the unseen?’ is its second object. — reasons: Al-Samīn says omission of the first object makes the wording concise.
53:34/q03 What does akdā mean here? [وَأَكْدَىٰٓ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the verb denotes cessation after giving, difficult refusal, reproach that spoils a gift, dwindling yield, or the gift's smallness; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ uses the coordination and poem's explicit bi-mannihi to separate these.)
  53:34/q03/p1 He stopped, cut off, or withheld what he had begun giving or doing. — reasons: Abū ʿUbayda, Ibn Qutayba, Mujāhid, al-Zajjāj, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Kisāʾī, al-Farrāʾ, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Abū Isḥāq, and later commentators trace it to a digger encountering an impenetrable hard layer in a well and giving up; Ibn Qutayba, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn extend it to any unfinished endeavor. Al-Ḥuṭayʾa's verse illustrates a small gift followed by cessation while praising generosity. Other Arabic examples involve rock or mountain, exhausted fingers or hand, thick nails, and meager plant or house yield. Al-Rāzī prefers the related idiom 'I repelled him'; Bursevī cites Qāmūs and Tāj al-maṣādir. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ prefers stinginess after the small gift because the coordinated verbs require a new sense.
  53:34/q03/p2 He made the gift difficult, refused firmly, or imposed hard terms. — reasons: Ibn Zayd cites bargaining and Arabic usages of hard digging, rough nails, weary fingers, and scant plant yield. Al-Naḥḥās speaks of obstructing the recipient; al-Mubarrad says forceful refusal. Kuran Yolu understands rigidity as extreme stinginess and an imposing attitude.
  53:34/q03/p3 He spoiled the little gift by reproaching its recipient. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās adduces Arabic poetry, an unnamed poet says giving little followed by reproach contrasts with praiseworthy kindness, and al-Suyūṭī uses that poem. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says the poem's reproach is explicit in bi-mannihi and cannot be read into thumma akdā itself.
  53:34/q03/p4 It describes a small or dwindling yield or good. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī notes a plant with little yield and, via Abū Zayd, land with delayed growth; Abū Ḥayyān cites diminished house income and Abū Zayd's description of a man's dwindled generosity.
  53:34/q03/p5 Akdā itself means a small gift. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ attributes this to al-Rāghib but argues the coordination with 'gave a little' requires a distinct meaning.
53:34/q04 How is the final alif read in this word? [وَأَكْدَىٰٓ · readings]
  53:34/q04/p1 Abū ʿAmr reads listed nonfinal words with an open vowel on routes that incline verse endings. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī adds that for Mūsā Abū ʿAmr follows his usual between-vowels pronunciation where imāla is transmitted; the note does not explicitly name akdā.
53:34/q05 Who is depicted, according to the reports and explanations? [* · referent] (turns on: Whether the relative expression points to a recoverable historical incident or sketches a type; reports name several people and differ on the exchange, while Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Rāzī, Ālūsī, Islahi and Kuran Yolu challenge particular reports.)
  53:34/q05/p1 Al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra, who responded to the Prophet but was dissuaded and paid a man claiming to bear his punishment. — reasons: Mujāhid and Ibn Zayd are frequent transmitters; al-Bayḍāwī calls this the majority account, while al-Ālūsī prefers it as best known and most fitting with 53:35. Reports vary between his following, accepting, or nearing Islam; the reproacher invokes ancestral religion, offers to bear punishment for money, and receives only part. Al-Ṭabarī's Ibn Zayd adds bargaining and a witnessed written agreement. Muqātil instead speaks of brief verbal good. Islahi says the account lacks a sound chain and fits the verses poorly; Kuran Yolu disputes the reliability and Quranic fit of such episodes.
  53:34/q05/p2 ʿUthmān, whose foster brother ʿAbd Allāh ibn Saʿd ibn Abī Sarḥ induced him to curtail charity in return for assuming his sins. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Suddī, al-Kalbī and al-Musayyib transmit the account: ʿUthmān gave a camel, sometimes with gear, stopped or reduced charity, then resumed it; al-Thaʿlabī links turning away to Uḥud. Al-Zamakhsharī adds a witnessed deal; one report calls the brother maternal. Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it false and says ʿUthmān is innocent; al-Rāzī finds it unestablished and contrary to his character; al-Ālūsī calls it baseless; Ibn ʿĀshūr relays Ibn ʿAṭiyya's objection that ʿUthmān would not heed him.
  53:34/q05/p3 Al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil, who sometimes agreed with the Prophet; the gift may be money. — reasons: Al-Suddī is repeatedly cited for his partial agreement; Ibn ʿAbbās is separately reported to identify al-ʿĀṣ. Ibn ʿAṭiyya specifies money in al-Suddī's view.
  53:34/q05/p4 Abū Jahl, who acknowledged the nobility of Muhammad's commands but did not believe. — reasons: Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb al-Quraẓī is the transmitter; the reported verbal assent concerns noble manners or character.
  53:34/q05/p5 Al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, who paid five camels to a poor emigrant after apostasy and promised to bear his sin. — reasons: Al-Ḍaḥḥāk supplies the identification and five-camel detail; al-Ālūsī and Ibn ʿĀshūr repeat it.
  53:34/q05/p6 An unnamed convert whose former associate offered to take his punishment for payment. — reasons: Ibn Zayd reports pressure to return to ancestral religion, additional payment demanded, and a witnessed written agreement; the later narrations preserve these details without naming him.
  53:34/q05/p7 An unnamed man seeking a mount for an expedition, offered transport in exchange for another's sins or good deeds. — reasons: ʿIkrima says a friend lent a camel in exchange for taking on the friend's sins. Darrāj instead says the Prophet could not provide transport and another man supplied a mount to catch the army, with the passage extending through 53:41; the accounts differ in who assumes whose sins.
  53:34/q05/p8 An unnamed man heading to the Prophet for good who surrendered his provisions to a pagan offering to bear his sin. — reasons: ʿAṭāʾ ibn Yasār reports the exchange; unlike the expedition reports, the man is on his way to visit the Prophet.
  53:34/q05/p9 A person who disbelieved after believing. — reasons: Al-Nasafī attributes this application to Ibn ʿAbbās without naming the person.
  53:34/q05/p10 A representative, hypothetical miserly idolater, standing for wealthy Quraysh and similarly presumptuous people. — reasons: Islahi argues that al-ladhī and al may introduce a parable rather than a historical individual; 16:92's woman unraveling yarn is his analogy. Walīd, Abū Jahl and others would recognize themselves in it. He disputes the unsound, ill-fitting al-Walīd story and the assumption that a relative pronoun requires a proper-name referent.
