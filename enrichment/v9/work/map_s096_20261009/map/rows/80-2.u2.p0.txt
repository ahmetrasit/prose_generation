## THE CURRENT MAP
80:2/q01 How is أن جاءه construed? [أَن جَآءَهُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether أن is causal or temporal; for the causal construal, its case and which preceding verb governs it; an interrogative reading requires an implied clause.)
  80:2/q01/p1 It gives the cause: he frowned or turned away because the blind man came. — reasons: Al-Farra, al-Tabari, al-Zajjaj, Ibn Atiyya, Ibn al-Jawzi and al-Tabataba'i give the causal sense. Jishumi glosses the frown and turning of the face on his arrival. Al-Zajjaj and al-Nahhas call it accusative; al-Nahhas supplies an omitted li-anna. Al-Nasafi calls it a purpose expression. Al-Samin says both actions followed the arrival, and notes the familiar case dispute after deletion of the preposition. Al-Qurtubi explicitly calls the causal expression an accusative complement.
  80:2/q01/p2 The causal expression is governed by تولى, the nearer verb. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi names the nearest verb; al-Samin prefers the Basran analysis because it avoids ellipsis for the second competing verb; Bursevi also attributes it to Basran grammarians. Al-Baydawi allows this attachment. Ibn Jinni explicitly prefers the nearer verb and rejects attaching the expression to both verbs together.
  80:2/q01/p3 The causal expression is governed by عبس. — reasons: Al-Samin attributes this to Kufan grammarians; al-Nasafi and al-Baydawi record the alternative attachment. Ibn Jinni reports this alternative with the object of تولى left implicit.
  80:2/q01/p4 An omitted lām governs أن in the genitive, relating the arrival to both verbs. — reasons: Al-Nahhas reports genitive through omitted lām. Ibn Ashur calls lām deletion regular and links both verbs by syntactic competition.
  80:2/q01/p5 أن means إذ, ‘when.’ — reasons: Al-Nahhas records this as a third analysis; Makki transmits it according to al-Samin.
  80:2/q01/p6 On the interrogative reading an omitted verb is supplied from ‘he frowned and turned away.’ — reasons: Al-Tabrisi supplies the omitted clause and gives the sense ‘Has the blind man just come to him?’ Al-Qurtubi reports al-Hasan's analysis and pause after تولى. Ibn Jinni describes al-Hasan's question as incredulous and supplies the omitted verb from عبس وتولى.
80:2/q02 How is the opening أن read and where is the pause? [أَن جَآءَهُ · readings] (turns on: The opening hamza's number and length, interrogative force, and whether recitation pauses after تولى.)
  80:2/q02/p1 The usual declarative reading has one short hamza and continues without a pause after تولى. — reasons: Al-Samin calls this the usual reading; al-Qurtubi says its recitation does not pause after تولى.
  80:2/q02/p2 It is read interrogatively with a lengthened opening hamza and a pause after تولى. — reasons: Al-Farra and al-Tabari compare an interrogative reading of 68:14. Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi attribute this to al-Hasan, Ibn Atiyya also to Isa. Ibn al-Jawzi lists Ubayy, al-Hasan, Abu al-Mutawakkil and Abu Imran. Al-Tabrisi calls al-Hasan's reading noncanonical. Reports vary in describing its hamza and length. Ibn Jinni describes al-Hasan's opening as madd, with the stop after وتولى.
  80:2/q02/p3 It is read interrogatively with two open hamzas, sometimes with an intervening alif. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi names Ibn Masud and Ibn al-Sumayfi for two short hamzas. Al-Samin names Zayd ibn Ali, Isa and Abu Imran al-Jawni for two hamzas and cites al-Zamakhshari's آأن with intervening alif; al-Baydawi reports that form. Al-Farra's report also describes two open hamzas. The interrogative asks whether the arrival caused the frown.
80:2/q03 Who is the blind man? [ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ · referent]
  80:2/q03/p1 He is Ibn Umm Maktum, the blind Muslim who came seeking instruction. — reasons: Mujahid and Qatada place him in Banu Fihr; reports variously supply personal names and lineages, treated separately below. Ibn Ashur says the definite description refers to a particular known visitor. Islahi reports exegetical agreement. The occasion reports connect his visit to instruction, guidance or recitation.
80:2/q04 What personal name is reported for Ibn Umm Maktum? [ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ · reports] (turns on: The differing personal names and lineages transmitted for the man called Ibn Umm Maktum.)
  80:2/q04/p1 He is Abd Allah ibn Shurayh ibn Malik ibn Rabiah, with lineage variants. — reasons: Al-Baghawi supplies the Fihri lineage; al-Tabataba'i quotes al-Majma with it, and Asad calls him Abd Allah ibn Shurayh. Bursevi and Alusi list Abd Allah ibn Shurayh ibn Malik among alternative names. Names and ancestor spellings vary in reports.
  80:2/q04/p2 He is Amr ibn Qays ibn Zaida. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi gives Amr ibn Qays; Bursevi identifies this person as Khadija's cousin. Alusi says this is the most famous identification according to Jami al-usul. Muqatil's account also calls him Umm Maktum's son Amr ibn Qays. Al-Qurtubi gives the fuller name Amr ibn Qays ibn Zaida ibn al-Asamm and identifies him as Khadija's maternal cousin.
  80:2/q04/p3 He is Abd Allah ibn Zayd or Zaida. — reasons: Qatada's report in al-Tabari has Abd Allah ibn Zayd; al-Mawardi records Abdallah ibn Zaida of Banu Fihr. The forms vary.
  80:2/q04/p4 He is Abd Allah ibn Amr. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi reports this as another name; Alusi lists it among the alternatives.
  80:2/q04/p5 He is Abd Allah ibn Abi Musrah al-Amar. — reasons: Muqatil gives this name alongside Amr ibn Qays and lineages.
80:2/q05 Who was Umm Maktum? [ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ · reports] (turns on: The genealogy behind the man's kunya; reports also differ on the mother's given name.)
  80:2/q05/p1 She was his mother. — reasons: Al-Nasafi names her as mother; al-Sadi corrects al-Kashshaf and cites Ibn Abd al-Barr, calling her Atika bint Amir. Alusi calls Umm Maktum the mother's kunya but names Atika bint Abd Allah al-Makhzumiyya. The reported personal name varies. Al-Qurtubi names his mother Atika daughter of Amir of Makhzum.
  80:2/q05/p2 She was his paternal grandmother. — reasons: Al-Farra identifies her this way. Bursevi cites al-Sadi's objection to the Kashshaf's version, and Alusi says al-Zamakhshari was wrong on this point.
80:2/q06 What does al-aʿmā mean here? [ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the word is discussed in its literal use for this man or its wider figurative range.)
  80:2/q06/p1 It denotes physical loss of sight. — reasons: Al-Damghani cites this visitor and 24:61; Ibn al-Jawzi states blindness of sight. Bursevi recognizes this literal sense alongside a figurative sense. The report of his needing others to identify dawn confirms his physical blindness.
  80:2/q06/p2 Blindness can figuratively mean loss of insight. — reasons: Bursevi gives this additional Arabic sense, while saying the definite article identifies the familiar Ibn Umm Maktum.
80:2/q07 Was Ibn Umm Maktum born blind? [ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ · reports] (turns on: Conflicting biographical reports about when his blindness began.)
  80:2/q07/p1 He was born blind. — reasons: Alusi reports this and says it explains his mother's kunya.
  80:2/q07/p2 He became blind after once seeing. — reasons: Alusi records this as the alternative report.
80:2/q08 Why call the visitor ‘the blind man’? [ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the description primarily explains his interruption, heightens his claim to compassion, or sharpens the reproach.)
  80:2/q08/p1 His blindness excuses his interruption and makes his need for care especially clear. — reasons: Al-Baydawi, Abu Hayyan, al-Biqai and Ibn Ashur say it prompts compassion or softens the rebuke; al-Biqai says it explains his insistence and repairs his hurt. Elmali extends the lesson to all guidance seekers with excuses or needs. Al-Tabataba'i stresses his fear of God and need for guidance.
  80:2/q08/p2 The description sharpens reproach for turning away from someone blind. — reasons: Al-Baydawi says it may suggest that blindness itself prompted the aversion; al-Tabataba'i says the fearful seeker deserved special attention. Ibn Atiyya contrasts disability with the unbeliever's wealth and human inclination to slight disability.
  80:2/q08/p3 Naming an impairment is permissible when useful or as a clear identifying nickname. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya cites transmitters known as al-Amash, al-Araj and al-Aftas.
80:2/q09 Who frowned and turned away? [جَآءَهُ · referent] (turns on: Whether third-person wording and the Prophet's established character allow or rule out identifying him as the actor; an al-Sadiq report names an Umayyad man.)
  80:2/q09/p1 The Prophet did so when Ibn Umm Maktum came. — reasons: The common occasion reports make the Prophet the subject. Bursevi explicitly identifies the pronoun. Islahi says the later ‘you know’ addresses the Prophet. The third-person opening is explained as a rhetorical choice by several authors.
  80:2/q09/p2 Someone else, specifically an Umayyad man in al-Sadiq's report, frowned and turned away. — reasons: Al-Sadiq's account places the Umayyad man with the Prophet. Jishumi and al-Tabrisi point to the third-person wording and the Prophet's praised character (68:4; 3:159); al-Murtada says neglecting poor Muslims does not resemble his conduct. Al-Tabataba'i adds early Meccan commands in 26:214–15 and 15:88,94 and argues that wealth preference is rationally ugly even without a prohibition.
80:2/q10 What happened when the blind man came? [جَآءَهُ ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ · reports] (turns on: Competing accounts of the occasion and the actor; transmission assessments and variant details of the interruption.)
  80:2/q10/p1 He sought instruction while the Prophet invited Quraysh leaders; the Prophet turned from him and revelation followed. — reasons: Reports say he repeatedly asked for guidance, Qur'an teaching, a recitation or explanation, unaware the Prophet was occupied. The Prophet hoped the leaders' conversion would draw followers.
