74:31/q22 How does God guide and misguide through this example? [يُضِلُّ ٱللَّهُ مَن يَشَآءُ وَيَهْدِى مَن يَشَآءُ · theology] (turns on: Whether God creates the response, reveals a chosen response through a test, withholds aid, judges it, or manifests primordial disposition.)
  74:31/q22/p1 God guides receptive believers and leaves mocking deniers astray according to His will and knowledge. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says God abandons some from truth and enables others; al-Rāzī says the verse supports his companions' doctrine and counters Muʿtazilī alternatives. Al-Nasafī attributes creation of acts to God while knowing each person's choice; Ibn ʿĀshūr grounds outcomes in created dispositions, desires, invitations and resistance, with human responsibility in 2:286. Islahi invokes God's wise law, and Kur'an Yolu stresses freely accepted or rejected guidance.
  74:31/q22/p2 The test God imposes reveals preexisting choices, so misguidance is attributed to the test's divine origin. — reasons: Jishumī and al-Ṭabrisī stress obligation and manifestation rather than compelling disbelief; al-Rāzī reports a Muʿtazilī version in which verses increase faith or impurity according to response, citing 9:124–125.
  74:31/q22/p3 Misguidance means withholding divine aid, judging a person astray, or leading the condemned away from Paradise. — reasons: Al-Rāzī reports separate Muʿtazilī glosses of withholding luṭf, judging, and eschatological exclusion; Jishumī reports Abū Muslim's exclusion from reward, another judgment interpretation, and Abū ʿAlī's punishment gloss; al-Ṭabrisī reports the Paradise-path reading.
  74:31/q22/p4 God guides according to primordial felicity and misguides according to primordial wretchedness. — reasons: Qāshānī's inward account and Bursevī's pre-eternal dispositions emphasize fixed essences.
74:31/q23 What are God's junūd? [جُنُودَ رَبِّكَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether junūd embraces all divine agents, specifically Hell's angelic forces, or a philosophical scheme.)
  74:31/q23/p1 His hosts or armies: angels and other creatures executing His command. — reasons: Al-Dāmghānī gives angels here and lists other lexical senses; Bursevī includes honey among divine troops, and Ibn ʿĀshūr explains the army metaphor and the honor in 'your Lord'. Kur'an Yolu narrows the local sense to Hell's attendants.
  74:31/q23/p2 Specifically the angelic helpers behind the nineteen keepers. — reasons: ʿAṭāʾ and later sources say each chief has innumerable attendants; al-Ālūsī reports a hadith of the Fire's many reins and angels.
  74:31/q23/p3 Ten intellects and nine souls. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr rejects this Greek philosophical interpretation as unsupported and as ignoring the verse's close.
74:31/q24 What does only God knowing His hosts establish? [وَمَا يَعْلَمُ جُنُودَ رَبِّكَ إِلَّا هُوَ · interpretation]
  74:31/q24/p1 His hosts vastly exceed nineteen, and only He knows their detailed number, nature, power, roles, and wisdom. — reasons: This rebuts Abū Jahl's taunt that Muhammad's Lord had only nineteen helpers. Al-Rāzī says God can create pain directly, down to a hair or vein; al-Bayḍāwī and Ibn ʿĀshūr distinguish revealed count from unknowable total properties. Al-Ālūsī rejects al-Ṭībī's exclusive 'striking power' gloss as textually unclear. Al-Sulamī extends limited creaturely knowledge to divine names and attributes.
74:31/q25 What reports illustrate the multitude of God's angels? [جُنُودَ رَبِّكَ · reports]
  74:31/q25/p1 Heaven is densely filled with worshipping angels, with many beyond human or even Gabriel's knowledge. — reasons: Reports vary: the sky groans with no finger, palm, handspan or foot space empty; seventy thousand enter Bayt al-Maʿmūr daily without return; Ismāʿīl commands twelve thousand with twelve thousand each or seventy thousand with one hundred thousand each; Moses hears of twelve tribes innumerable as dust. Ibn Kathīr records gradings including ḥasan gharīb, strange chains, and a highly objectionable ʿUmar report, and reports other constant worshippers. Al-Ālūsī declines to affirm a ranking of hosts.
74:31/q26 How is 'none knows ... except Him' parsed? [وَمَا يَعْلَمُ جُنُودَ رَبِّكَ إِلَّا هُوَ · grammar]
  74:31/q26/p1 Huwa is the nominative subject of yaʿlam, not an accusative exception; the object is fronted for restriction. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās gives the case argument; al-Samīn says fronting the object is required and a returning pronoun attaches to it.
74:31/q27 What does 'it' refer to in the final reminder? [وَمَا هِىَ إِلَّا ذِكْرَىٰ لِلْبَشَرِ · referent] (turns on: Which feminine antecedent the pronoun resumes: Hell, worldly fire, discourse, or the disclosed number and hosts.)
  74:31/q27/p1 Saqar or Hellfire and its description. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī, Mujāhid, Qatāda and many others take the Fire as warning; Abū Ḥayyān prefers Saqar. Ibn ʿĀshūr says an omitted 'mention' or 'description' may be understood and notes that immediately after 74:29 the pronoun would unambiguously mean Saqar.
  74:31/q27/p2 The worldly fire, reminding people of the Fire to come. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj, Ibn ʿĪsā and others compare earthly fire with afterlife fire; Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 56:71–73 and calls this semantic redirection, while al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls it weakest.
  74:31/q27/p3 The Qur'anic verses, sūra, preceding address, or warning about Saqar. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports 'the situation, address and warning'; Rāzī says only believers benefit from the ambiguous verses; Islahi prefers verses depicting Hell; Asad translates 'all this'.
  74:31/q27/p4 The number nineteen or the angelic hosts, reminding people that God needs few helpers. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī cites proximity to 'troops'; Ibn ʿĀshūr prefers the number, while Study Quran prefers hosts. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī explains the feminine form by the feminine predicate. Al-Ālūsī says the few mighty beings show divine greatness.
74:31/q28 What is the reminder meant to do? [ذِكْرَىٰ لِلْبَشَرِ · interpretation]
  74:31/q28/p1 It warns humanity of Hell and divine power so people fear, obey, and avoid the deeds leading there. — reasons: Mujāhid says fear should lead to obedience; al-Ṭabrisī says avoidance of deserving Saqar. Ibn ʿĀshūr says debate about the number misses the warning; Islahi says warning removes excuses while preserving choice. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the address encompasses humanity, including jinn by implication according to Bursevī.
  74:31/q28/p2 It signals the allegory's purpose and cautions against literalizing descriptions of the unseen. — reasons: Asad compares 2:26; Islahi stresses that the reality of afterlife images is known to God alone.
74:31/q29 How does the closing reminder fit the preceding passage? [وَمَا هِىَ إِلَّا ذِكْرَىٰ لِلْبَشَرِ · links]
  74:31/q29/p1 The explanation of the keepers is a parenthesis after Saqar's description, and the verse resumes its warning. — reasons: Qāshānī marks the parenthesis from 'We made' to 'except Him'; al-Ālūsī links the close back to 'I shall roast him in Saqar' in 74:26; Ibn ʿĀshūr notes placement after 74:29 affects the pronoun.
  74:31/q29/p2 The verse immediately continues the prior count, answers mockery, and differentiates readers' responses. — reasons: Islahi cites the opening conjunction and contrasts an independent insertion at 73:20; Ibn ʿĀshūr links the passage to challenges against the keepers and the distinction between disbelievers and believers.
74:31/q30 Is this passage Meccan or Medinan? [* · sciences] (turns on: Whether references to Jews and hypocrites imply a Medinan date or are compatible with Meccan revelation.)
  74:31/q30/p1 It belongs to the Meccan sūra and may predict later Medinan events. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr cites reported consensus and absence from lists of Medinan exceptions; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects an uncorroborated Medinan attribution to Muqātil. Islahi says the conjunction joins this verse to the preceding Meccan verse; al-Ālūsī and Elmalılı say Jewish questioning does not prove Medinan revelation.
  74:31/q30/p2 Verse 74:31 or a section from it was revealed in Medina. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr reports al-Ālūsī's ambiguous attribution to Muqātil or the passage from here onward without chain or corroboration; Ibn al-Jawzī says Muqātil treats the sick-hearted as Medinan Jews.
74:31/q31 How is the neighboring count of nineteen read? [عِدَّتَهُمْ · readings] (turns on: The vocalization and structure of the numeral in adjacent 74:30.)
  74:31/q31/p1 The standard compound denotes nineteen. — reasons: Jishumī explains the accusative compound as two nouns joined into one name.
  74:31/q31/p2 The compound has a quiescent ʿayn, or tisʿa is built with ḍamma on its tāʾ. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports Abū Jaʿfar and Ṭalḥa's quiescent ʿayn to avoid consecutive vowels, and Anas, Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Quṭb and Ibrāhīm ibn Qutta's ḍamma to avoid five fatḥas.
  74:31/q31/p3 Readings with aʿshur or wa-ʿashr yield a plural or annexed expression, sometimes explained as ninety. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports Anas, Sulaymān ibn Qutta, al-Lawāmiʿ, al-Zamakhsharī and Ibn Jinnī; analyses differ between plural 'tens', nine leaders with followers, and nine groups of companions.
74:31/q32 What is the syntax of the final 'for mankind'? [لِلْبَشَرِ · grammar]
  74:31/q32/p1 The lām is governed by dhikrā and is additional. — reasons: Al-Samīn gives this parsing.
74:31/q33 What verbal artistry occurs at 'mankind'? [لِلْبَشَرِ · rhetoric]
  74:31/q33/p1 The two occurrences of al-bashar create exact paronomasia. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī compares this verse with 74:29, 'scorching the skin'.
