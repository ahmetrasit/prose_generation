## THE CURRENT MAP
20:12/q01 How is إني read and construed after the call? [إِنِّىٓ · readings] (turns on: Whether the particle begins direct speech or is syntactically governed by the call; the final yāʾ also varies.)
  20:12/q01/p1 Kasra: direct quoted speech after Moses was called. — reasons: Al-Tabari prefers it because يا موسى intervenes; al-Farisi cites the ensuing direct quotations at 20:13–14. Basrans posit an omitted قول; Kufans treat نادى as speech. Abu Ubayd chooses the common reading. Nafi opens the final yāʾ while other kasra readers generally quiet it; the two Medinese readers, Ibn Kathir and Abu Amr open it in the five annexed yāʾs enumerated by Ibn al-Jazari.
  20:12/q01/p2 Fatḥa: a subordinate statement giving the content or cause of the call. — reasons: Ibn Kathir and Abu Amr, also Abu Jafar in several lists, read annī; the yāʾ is open. Al-Zajjaj gives the clause accusative position; others supply bi with نودي, supported by a poetic ناديت باسم example. Ibn Atiyya allows causal 'because'; al-Samin finds it weak, and al-Alusi finds the intervening vocative awkward. Al-Alusi instead supplies أعلم. Qiraat-ER offers 'Know that I am your Lord'. Ibn al-Jazari finds no explicit report for inferring Abu Jafar's yāʾ vowel. Al-Farisi adds that neither quoted clause can serve as deputy subject of passive نودي; the earlier Moses can.
20:12/q02 What grammatical role does أنا play? [أَنَا۠ · grammar]
  20:12/q02/p1 It can be a subject, an emphasis, or a separator. — reasons: Al-Samin says it may be a subject with ربك as predicate, an emphasis of the first-person pronoun, or a separator.
20:12/q03 Why repeat the first-person pronoun in إني أنا ربك? [إِنِّىٓ أَنَا۠ رَبُّكَ · rhetoric]
  20:12/q03/p1 The repetition confirms the speaker's identity and removes Moses' doubt. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari calls it strengthened proof and secure recognition; Ibn al-Jawzi compares 15:89. Al-Tabarsi, al-Razi, al-Baydawi, al-Alusi and Bursevi likewise stress certainty.
20:12/q04 Why say 'your Lord' here? [رَبُّكَ · rhetoric]
  20:12/q04/p1 It marks a direct, personal audience with Moses and fits this sura's focus on his worship and mission. — reasons: Tabatabai contrasts the later 'I am Allah,' which states the revealed message. Al-Samarrai contrasts al-Naml and al-Qasas, which say 'Lord of the worlds'; Ta Ha directly instructs Moses in worship and prayer, promises signs and recounts his life.
20:12/q05 How did Moses know who was speaking? [إِنِّىٓ أَنَا۠ رَبُّكَ · theology] (turns on: Whether revelation itself supplies certainty or Moses needs an inferential sign.)
  20:12/q05/p1 God gave Moses immediate, necessary certainty in revelation. — reasons: Al-Alusi favors knowledge created in Moses without inference. Tabatabai says a prophet's initial revelation is directly certain; 42:51 frames divine communication, and rational proof would make prophecy an acquired inference.
  20:12/q05/p2 He inferred divine identity from a miracle or distinctive signs. — reasons: The Mu'tazila require a miracle because necessary knowledge would remove moral responsibility; al-Alusi records prior or later miracle views and questions whether unique-sounding attributes alone withstand satanic suggestion. A report from Wahb says the omnidirectional voice and God's nearness assured Moses; other reports describe hearing from all six directions and through all his limbs, or an unchanged sound regardless of distance.
20:12/q06 How was God's speech heard by Moses? [إِنِّىٓ أَنَا۠ رَبُّكَ · theology] (turns on: The nature of divine speech and whether an audible form is direct, created, or a representation of inner speech.)
  20:12/q06/p1 Moses heard God's own eternal verbal speech directly, without an intermediary. — reasons: A Sunni group in al-Alusi says successive letters need temporal sequence only with an organ such as a tongue; without one the speech can be heard at once.
  20:12/q06/p2 He heard created sound in the tree, while God's speech remains eternal. — reasons: Al-Alusi attributes this to Sunni scholars of Transoxiana.
  20:12/q06/p3 The divine verbal speech was originated. — reasons: Groups al-Alusi calls innovators differ over whether events can subsist in God; those denying this locate the created sound in a body.
  20:12/q06/p4 He spiritually received inner speech that was later represented as words. — reasons: Al-Ashari describes hearing beyond letters, sound and rational comprehension; another account compares familiar word forms produced in intellect and common sense to a dream. Al-Baydawi likewise says spiritual reception is then represented bodily without fixed organ or direction.
20:12/q07 Can this command coexist with eternal divine speech? [فَٱخْلَعْ · theology] (turns on: Whether an eternal command can have an addressee who comes to exist later.)
  20:12/q07/p1 An eternal imperative to Moses before his existence is absurd. — reasons: The Mu'tazila compare it to addressing absent people in an empty house.
  20:12/q07/p2 God's speech is eternal but was not an imperative or prohibition in eternity. — reasons: Al-Razi reports this first reply from his school.
  20:12/q07/p3 The eternal speech is already an order, and Moses later becomes its addressee without a change in it. — reasons: Al-Razi's school compares eternal divine power yielding capacity for action later.
20:12/q08 How does ف connect the sandal command to the declaration? [فَٱخْلَعْ · grammar]
  20:12/q08/p1 The command follows as a consequence of the declaration of God's lordship. — reasons: Al-Alusi says the preceding self-identification motivates removing the sandals.
20:12/q09 What do اخلع and نعل mean literally? [فَٱخْلَعْ نَعْلَيْكَ · meaning]
  20:12/q09/p1 The command is to take off the two coverings of Moses' feet. — reasons: Al-Wahidi gives khalaʿ as remove and naʿl as a foot covering; Abu Hayyan says it can be leather, iron, or wood. Al-Alusi notes naʿl is feminine, gives its diminutive, and cites al-Farra's poetry for an alternate vowel on its second radical.
20:12/q10 What were Moses' sandals made of? [نَعْلَيْكَ · reports] (turns on: Whether the footwear was impure donkey hide, pure cowhide, or divinely created.)
  20:12/q10/p1 They were made of hide from an unritually dead donkey, often said to be untanned. — reasons: Qatada, Ka'b, Ali, Ikrima and others transmit it; Ibn Masud attributes a report to the Prophet. Al-Tabari says that report's chain needs scrutiny; al-Tirmidhi calls it gharīb and al-Qurtubi distinguishes the weak Kufan Humayd ibn Ali from trustworthy Meccan Humayd ibn Qays. Al-Alusi calls the transmitted report unusual. Some say donkey hide without specifying carrion.
  20:12/q10/p2 They were ritually pure or slaughtered cowhide. — reasons: Al-Hasan, Mujahid, Sa'id ibn Jubayr and others give this alternative; Kashifi explicitly prefers processed cowhide.
  20:12/q10/p3 God created the sandals. — reasons: Abu Hayyan records this report along with Moses casting them beyond the valley.
20:12/q11 Why was Moses told to remove the sandals? [فَٱخْلَعْ نَعْلَيْكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the command responds to impure footwear, direct contact with blessing, reverent decorum, or the end of a practical need.)
  20:12/q11/p1 Their dead-animal hide was impure and should not enter the sacred valley. — reasons: Al-Muqatil, Ka'b, Qatada and others identify carrion donkey hide. Al-Tabari objects that neither Quran nor authoritative report establishes this; the verse itself grounds the order in the valley's sanctity. Al-Razi notes tanning would purify hide under a Prophetic ruling, raising an abrogation issue if carrion was the reason.
  20:12/q11/p2 His bare feet should touch and receive the holy ground's blessing. — reasons: Sa'id likens this to entering the Kaaba barefoot; Ibn Abi Najih, Ibn Abbas, Mujahid and others stress direct contact. Ka'b combines this with donkey hide; al-Hasan and others instead cite pure cowhide. Al-Tabari prefers this because the command is linked to sanctity, without sound proof for impurity. Abu Sufyan says Moses' feet also blessed the valley. Bursevi says the exchange of blessing is mutual.
  20:12/q11/p3 Bare feet express humility and reverence before God or the sacred place. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan prefer royal-court etiquette and say the sandals' substance does not matter; al-Zamakhshari and al-Nasafi see the Quran as honoring the place. Al-Razi cites the following valley clause; early Muslims' barefoot circumambulation, Sa'id's Kaaba analogy, Malik's reverence for Medina, Bishr al-Hafi's bare feet and Abu Hanifa's contrasting royal-carpet anecdote illustrate decorum. Islahi adds cleanliness and decency; Kuranyolu sees spiritual preparation for revelation.
  20:12/q11/p4 The sandals' protection against dirt and insects was no longer needed in the pure, safe valley. — reasons: Abu Muslim says God reassured Moses that the place was pure and he was safe.
20:12/q12 What did Moses do with the sandals? [فَٱخْلَعْ نَعْلَيْكَ · reports]
  20:12/q12/p1 He removed them and cast them behind or beyond the valley. — reasons: Al-Baghawi, al-Zamakhshari, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi and al-Alusi transmit this detail; Qatada says he then approached.
20:12/q13 What inward act do the two sandals represent? [فَٱخْلَعْ نَعْلَيْكَ · inward] (turns on: The symbolic referents proposed for the paired sandals and how far a figurative reading may go.)
  20:12/q13/p1 Cast off attachment to both this world and the next to devote oneself wholly to God. — reasons: Al-Qushayri, al-Razi, Bursevi, al-Ghazzali and Sufi transmitters give this; Ibn Ajibah calls the two sandals realms of existence and the removal a way to witness the Existentiator. Al-Alusi calls the allegory remote. Study Quran notes the sandals' later Sufi poetic and literary use, including Ibn Qasi's Taking Off the Sandals.
