## THE CURRENT MAP
74:43/q01 Who speaks this confession, and to whom? [قَالُوا۟ · referent]
  74:43/q01/p1 The criminals or inhabitants of Hell answer those who ask them about Saqar; the people of the right hand are identified as the questioners. — reasons: Qurṭubī calls the speakers the people in Hell; al-Ālūsī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī call them the guilty or criminals. Iṣlāḥī identifies the questioners as the people of the right hand in Paradise, who ask those in Hell and receive an open confession. Ibn Kathīr also calls the speakers criminals.
74:43/q02 How does the confession fit the preceding question about Saqar? [قَالُوا۟ لَمْ نَكُ مِنَ ٱلْمُصَلِّينَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether 74:42 directly quotes the question put to the criminals or follows an omitted exchange with informed people.)
  74:43/q02/p1 The questioners' words and the criminals' answer are quoted directly; the answer supplies the implied content of what put them in Saqar. — reasons: Al-Samīn says لم نك من المصلين supplies the implied content of سلكنا and answers ما سلككم, clarifying Abū al-Baqāʾ. Ibn ʿĀshūr reports al-Zamakhsharī's view that the question is not an explanation of the preceding discussion but a quotation of the questioners followed by the criminals' answer (74:42–43).
  74:43/q02/p2 The dialogue includes omitted speech: those discussing the criminals ask informed people among them, who report having asked the criminals. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr supplies this omitted exchange across 74:40–43.
74:43/q03 Why is the nūn omitted in نك? [نَكُ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the shortened form is explained by the speakers' state or by ease in frequent usage.)
  74:43/q03/p1 Its omission expresses the speakers' extreme distress and deficient natural disposition. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says they are too distressed to utter even a dispensable letter and that the omission signals their lack of a good natural disposition.
  74:43/q03/p2 The nūn is omitted for ease because this form is frequent. — reasons: Al-Būrsawī gives frequency and ease as the explanation for نكن becoming نك.
74:43/q04 What Arabic patterns of final weak-letter retention and deletion bear on the shortened wording? [نَكُ · grammar]
  74:43/q04/p1 Final weak letters can be retained or deleted according to noun or verb status, case, pause, and cadence. — reasons: Sībawayh says nouns such as qāḍ, ghāz, and ʿamī commonly lose final yāʾ at pause as before tanwīn; nouns are especially prone because deletion also occurs outside cadence. Some Arabs delete it even in definite forms by analogy with indefinite nouns and because yāʾ plus kasra is heavy. Abū al-Khaṭṭāb and Yūnus report trusted speakers retaining it without tanwīn, where clear pronunciation is better; Sībawayh says retention of yāʾ and wāw is more analogical, though deletion is common, and cites Zuhayr for retention in cadence. In the accusative, yāʾ must remain clear because it survives connected speech. The pausal form marrī compensates for not dropping both hamza and yāʾ. Verbs normally retain final yāʾ at pause because it remains in connected speech; lā adri and mā adri are restricted frequent exceptions, compared with lam yak. Qurʾanic cadence and rhyme may delete final weak letters otherwise kept in speech; Sībawayh cites endings at 89:4, 18:64, 40:32, and 13:9.
74:43/q05 Is final yāʾ retained in the vocative yā qāḍī? [نَكُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the non-nunated vocative follows al-qāḍī or the vocative tendency to omit material.)
  74:43/q05/p1 The vocative retains yāʾ: yā qāḍī. — reasons: Al-Khalīl prefers this by analogy with al-qāḍī because the vocative is not nunated.
  74:43/q05/p2 The vocative drops yāʾ: yā qāḍ. — reasons: Yūnus prefers this, and Sībawayh calls it stronger: deletion occurs outside vocatives, and vocatives readily omit material, as in yā ḥār and yā ṣāḥ.
74:43/q06 How is a noun defined in the grammatical discussion? [نَكُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether independent knowability and absence of external time distinguish nouns despite the cited particles, pronouns, and time words.)
  74:43/q06/p1 A noun is a word denoting a meaning independently knowable without indicating when that meaning occurred. — reasons: Al-Rāzī uses 'word' as the nearest genus rather than 'utterance,' also excluding writing, knots, and gestures. He replies that independently graspable attachment differs from the particle bāʾ in 'I wrote with the pen.' He also distinguishes time, day, and tomorrow, which denote time itself, from verbs that add an external tense; morning and evening verbal nouns include time in their meaning, as past and future forms show.
  74:43/q06/p2 The definition is challenged by counterexamples concerning independent meanings, particles, noun forms, and temporal words. — reasons: Al-Rāzī reports the objection that anything known must first be conceived independently; a particle's concept can itself be known as non-independent. Another objection invokes the independently knowable senses of bāʾ and min, while grammarians cite interrogatives and conditionals such as kam, kayfa, matā, idhā, mā, and relative pronouns as nouns with non-independent meanings. Time, tomorrow, and today are also said to challenge the exclusion of time. Al-Rāzī replies to the particle and temporal objections.
74:43/q07 What does not being among those who pray mean? [لَمْ نَكُ مِنَ ٱلْمُصَلِّينَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether prayer here denotes the prescribed rite, worship generally, belief and religious identity, or allegiance to the Imams; Asad invokes the early date of the sūra.)
  74:43/q07/p1 They did not perform the obligatory prayer prescribed by the religious law. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī and al-Bayḍāwī specify obligatory prayer; al-Ālūsī says the guilty admit this to their questioners. Al-Būrsawī says they failed in the obligatory prayers as well as denying their obligation. Al-Biqāʿī says only prayer that counts and is performed as commanded qualifies; firmly established prayer prevents this end, whereas their supposed prayer was unacceptable.
  74:43/q07/p2 They did not pray to or worship God in worldly life. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī says they did not pray to God in the world. Ibn Kathīr reads failure to pray alongside failure to feed the poor as neither worshipping God nor doing good to fellow humans. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī takes prayer as worshipful turning toward God despite varying forms across true revealed religions.
  74:43/q07/p3 They lacked conscious belief in God or affirmation of His oneness. — reasons: ʿAṭāʾ explains it as failure to affirm God's oneness in worldly life. Asad takes it broadly as conscious belief in God because canonical ṣalāh was not yet obligatory when this early sūra was revealed.
  74:43/q07/p4 They were not among the Muslims or believers who pray. — reasons: Al-Kalbī glosses the phrase as not being among Muslims; al-Qurṭubī explains the prayers as believers who pray.
  74:43/q07/p5 Their denial of prayer encompasses faith, humility, worship, and the religion's required beliefs and commands. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya says negating prayer subsumes faith in God, recognition, humility, worship, and the religion's commands and required beliefs.
  74:43/q07/p6 They did not believe that prayer was obligatory. — reasons: Al-Nasafī gives disbelief in prayer's obligation as their reason; al-Būrsawī includes failure to acknowledge its obligation along with nonperformance.
  74:43/q07/p7 They were not followers of the Imams. — reasons: The Study Quran reports this as one Shiite interpretation, alluding to failure to follow Shiite leaders.
74:43/q08 Why does the confession begin with prayer and action? [لَمْ نَكُ مِنَ ٱلْمُصَلِّينَ · rhetoric]
  74:43/q08/p1 Prayer comes first because it is the foremost deed and the first matter of reckoning; beginning with action urges prompt obedience and can give conviction form. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī calls prayer the greatest deed and says reckoning begins with it. He says action comes first because practicing is easier than acquiring knowledge, teaches prompt obedience to a trustworthy command, and gives conviction a form. Iṣlāḥī calls prayer the foremost deed and the practice distinguishing believers from disbelievers, as all prophets taught.
74:43/q09 What does abandoning prayer and other duties reveal about unbelievers' legal accountability? [لَمْ نَكُ مِنَ ٱلْمُصَلِّينَ · law] (turns on: Whether the confession and punishment show a legal duty to perform worship during unbelief, beyond afterlife accountability.)
  74:43/q09/p1 Unbelievers are accountable for revealed obligations and punished for neglecting them in the afterlife. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī cites the unbelievers' failure to pray and feed the poor and their denial of Judgment. Al-Biqāʿī says their deeds before faith would not be valid, yet they are punished for religious-law obligations. Al-Tawḍīḥ, as reported by al-Būrsawī, says unbelievers are addressed by faith, penalties, and transactions by consensus, and by worship duties for afterlife accountability.
  74:43/q09/p2 Unbelievers are legally required to perform worship duties in this life. — reasons: Al-Būrsawī reports the Iraqi scholars among his teachers holding this view; al-Tawḍīḥ reports that worldly performance is disputed.
  74:43/q09/p3 Their regret does not establish a command to perform worship before belief, and some scholars deny a worldly performance requirement. — reasons: Al-Būrsawī reports that scholars of his region disagree with the Iraqi position. A Hanafi response says sinners regret missed opportunities for good and believers' rewards, but regret alone does not prove an earlier command to act. Al-Tawḍīḥ reports this as disputed.
74:43/q10 Does failure to perform prayer itself warrant blame or punishment? [لَمْ نَكُ مِنَ ٱلْمُصَلِّينَ · law]
  74:43/q10/p1 Neglect of an obligation warrants blame and punishment. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī draws this inference from the sinners' confession.
