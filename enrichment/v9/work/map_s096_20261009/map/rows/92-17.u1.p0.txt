## THE CURRENT MAP
92:17/q01 What does being kept from it entail? [وَسَيُجَنَّبُهَا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the promise describes complete distance from Hell or a contrast with the severe torment others can suffer.)
  92:17/q01/p1 God sets the God-fearing person far from the Fire, so he does not enter it. — reasons: Al-Wahidi, al-Jishumi, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, and al-Alusi explain the verb as distancing or putting to one side; al-Jishumi pictures him on Paradise's side. Al-Baydawi and al-Biqa'i specify that full avoidance of idolatry and sins prevents entry; Elmalılı adds an easy passage to Paradise. Al-Tabari calls it protection from the blazing Fire.
  92:17/q01/p2 Lesser believers may enter Hell yet be spared its most severe burning or leave after punishment. — reasons: Al-Baydawi distinguishes entry from burning for one avoiding shirk but committing sins. Al-Biqa'i says such a sinner may enter and later leave. Ibn al-Munir, quoted by al-Alusi, describes ṣaly as roasting between layers of embers; this allows lesser punishments. Al-Alusi says the most pious do not enter.
92:17/q02 How is yujannabuhā constructed? [وَسَيُجَنَّبُهَا ٱلْأَتْقَى · grammar] (turns on: Whether the object is explicit and how the passive promotes the person protected.)
  92:17/q02/p1 Jannaba takes two objects; in the passive al-atqā becomes the subject and the Fire pronoun remains its other object. — reasons: Al-Alusi derives the verb from putting a person's side away and notes its common distancing sense; his two summaries give differing object numbering while agreeing on the passive construction.
  92:17/q02/p2 When an object is unexpressed, jannaba can mean keeping someone from good. — reasons: Al-Raghib, quoted by al-Alusi, connects this usage with putting a person's side away; the explicit Fire pronoun here specifies the object.
92:17/q03 What does the future passive wording suggest? [وَسَيُجَنَّبُهَا · rhetoric]
  92:17/q03/p1 The future particle promises certainty after a respite, and the passive portrays separation from Hell as effortless. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i draws both effects from the future particle and passive form.
92:17/q04 What degree of piety does al-atqā express? [ٱلْأَتْقَى · meaning] (turns on: Whether the afʿal form is an ordinary descriptor, an absolute rank, or a comparison within a stated group.)
  92:17/q04/p1 The form can mean simply a pious person, naming the pious class. — reasons: Al-Tabari and al-Tabrisi cite Tarafa's awḥad for wāḥid; al-Qurtubi adds akbar for kabīr and ahwan for hayyin, mentioning 30:27. Al-Alusi repeats the poetic evidence but says generic elatives alone do not settle the restriction difficulty. Al-Baghawi and Abu Ubayda, quoted by al-Nasafi, also use the ordinary class sense.
  92:17/q04/p2 It is an intensive or superlative: the most God-fearing in degree. — reasons: Al-Jishumi derives it and al-ashqā as elatives from taqī and shaqī; Ibn Ashur calls both common intensifying forms. Bint al-Shati notes that this al-atqā is unrestricted, unlike atqākum in 49:13. Al-Qurtubi describes the person as pious and fearful.
  92:17/q04/p3 It is the most God-fearing among those who might fear worldly loss and who nevertheless spend for God. — reasons: Tabataba'i says the comparative identifies the giver among people mindful of poverty or loss; he rejects an absolute superlative confined to one person or one per age.
  92:17/q04/p4 It may be the most pious among Paradise's inhabitants rather than among all humanity. — reasons: Al-Jishumi notes that Paradise includes children, the insane, compensated people, and houris, who are outside the warned moral audience.
92:17/q05 Whom does al-atqā describe? [ٱلْأَتْقَى · referent] (turns on: Whether reports of an occasion fix the referent and whether the singular definite elative also covers others.)
  92:17/q05/p1 Abu Bakr is its named instance. — reasons: Al-Farra, Ibn Abbas, Urwa and many commentators name him; al-Wahidi, Ibn Atiyya, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Razi, and al-Itqan report consensus in their respective circles. Ibn Kathir treats him as foremost under the wording. His freeing of persecuted slaves and selfless spending are cited as fitting traits; al-Razi rejects Ali because prophetic care would count as a favor to repay. Al-Alusi reports sound chains from Ibn Masud and Ibn Abbas.
  92:17/q05/p2 Abu al-Dahdah is its named instance. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi ties the designation to giving away the purchased palm solely for God's face; al-Qurtubi gives the same contextual alternative. Tabataba'i says al-Qummi's report has Shia routes from the Imams and regards it as reliable Shia evidence; al-Alusi reports al-Suyuti's judgment that the Ibn Abbas palm report is weak.
  92:17/q05/p3 Ali is its named instance. — reasons: Al-Razi and Tabataba'i report that some Shia cite 5:55, the alms while bowing verse, for Ali. Al-Razi counters that the Prophet's upbringing of Ali creates a prior worldly favor relevant to the following verses; Tabataba'i rejects the claim that all Shia sources hold this view.
  92:17/q05/p4 The description applies generally to every pious believer or giver with these qualities. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya includes everyone sharing the traits despite Abu Bakr as occasion; Ibn Kathir and Kuran Yolu likewise preserve the general wording. Tabataba'i says a descriptor is general even with a specific occasion. Al-Itqan objects that the singular definite elative has an antecedent, cannot be a relative al, and lacks a generalizing form.
  92:17/q05/p5 The wording is particular to one person rather than a general class. — reasons: Al-Itqan invokes consensus on Abu Bakr, the singular form, its antecedent, and a comparative that separates one referent from others; it denies that al is relative here.
92:17/q06 What event is reported behind the pious giver? [ٱلْأَتْقَى · reports] (turns on: Which donation story is transmitted and, within the Bilal story, how he was purchased.)
  92:17/q06/p1 Abu Bakr bought and freed vulnerable people persecuted for faith. — reasons: Urwa counts seven, including Bilal, Amir ibn Fuhayra, al-Nahdiyya and daughter, Zunayra, Umm Isa, and a Banu al-Muammal bondwoman. Ibn al-Zubayr reports his father's advice to buy strong defenders and Abu Bakr's reply about protecting his own back; versions phrase the reply as seeking protection, while al-Wahidi says worldly protection was not his aim. Al-Tha'labi says he spent forty thousand. Biqa'i adds Zahra's recovered sight after rejecting idols; the Study Quran tells of Bilal's defiance and a charge that Abu Bakr repaid a favor.
  92:17/q06/p2 Abu Bakr purchased Bilal for gold after his torture and testimony to God's oneness. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi's Ata report has the Prophet reassure Bilal and Abu Bakr pay gold; the Study Quran gives the idol desecration, testimony and the Prophet's assurance, then the gold purchase. The idolaters alleged a prior favor, answered by revelation.
  92:17/q06/p3 Abu Bakr obtained Bilal by exchanging Nustas, a wealthy slave who refused Islam. — reasons: Sa'id ibn al-Musayyib reports this form of the transaction and says revelation affirmed that Abu Bakr sought God alone and would be repaid in the hereafter.
  92:17/q06/p4 Abu al-Dahdah bought a palm and donated it to secure its owner a promised palm in Paradise. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi says the original owner refused the Prophet's offer, then Abu al-Dahdah bought and donated the palm; the Prophet later spoke of his abundant clusters in Paradise. Al-Qurtubi names the miser as the Ansari owner. Tabataba'i transmits a version with forty palms as the purchase price, while another names Abu al-Dahdah; al-Suyuti calls the Ibn Abbas report weak.
92:17/q07 Does this verse establish Abu Bakr's rank after the Prophet? [ٱلْأَتْقَى · theology] (turns on: Whether a verse about piety identifies an absolute personal rank and whether the reported referent is exclusive.)
  92:17/q07/p1 Abu Bakr is the community's best after the Prophet. — reasons: Al-Razi joins the most God-fearing here to the most honored in 49:13: visible piety reveals otherwise hidden rank. He says consensus narrows the candidate to Abu Bakr or Ali, then excludes Ali because of the Prophet's prior care, invoking 25:57. Al-Itqan reports this reasoning. Al-Biqa'i adds the promise of satisfaction and a report that God and believers accept Abu Bakr.
  92:17/q07/p2 The description does not prove that one person is absolutely best of all people. — reasons: Tabataba'i rejects the absolute rank inference and the assertion that all Shia sources name Ali; al-Alusi describes the Imam's rank argument as disputable.
92:17/q08 How do the two apparent restrictions about Hell fit together? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether 'only' yields a logical converse, whether ṣaly means any entry or severe torment, and whether the superlatives mark classes or exemplary figures.)
  92:17/q08/p1 The paired superlatives are rhetorical portraits of exemplary opponents, not strict exclusions of everyone else. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari likens the Fire and Garden to places made for the leading disbeliever and believer; examples are Abu Jahl or Umayya and Abu Bakr. Al-Kashf approves. Al-Alusi prefers this after weighing alternatives, though he notes the Mu'tazilite premise about grave sinners. Islahi rejects a literal inference that only the extremes are damned or saved.
  92:17/q08/p2 The earlier ṣaly is exceptionally severe burning, so other sinners may enter or receive lesser punishment. — reasons: Ibn al-Munir's lexical report likens ṣaly to placing an animal between layers of coals. Al-Alusi accepts this resolution; al-Baydawi and al-Biqa'i distinguish sinful believers' temporary entry from the wretched unbeliever's abiding fate.
