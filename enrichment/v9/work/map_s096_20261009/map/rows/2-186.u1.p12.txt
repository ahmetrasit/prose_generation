2:186/q11 What role does supplication have beside decree and ordinary causes? [دَعْوَةَ ٱلدَّاعِ · theology] (turns on: The disagreement turns on whether prayer is a cause within decree or futile before fixed outcomes, and on whether surrender calls for silence.)
  2:186/q11/p1 Prayer is a real cause within God's decree, while God remains sovereign over effects. — reasons: THALABI, d. 427 (بعضهم): Some restrict the general promise: prayers are answered only if God wills, accord with decree, avoid impossibility, or are beneficial; Abu Sa'id's report says a sinless, non-severing prayer receives one of three outcomes. THALABI, d. 427 (إبراهيم بن أدهم): Ibrahim ibn Adham says prayers go unanswered because people know God and the Prophet without obeying or following the Sunna, neglect the Qur'an and gratitude, ignore the afterlife and death, and fixate on others' faults. BAGHAWI-FULL, d. 516 (author): Supplication has manners and conditions that cause acceptance; those who fulfill them are eligible for an answer, while those who violate them transgress and do not deserve one. IBNATIYYA-FULL, d. 542 (النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم): The Prophet prayed that his community would not fight among themselves, but that prayer was denied because destiny had decreed otherwise. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (النبي عليه الصلاة والسلام): A saying attributed to the Prophet states that God decreed the measures before creating creation by a specified number of years; opponents cite it to argue that prayer cannot change destiny. QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 (ابن عطاء): Ibn Ata likens supplication to a structure with pillars, wings, causes, and times: when these fit, it gains strength, ascends, and succeeds. ABUHAYYAN-FULL, d. 745 (author): Prayer is a supreme act of servitude because it displays dependence on God; revelation and the prophets command and practice it, refuting those who say it has no benefit. ABUHAYYAN-FULL, d. 745 (قائلون ممن يسمون علماء الحقيقة): One group says prayer is appropriate for afterlife matters but unnecessary for worldly needs because God already undertakes them. BURSEVI, d. 1127 (author): Supplication has conditions and courtesies that cause an answer; one who neglects them commits excess and does not merit a response. ELMALILI, d. 1361 (author): The prophetic answer preserves a middle position: neither absolute compulsion nor unrestricted freedom, but a balance of divine determination and human choice, with people facilitated rather than coerced. ELMALILI, d. 1361 (author): The predestination objection is incomplete: some outcomes are decreed conditional on supplication, just as satiety depends on eating and success on seeking and effort. ELMALILI, d. 1361 (author): Created beings, not God, are subject to decree; prayer acknowledges divine precedence and is therefore a high form of worship. ELMALILI, d. 1361 (author): Since events are tied to causes and effort follows laws, supplication is itself one of the causes through which decreed outcomes occur. ELMALILI, d. 1361 (author): The second fatalist objection says events are fixed by an eternal cause: what it decreed is necessary, what it did not decree impossible, so prayer can neither increase nor decrease destiny. ELMALILI, d. 1361 (author): The verse rebukes people who shout and make a commotion in prayer because they imagine God is difficult to hear or far away. TABATABAI, d. 1402 (اليهود وغيرهم): An objection says the requested need is either already decreed, and therefore necessary, or not decreed, and therefore impossible; either way prayer cannot affect it. TABATABAI, d. 1402 (author): Predetermination does not make a thing independent of its causes; prayer itself is one cause, so when it occurs its effect can occur. This is what the reports mean by saying prayer is part of decree. TABATABAI, d. 1402 (رسول الله صلى الله عليه وآله وسلم): A Prophetic report says that nothing averts decree except prayer. TABATABAI, d. 1402 (أبو الحسن موسى عليه السلام): Abū al-Ḥasan Mūsā urges prayer because it averts a decreed calamity even when its implementation is all that remains. TABATABAI, d. 1402 (الصادق عليه السلام): Al-Ṣādiq again says prayer averts a firmly decreed outcome and calls it the key to mercy and success in every need; what is with God is attained only by prayer. TABATABAI, d. 1402 (author): The verse gives both the rule of answered prayer and its cause: people are God's servants, so He is near them, and His nearness grounds His unrestricted response. TABATABAI, d. 1402 (author): The prayer passages just cited contain prayer’s pillars and etiquette; their central requirement is sincerity, with heart and tongue aligned, detachment from every cause besides God, and reliance on Him. TABATABAI, d. 1402 (author): The preceding reports call for sincerity in prayer; they do not deny created causes, which God appoints as intermediaries rather than independent sources of effect. KURANYOLU-TEFSIR (author): Giving, withholding, and supplication all belong to destiny; asking does not oppose destiny but occurs within it. People should understand destiny as what will happen, not as a demand that events be otherwise. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (النبي عليه الصلاة والسلام): The Prophet is quoted as saying that the pen has dried concerning what will be, offered as support for the claim that prayer has no effect. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (author): Razi says the report holds people to both prior decree and action: it does not cancel outward work, whose performance is itself decreed and facilitated during life. ELMALILI, d. 1361 (Bazıları): Some object that predestination makes supplication futile: what is decreed will occur, while what is not decreed will not occur. ELMALILI, d. 1361 (author): Elmalılı also cites the saying that the pen has dried concerning what will be. ELMALILI, d. 1361 (author): Elmalılı rejects as ignorant the claim that prayer is useless, especially among advocates of a blind natural force and adherents of necessitarian or compulsion theories. TABATABAI, d. 1402 (اليهود): The Jews hold that after God created things and fixed their decrees, control passed from Him; thus there is no abrogation, badāʾ, or response to prayer. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (author): Razi calls the first objection contradictory: if prayer's result is already known to occur, refuting prayer is pointless; if it is known not to occur, denial is unnecessary.
  2:186/q11/p2 Created means should be used as dependent pathways to God. — reasons: IBNJAWZI-ZAD, d. 597 (أبو عبيدة وابن قتيبة والزجاج): Abu Ubayda, Ibn Qutayba, and al-Zajjaj take ujibu as “I answer”; the command falyastajibū therefore means “let them answer Me,” supported by a poem using istajaba for answering. TABATABAI, d. 1402 (author): Asking and seeking help from God means relying on Him as the true source: ordinary causes have only the limited, God-given role of pathways and intermediaries, so exclusive reliance on them is misplaced. TABATABAI, d. 1402 (author): This teaching does not abolish created causes: a person asks with the heart, speaks with the tongue, and employs bodily organs, just as giving, seeing, and hearing require a hand, eye, and ear. TABATABAI, d. 1402 (author): Even people who deny that ordinary causes act by themselves may assume their mediation is indispensable; al-Tabataba'i says this assumption conflicts with God's complete sovereignty. TABATABAI, d. 1402 (author): Causation and the mediation of causes are real, but neither reason nor revelation confines events to their ordinary causes; only intrinsic impossibilities remain unattainable. TABATABAI, d. 1402 (author): Another failure is a heart attached to ordinary causes or imagined powers as sufficient or effective; despite verbal invocation, the person has not truly and exclusively asked God. KURANYOLU-TEFSIR (author): God orders events in the material world through laws and causes, using intermediaries such as parents for birth, clouds for rain, causes for death, and organs for hearing and sight. KURANYOLU-TEFSIR (author): Seeking God without means is like asking for birth without parents or sight without eyes; relying only on means while forgetting God is equally mistaken. Islam calls for using means while turning to God, who controls causes and outcomes.
  2:186/q11/p3 Some hold that leaving prayer better expresses trust or surrender. — reasons: RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم عن الله): A divine saying transmitted through the Prophet promises a greater gift to one whom remembrance keeps from asking; opponents use it to favor leaving supplication. RAZI-FULL, d. 606 (بعض الجهال): From these arguments, the opponents conclude that leaving supplication is preferable. ABUHAYYAN-FULL, d. 745 (قائلون منهم): Another group says to pray when the praying state improves the heart and inner self, but to refrain when silence is better. ABUHAYYAN-FULL, d. 745 (قائلون منهم): Some say always to leave prayer as a sign of trust, non-objection, and surrender of choice. ABUHAYYAN-FULL, d. 745 (قائلون منهم): Some say abandoning sins itself functions as prayer, since God then takes charge of one’s affairs. ELMALILI, d. 1361 (Bazıları): Some cite Gabriel’s and Abraham’s surrendering sayings, acceptance of divine decree, and a sacred hadith preferring remembrance to asking to argue that leaving supplication is better.
