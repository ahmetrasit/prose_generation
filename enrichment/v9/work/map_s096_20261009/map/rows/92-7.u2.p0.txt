## THE CURRENT MAP
92:7/q01 What does the future marker sa- imply? [فَسَنُيَسِّرُهُۥ · grammar]
  92:7/q01/p1 Facilitation continues from now until the end of life. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr compares 12:98; the previously described deeds already occurred, so the promise sustains and increases them.
92:7/q02 Why does God speak in the first-person plural? [فَسَنُيَسِّرُهُۥ · rhetoric]
  92:7/q02/p1 It marks God's authoritative promise and the divine power needed to ease obedience. — reasons: Biqāʿī says obedience, though intrinsically easy, burdens the self; Corpus Coranicum says the plural authorizes the paired promises alongside the oath.
92:7/q03 What does nuyassiruhu mean here? [فَسَنُيَسِّرُهُۥ · meaning]
  92:7/q03/p1 God prepares and makes a person ready for a course or outcome. — reasons: Kashshāf, Bayḍāwī, Ālūsī and Elmalılı compare saddling and bridling a horse for riding; Ibn ʿĀshūr says taysīr literally makes something easy to obtain, citing 20:26 and 54:17, and treats 'prepare' as figurative in one analysis.
92:7/q04 How does the object and li- phrase work with nuyassiruhu? [فَسَنُيَسِّرُهُۥ لِلْيُسْرَىٰ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the ordinary object-beneficiary relation is inverted, li- expresses purpose with figurative preparation, or the words name states while the verb remains literal.)
  92:7/q04/p1 The literal syntax is rhetorically inverted: God eases the easy path for the person. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr observes that normally the direct object is what becomes easy and li- marks its beneficiary, as 20:26 and 54:17 show; inversion intensifies the facilitation. He compares presenting a she-camel to a watering trough.
  92:7/q04/p2 The verb figuratively means prepare; li- gives the purpose, with entry into Paradise or Hell understood. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr compares easy entry to speed rather than delay (50:44); Hell's parallel wording is mocking.
  92:7/q04/p3 The verb remains literal without inversion: the easy and hard terms name states, and facilitation means persistence in deeds. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr cites the Bukhārī report that each is facilitated for the work for which created.
92:7/q05 Whom does the pronoun refer to? [فَسَنُيَسِّرُهُۥ · referent] (turns on: Whether the preceding description is taken generally or identified with the reported occasions involving Abū Bakr or the palm purchase.)
  92:7/q05/p1 The giver who fears God and affirms His promise, described in the preceding verses; the pattern applies generally. — reasons: Ṭabarī identifies the generous God-fearing believer; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the clauses mark faith, with omitted object of 'fears' understood as God and compares 74:43–44. Rāzī says the sūra's human striving and Fire warning show general meaning (92:4, 92:14).
  92:7/q05/p2 Abū Bakr is the exemplary giver. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī says revelation concerned him; reports describe freeing tortured or weak converts, with his father urging strong slaves for protection and Abū Bakr seeking God's reward. The Bilāl account names Umayya ibn Khalaf and a garment and ten coins; Ibn Masʿūd also names Ubayy among opposed figures. Ibn ʿAbbās names Abū Sufyān as miser in a report through al-Kalbī and Abū Ṣāliḥ.
  92:7/q05/p3 Abū al-Daḥdāḥ is the giver, set against a stingy palm owner. — reasons: ʿAṭāʾ's versions say a palm dropped dates into a poor neighbor's yard, the owner refused a promised palm in Paradise, and Abū al-Daḥdāḥ bought or traded his orchard for it and gave it to the family. Ibn ʿAbbās's version says forty palms; reports vary in detail.
92:7/q06 What does God make easy for this person in practice? [فَسَنُيَسِّرُهُۥ لِلْيُسْرَىٰ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the promised ease is chiefly enabled conduct, renewed conduct, a pleasant state in both lives, or entry to Paradise; Ṭabāṭabāʾ explicitly favors the afterlife emphasis.)
  92:7/q06/p1 God enables pleasing and righteous deeds until obedience becomes easy. — reasons: Ṭabarī locates these deeds in this life and Paradise as their reward; Māwardī and Qurṭubī stress making the means easy; Qushayrī adds love of faith and excellence, hatred of disobedience, and desire for nearness. Kashshāf compares opening the breast to Islam (6:125); Rāzī contrasts hypocritical sluggishness (2:45, 4:142, 9:38). Ṭabrisī stresses repeated, eager obedience; Islahi adds removal of obstacles and strengthened desire (29:69); Kur'an Yolu says generosity becomes a stable trait; Quṭb describes calm ease spreading through worldly life.
  92:7/q06/p2 God eases a return to earlier obedience, especially renewed giving. — reasons: Farrāʾ and Ibn Qutayba describe a return to righteous acts; Muqātil specifies repeated giving, while Qushayrī mentions resuming worship. Rāzī contrasts return to withholding and denial of financial rights; Ibn ʿĀshūr reasons that acts already described precede the future promise.
  92:7/q06/p3 God readies the person for an easy and pleasant worldly and afterlife condition, culminating in Paradise. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya says both lives are good and pleasing, while the opposite condition is bad in both; Abū Ḥayyān, Bayḍāwī, Ālūsī, and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ discuss a comfortable state or disposition. Biqāʿī mentions mercy, a good life and Paradise; Elmalılı includes easy reckoning (84:8–9). Ṭabāṭabāʾ explicitly prefers an ease-filled afterlife reached through deeds over mere ease of doing them.
  92:7/q06/p4 God makes entry into Paradise easy and honorable. — reasons: Ṭabrisī mentions the angels' greeting and good news; Rāzī cites 13:23–24 and 39:73 for greeting at entry; Ibn ʿĀshūr's preparation analysis supplies entry understood after li- and likens quick entry to ease (50:44).
92:7/q07 What is al-yusrā? [لِلْيُسْرَىٰ · meaning] (turns on: Whether yusrā names deeds or their course, Paradise and its reward, or a broader easy state spanning both lives.)
  92:7/q07/p1 The easy course of goodness, obedience and deeds pleasing to God. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās repeatedly glosses it as good; Ṭabarī, Thaʿlabī, Baghawī and Nasafī call it approved deeds. Al-Qaffāl says obedience is named for its restful outcome, contrasted with sins ending in hardship; some describe the route to Paradise. Ibn ʿĀshūr says it broadly means what benefits without hardship.
  92:7/q07/p2 Paradise, the good destination or ease of afterlife bliss. — reasons: Zayd ibn Aslam and Mujāhid are named repeatedly; Ṭabrisī includes angelic welcome; Rāzī says the promised Paradise makes strenuous worship bearable. Baghawī maps it to Abū al-Daḥdāḥ's reward; Elmalılı and Islahi cite the easy reckoning in 84:7–9. Ṭabāṭabāʾ prefers this afterlife sense over good works alone.
  92:7/q07/p3 The pleasant and least burdensome condition or easy disposition in this life and the next. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān explicitly include both lives; Bayḍāwī and Ālūsī speak of a trait leading to rest and Paradise. Ibn ʿĀshūr allows an easy rank or place; Qur'an Yolu includes a greater capacity for virtue, and Quṭb details ease radiating into everyday affairs.
92:7/q08 What form and implied noun underlie al-yusrā? [لِلْيُسْرَىٰ · grammar]
  92:7/q08/p1 It is the feminine 'easier', qualifying an implied way, trait, deed, outcome, or class of deeds. — reasons: Jishumī relates yusr to comfort and abundance. Rāzī proposes a group of acts, an implicit feminine khilla or faʿla, return to an act, or a feminine way; Islahi suggests al-ʿāqibah, outcome. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the definite article may be generic or referential, and feminine forms may categorize deeds by consequences.
92:7/q09 What force does the feminine form yusrā have compared with yusr? [لِلْيُسْرَىٰ · meaning]
  92:7/q09/p1 It conveys the extreme of ease. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ contrasts yusrā and ʿusrā with verbal nouns yusr and ʿusr, comparing the Qur'anic greatest seizure and greatest Fire (79:25; 92:14).
92:7/q10 How is al-yusrā read? [لِلْيُسْرَىٰ · readings]
  92:7/q10/p1 Abū Jaʿfar reads the sīn with ḍamma in both al-yusrā and al-ʿusrā. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī reports his vocalization; Qiraat-ER says inserting a short vowel between sīn and rāʾ is phonological and leaves root and meaning intact. NASHR reports that Abū ʿAmr also gives the sīn ḍamma in the paired yusr and ʿusr forms, including dhū ʿusra; it cross-references Abū Jaʿfar's ḍamma across those related forms here and elsewhere.
92:7/q11 Why are the two paths called easy and hard? [لِلْيُسْرَىٰ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the wording names deeds by their eventual effects or figures the resulting condition through a rhetorical transfer.)
  92:7/q11/p1 The names describe their outcomes: obedience ends in ease and rest, sin in toil and hardship. — reasons: Al-Qaffāl gives the causal explanation; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Ibn ʿĀshūr say the deeds take their names from their eventual results. Rāzī adds that expected Paradise makes hard worship easy to bear. Zamakhsharī's account is reported by Abū Ḥayyān and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ.
  92:7/q11/p2 The wording uses a metaphor, metonymy, or figurative attribution for a comfortable outcome. — reasons: Ālūsī names the three rhetorical analyses; Corpus Coranicum notes that pairing near-homophonous yusr and ʿusr with yassara veils the destinations and makes facilitation toward hardship paradoxical, with comparison to 94:5–6.
