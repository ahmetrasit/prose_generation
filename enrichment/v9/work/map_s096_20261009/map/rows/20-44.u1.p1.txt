20:44/q10 Whose words or hope does the tentative clause express? [لَعَلَّهُۥ · referent] (turns on: The usual reading locates hope in the two messengers; al-Ḥasan assigns the words to Aaron and Ibn ʿĀshūr allows a figurative divine voice.)
  20:44/q10/p1 It gives Moses and Aaron's hope as they act; divine knowledge of Pharaoh's future is unaffected. — reasons: Sībawayh's paraphrase is 'go with your hope and expectation.' Prophets lack knowledge of the unseen. Al-Ṭabrisī and al-Wāḥidī say hopeful effort improves preaching; al-Bayḍāwī contrasts earnest effort with feigned work. Al-Burhān says possible events are certain to God but uncertain to creatures.
  20:44/q10/p2 It quotes Aaron's words to Moses. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan gives this as the speaker of 'perhaps Pharaoh will remember or fear.'
  20:44/q10/p3 It figuratively voices God's invitation or request in human terms. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr offers this alternative; al-Ālūsī mentions figurative general request while choosing ordinary hope.
20:44/q11 What does the hope clause attach to grammatically? [لَعَلَّهُۥ · grammar] (turns on: The question is whether the clause modifies the command to speak or the earlier command to go.)
  20:44/q11/p1 It is circumstantial to the implied dual subject of 'speak.' — reasons: Al-Ālūsī prefers attachment to قولا and says أو keeps both outcomes possible.
  20:44/q11/p2 It is circumstantial to the dual subject of the earlier 'go.' — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī allows attachment to ذهبا or قولا; al-Ālūsī reports the former but prefers the latter.
20:44/q12 What does يَتَذَكَّرُ mean? [يَتَذَكَّرُ · interpretation] (turns on: The glosses vary between intellectual recognition, recollection of lordship, and practical repentance.)
  20:44/q12/p1 Reflect on the reminder and acknowledge the truth, yielding to it. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī, Abū Ḥayyān, and al-Ālūsī describe reflection leading to fairness. Al-Biqāʿī specifies God's care through Pharaoh's changing life stages. Ibn ʿĀshūr says recognition corrects his false confidence. Ṭabāṭabāʾī makes it acceptance of the reminder, proof, and faith.
  20:44/q12/p2 Recall God's lordship and his own servitude. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī defines the mission's desired recollection this way.
  20:44/q12/p3 Turn back from forbidden conduct or misguidance. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr contrasts this with fear as attaining obedience and cites 87:10; the Study Quran reports heeding the warning or turning from misguidance.
20:44/q13 What does يَخْشَىٰ mean? [يَخْشَىٰ · interpretation] (turns on: The notes differ over fear from a possibly true warning, fear of God leading to obedience, and fear of divine power.)
  20:44/q13/p1 Fear that the warning is true and denial may bring punishment or ruin. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Biqāʿī, and al-Ālūsī stress fear despite incomplete certainty. Al-Biqāʿī says it might lead Pharaoh to release Israel. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls this cautious compliance. Al-Ālūsī prefers it to a generic account of divine punishment.
  20:44/q13/p2 Fear God, submit, and become obedient to Him. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī connects fear with the threatened punishment; al-Baghawī says fear and submission to Islam. Ibn Kathīr defines fear as attaining obedience, citing 87:10. The Study Quran says fear may return Pharaoh to God. Al-Burṣawī links reverent fear especially with the learned, citing 35:28.
  20:44/q13/p3 Become wary of God's power and punishment. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports this explanation but favors fear that the messengers' warning may be true.
20:44/q14 Why is remembering mentioned before fearing? [يَتَذَكَّرُ يَخْشَىٰ · rhetoric] (turns on: The disagreement concerns whether fear is the lesser response after recognition is offered, or a stage leading toward it.)
  20:44/q14/p1 Recognition and wholehearted acceptance come first; fear of a possibly true warning is a lesser fallback. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī says remembrance follows recognized truth, fear suspected truth. Rāzī ranks persistent truth, suspension, and persistent falsehood and says either improvement beats Pharaoh's fixed rejection. Al-Ālūsī calls remembrance the response of the grounded.
  20:44/q14/p2 Fear can precede full acceptance, so either may bring Pharaoh toward faith. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī says fear comes before acceptance of the reminder, making the pair 'believe or approach belief and answer them.'
20:44/q15 What does أَوْ do between the two outcomes? [أَوْ · grammar] (turns on: Some read the conjunction as additive; others retain two possible outcomes, while the lexical notes also discuss ordinary uncertainty.)
  20:44/q15/p1 It is additive, equivalent to 'and.' — reasons: Al-Suddī, exegetes cited by Ibn Qutayba and Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Dāmghānī, al-Burhān, and al-Itqān take it this way. Examples include 77:6, 80:3, 20:113, a Jarīr verse and a Tawba verse. Ibn al-Jawzī also reports al-Farrāʾ's poetic 'rather' and ordinary choice, but those are general functions of أو, not asserted for this clause.
  20:44/q15/p2 It permits either remembrance or fear as a sought outcome, without excluding both. — reasons: Al-Burhān says either outcome may be sought. Al-Irshād, quoted by al-Burṣawī, says أو excludes neither. Al-Ālūsī likewise says the particle prevents excluding either.
  20:44/q15/p3 It can express uncertainty between alternatives. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba illustrates with seeing ʿAbd Allāh or Muḥammad; Ibn al-Jawzī gives Zayd or ʿAmr and also an ordinary choice between a coin and silver value. Al-Dāmghānī's excerpt begins a separate awwal entry on first unbelief and belief but gives no local application.
20:44/q16 Who might remember or fear? [لَعَلَّهُۥ · referent] (turns on: The pronoun's immediate target is Pharaoh, while reported readings transfer the hoped for response to witnesses.)
  20:44/q16/p1 Pharaoh himself. — reasons: This is the surface referent throughout the address; Muqātil and others explain the hope as his chance to heed.
  20:44/q16/p2 A witness or others who hear and see God's gentleness. — reasons: Al-Ḥusayn ibn al-Faḍl says a witness might learn from kindness to the claimant of divinity; al-Nasafī reports 'a reminder may remember or a fearful person may fear,' adding that many did. Al-Tustarī names magicians and others whom God knows He will guide.
20:44/q17 Did Pharaoh remember or fear, and when? [يَتَذَكَّرُ يَخْشَىٰ · reports] (turns on: Reports place a response at drowning, before Hāmān's counsel, or in the Nile episode; another report emphasizes nonbelief.)
  20:44/q17/p1 He remembered, feared, and professed faith only while drowning, too late to benefit him. — reasons: Al-Warrāq, Muʿādh, Qurṭubī, al-Nasafī, and al-Burṣawī connect the response to 10:90. Al-Ālūsī rejects applying this late response to the clause, and rejects a salvation argument built from it.
  20:44/q17/p2 He remembered and feared earlier, but Hāmān diverted him from following Moses. — reasons: Al-Nasafī says Pharaoh wanted to follow Moses but made no decision without Hāmān. Al-Baghawī and al-Ṭabrisī say he liked the offered youth, rule, pleasures, and Paradise until Hāmān shamed him as a deity becoming a subject. Qurṭubī adds that his believing wife urged faith, Hāmān offered to restore his youth and dyed his beard black, reportedly first to do so; Abū Ḥayyān names Āsiyah.
  20:44/q17/p3 He prostrated when the Nile receded and might have recalled God's forbearance when it followed his horse. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī transmit this Nile episode; Abū Ḥayyān frames the hoped for remembrance and fear around divine generosity and punishment.
  20:44/q17/p4 He was divinely fated not to believe. — reasons: Kaʿb's Torah report says God commanded gentle speech while announcing He would harden Pharaoh's heart. The theologians and commentators also discuss God's advance knowledge of nonbelief, without making it a narrative of prior remembrance.
