## THE CURRENT MAP
79:19/q01 What guidance does Moses offer Pharaoh? [وَأَهْدِيَكَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether guidance here names knowledge of God, the practices of worship and obedience, the path to divine approval, or an inward realization; Tabrisi expressly gives knowledge and the path to approval as alternatives.)
  79:19/q01/p1 He offers to bring Pharaoh to knowledge of God, His oneness, attributes, greatness, and the truth of His religion. — reasons: Muqatil specifies God's greatness. Qushayri says Moses would establish his message with signs and teach the truth of religion. Tabrisi says knowledge of Pharaoh's Creator and Sustainer is one explanation. Nasafi specifies recounting God's attributes. Bursevi understands an omitted complement, 'knowledge of your Lord.' Bint al-Shati connects direction to the straight path with the root's raised rock in water and daylight, both making a route clear. Ibn Ashur defines guidance as pointing out a path to a desired goal, conditional on the addressee's acceptance. Jishumi calls it an indication and path to knowledge, rather than faith itself. Razi allows instruction and indication to help disclose truth. Biqai calls the Lord creator, benefactor, and nurturer, who teaches pleasing deeds and blameworthy traits.
  79:19/q01/p2 He calls Pharaoh to worship and obey God and affirm His oneness. — reasons: Wahidi reports Ibn Abbas and Muqatil on worship. Baghawi and Ibn al-Jawzi add affirming God's oneness and fearing His punishment. Qurtubi emphasizes obedience and heeding God. Abu Hayyan identifies the promised purification with guidance to oneness and knowledge. Biqai says the Lord teaches pleasing actions and blameworthy traits.
  79:19/q01/p3 He directs Pharaoh toward God's approval and reward. — reasons: Tabrisi offers this as an alternative to teaching him to know his Creator and Sustainer; Ibn Ashur describes guidance as pointing along a path to a desired goal if the person accepts it.
  79:19/q01/p4 Inwardly, guidance is knowledge of the unity of the divine Essence, softening and dissolving selfhood. — reasons: Qashani reads the preceding purification as annihilation of selfhood and this guidance to fear as knowledge of the Essence's unity.
79:19/q02 Is a human guide indispensable for knowing God? [وَأَهْدِيَكَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ · theology] (turns on: Whether Moses' offer makes a teacher a necessary condition for knowledge or shows the benefit of instruction and indication.)
  79:19/q02/p1 Yes: knowledge of God must come through a guide, and this guidance is the mission's chief aim. — reasons: Razi reports proponents citing Moses' explicit offer of guidance as proof. They argue that purification already includes all obligatory duties, so the added guidance must be central, and that ending the address with guidance marks its noblest objective.
  79:19/q02/p2 No: instruction can help disclose truth, but knowing God is not impossible without a teacher. — reasons: Razi expressly accepts the usefulness of instruction and indication while rejecting their necessity. Jishumi describes guidance as an indication and path to knowledge rather than faith itself. Ibn Ashur says pointing out the route leads to the goal only if the addressee accepts it.
79:19/q03 Why does Moses call God 'your Lord'? [رَبِّكَ · rhetoric]
  79:19/q03/p1 The familiar title gently presses God's lordship over Pharaoh and opens a path to monotheism and fear. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says it could win a hearing without provoking Pharaoh, so faith may enter gradually after Moses' proof. Bint al-Shati says it establishes the claim against Pharaoh's denial of Moses' Lord and prepares for fear, which requires faith in his Lord. Biqai explains Lord as creator, benefactor, and nurturer.
79:19/q04 What does 'then you will fear' mean? [فَتَخْشَىٰ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the fear is explained by religious conduct and punishment or by Qashani's inward account of divine unity and loss of selfhood.)
  79:19/q04/p1 Pharaoh would fear God and His punishment, with reverence expressed in duties and avoidance of disobedience. — reasons: Jishumi equates khashya with khawf, fear. Nahhas specifies fear of punishment through avoiding disobedience. Wahidi's gloss adds salvation through fear and affirmation of oneness; the scholars of meaning specify performing required duties from fear of punishment. Tabrisi adds leaving prohibited acts; Baydawi and Biqai add both duties and prohibitions. Elmalili specifies reverent fear of wrath and punishment. Ibn Ashur says religious usage means fear of God and the verb is intransitive here because God is its understood object. Tabatabai's reported repentance reading makes this faith-driven fear motivate obedience and restrain sin.
  79:19/q04/p2 Inwardly, fear is knowledge of divine unity that softens and dissolves selfhood. — reasons: Qashani links it with the preceding purification understood as annihilation of selfhood.
79:19/q05 Why does fear follow guidance? [وَأَهْدِيَكَ فَتَخْشَىٰ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the explanation emphasizes knowledge as fear's cause, Pharaoh's acceptance of guidance, or an inward transformation.)
  79:19/q05/p1 Knowledge of God gained through guidance produces reverent fear, which then grounds obedience and good action. — reasons: Sulami reports Muhammad ibn Ali al-Tirmidhi calling fear the fruit of sound guidance. Ibn Atiyya places knowledge after guidance and fear after knowledge, citing 35:28. Razi says guidance precedes fear and knowledge precedes obedience, citing 16:2 and 20:14; he also cites 35:28. Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi, Alusi, Ibn Ashur, and Tabatabai also invoke 35:28, that the learned fear God. Nasafi reports a sage saying one who knows God cannot disobey Him even for an instant. Elmalili says lack of knowledge leaves one without reverence or moral guard and leads to wrongdoing. Bint al-Shati says fear requires faith in one's Lord. Bursevi frames knowledge as adornment following purification as cleansing. Ibn Ashur reports al-Wasiti's successive stages: the beginning of knowledge is fear, followed by reverence, glorification, awe, and finally annihilation.
  79:19/q05/p2 Fear follows if Pharaoh accepts the offered guidance and its path to the goal. — reasons: Ibn Ashur conditions the sequence on Pharaoh's readiness for purification and says guidance points to the goal only if the guided person accepts it; the order of clauses follows the order of events.
  79:19/q05/p3 Inward knowledge of the divine Essence's unity yields fear by softening and dissolving selfhood. — reasons: Qashani connects the guidance to fear with annihilation of selfhood in purification.
79:19/q06 What conduct or outcome does fear produce? [فَتَخْشَىٰ · interpretation]
  79:19/q06/p1 It motivates duties and every good while restraining disobedience and evil; feeling secure emboldens wrongdoing. — reasons: Nahhas and Tabrisi specify avoiding disobedience and prohibitions. Wahidi's scholars of meaning, Baydawi, and Biqai specify carrying out duties; Baydawi and Biqai also specify leaving prohibitions. Wahidi's gloss adds salvation and affirming oneness. Razi, Nasafi, Biqai, Bursevi, and Alusi contrast fear as a source or foundation of good with false security as an invitation to evil. Nasafi reports a sage's claim that one who knows God cannot disobey for an instant. Elmalili says the ignorant lack reverence and moral restraint. Tabatabai's reported reading has faith-driven fear promote obedience and check sin.
79:19/q07 Why is fear singled out as the result of guidance? [فَتَخْشَىٰ · rhetoric]
  79:19/q07/p1 Fear is the foundation of good conduct, so naming it concisely conveys the other goods it brings. — reasons: Abu Hayyan calls it the core of the matter. Razi, Nasafi, Biqai, Bursevi, and Alusi call it the source or foundation of good and contrast it with feeling secure, which invites evil. Ibn Ashur explicitly calls mention of fear alone concise and eloquent.
79:19/q08 How is 'then you will fear' joined to the preceding offer? [وَأَهْدِيَكَ فَتَخْشَىٰ · grammar]
  79:19/q08/p1 It is joined to what precedes and presents fear as the outcome after guidance. — reasons: Nahhas says فتخشى is coordinated with what precedes. Ibn Ashur says the clauses follow the order of events; waw can express sequence contextually, and readiness for purification leads to guidance and fear.
79:19/q09 What Prophetic saying illustrates the benefit of fear? [فَتَخْشَىٰ · reports]
  79:19/q09/p1 Whoever fears sets out at night, and whoever sets out reaches the destination. — reasons: Razi reports the Prophet's comparison of the fearful traveler reaching his goal with God-fear attaining good. Nasafi and Bursevi quote the saying. Alusi reports it from Abu Hurayra through al-Tirmidhi. Ibn Ashur attributes it to Abu Hurayra in al-Tirmidhi's Jami, phrases the departure as early from fear, and gives no grading.
79:19/q10 What broader Quranic uses of fear and humility illuminate this phrase? [فَتَخْشَىٰ · links] (turns on: The cited usages concern different bearers and expressions of fear or humility, rather than a stated dispute.)
  79:19/q10/p1 The Quran attributes fear of God to messengers, followers of the Reminder, believers, scholars, and people God approves. — reasons: Bint al-Shati cites 36:11, 50:33, 21:49, 35:18, 67:13, 13:21, 23:57, 98:8, 33:37, 5:3, 5:44, 9:13, 2:150, 4:77, 9:18, and 3:173.
  79:19/q10/p2 Attributing fear to mountains and stones portrays lifeless matter as able to perceive, respond, fear God, and humble itself. — reasons: Bint al-Shati cites 59:21 and 2:74.
  79:19/q10/p3 Khusūʿ is inward humility toward God in worldly life, expressed outwardly through voice, face, and gaze. — reasons: Bint al-Shati says the heart is humble and outward expressions reflect it; she cites 17:107–109 for Quranic khusūʿ directed to God and describing believers in this life.
