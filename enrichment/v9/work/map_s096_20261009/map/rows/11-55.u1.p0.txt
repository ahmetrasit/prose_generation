## THE CURRENT MAP
11:55/q01 What does “besides Him” refer to here? [مِن دُونِهِۦ · referent]
  11:55/q01/p1 The gods or idols worshipped alongside God. — reasons: Muqatil and Baghawi identify the referents as their gods or idols. Nasafi takes the phrase as association of gods with God, of which Hūd declares himself innocent. Ibn Ashur says the following collective challenge confirms the reference to idols.
11:55/q02 What does kayd mean? [فَكِيدُونِى · meaning]
  11:55/q02/p1 It is scheming to cause harm or vexation. — reasons: Jishumi equates kayd and makīda with scheming harm, traces the root to handling or working on something, and notes that kayada bi-nafsihi may mean yielding up life. Biqai specifies a covert scheme meant to provoke or vex.
11:55/q03 Whom does Hūd challenge to plot? [فَكِيدُونِى جَمِيعًۭا · referent] (turns on: Whether the idols share the direct grammatical address or are brought into a challenge directed to their worshippers.)
  11:55/q03/p1 His people together with their gods or idols, and all who stand with them. — reasons: Muqatil, Tabari, Wahidi, Baghawi, Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, Ibn Jawzi, Qurtubi, Nasafi, Ibn Kathir, Alusi, Elmalili, Tabatabai, and Study Quran include people and idols. Ibn Ishaq includes everyone with the addressees. Biqai allows each person separately or all collectively with idols. Alusi reports this direct address as one option. Ibn Ashur says the idols are included in the challenge to meet the people's belief, while the people are the grammatical addressees.
  11:55/q03/p2 The human audience alone is addressed, although the challenge still implicates their idols. — reasons: Alusi reports an interpretation addressed to the people alone. Ibn Ashur explicitly makes the people the addressees, with their idols included for the sake of their belief and humiliation.
11:55/q04 What action does Hūd tell them to take against him? [فَكِيدُونِى · interpretation]
  11:55/q04/p1 They should unite in a hostile plot and attempt whatever harm, even killing, they can. — reasons: Wahidi Basit mentions hostility, anger, or striking him; Baydawi mentions trying to kill him. Ibn Atiyya and Nasafi stress his open repudiation and lack of fear. Ibn Kathir qualifies the gods' participation with 'if real.' Alusi says they should gather with every idol and cooperate; his conditional formulation tests their claim that the gods can harm Hūd. Islahi says zeal for monotheism makes Hūd interrupt the accusation and issue the challenge.
11:55/q05 What does the command to plot imply about their power to harm Hūd? [فَكِيدُونِى · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the imperative asserts that harm is impossible or only that Hūd does not fear possible harm; Tabatabai tests this by the following declaration of reliance on God.)
  11:55/q05/p1 It is a taunting, impossible challenge exposing their inability. — reasons: Ibn Ashur calls the imperative permission used as a taunt, comparing 77:39 and refuting 11:54. Tabatabai calls both imperatives deliberately impossible; the gods cannot harm or avenge themselves, and even the strong people cannot kill or punish him. Kashshaf and Baydawi stress the idols' lifelessness and failure to act. Alusi reasons that powerful people's inability makes the idols' inability still clearer; Nasafi rejects the claim that idols made Hūd mad.
  11:55/q05/p2 Hūd declares his fearlessness even supposing they can harm him. — reasons: Tabatabai reports this possible reading, comparing the magicians' words to Pharaoh in 20:72, but says Hūd's subsequent reliance on God rules it out.
11:55/q06 What is the syntax of “all together”? [جَمِيعًۭا · grammar]
  11:55/q06/p1 It is a circumstantial accusative describing the subject of “plot against me.” — reasons: Samin al-Halabi identifies جميعا as ḥāl from the subject of فكيدون.
11:55/q07 What force does “all together” add? [جَمِيعًۭا · rhetoric]
  11:55/q07/p1 It makes the challenge encompass the whole group and their idols. — reasons: Tabatabai says including all people and idols completes the proof of Hūd's truth and their falsehood. Ibn Ishaq includes everyone with the addressees; Biqai says individuals may act separately or jointly with idols. Samarrai says 'all' answers their claim that 'some' deity harmed Hūd in 11:54.
11:55/q08 Why is the challenge introduced with fa? [فَكِيدُونِى · rhetoric]
  11:55/q08/p1 It makes the challenge follow from their claim and Hūd's repudiation. — reasons: Alusi derives the challenge from both their hint that their gods can harm him and Hūd's disavowal of them. Samarrai likewise relates the confrontation to the charge that a deity harmed him.
11:55/q09 What does thumma contribute before the ban on delay? [ثُمَّ · rhetoric]
  11:55/q09/p1 It raises the challenge to the stronger demand that they attack without respite. — reasons: Biqai says an immediate attack is more alarming and difficult, so thumma intensifies the challenge. Ibn Ashur calls it a rise in rhetorical rank expressing utmost contempt and their inability to harm Hūd.
11:55/q10 What does “do not give me respite” mean? [لَا تُنظِرُونِ · meaning]
  11:55/q10/p1 Do not delay me; act at once without granting time to consider. — reasons: Ibn Abbas says not to delay or give respite; Dahhak says not to postpone. Ibn Atiyya specifies immediate use of whatever harm they can manage. Tabrisi distinguishes inẓār, respite to consider one's affairs, from simple taʾkhīr, placing something later. Wahidi Wajiz, Baghawi, Qurtubi, and Itqan repeat the no-delay gloss; Itqan phrases it 'wa-lā tunẓirūn.'
11:55/q11 How is the first-person yā of “plot against me” read? [فَكِيدُونِى · readings]
  11:55/q11/p1 The other readers retain its yā in both connected recitation and pause. — reasons: Samin gives this reading and contrasts the universal omission of the yā in 77:39; his wording 'all other readers' implies a contrasted reader not specified in this note.
11:55/q12 How is the final first-person yā of “do not give me respite” read? [لَا تُنظِرُونِ · readings]
  11:55/q12/p1 Yaqub retains the additional yā in connection and at pause. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari attributes the retained extra yā in ثم لا تنظروني to Yaqub. Qiraat-ER describes adding yā after nūn al-wiqāya as extending the sound of the first-person pronoun without changing meaning.
11:55/q13 Why is the full first-person yā used here in comparison with Aʿrāf? [فَكِيدُونِى · rhetoric]
  11:55/q13/p1 The longer form suits Hūd's longer, more intense confrontation and repeated first-person yā. — reasons: Samarrai contrasts this open challenge with the less confrontational setting in Aʿrāf 7:194–195, observes a longer exchange here and repeated first-person yā in 11:54–57 versus one instance at 7:196, and says full yā generally accompanies detailed contexts while kasra replacing it accompanies concise expression, apart from verse endings and vocatives. He also observes that audibly repeated yā and the full form occur more often in their respective contexts.
11:55/q14 How does the order of fa and thumma here compare with Aʿrāf? [فَكِيدُونِى ثُمَّ · links]
  11:55/q14/p1 Hūd puts fa before the challenge and thumma before denying respite, reversing Aʿrāf's order. — reasons: Samarrai says fa signals immediacy and thumma delay. Aʿrāf's early focus on swift worldly punishment suits immediate denial of respite, while Hūd emphasizes respite and delayed punishment (7:4; 11:3, 11:8). He contrasts concise Noah and swift ʿĀd and Ṣāliḥ narratives in Aʿrāf with Hūd's longer exchanges, later judgment details, and different rescue-punishment sequence (7:63–64, 7:72, 7:78; 11:32–33, 11:58–60, 11:66–67). He says the same ordering pattern appears elsewhere (7:11, 7:95, 7:103; 11:61).
11:55/q15 What does the challenge establish? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The notes draw two compatible proofs from the same failed challenge: the idols' impotence and Hūd's divine protection.)
  11:55/q15/p1 Their idols are powerless and their claim that an idol afflicted Hūd is false. — reasons: Kashshaf calls idols inert and unable to harm or avenge themselves; Baydawi says failure forces that admission. Nasafi says they cannot harm or benefit, much less make Hūd mad. Alusi reasons from the united people's inability to the idols' still greater inability. Ibn Ashur connects the taunt with 11:54; Tabatabai calls their inaction rational proof against the partners' divinity and the alleged affliction. Kuranyolu says the challenge refutes their claim. Study Quran's Kl says neither people nor gods can do anything.
  11:55/q15/p2 Hūd's survival against a united, powerful enemy is a miracle and sign of his prophethood. — reasons: Zajjaj calls a lone messenger's unharmed challenge one of the greatest, clearest signs. Abu Ishaq, Ibn Atiyya, Ibn Jawzi quoting Zajjaj, Razi, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Biqai, Alusi's reported account, and Tabatabai stress the enemy's numbers or strength and failure to harm him. Razi says no lone man would invite immediate maximal hostility without certainty of God's protection. Tabatabai says the people's inability confirms his truthful message. Elmalili calls his confidence exceptionally clear.
11:55/q16 What sustains Hūd's confidence against his enemies? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether confidence is explained through trust in God's aid or through al-Tirmidhi al-Hakim's account of contemplative foreknowledge.)
  11:55/q16/p1 Trust in God's promised aid and protection. — reasons: Abu Ishaq says Hūd knows his many united enemies cannot reach him because he trusts God's aid. Qurtubi says the challenge shows complete confidence in God's help. Baydawi says their failure shows divine protection; Abu Hayyan notes sincerity and confidence in God's promised support. Alusi's reported account sees trust in protection; Ibn Ashur says the following explanation in 11:56 makes threats powerless. Kuranyolu mentions submission to God.
