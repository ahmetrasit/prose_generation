## THE CURRENT MAP
36:14/q01 How does 'when We sent' connect grammatically to the preceding verse? [إِذْ أَرْسَلْنَآ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the second time clause restates the first or supplies the time of the messengers' arrival.)
  36:14/q01/p1 It is apposition to the earlier 'when they came' in 36:13, explaining the arrival as a sending. — reasons: Al-Wahidi calls it apposition; al-Nasafi and Bursevi identify the second idh as apposition to the first. Al-Tabataba'i and Islahi say it elaborates the earlier mention of messengers coming. Al-Razi reports this possible parsing.
  36:14/q01/p2 The time clause modifies 'they came': they came when God sent them. — reasons: Al-Razi prefers this as clearer and stresses that the messengers did not arrive on their own initiative.
36:14/q02 Who sent the messengers, and were they prophets or Jesus's disciples? [أَرْسَلْنَآ مُّرْسَلُونَ · referent] (turns on: The force of God's 'We sent' and the people's objection to human messengers, weighed against reports that Jesus acted under God's command and whether the miracles prove prophethood.)
  36:14/q02/p1 Jesus sent his disciples under God's command, so God attributes their commission to Himself. — reasons: Qatada's account names Antioch and says Jesus sent two disciples then a third; Ibn Jurayj and others say an envoy of God's messenger can be called God's envoy. Al-Thalabi, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, Bursevi, Elmalili and Study Quran explain the attribution through God's authorization. Al-Razi says their rejection consoles Muhammad as rejection of him. Al-Alusi answers the objection from their miracles: these may be Jesus's confirming signs through representatives. Al-Tabrisi cites Wahb and Ka'b; al-Ayyashi's variant says Jesus sent his legatee Shimon to rescue the first two. Bursevi infers that on this account the apostles were not prophets. Al-Razi and Bursevi compare an authorized subagent, whom the principal may dismiss but the intervening agent may not.
  36:14/q02/p2 God sent prophets directly; the words describe divine messengers in their own right. — reasons: The apparent 'We sent' favors this reading for Ibn al-Jawzi, Elmalili and al-Alusi. Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi point to the people's objection that the men were mere humans; Islahi adds their sworn claim in 36:16. Al-Alusi cites miracles as an argument proper to prophets, and says one scholar saw them as confirming Jesus's law as Aaron supported Moses. Study Quran cites mursal for Muhammad in 36:3 and 2:252 and its use for other prophets. Al-Tabataba'i's transmitted variant distinguishes God's three envoys from Jesus's later dispatch of Shim'un.
  36:14/q02/p3 The first two are Moses and Aaron, with Moses the primary messenger and Aaron his assistant. — reasons: Islahi says they are the sole pair sent simultaneously to one people in prophetic history, as earlier surahs discuss; his account identifies the supporting third as Pharaoh's believing kinsman and treats the plural claim as collective embassy speech.
36:14/q03 When were Jesus's disciples dispatched? [أَرْسَلْنَآ · reports] (turns on: The transmitted chronology of Jesus's ascension and the disciples' dispersion.)
  36:14/q03/p1 They were sent after Jesus was raised to heaven. — reasons: Al-Kalbi dates the event to the raising. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan add that one made to resemble Jesus was crucified and the disciples dispersed. Al-Qurtubi and Elmalili also place the sending after the raising.
  36:14/q03/p2 God ordered Jesus to send the apostles to cities before his ascension. — reasons: Kashf al-Asrar, as reported by Bursevi, says two went to Antioch before the ascension.
36:14/q04 To which place and people were the messengers sent? [إِلَيْهِمُ إِلَيْكُم · referent] (turns on: Whether the traditional Antioch account fits the Quranic destruction and the later history of Christianity there.)
  36:14/q04/p1 They went to idol-worshipping Antioch and its people. — reasons: Al-Farra, Qatada, tafsir reported by al-Zajjaj, Ibn Kathir and many others name Antioch. Reports call its king a pagan pharaoh, or Antikhus son of Antikhus son of Antikhus. Bursevi and al-Alusi say the inhabitants worshipped idols; Kuranyolu reports an identification as Romans. Their king threatened them in the Ibn Abbas–Ka'b–Wahb report.
  36:14/q04/p2 The town and its inhabitants remain unnamed and cannot securely be identified as Antioch. — reasons: Ibn Kathir notes leading scholars' objection. Islahi argues that no such destruction appears in Gospel or biblical history, Antioch embraced Jesus, Jesus's mission was to Israel, and he barred disciples from other nations. Kuranyolu cites Acts 11:19–26, 14:8–28 and 15:22, 35–36: Barnabas, Peter and Paul reached Antioch after the ascension, met no such resistance, and the city became a Christian center; neither Quran nor sound hadith names the town, inhabitants or envoys. Kuranyolu cites Ibn Atiyya's omission of conflicting unreliable accounts.
36:14/q05 Why does the verse say 'to them' rather than 'to the town'? [إِلَيْهِمُ · rhetoric]
  36:14/q05/p1 Sending properly takes people as its destination, and naming the people prepares for their rejection. — reasons: Al-Alusi notes that rejection also attaches more clearly to people; the explanatory fa resembles the sequence after Moses struck the stone and water gushed forth in 2:60.
36:14/q06 Who were the first two messengers? [ٱثْنَيْنِ · referent] (turns on: Conflicting transmitted names; some commentators decline identification because no Quranic or sound-hadith name settles the matter.)
  36:14/q06/p1 They were Ṣādiq and Ṣadūq. — reasons: Ibn Abbas, Ka'b and Wahb give these names; spelling varies as Ṣadūq/Sadduq, and the third is Salūm/Shalūm. The reports through Ibn Ishaq also name Antioch and its king. Al-Nasafi includes their meeting with Habib.
  36:14/q06/p2 They were Shimʿūn/Simon and Yūḥannā/John. — reasons: Shuʿayb al-Jubbāʾī, Shu'ba and others identify this pair and call the third Paul; spellings of Simon and John vary.
  36:14/q06/p3 They were Yaḥyā and Yūnus. — reasons: Wahb and al-Baydawi report this pair; Bursevi holds it and says the mission was by God's command. Al-Baydawi also reports the unelaborated alternative that they were others.
  36:14/q06/p4 They were Yūḥannā/John and Būluṣ/Paul. — reasons: Wahb and some later reports give this pair; Elmalili's reported combination makes Simon Peter the third.
  36:14/q06/p5 They were Tūmān and Yūnus. — reasons: Muqatil gives these names in his account.
  36:14/q06/p6 They were Ṭārūs and Mārūs. — reasons: Ibn Abbas gives the pair in al-Tha'labi.
  36:14/q06/p7 They were Tūmān and Mānūs. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi reports this as Muqatil's naming; the Muqatil notes elsewhere give Tūmān and Yūnus or Būluṣ, so the transmitted second name varies.
  36:14/q06/p8 They were Simʿān and Yaḥyā. — reasons: Al-Naqqash supplies these names.
  36:14/q06/p9 They were Tūmān and Būluṣ. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi and al-Alusi attribute this pair to Muqatil.
  36:14/q06/p10 They were Nāzūs and Mārūs. — reasons: Al-Alusi lists this as another reported pair.
  36:14/q06/p11 They were Moses and Aaron. — reasons: Islahi argues that only these two were sent simultaneously to one people and that Moses was primary while Aaron helped.
  36:14/q06/p12 Their names cannot be established from the Quran or sound hadith. — reasons: Kuranyolu says reports of the names and treatment conflict; it cites Ibn Atiyya's decision to omit unreliable material. Al-Baydawi reports an unspecified alternative to Yaḥyā and Yūnus.
36:14/q07 Why were two messengers sent together? [ٱثْنَيْنِ · interpretation]
  36:14/q07/p1 Each could support and strengthen the other's mission. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i says the two mutually supported the call to faith; Islahi's Moses–Aaron reading likewise treats Aaron as Moses's assistant.
36:14/q08 How did the townspeople first respond to the two messengers? [فَكَذَّبُوهُمَا · reports] (turns on: The verse states denial; further reports differ on the physical treatment, while later words explain the people's objection to human envoys.)
  36:14/q08/p1 They denied the two, and reports say they beat or imprisoned them. — reasons: Ibn Abbas says both beating and imprisonment. Wahb says the king jailed them and ordered one hundred lashes; other accounts say beating or imprisonment. The denial persisted despite signs, according to al-Biqa'i. Kuranyolu notes the reports of treatment conflict; Ibn Atiyya omitted unreliable accounts. Al-Tabari's report has the pagan king threaten the three after the third joined.
  36:14/q08/p2 The townspeople denied their divine commission because they were humans like themselves. — reasons: Yahya ibn Sallam and al-Qurtubi record the objection; al-Alusi and Ibn Atiyya use it to support a direct prophetic commission, while al-Alusi also records the apostolic response.
36:14/q09 What does fa-ʿazzaznā mean here? [فَعَزَّزْنَا · meaning] (turns on: Whether the verb denotes adding strength or prevailing over opponents, and whether the light and doubled forms carry one sense or distinct senses.)
  36:14/q09/p1 God strengthened and reinforced the pair or their mission by adding a third. — reasons: Mujahid glosses it as reinforcement; Ibn Zayd explains that making two into three supplies strength. Ibn Jurayj and Mujahid also say 'increased.' Ibn Qutayba compares strengthening a heart and firm camel flesh; al-Zamakhshari, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi and al-Alusi cite rain compacting earth or flesh hardening.
