96:1/q12 Why is the alif of ism written or omitted? [بِٱسْمِ · grammar]
  96:1/q12/p1 Frequent use and spoken elision omit the alif in bism Allāh, while bi-smi rabbika retains it. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj says bism Allāh drops the alif because spoken elision and repeated use made it familiar, but bi-smi rabbika retains it. BURHAN (علماء الظاهر): The outward scholars explain the omission as abbreviation from frequent use: the alif of ism is dropped only when appended to Allah and preceded by ba according to the majority.
96:1/q13 What does invoking the divine name do? [بِٱسْمِ رَبِّكَ · interpretation] (turns on: The name's relation to its bearer and the invoker.)
  96:1/q13/p1 Honoring God's name honors God and His attributes, praising and glorifying Him. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj says a name points to its meaning; Jishumī says honoring a name honors its uniquely signified bearer, and al-Ṭabrisī links invocation with God's beautiful names (17:110; 87:1). BIQAI: The commentators say this indicates that recitation is not complete without invoking God's name; honoring the name honors its bearer and all that is attributed to Him. BIQAI-FULL (قالوا): They say recitation is not complete without naming God; naming Him honors both the name and its bearer and all His attributes.
  96:1/q13/p2 Invoking God's name deters Satan and turns permissible acts into worship. — reasons: Rāzī says naming God before permissible acts repels Satan and can dedicate those acts to worship.
  96:1/q13/p3 Hearing the name may produce sober knowledge or bewildered effacement. — reasons: Qushayrī distinguishes sobriety through knowledge and proof from effacement through immediate recognition of God's dominion.
96:1/q14 Why is God called 'your Lord' here? [رَبِّكَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether it foregrounds nurturing, reassurance, unique allegiance, or active lordship.)
  96:1/q14/p1 The title recalls God's nurture and care for Muhammad and reassures him amid fear. — reasons: Rāzī argues the frightened Prophet is reassured that the one who began caring for him will complete that care; creation from nonexistence is itself nurturing. Abū Ḥayyān stresses protection and welfare. BIQAI: Rather than the all-encompassing Greatest Name, the verse uses the nurturing Lord's attribute, gathering the other attributes and comforting the Prophet as the first revelation came while he loved solitude in worship at Ḥirāʾ. BIQAI-FULL: The Lord's nurturing attribute, rather than the encompassing divine name, comforts the Prophet and recalls the favors recalled in al-Sharḥ; the command came when he loved retreat for worship in Ḥirāʾ.; cites 94:1-8 BIQAI-FULL: Both names point to the command: the Lord who nurtures deserves thanks, while reading signals the command to worship and implies salvation at the final judgment. ALUSI: Calling God the Prophet's Lord evokes nurturing and gradual development toward fitting perfection; the possessive pronoun signals that revelation brings him to the highest human completion. ALUSI: The description recalls God's first favor to the Prophet and signals God's power to teach him to read in the gentlest way. ALUSI-FULL: Who created recalls God's original favor and ability to teach reading; it may mean the one who creates or the one who created everything, and it rules out idols as true lords because Arabs did not ascribe creation to them. ALUSI-FULL: Calling God Lord and adding the possessive for Muhammad recalls divine nurturing and his gradual attainment of human perfection through recurring revelation. IBNASHUR: Calling God “your Lord” rather than using the proper name Allah evokes care for the one nurtured and, with the possessive pronoun, rejects rivals claimed as lords; the verse grounds Islamic monotheism. BINTSHATI (الفخر الرازي): Al-Razi says Rabbika comforts the Prophet: God has nurtured him from an embryo into a knowing believer, so he should neither fear nor expect abandonment; the command also obliges him to act. BINTSHATI (أبو حيان): Abu Hayyan says Rabbuka comforts Muhammad and identifies his exclusive Lord; the Creator attribute follows because Arabs called idols lords, though idols cannot create.
  96:1/q14/p2 The possessive address marks God's special relation to Muhammad and excludes rival lords. — reasons: Rāzī cites 17:1 and 4:80 for reciprocal relation between God and Messenger; Abū Ḥayyān says the possessive address signals intimacy and no other lord. IBNASHUR: Calling God “your Lord” rather than using the proper name Allah evokes care for the one nurtured and, with the possessive pronoun, rejects rivals claimed as lords; the verse grounds Islamic monotheism.
  96:1/q14/p3 Lordship through divine acts makes worship incumbent, unlike an abstract essential name. — reasons: Rāzī considers Allāh a higher essential name, yet divine action as Lord makes worship obligatory. Against—BINTSHATI: She hesitates over al-Razi’s distinction that rabb is an attribute of action whereas Allah is an essential name, with worship here commanded through the former.
  96:1/q14/p4 God first calls Himself the Prophet's Lord; after obedience the servant is called God's own. — reasons: Rāzī says God's benefit reaches the servant first; after obedience or repentance God calls him His servant, citing 39:53.
  96:1/q14/p5 It answers Muhammad's search for the one worthy of worship. — reasons: BINTSHATI: For the solitary seeker at Hira, the first revealed words guided him to the truth he sought and ended his long uncertainty like a new dawn. BINTSHATI: She centers Rabbika on the Prophet’s long search for guidance: the Creator revealed Himself as the Lord worthy of worship over created Arabian idols.
96:1/q15 Who or what is denoted by 'the one who created'? [ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ · referent] (turns on: Whether creation is unrestricted, refers to humanity, or denotes ownership of creation.)
  96:1/q15/p1 God, Creator of all creatures and things. — reasons: Al-Kalbī, reported by al-Wāḥidī and al-Baghawī, says all creatures; al-Kashshāf and al-Nasafī say the unqualified wording includes everything. ALUSI: Khalaqa can be intransitive, meaning the one who creates, or have a general omitted object, meaning the one who created everything; either way its force is universal. ALUSI-FULL: Who created recalls God's original favor and ability to teach reading; it may mean the one who creates or the one who created everything, and it rules out idols as true lords because Arabs did not ascribe creation to them. STUDYQURAN: The first mention of creation may mean that God creates all existence from nothing.
  96:1/q15/p2 God, who created the human being, as the following verse specifies. — reasons: Muqātil refers it to the human; al-Samīn says the second 'created' can specify the first, magnifying human creation.
  96:1/q15/p3 The One who owns creation. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī reports the alternative that the clause denotes ownership of creation, before the text singles out humanity.
  96:1/q15/p4 God manifesting Himself in created form.
96:1/q16 How is the relative clause 'who created' parsed? [ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ · grammar] (turns on: Its possible case and syntactic attachment.)
  96:1/q16/p1 Genitive adjective modifying rabb. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās gives the genitive reading as an adjective of rabb.
  96:1/q16/p2 Nominative with an implied subject. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās allows nominative with an understood subject.
  96:1/q16/p3 Accusative of specification, 'I mean the one who created'. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās allows accusative of specification, 'I mean'.
