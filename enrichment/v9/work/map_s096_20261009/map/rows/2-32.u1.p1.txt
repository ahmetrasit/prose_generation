 2:32/q11/p1 It is relative, ‘that which You taught us,’ most plausibly nominative in apposition. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān prefers nominative relative mā; Samīn compares lā rayba fīhi and supplies an omitted resumptive if ʿilm means maʿlūm. Naḥḥās compares lā ilāha illā Allāh; al-Ālūsī reports the majority's relative analysis, allowing apposition or accusative exception. Al-Burhān notes relative mā after illā.
  2:32/q11/p2 It is a verbal noun, ‘Your teaching of us,’ taking exception case or nominative apposition. — reasons: Qurṭubī gives the verbal-noun sense. Samīn allows accusative as exception and denies government by the noun of lā, which would then have to decline. Bursawī makes the phrase nominative as a substitute for the position of lā ʿilm; Naḥḥās permits accusative after a complete clause.
  2:32/q11/p3 It is nominative as a substitute for lā's predicate. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya analogizes lā ilāha illā Allāh; Samīn objects that exception comes from the judged subject rather than its predicate.
  2:32/q11/p4 It is the accusative object of the following ʿallamtanā. — reasons: Al-Zahrawī's proposal is transmitted by Ibn ʿAṭiyya; Abū Ḥayyān and Samīn object that a relative clause cannot govern its antecedent. Al-Ālūsī also transmits a remote disconnected-exception rescue.
  2:32/q11/p5 It can be conditional in a disconnected exception, with an omitted answer. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān offers this as strained, and Samīn reports the shaykh's rescue; al-Ālūsī repeats it. It gives 'but whatever You taught us, we knew' or future learning, with no prior knowledge affirmed.
2:32/q12 What does God's teaching imply about the source of knowledge? [عَلَّمْتَنَآ · theology] (turns on: Whether establishing evidence counts as God's teaching or teaching requires His production of the resulting knowledge.)
  2:32/q12/p1 God produces acquired knowledge in creatures. — reasons: Rāzī presents the Ashʿarite inference and argues evidence alone is not teaching since the learner's own consideration would produce knowledge. He says God alone can produce knowledge in another and cites 55:2. Ṭabrisī also says all knowledge traces to God, though by necessary creation and proof for inference.
  2:32/q12/p2 God teaches by instruction or by establishing evidence from which a learner reasons. — reasons: Rāzī reports the Muʿtazilite account but rejects evidence alone as sufficient teaching. Ṭabrisī describes necessary knowledge produced by God and inferential knowledge based on proofs He establishes. An astrologer could answer that planetary motions are divine signs, showing the breadth of this account.
2:32/q13 May God be called ‘Teacher’ on the strength of ‘You taught us’? [عَلَّمْتَنَآ · theology]
  2:32/q13/p1 God truly teaches, but ordinary naming custom restricts the unqualified title ‘Teacher.’ — reasons: Rāzī says custom reserves teacher for professional instruction, as in a bequest excluding schoolmasters from 'learners'; apart from custom, the title is fitting because God produces knowledge, as 55:2 states. Bayḍāwī likewise allows attribution of teaching but avoids al-Muʿallim as a divine title.
2:32/q14 Can the angels or humans know the unseen independently? [لَا عِلْمَ لَنَآ إِلَّا مَا عَلَّمْتَنَآ · theology] (turns on: Whether created celestial signs meet the verse's requirement of divine instruction.)
  2:32/q14/p1 No: unseen knowledge belongs to God and reaches creatures only through His teaching or revelation. — reasons: Ṭabarī's Ibn ʿAbbās says only God knows the unseen except what He teaches Adam and others. Rāzī cites 6:59 and 72:26–27 and argues humans are still less able than angels. Ṭabarī rebukes claims by diviners and astrologers without report or proof.
  2:32/q14/p2 Celestial signs could count as God's instruction of unseen conditions. — reasons: Rāzī reports an astrologer's reply to a Muʿtazilite: God created planetary motions as signs of terrestrial events, so interpretation could be called divine teaching.
2:32/q15 What is the scope and growth of angelic knowledge? [عِلْمَ لَنَآ · theology] (turns on: Whether angels can acquire further knowledge or their rank and perfections are fixed.)
  2:32/q15/p1 Angelic knowledge is limited to what God reveals and lacks independent inference. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says angels receive inspired or commanded meanings and cannot derive new ones. Elmalılı says they act on revealed text rather than human-style analogy; their limited knowledge avoids compounded ignorance. Study Quran stresses lack of universal comprehensive scope.
  2:32/q15/p2 Angels can gain knowledge and perfection. — reasons: Bayḍāwī opposes philosophers who infer fixed angelic rank from 37:164; Adam's teaching episode permits increase.
  2:32/q15/p3 Angelic perfections accompany existence; their answer shows they cannot acquire human ranks of knowledge. — reasons: Qāshānī takes their admission as testimony that they fall short of human perfection.
2:32/q16 What does “al-ʿAlīm” mean here? [ٱلْعَلِيمُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the name's scope is stated generally or applied to the angels' hidden thoughts.)
  2:32/q16/p1 God knows comprehensively without being taught, including hidden, past, and future things. — reasons: Ṭabarī says God needs no teacher; Ibn ʿAbbās calls His knowledge perfect. Ibn Isḥāq specifies what angels conceal; Baghawī says His creation. Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ṭabrisī, Rāzī, Ibn al-Jawzī and Qurṭubī emphasize the intensive faʿīl form. Rāzī says only God merits it absolutely. Bayḍāwī says nothing is hidden; Bursawī connects it with 2:30.
  2:32/q16/p2 It specifically points to God's knowledge of the angels' concealed claim. — reasons: Ibn Isḥāq glosses the name by what they hide; Bayḍāwī says God knows the hidden conditions of heaven and earth, including theirs.
2:32/q17 What does “al-Ḥakīm” mean, and what sort of divine attribute is it? [ٱلْحَكِيمُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether ḥikma denotes judging, knowledge, precise action, restraint, or a perfection combining knowledge and action.)
  2:32/q17/p1 The wise judge or ruler, perfect in judgment and just decree. — reasons: Ṭabarī gives Ibn ʿAbbās's perfected judgment and another judge gloss. Wāḥidī cites Ibn al-Muẓaffar on ḥukm as knowledge or just judgment, 19:12, and al-Nābigha's poetic decision. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Qurṭubī note intensive faʿīl. This can be an attribute of essence when wisdom means knowing.
  2:32/q17/p2 The one who perfects, orders, and makes acts precise, an attribute of action. — reasons: Thaʿlabī, Wāḥidī and Abū Ḥayyān explain faʿīl as causative mufʿil, citing samīʿ and alīm parallels. Mawardī, Jishumī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Qurṭubī and others call this action; Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 2:117 and the poetic najība for adjective-pattern variation, resisting a forced possessive gloss. Rāzī prefers this to avoid repeating al-ʿAlīm.
  2:32/q17/p3 The one who knows or possesses wisdom, an essential attribute. — reasons: Thaʿlabī, Jishumī, Ṭabrisī, Rāzī and al-Ālūsī distinguish knowledge or possessed wisdom from action. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites al-Ghazālī on God's eternal, exact knowledge of the noblest object and Lutf Allāh on knowledge joined to perfect governance.
  2:32/q17/p4 The one who restrains or prevents corruption. — reasons: Mawardī compares a horse's bridle; Wāḥidī cites al-Aṣmaʿī, al-Azharī, Jarīr, Labīd and al-Nakhaʿī on restraint of horses, fools, or orphans. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Qurṭubī add Jarīr and Zuhayr; Jishumī, Ṭabrisī and Samīn say wisdom prevents ignorance and flawed action.
  2:32/q17/p5 Wisdom adds a perfection beyond knowledge: right, objection-free action and governance. — reasons: Bayḍāwī says a mere duplicate of 'All-Knowing' would be redundant; Ibn ʿĀshūr says wisdom exceeds knowledge as 'eloquent poet' adds to poet. Qushayrī says God's acts are right, without folly. Rāzī prefers this sense; Ibn Kathīr extends it to creation, command, giving and withholding instruction.
2:32/q18 How is “anta” parsed and what does it convey? [أَنتَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether anta is a separator, accusative emphasis, or an independent nominative subject.)
  2:32/q18/p1 It is a separating pronoun, emphasizing and restricting the divine predicates. — reasons: Naḥḥās reports the Kūfan ʿimād; Ṭabrisī, Nasafī, Bayḍāwī, al-Ālūsī and Ibn ʿĀshūr allow faṣl. Samīn notes dispute over its case: al-Farrāʾ follows the preceding word, al-Kisāʾī the following. Ibn ʿĀshūr says it may correct presumed angelic knowledge or confine complete knowledge and wisdom to God.
  2:32/q18/p2 It emphasizes the second-person pronoun governed by inna. — reasons: Naḥḥās treats it as accusative emphasis; Bayḍāwī notes dependent emphasis is allowed where a standalone object is not; Samīn and al-Ālūsī report this parse.
  2:32/q18/p3 It is a new subject, with al-ʿAlīm (and al-Ḥakīm) as predicate; the clause predicates inna. — reasons: Naḥḥās, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ṭabrisī, Nasafī, Bayḍāwī, Samīn and al-Ālūsī report this full-clause analysis.
2:32/q19 How does “al-Ḥakīm” relate syntactically to “al-ʿAlīm”? [ٱلْعَلِيمُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the second divine name is attributive or predicative.)
  2:32/q19/p1 It is an adjective modifying al-ʿAlīm. — reasons: Naḥḥās states the modifying analysis; Samīn lists it as an option and says both forms carry intensive force.
  2:32/q19/p2 It is a second predicate. — reasons: Samīn and Nasafī allow the two names as predicates after anta or after inna if anta is a separator.
2:32/q20 Why does “al-ʿAlīm” precede “al-Ḥakīm”? [ٱلْعَلِيمُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the order follows conceptual dependence, the immediate verbal context, or both.)
  2:32/q20/p1 Knowledge is prior because wisdom depends on it. — reasons: Samīn, al-Burhān and al-Ālūsī say sound judgment follows knowledge; al-Burhān adds that knowledge may lead as an essential attribute.
  2:32/q20/p2 It echoes the preceding talk of knowledge and God's command to inform Him. — reasons: Al-Burhān points to 'we have no knowledge'; Samīn and al-Ālūsī cite the same
