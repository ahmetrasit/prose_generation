1:3/q31 What is the Fātiḥa's larger structure and purpose? [* · links] (turns on: How its praise, mercy, judgment, worship and guidance organize the sūra and its relation to the Qur'an.)
  1:3/q31/p1 It proceeds from divine praise and attributes to worship, then the servant's need for guidance. — reasons: Rāzī maps refuge, basmala, praise, lordship, mercy, judgment, worship and avoidance of error; Quṭb and Study Quran group God's nature, relation, and human states. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it a literary preface preparing hearers to cast off obstinacy; Biqāʿī and al-Ḥarālī call it the Qur'an's condensed universals and watchfulness.
  1:3/q31/p2 Its diverse verses make purposeful, inimitable guidance rather than needless length. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī answers critics who claim judgment and worship already contain its meaning: the Qur'an gathers earlier scriptures' admonition and praise, adds commands, warnings, stories, arguments and parables, and its unmatched short-sūra composition certifies prophethood. The Fātiḥa moves listeners to gratitude, reliance on God and fear of sin.
  1:3/q31/p3 Its arrangement is inimitable and obligatory in the recited order. — reasons: Biqāʿī cites al-Iṣfahānī on inimitable order and says rearranging or inserting foreign matter in prayer invalidates the recitation.
  1:3/q31/p4 Al-Baqara answers its final guidance request. — reasons: Biqāʿī says the next sūra presents the Book as guidance, models pious and astray groups, and stresses resurrection and inward faith; its cow story, hardened hearts and titles Radiant and Hump fit those themes.
1:3/q32 How does the sūra encompass scripture and its themes? [* · interpretation] (turns on: How its brief expressions imply the Qur'an's whole range and earlier revelation.)
  1:3/q32/p1 It includes the Qur'an's central theology, duties, wisdom, and outcomes. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī divides contents into praise, worshipful command, promise and warning; al-Bayḍāwī gives theoretical wisdom, practical rulings and destinies. Ibn ʿĀshūr says explicit and implied aims prompt fuller study; al-Ḥarālī maps seven universals or 'letters' to Fātiḥa phrases and contrasts its compactness with the dispersed Qur'an.
  1:3/q32/p2 Its compression can be traced into the basmala and even its opening bāʾ and point. — reasons: Study Quran transmits a saying from ʿAlī and reads the point as the Pen's first ink on the Preserved Tablet (68:1; 96:4; 85:22). Al-Ḥarālī calls basmala to Fātiḥa as Fātiḥa to Qur'an.
  1:3/q32/p3 The report calling it two-thirds counts modes of indication. — reasons: Al-Zarkashī and Nāṣir al-Dīn b. al-Mīlaq say inclusion and implication give two of three kinds, without direct correspondence.
  1:3/q32/p4 The report calling it two-thirds counts kinds of rights. — reasons: Nāṣir al-Dīn b. al-Mīlaq counts God's right, servants' right on God and interpersonal rights; the sūra explicitly treats the first two.
1:3/q33 How do divine power names relate to these mercy names? [* · theology]
  1:3/q33/p1 Qādir, Qadīr, Muqtadir and related names express divine power; kingship specifies it. — reasons: Rāzī cites 6:65, 75:3, 75:40, 67:1, 18:45, 54:55, 77:23, 3:26, 25:26, 57:2, 22:74 and 51:58. He distinguishes sovereign malik from owner mālik as the higher rank, though the note's cross-reference to 1:3 is loose.
1:3/q34 How does the mercy verse correspond to the seven acts of prayer? [* · inward] (turns on: The alignment of seven recited verses with bodily movement and spiritual ascent.)
  1:3/q34/p1 It corresponds especially to rising from bowing, when mercy raises the humbled servant. — reasons: Rāzī maps seven verses to standing, bowing, rising, two prostrations, rising between them and sitting. He cites the Prophet on God's merciful look during praise on rising, then maps the acts to seven stages of human formation (23:12-14; 53:42).
  1:3/q34/p2 The whole prayer joins bodily movement with ascent of the soul. — reasons: Rāzī describes Muhammad's bodily and spiritual ascents, ranks of spirits and angels, seventy veils of light, bodily purification, choice of companions, raised hands, takbīr, opening glorification, blessed name, exalted majesty and final acknowledgment of God's incomparability. These prepare the reciter to rise toward the source of mercy.
1:3/q35 What moral transformation does reciting the seven verses seek? [* · inward] (turns on: How individual phrases remove vices or express the servant's spiritual path.)
  1:3/q35/p1 Each verse counters a vice and the guidance petition seeks purified perception. — reasons: Rāzī maps praise to desire, lordship to greed, judgment to anger, worship and aid to pride and vanity, guidance to caprice, favored path to unbelief and doubt, and final phrase to innovation. He also pictures the blessed as mirrors of sanctity.
  1:3/q35/p2 Four divine attributes draw the self's four faculties toward God. — reasons: Bursevī relays the Najmiyya interpretation without detailing the faculties in this note.
  1:3/q35/p3 Five divine names map to human angelic, devilish, predatory, animal and bodily dimensions. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī cites 13:28, 23:97, 25:26, 5:4 and 55:31 as illustrations.
1:3/q36 Must the Fātiḥa be recited in prayer? [* · law] (turns on: Whether the sūra's special place in prayer makes its recitation a pillar, and possible substitutes.)
  1:3/q36/p1 Its recitation is required and prayer without it is deficient or invalid. — reasons: Rāzī invokes the Prophet's and caliphs' practice, worldwide practice (4:115), 'no prayer without the Opening,' obligatory 73:20, safer course, 24:63, and its irreplaceable lordship-servitude synopsis (20:14; 15:87). He infers a pillar from the divine report naming the Fātiḥa prayer. Quṭb cites both Ṣaḥīḥs; Ibn ʿĀshūr notes a sound deficient-prayer report and says each rakʿa recalls Qur'anic aims.
  1:3/q36/p2 An alternative praise formula can be used in later cycles. — reasons: Study Quran reports Shiite law allowing either the Fātiḥa or another praise formula in the third and fourth rakʿas.
1:3/q37 What merits and uses are reported for the Fātiḥa? [* · sciences] (turns on: Its superlative, healing, repeated and devotional status.)
  1:3/q37/p1 It is the greatest incomparable sūra and seven oft-repeated. — reasons: Abū Hurayra's report says nothing like it was given in Torah, Gospel, Psalms or Qur'an (15:87). Study Quran repeats its unrivalled status; al-Suyūṭī reconciles al-Baqara's 'greatest' report by restricting it to other sūras. Biqāʿī connects the Prophet's mission to perfect noble character with ʿĀʾisha's report that his character was the Qur'an.
  1:3/q37/p2 Seven oft-repeated refers to seven verses, prayer repetition, intercession, or two revelations. — reasons: Bursevī adds reported reward equivalent to the Qur'an or closure of seven Hell-gates; Study Quran links the title to 15:87.
  1:3/q37/p3 It heals bodily or spiritual affliction and is recited at life events. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr cites Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī's Bukhārī healing recitation over a sting; Study Quran explains the Cure title and mentions funerals and other events.
  1:3/q37/p4 Its revelation was said to frighten Satan. — reasons: Study Quran records the attribution to Mujāhid and alternative attribution to the Prophet.
  1:3/q37/p5 Its legal contents are said to be extraordinarily deep. — reasons: Ibn al-ʿArabī cites a teacher's thousand commands, prohibitions, rulings and reports, and Ibn ʿUmar's eight years teaching it.
1:3/q38 How does the Qur'an's order relate to its descent? [* · sciences]
  1:3/q38/p1 Recitation order can differ from revelation order. — reasons: Biqāʿī calls 2:281 the last revealed while its place differs from descent order, citing 75:17; al-Ḥarālī cites 33:50 and 33:52. He distinguishes gradual transition between rulings from downward descent and says the Qur'an descended to the lowest heaven before reaching the Prophet over his mission.
1:3/q39 Does this sequence establish a rational duty of gratitude before revelation? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether God's praiseworthiness in creation and mercy itself proves a pre-revelation obligation.)
  1:3/q39/p1 It does establish gratitude by reason. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports the argument that creation, mercy and future judgment establish God's praiseworthiness, but objects that the cited proof is a revealed verse.
  1:3/q39/p2 It cannot prove a pre-revelation rational duty by citing revelation. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says the premise comes from scripture, so the argument only establishes a scriptural obligation.
1:3/q40 What qualities justify the praise around this verse? [* · interpretation]
  1:3/q40/p1 God creates, owns, blesses and deserves worship; mercy is both worldly and religious. — reasons: Jishumī says al-Raḥmān gives blessings of worldly life and religion, al-Raḥīm has expansive mercy, and all praise belongs to the creator-owner.
