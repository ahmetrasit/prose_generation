## THE CURRENT MAP
87:6/q01 What does “We shall make you recite” mean? [سَنُقْرِئُكَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether iqraʾa here is teaching recitation generally or listening for correction.)
  87:6/q01/p1 God will teach Muhammad the Qur’an and make him a reciter. — reasons: Baghawī and Thaʿlabī specify Gabriel’s recitation; Mālik, transmitted by Ibn Wahb in Qurṭubī, glosses it as teaching and retention. Bursevī cites Tāj al-maṣādir for keeping the Qur’an in the ear. Nasafī’s shorter note awkwardly says 'until he forgets,' while its full version says 'until he does not forget.'
  87:6/q01/p2 Iqraʾa can mean receiving a reading by listening so errors can be corrected. — reasons: Jishumī and Ṭabrisī also derive qirāʾa from gathering letters. Al-Majmaʿ, cited by Ṭabāṭabāʾī, gives the correction sense.
  87:6/q01/p3 Reading is joining letters and words in measured recitation. — reasons: Al-Mufradāt, cited by Ṭabāṭabāʾī, excludes mere gathering or uttering a single letter.
87:6/q02 How will God make the Prophet recite? [سَنُقْرِئُكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the divine making occurs through Gabriel’s instruction, inspiration, special memory, or an inward reading.)
  87:6/q02/p1 Gabriel conveys and recites the revelation, and God enables Muhammad to retain and deliver it. — reasons: Zajjāj says the unlettered Prophet teaches his companions without needing repetition. Wāḥidī reports Abū Isḥāq and Qatāda likewise. Bayḍāwī allows Gabriel’s recital or inspired reading. Bursevī includes revelation now and later. Ālūsī says divine attribution is mediated by Gabriel. Elmalılı names Gabriel the Trustworthy and Holy Spirit and cites 42:52; he specifies reciting from memory, not reading a text.
  87:6/q02/p2 God inspires the Prophet to become a reader. — reasons: Rāzī relays Wāḥidī’s explanation. Bayḍāwī presents it alongside Gabriel’s recitation; Elmalılı says revelation makes him possess the Qur’an and recitation. Ālūsī reports Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq’s possibility that he could read writing without instruction though he did not write.
  87:6/q02/p3 Gabriel repeats the revelation until the Prophet memorizes it. — reasons: Rāzī distinguishes repeated instruction from memorization after one hearing; Kashshāf connects repetition with his earlier haste and possible lapse.
  87:6/q02/p4 God expands or strengthens the Prophet’s heart so one hearing yields lasting memorization. — reasons: Rāzī contrasts this with repeated instruction.
  87:6/q02/p5 God gathers the Qur’an or its knowledge in his heart and eases its recitation and practice. — reasons: Muqātil and Ibn al-Jawzī say the Qur’an is gathered in his heart; Bursevī cites Kashf al-Asrār and 75:17 for heart and tongue. Rāzī connects comprehensive knowledge with easy action and 87:8. Iṣlāḥī cites 75:16–19 for collection, recital, following and explanation.
  87:6/q02/p6 God enables exact recitation without addition, omission, or distortion. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects the correction sense: no known case shows a misreading or forgotten revelation corrected by God.
  87:6/q02/p7 He reads the universal Qur’an of realities in his book of preparedness and Qur’anic intellect. — reasons: Qāshānī says he recalls this without forgetting.
87:6/q03 What does the future prefix in “We shall make you recite” convey? [سَنُقْرِئُكَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether sīn chiefly strengthens the pledge or points to its ongoing fulfillment.)
  87:6/q03/p1 It intensifies or confirms the divine promise. — reasons: Bursevī and Ālūsī report the emphatic sense; Ibn ʿĀshūr says a future marker confirms the coming act.
  87:6/q03/p2 It promises continuing and renewed recitation, including revelation yet to come. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr reasons that the act had begun and the Qur’an would keep growing; Biqāʿī speaks of repeated instruction. Corpus Coranicum calls it a promise of further revelation.
87:6/q04 Why is God the first-person speaker who “makes recite”? [سَنُقْرِئُكَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the question concerns the change of voice or the mediated attribution of recitation.)
  87:6/q04/p1 The shift to the majestic first-person plural suits direct divine good news and heightens its authority. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum calls the shift from third person singular iltifāt; Abdel Haleem says it dramatizes divine authority without implying redaction. Ibn ʿĀshūr says direct address fits the giver of glad tidings.
  87:6/q04/p2 God is credited with recitation because He brings about and commands the words recited through Gabriel. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr calls this rational attribution; Ālūsī calls it metaphorical attribution through Gabriel.
87:6/q05 How is the hamza of “We shall make you recite” treated in pausal readings? [سَنُقْرِئُكَ · readings] (turns on: The extent of al-Akhfash’s substitution rule and the weight of written form versus transmitted pause practice.)
  87:6/q05/p1 The hamza may be replaced with yāʾ where the written form supports it, as in sanaqriʾuka. — reasons: Al-Dānī and the majority attribute broad substitution after kasra or ḍamma to al-Akhfash; Abū al-ʿIzz permits it for Ḥamza at pause. Al-Dānī favors matching the rasm. Ibn al-Jazarī says the rasm uses yāʾ here rather than customary wāw.
  87:6/q05/p2 Substitution is restricted to final radical hamza; medial cases use Sibawayh’s between-between easing. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī prefers what al-Akhfash’s own book permits and notes authorities who rejected his supposed broad method; al-Dānī combines substitution where rasm supports it with Sibawayh’s easing elsewhere.
  87:6/q05/p3 Substitution or easing varies with word boundary, region, vowel, and rasm. — reasons: Abū al-ʿAlāʾ permits substitution for a ḍamma hamza after kasra across or within words; Abū al-ʿIzz reports regional variation. Some non-Ḥijāzī Arabs use wāw, yāʾ or alif for final hamza by case. Ibn al-Jazarī distinguishes when rawm or ishām remains possible according to whose vowel governs replacement.
  87:6/q05/p4 The al-Kisāʾī method attributed through Khalaf has no practical basis. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī agrees with al-Dānī and rejects al-Shāṭibī’s allusion for lack of transmission or analogy.
87:6/q06 What does “forget” mean here in Arabic usage? [تَنسَىٰٓ · meaning] (turns on: The lexical sense and vocalization of nisyān.)
  87:6/q06/p1 It is the opposite of remembering: known information fails to come to mind, briefly or for long. — reasons: Damghānī cites 18:63 and 18:73 and glosses this verse as lasting retention. Ṭabrisī calls it loss of meaning from the mind, like absent-mindedness, rather than simply loss of acquired knowledge. Ibn ʿĀshūr allows brief or long failure to recall.
  87:6/q06/p2 Nisyān in this sense has kasra on nūn and sukūn on sīn, unlike the similarly named anatomical term. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī calls the other, fully vowelled form the twin of the sciatic nerve.
87:6/q07 What is the nature of forgetting? [تَنسَىٰٓ · theology] (turns on: Whether forgetting is a created act or a privation of knowledge.)
  87:6/q07/p1 Forgetting is an act created by God. — reasons: Jishumī reports Abū ʿAlī; Ṭabrisī attributes the point to Abū ʿAlī al-Jubbāʾī.
  87:6/q07/p2 Forgetting is the loss of ordinarily necessary knowledge, not an entity or act. — reasons: Jishumī reports Abū Hāshim in disagreement with Abū ʿAlī.
87:6/q08 Is “you will not forget” a statement or a prohibition? [فَلَا تَنسَىٰٓ · grammar] (turns on: Whether lā negates a predicted event or prohibits an avoidable cause of forgetting; the retained final alif also matters.)
  87:6/q08/p1 It is an asserted negation that functions as God’s promise of preserved memory. — reasons: Naḥḥās prefers exegetical support and says most linguists reject commanding involuntary forgetting. Damghānī cites 20:108 for assertive lā; Burhān describes its general negative use. Rāzī likens this to promising clothing so the addressee will not be naked; he says it brings stronger reassurance because forgetting is under God’s control. Ibn ʿĀshūr says report form serves a guarantee.
  87:6/q08/p2 It is a prohibition: do not neglect recitation, review, or obedience lest you forget. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān say review is within the Prophet’s power though forgetting itself is not. Samīn answers Makkī that prohibitions commonly target avoidable causes. Rāzī and Ālūsī report an alif retained for rhyme. Ālūsī’s preferred rescue is a report intended as prohibition of voluntary forgetting; Ibn Kathīr’s reported command exempts abrogated material.
87:6/q09 Why is the final long vowel written or retained in “tansā”? [تَنسَىٰٓ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the form is explained by rhyme, orthography and pause, or a jussive retaining a weak letter.)
  87:6/q09/p1 It maintains the rhyme or regular verse endings. — reasons: This is especially invoked for a prohibitive jussive. Bayḍāwī and Ālūsī compare the form al-sabīlā at 33:67; the shorter Bayḍāwī note cites 17:67. Abū Ḥayyān reports it but later rejects it as weak.
  87:6/q09/p2 The written form reflects the word’s original form or a pausal restoration, rather than proving prohibition. — reasons: Al-Marzūqī says a deleted alif is restored at pause. Qurṭubī appeals to the yāʾ in every codex and reading when preferring an actual exception.
  87:6/q09/p3 A jussive can retain a weak letter. — reasons: Ālūsī reports this second prohibitive account but finds it unsatisfactory.
87:6/q10 What does “you will not forget” encompass? [فَلَا تَنسَىٰٓ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the protected object is Qur’anic recitation, its practice, the law and sūras, or every remembered thing.)
  87:6/q10/p1 The Prophet retains the Qur’an God recites to him, so revelation can be conveyed faithfully. — reasons: Zajjāj cites 15:9 for divine preservation. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says no verse is irretrievably lost; Ibn ʿĀshūr stresses full delivery as revealed even as the Qur’an grows. Qutb says this removes the burden of guarding every letter and comforts the community. Bayḍāwī, Bursevī
