## THE CURRENT MAP
21:1/q01 What does iqtaraba mean and add to qaruba? [ٱقْتَرَبَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the augmented form conveys emphasis beyond the shared root meaning.)
  21:1/q01/p1 It means to draw near; the augmented form can emphasize nearness. — reasons: Al-Mubarrad, al-Baḥr, al-Biqāʿī, and Ṭabāṭabāʾī find emphasis in its expanded form; al-Biqāʿī connects it to this being the final community. Jishumī says qurb is opposite distance, literal spatially but also temporal or circumstantial.
  21:1/q01/p2 It has the same basic sense as qaruba, without requiring additional force. — reasons: Jishumī treats qurb and iqtirāb as equivalent; Abū Ḥayyān and others also give the basic equivalence while reporting possible emphasis.
21:1/q02 What is drawing near in this verse? [ٱقْتَرَبَ حِسَابُهُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether ḥisāb directly names final accounting, its time, a worldly requital, or death.)
  21:1/q02/p1 The resurrection and God's actual accounting of deeds draw near. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī explicitly rejects supplying 'time' as an omitted noun: the account itself approaches. The accounting discloses deeds and leads to recompense; compare 38:16 and 40:28. Al-Rāzī says spatial approach is impossible.
  21:1/q02/p2 The time of the Hour or of people's reckoning draws near. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj supplies 'time'; al-Rāzī says the proximity must be temporal; 54:1 and 21:97 provide parallels. Al-Ālūsī says saying 'account' evokes the Hour's fearful effects; Ṭabāṭabāʾī objects that the context would then say Day or time.
  21:1/q02/p3 A near worldly punishment, especially Mecca's defeat at Badr, is meant. — reasons: Al-Ḍaḥḥāk says Meccans mocked delayed punishment and were killed at Badr. Al-Biqāʿī includes Badr and the Conquest; al-Būrsawī prefers Badr because death immediately connects to resurrection. Iṣlāḥī connects near accountability to a messenger's conclusive warning and a nation's destruction.
  21:1/q02/p4 It concerns each person's near death or minor resurrection. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī says understanding resurrection would allow witnessing one's account now. Al-Qurṭubī says each person's death is their Hour; Study Quran reports death as sufficient reason to call reckoning near.
  21:1/q02/p5 It concerns what people have been promised, including worldly affairs. — reasons: Ibn Jurayj calls it what was promised; the report of Abū Hurayra in al-Durr connects the approaching account with worldly affairs.
21:1/q03 Why is the account described as near despite the delay? [ٱقْتَرَبَ حِسَابُهُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: What measure of nearness the verse uses: elapsed world time, certainty, divine measure, a progressing deadline, prophetic signs, or personal death.)
  21:1/q03/p1 More of the world's or the period of obligation has passed than remains. — reasons: Al-Māwardī, People of Meanings, and later commentators give this comparison. Al-Zamakhsharī cites the final Prophet, his 'breath of the Hour' saying, and a sermon comparing the remainder to dregs. Al-Rāzī cites his sealing of prophecy, two fingers; Study Quran notes that iqtaraba may imply nearer destination than origin. Al-Ālūsī records an objection that elapsed duration is unnecessary and a reply tying approach to the Prophet's mission.
  21:1/q03/p2 Every event still to come is near; what has passed is distant. — reasons: Al-Māwardī and People of Meanings give this maxim. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī and al-Bayḍāwī contrast awaited events with the irretrievable past; a poet supplies the same contrast. Al-Ālūsī reports the objection that certainty alone may not explain the eventive past tense.
  21:1/q03/p3 It is near by God's measure, knowledge, or decree. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī cite 22:47, and al-Bayḍāwī cites 70:6–7. Al-Ālūsī explains knowledge or decree without spatial proximity, cites 18:12, and reports an objection that divine proximity does not renew; Study Quran says God knows all moments together.
  21:1/q03/p4 Each passing hour brings the reckoning closer after it was distant. — reasons: Al-Būrsawī and Abū al-Suʿūd take approach as a renewed relation. Al-Ālūsī says actual temporal approach suits the threat better than a proximity existing unchanged since creation.
  21:1/q03/p5 The Prophet's mission and signs show how near the Hour is. — reasons: The Prophet compares his coming with the Hour to adjacent index and middle fingers; another report has the Trumpet already at the angel's mouth; the afternoon-to-sunset analogy makes this community late in history. Al-Ālūsī's learned critic traces approach to the mission.
  21:1/q03/p6 Certain death makes accountability near for each person. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī says a person's death is their own Hour; Study Quran cites the same consideration independently of the world's end.
21:1/q04 Why use a past-tense form for the approaching account? [ٱقْتَرَبَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the tense marks certainty or a temporally occurring approach.)
  21:1/q04/p1 It expresses certainty that the Day of Judgment will come. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu gives the certainty of its occurrence as the reason for the past tense.
  21:1/q04/p2 It presents approach as already begun, with real temporal progress. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports that an unchanging nearness from creation would call for an ongoing form; a critic answers that most of the world's span had elapsed by the Prophet's mission. He also reports that mere certainty of occurrence may fail to explain the verb's eventive force.
21:1/q05 How does the lām in lil-nās work? [لِلنَّاسِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the preposition attaches to 'approached', stands for min or ilā, or reinforces the possessive construction.)
  21:1/q05/p1 It is governed by iqtaraba as its complement. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Bayḍāwī give this as the straightforward analysis; al-Nasafī calls it an added connective. Abū Ḥayyān objects to possessive emphasis as unattested, ungoverned, and out of order; al-Ālūsī prefers verbal attachment.
  21:1/q05/p2 It stands for min, expressing approach from or relative to the people. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Māwardī's successors and al-ʿUyūn give min. Al-Ālūsī says min can mark an endpoint, cites al-Shumnī and Ibn Mālik's 'approached from him' for 'approached him', and rejects deriving it from a starting point; al-Raḍī explains preposition use by analogy with an opposite verb.
  21:1/q05/p3 It means toward the people, like ilā. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī says min would measure from the object and ilā toward it; the verse depicts accounting coming at the people. Al-Ālūsī accepts ilā as the more characteristic endpoint marker but rejects restricting the phrase to it.
  21:1/q05/p4 It reinforces the possessive relation in 'their account'. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī compares Arabic expressions such as 'their departure drew near for the people'; al-Bayḍāwī says it develops 'people's account' to 'their account'. Al-Samīn defends analogy with lā abā laka; Abū Ḥayyān and the Shaykh object that examples differ, the governor is missing, and the lām is wrongly ordered. Al-Ālūsī finds it strained.
  21:1/q05/p5 It cannot be parsed by replacing lil-nās with a later group or by displacing a hidden element. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās rejects placing an implicit element before the explicit element that it is meant to follow. Al-Zarkashī rejects strained substitution of 'those who wronged' for 'the people' and compares another legally driven parsing.
21:1/q06 Why put 'for the people' before 'their account'? [لِلنَّاسِ حِسَابُهُمْ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether fronting primarily hastens alarm, evokes pursuit, or strengthens the possessive relation.)
  21:1/q06/p1 It delivers the threat immediately and makes the delayed subject frightening. — reasons: Al-ʿUyūn says fronting hastens dread; al-Biqāʿī and others say delaying 'their account' leaves listeners imagining what terrible thing approaches. Al-Ālūsī says this can be the intended effect even if heedless hearers do not fear.
  21:1/q06/p2 It pictures the people as pursued by an approaching danger. — reasons: A rhetorician cited by al-Ālūsī says the verb and fronted phrase depict an advancing thing chasing a fleeing target, conveying danger. Abū al-Suʿūd adds that attributing approach to the account magnifies it as a pursuer that inevitably catches people; al-Ālūsī approves.
  21:1/q06/p3 It reinforces the account's attribution and restriction to those people. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī says the fronted expression, lām and definite article together strengthen the possessive sense and restriction; al-Ālūsī reports objections to the underlying parsing.
21:1/q07 Who are 'the people'? [ٱلنَّاسِ · referent] (turns on: Whether the general noun keeps its full range or is restricted by the following descriptions and historical threat.)
  21:1/q07/p1 They are the Meccan polytheists or deniers of resurrection. — reasons: Al-Kalbī, Ibn ʿAbbās and others cite the ensuing heedlessness, playful hearing and accusation of magic in 21:2–3. Al-Ālūsī says extending those traits to monotheist sinners is strained. Iṣlāḥī says using the general term for them conveys aversion and regret.
  21:1/q07/p2 They are all morally responsible people, though Quraysh was immediately addressed. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Qurṭubī preserve the general warning. Al-Rāzī limits it to those subject to reckoning; al-Biqāʿī includes addressees and others. Al-Ālūsī reports a generic noun allowing majority traits or later pronouns narrowing to Meccan polytheists.
  21:1/q07/p3 They are all deniers of resurrection, beyond Meccan polytheists. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān explicitly gives all resurrection deniers as an alternative to the Meccan polytheists.
  21:1/q07/p4 They are Quraysh or all Arabs facing worldly reprisal. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī ties this scope to interpreting the account as Badr or the Conquest.
