نَ أُوتُوا۟ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the referent is all scriptural communities, only Jews, believing recipients, or inward intellectual types.)
  74:31/q10/p1 Jews and Christians, including believing scripturalists. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī calls this most likely; al-Qurṭubī cites believers such as ʿAbd Allāh ibn Salām; Ibn ʿAbbās specifically understands believing recipients, while al-Qurṭubī also reports all scripturalists. Islahi describes sincere recipients who stood beside believers.
  74:31/q10/p2 Jews specifically, including those whose certainty fails to become faith. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr argues Jewish scholars held the knowledge and were in contact with Mecca; 2:146 describes concealed truth. He says knowledge without action becomes evidence against them.
  74:31/q10/p3 People endowed with discriminating intellect, or imitators who received scripture. — reasons: Qāshānī's inward reading contrasts intellectual certainty, direct unveiling, and unverified imitators.
74:31/q11 How does li-yastaqīna relate to 'we made'? [لِيَسْتَيْقِنَ · grammar] (turns on: The syntactic governor of the purpose clause and whether its lām denotes intention or consequence.)
  74:31/q11/p1 Its lām expresses purpose and attaches to jaʿalnā, coordinating the following faith and doubt clauses. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās calls the lām originally prepositional, with repetition optional; Abū Ḥayyān and Ibn ʿĀshūr say it does not depend on fitna; al-Intiṣāf allows its reach across the exception; al-Rāzī also supplies an omitted 'and'. Bursevī reads the sin as seeking certainty; Ibn ʿĀshūr says istīqān intensifies certainty.
  74:31/q11/p2 It depends on an omitted 'we did this' rather than directly on jaʿalnā. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn report this alternative.
  74:31/q11/p3 The lām expresses consequence rather than an intended purpose. — reasons: Islahi takes the trial's beneficial results as consequences for sincere readers.
74:31/q12 How do believers increase in faith? [وَيَزْدَادَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوٓا۟ إِيمَٰنًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether faith grows by added assent and corroboration, its fruits or quality, or inward unveiling.)
  74:31/q12/p1 They affirm the newly revealed report of nineteen and gain confidence when scripture readers corroborate it. — reasons: Sources variously describe additional confirmation, assent to new revelation, witness to Muhammad's truthfulness, stronger recognition of God's wisdom and power, and more secure conviction after weighing objections. Al-Rāzī distinguishes increase in faith's fruits from essence; Ibn ʿĀshūr holds assent's quality fixed and its objects cumulative.
  74:31/q12/p2 Faith grows through unveiling and direct witnessing beyond intellectual certainty. — reasons: Qāshānī contrasts discriminating intellect, verified realization, and imitation.
74:31/q13 What does the promise of no doubt add? [وَلَا يَرْتَابَ ٱلَّذِينَ أُوتُوا۟ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ · rhetoric]
  74:31/q13/p1 It reinforces certainty and growing faith, ensuring stable conviction about the report and prophethood. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj and al-Ṭabarī focus on the number; Jishumī adds God's power and prophethood. Al-Rāzī notes forgotten premises can reopen doubt; Abū Ḥayyān, al-Bayḍāwī, and al-Ālūsī describe settled minds despite apparent counterevidence. Ibn ʿĀshūr contrasts knowledge without action among some scripture readers; al-Biqāʿī contrasts deliberate doubt.
  74:31/q13/p2 The repetition and naming of both groups prevent an ambiguous pronoun and emphasize the two distinct responses. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says a pronoun might refer only to believers and notes the active participle's connotation of enduring faith; al-Biqāʿī says God's passive giving of scripture needs no named giver.
74:31/q14 Who have disease in their hearts? [ٱلَّذِينَ فِى قُلُوبِهِم مَّرَضٌۭ · referent] (turns on: Whether the Meccan verse predicts later hypocrisy, describes contemporary doubt or opposition, or points to Jewish envy.)
  74:31/q14/p1 Hypocrites, especially those later appearing in Medina, with doubt mingled in their hearts. — reasons: Many explain the Meccan verse as foretelling Medinan hypocrisy; al-Wāḥidī and al-Rāzī defend predictive revelation as miraculous. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī argues hypocritical motives could already exist in Mecca, invoking 29:10–11 and 22:11. Al-Rāzī also reports a broad unbeliever identification.
  74:31/q14/p2 Doubters, opponents, or wavering Meccans, not later hypocrites. — reasons: Al-Ḥusayn ibn al-Faḍl invokes the Meccan setting and absence of Meccan hypocrisy; Ibn ʿĀshūr names al-Akhnas and al-Walīd and defines disease as ill intent. Muqātil instead names doubting Jews of Medina and treats the verse as Medinan. Al-Rāzī distinguishes most doubting Meccans from those sure the Prophet was false; Asad calls them half-hearted.
  74:31/q14/p3 Jealous Jews whose envy of Ishmaelites and Muhammad produces mockery. — reasons: Islahi says disease here is envy, while sincere scripturalists respond differently; Muqātil names doubting Medinan Jews.
  74:31/q14/p4 Heart disease may denote inward vices such as ignorance, cowardice and miserliness. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports al-Rāghib's broad range but rejects extending punitive Quranic heart disease to every such trait; she limits it to hypocrisy, doubt, rancor, temptation and lust.
74:31/q15 Who are the unbelievers named beside the diseased-hearted? [وَٱلْكَٰفِرُونَ · referent] (turns on: Whether these unbelievers are Meccan polytheists or denying scripture communities.)
  74:31/q15/p1 Meccan Arab polytheists or those openly fixed in denial. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī prefers Muqātil's Arab identification since scripture readers would not deny their own book; al-Bayḍāwī and al-Biqāʿī stress settled denial.
  74:31/q15/p2 Jews and Christians. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī reports ʿAṭāʾ and al-Kalbī then rejects this against his Arab-polytheist reading; al-Qurṭubī reports it as the majority's view.
74:31/q16 What does the lām of 'so that they say' express? [وَلِيَقُولَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether their speech is a divine aim, an incidental consequence, or a prediction.)
  74:31/q16/p1 An eventual or unintended outcome, not the intended purpose of their objectionable speech. — reasons: The Muʿtazila, Ibn ʿĀshūr, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī and others compare 28:8; al-Ālūsī distinguishes intended guidance from incidental misguidance and allows both clauses as consequence; Bursevī resolves the problem of God intending mockery this way.
  74:31/q16/p2 A real divine purpose, since God can intend their response. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says his theological position allows this; al-Ṭabrisī reports a purpose by which questioning and reflection might lead to faith. Al-Ālūsī says divine acts can be explained by purposes.
  74:31/q16/p3 A prediction of what these people will say. — reasons: Jishumī gives this beside the consequential reading.
74:31/q17 How is mādhā arāda parsed? [مَاذَآ أَرَادَ ٱللَّهُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether mādhā is a single object or mā plus a relative predicate.)
  74:31/q17/p1 Mādhā is one interrogative unit serving as the object of arāda. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās and al-Ālūsī report this analysis.
  74:31/q17/p2 Mā is interrogative subject and dhā is a relative predicate meaning 'that which'. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās reports dhā as 'which'; al-Ālūsī supplies an omitted relative pronoun; Ibn ʿĀshūr derives this use from the demonstrative.
74:31/q18 What is meant by 'this example'? [بِهَٰذَا مَثَلًۭا · meaning] (turns on: Whether mathal means a plain account, mockingly named extraordinary number, or genuine allegory.)
  74:31/q18/p1 It means the account or description of the nineteen keepers. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī observes there is no explicit parable as in 2:26 and cites 13:35 and 48:29 for mathal as account; al-Layth cites 13:35, and Ibn ʿĀshūr compares 47:15. 'This' points to 74:30.
  74:31/q18/p2 They call the strange count a parable or proverb because it seems astonishing or symbolically incomplete. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Nasafī say nineteen rather than twenty appears defective to them; al-Rāzī says they suspect another sense is intended; al-Biqāʿī reports both astonishment and a nonliteral suspicion.
  74:31/q18/p3 It is an allegory of eschatological realities rather than a literal numerical claim. — reasons: Asad says those who literalize the count miss its purpose and compares 2:26.
74:31/q19 What do the questioners intend by asking what God wanted? [مَاذَآ أَرَادَ ٱللَّهُ بِهَٰذَا مَثَلًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the question is derisive denial or an opening to reflective belief.)
  74:31/q19/p1 They mock or deny divine authorship, asking how nineteen could frighten or punish multitudes. — reasons: Muqātil and Ibn Zayd describe skepticism at few guards; Jishumī distinguishes puzzlement from a direct numerical objection; al-Rāzī says hypocrites professed revelation outwardly while unbelievers rejected it. Al-Ālūsī and Kur'an Yolu stress the question is contemptuous, not sincere; Asad notes unbelievers would phrase it as 'your God'.
  74:31/q19/p2 Their question can initiate reflection leading to faith. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī reports this purposive alternative.
74:31/q20 What is the grammatical role of mathalan? [مَثَلًۭا · grammar]
  74:31/q20/p1 It is either a specification of hādhā or a circumstantial accusative from it. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī compares the she-camel called a sign in 11:64; al-Nasafī, al-Samīn and Bursevī transmit both; Ibn ʿĀshūr chooses the circumstantial reading.
