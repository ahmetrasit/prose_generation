## THE CURRENT MAP
29:16/q01 How is إبراهيم read and syntactically governed? [وَإِبْرَٰهِيمَ · readings] (turns on: The case reading and, for the accusative, the governing verb; the surrounding sending formula supports the preferred coordination.)
  29:16/q01/p1 In the common accusative reading, it is coordinated with Noah: God also sent Abraham. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj, al-Naḥḥās and al-Kisāʾī supply 'We sent' from 29:14. Al-Kashshāf and al-Nasafī connect it with Abraham's readiness to counsel. Al-Biqāʿī prefers this construction by the later Midian parallel; al-Ālūsī prefers it by 29:36. Al-Rāzī and al-Samīn identify the accusative as common. Al-Bursawī adds an implied command to recall the time of speech.
  29:16/q01/p2 In the accusative, an omitted 'Remember' governs Abraham. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī makes 'when he said' an inclusion apposition since the time contains the event. Al-Biqāʿī says recalling the exemplary forefather comforts the Prophet and instructs his people. Al-Ālūsī permits coordination of stories with different forms.
  29:16/q01/p3 In the nominative reading, an omitted predicate gives 'Among the messengers was Abraham.' — reasons: Al-Kashshāf names al-Nakhaʿī and Abū Ḥanīfa as readers; Abū Ḥayyān adds Abū Jaʿfar and Ibrāhīm. Al-Samīn makes Abraham the subject. Al-Ālūsī favors this supplied predicate in light of 29:14–15.
  29:16/q01/p4 The accusative Abraham is coordinated with the object pronoun of 'We saved him' in 29:15. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Kisāʾī and al-Samīn allow it. Al-Ālūsī reports Abū al-Baqāʾ and Ibn ʿAṭiyya offering it, but calls it poor.
  29:16/q01/p5 The nominative may instead supply 'among those to be mentioned' or 'among those saved.' — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports both estimates and prefers 'among the messengers' because of the surrounding verses.
29:16/q02 What is the grammatical function of إذ قال? [إِذْ قَالَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether idh modifies sending or belongs to an omitted 'remember'; Abū Ḥayyān disputes the appositional object analysis.)
  29:16/q02/p1 It marks the time of God's sending Abraham. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf says he could then counsel through age and knowledge; al-Bayḍāwī says judgment and recognition of truth had matured; al-Nasafī and al-Ālūsī say he was ready to exhort and guide.
  29:16/q02/p2 It is an inclusion apposition to Abraham under an omitted 'Remember.' — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī says the time contains the event; al-Samīn and al-Biqāʿī give the same parse. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Nasafī allow it with omitted 'remember.' Abū Ḥayyān objects that past-time idh is not a direct object, and al-Ālūsī reports his objection to making its indeclinable temporal form an object or substitution.
  29:16/q02/p3 An implied command asks the audience to remember the time of his speech. — reasons: Al-Bursawī combines coordination of Abraham with Noah and this implicit recollection.
29:16/q03 When was Abraham sent in relation to his call? [إِذْ قَالَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether 'when he said' requires sending to coincide with speech, and whether 29:18 attests an already existing mission.)
  29:16/q03/p1 He was sent when mature enough in age, judgment and knowledge to counsel his people. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī and al-Ālūsī make this the sense of the temporal phrase.
  29:16/q03/p2 He remained a messenger while speaking; sending is a continuing state. — reasons: Al-Rāzī raises the problem that preaching ordinarily follows sending and compares ongoing messengerhood to standing that lasts until a prince exits his house.
  29:16/q03/p3 He began calling people through divine guidance before formal sending, which happened while he preached. — reasons: Al-Rāzī offers this as another resolution of the order problem. Al-Ālūsī reports a similar proposal near adulthood but replies that Abraham's words at 29:18, if his, suggest prior sending; al-Saʿdiyya says the verse need not imply formal mission.
29:16/q04 How does Abraham's account connect with Noah's and Quraysh? [وَإِبْرَٰهِيمَ · links]
  29:16/q04/p1 It continues Noah's account by introducing another prophet God sent. — reasons: Yaḥyā ibn Sallām links it to 29:14; al-Zajjāj supplies the sending verb. Al-Biqāʿī cites the later Midian parallel, and al-Ālūsī joins the stories despite differing sentence forms.
  29:16/q04/p2 It offers Quraysh a parallel and recalls their forefather's rejection of idols. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān make the analogy explicit. Al-Biqāʿī says remembering Abraham comforts the Prophet and instructs his people.
29:16/q05 Who were Abraham's people in this account? [لِقَوْمِهِ · referent]
  29:16/q05/p1 His own people, identified as Nimrod and the idolaters of his city in Babylon. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān mention Nimrod and his idol-worshiping city; al-Bursawī names Babylon and includes Nimrod. Al-Biqāʿī stresses Abraham addressed his own people.
29:16/q06 What is reported about Abraham before he called his people? [وَإِبْرَٰهِيمَ · reports]
  29:16/q06/p1 He grew unusually fast, then went out to call his people. — reasons: Ibn Abī Ḥātim reports al-Suddī: each day of growth equaled a week and each week a month.
29:16/q07 What does اعبدوا الله ask Abraham's people to do? [ٱعْبُدُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ · interpretation] (turns on: The command is unpacked as exclusive monotheism, practical obedience or affirmation of God's existence; al-Rāzī explicitly gives alternative readings.)
  29:16/q07/p1 Worship God alone, affirming His oneness instead of idols or intermediaries. — reasons: Muqātil and Yaḥyā gloss worship as oneness; Ibn ʿAṭiyya sets the call among Nimrod's idolaters. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says exclusivity is implicit: idolaters worship angels and jinn as intermediaries to God. Kur'an Yolu asks whether idols have divine qualities. Al-Rāzī argues possible beings require a necessary existent, and two necessary beings would be composite through shared and distinguishing attributes; no partner can share God's status. Ibn Kathīr adds sincere worship, provision sought only from God, and thanks to the sole giver.
  29:16/q07/p2 Obey God by performing His worship and obligations. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās says 'obey'; al-Ṭabarī says to discharge duties. Al-Rāzī includes affirmation of God and rejection of shirk within obligations and prohibitions. Al-Biqāʿī says worship as God directs. Study Quran calls it a summary of appeals in 19:42, 19:48, 21:52, 21:66, 26:69, 26:76, 37:85 and 37:95.
  29:16/q07/p3 Affirm God's existence. — reasons: Al-Rāzī reports a paired reading: worship affirms God, while piety denies other gods; association with the sovereign is a grave offense.
29:16/q08 What does وَٱتَّقُوهُ ask them to do? [وَٱتَّقُوهُ · interpretation] (turns on: The explanations specify fear itself, abstention from forbidden deeds, or especially avoidance of shirk.)
  29:16/q08/p1 Fear God and His punishment. — reasons: Al-Suddī, Muqātil and Ibn ʿAbbās say fear God. Al-Qurṭubī specifies His punishment and torment; al-Biqāʿī links association with punishment. Ibn Kathīr and Study Quran speak of reverence alongside worship.
  29:16/q08/p2 Avoid sins and prohibited acts. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says to avoid sins while performing duties; al-Rāzī pairs obligations with abstention from prohibitions.
  29:16/q08/p3 Avoid associating anything with God. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says piety denies any other deity because a partner with the sovereign is a grave offense. Al-Biqāʿī, al-Bursawī and al-Ālūsī explicitly link fear to shunning shirk; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls the pair a summons to monotheism and a warning.
29:16/q09 With what is ذَٰلِكُمْ خَيْرٌ لَّكُمْ compared? [ذَٰلِكُمْ خَيْرٌۭ لَّكُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether khayr compares worship with idolatry, all goods, or nothing.)
  29:16/q09/p1 Worship and piety are better than their idolatry, unbelief or current practice. — reasons: Muqātil, al-Ṭabarī, al-Wāḥidī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Qurṭubī and al-Bursawī contrast idols or unbelief; al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī name current practice. Al-Bursawī says the comparative accommodates their false belief, though unbelief contains no good. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr glosses khayr as afḍal, 'better or more excellent.' Al-Jishumī and al-Baghawī state the advantage generally; Ibn Kathīr says it earns good and averts evil.
  29:16/q09/p2 Worship and reverent fear are better than everything beneficial. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says better than anything; al-Ālūsī reports comparison with all beneficial things.
  29:16/q09/p3 Khayr can mean simply 'good,' without comparison. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports this alternative alongside both comparative construals.
29:16/q10 Why are worship and piety better for people? [ذَٰلِكُمْ خَيْرٌۭ لَّكُمْ · interpretation]
  29:16/q10/p1 They bring benefit and avert harm in this life and the hereafter. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr says worship, fear, seeking provision from God and thanking the giver bring good and repel evil in both lives. Kur'an Yolu says obedience benefits people; disbelief harms worldly life and prevents salvation in the hereafter.
  29:16/q10/p2 They draw a person near to sovereign God and raise that person's rank. — reasons: Al-Rāzī argues humans cannot own heaven and earth, so their highest rank is nearness to the sovereign. He cites 96:19 on prostration and two divine sayings about drawing near through obligations and continued worship. An atheist denies the sovereign, while an idolater posits inferior partners; uniqueness, worship and piety yield higher status to one who understands the proofs.
  29:16/q10/p3 Serving God protects human dignity and personality. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu says serving others damages these, whereas worship and obedience to God benefit the worshiper.
