74:11/q06 How much of the passage was revealed about al-Walīd, and when? [* · sciences] (turns on: The extent assigned to the reported occasion and the chronology of al-Walīd's statement relative to the sūra's opening.)
  74:11/q06/p1 The verses from 74:11 through the threat of Saqar at 74:26 concern him. — reasons: Ibn Zayd specifies the passage beginning here and ending at the threat of being cast into Saqar.
  74:11/q06/p2 The revelation prompted by Quraysh's charge runs through 74:25. — reasons: Mujāhid's Dār al-Nadwa account says Quraysh spread the sorcery charge and the sūra was revealed through 74:25.
  74:11/q06/p3 This verse and the following nineteen verses were revealed about him. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports abundant accounts for this extent.
  74:11/q06/p4 Al-Walīd's later conduct means the whole sūra cannot have come at the very beginning of the mission. — reasons: Ālūsī draws this chronological consequence from identifying his later conduct as the occasion.
  74:11/q06/p5 The likely opening chronology places the pilgrimage deliberation after a forty-day hiatus, near the end of Dhū al-Qiʿda. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says revelation began in Ramadan and this was the strongest chronology for the later Quraysh preparation.
74:11/q07 What does the verse teach through the man's solitary creation and later blessings? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The notes develop complementary lessons: the duty of gratitude and the threat against arrogant denial.)
  74:11/q07/p1 God's gifts require gratitude; al-Walīd's pride and denial make his ingratitude especially grave. — reasons: Muqātil and Qatāda contrast the empty beginning with wealth and sons. Kashshāf says he repaid gifts with disbelief, shirk and religious mockery; Ibn Kathīr notes denial of signs and a claim that they were human speech. Qurʾan Yolu grounds gratitude in reason and justice, especially for life and material and spiritual gifts, and condemns its conversion to denial, polytheism and injustice. Iṣlāḥī criticizes the belief that wealth and rank prove divine favor or guarantee more in the Hereafter; 6:94 evokes departure without them. Study Quran and Asad stress human dependence on the Creator under either reading.
  74:11/q07/p2 The warning promises severe judgment for rejecters who trust their worldly standing. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī reads 74:11–17 as warning those expecting further advantage from wealth and rank. Ṭabāṭabāʾī extends the threat to disparagers of the Qurʾan who call it magic and mock its truths. Ibn Kathīr says God threatens the wicked man for ingratitude and false attribution of revelation to human speech.
74:11/q08 Why use this wording and single out the man? [* · rhetoric]
  74:11/q08/p1 Naming God as his Creator exposes his ingratitude and contrasts his original helplessness with later wealth and pride. — reasons: Māwardī and Qurṭubī say his hostility to the Messenger singled him out although everyone begins similarly. Kashshāf and Ibn ʿĀshūr show how the relative clause and 'alone' recall God's favor; Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 16:78 for birth without knowledge. Samarrāʾī says singular wording can also serve an individual punishment. Ibn ʿĀshūr defines waḥīd from waḥada as being alone in place or condition or distinguished in fame or story, with context determining the sense.
74:11/q09 How does this verse connect with surrounding verses and the sūra? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the al-Walīd statement occasions the sūra's opening or follows it determines the immediate link to 74:7.)
  74:11/q09/p1 It consoles the Prophet and continues the call to patience while God undertakes judgment. — reasons: Biqāʿī connects it to the preceding warning of judgment and the Prophet's grief over arrogant opponents. Ibn ʿĀshūr says al-Walīd's fabrication troubled him, so 'leave Me' clarifies 74:7; if the statement occasioned the sūra, intervening verses are parenthetical, whereas a later statement makes this a new section. He compares the threat to 68:44. Muqātil cites 51:52 to console the Prophet with earlier messengers accused of sorcery or madness. Damghānī and Iṣlāḥī compare 73:11, where prosperous deniers are left to God for a little respite.
  74:11/q09/p2 Al-Muddaththir parallels the preceding al-Muzzammil in theme and title. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī calls them counterparts with essentially the same central themes and names sharing a meaning.
74:11/q10 Was the command abrogated by the sword verse? [* · sciences] (turns on: The status of an exegetical claim of abrogation; Ibn al-Jawzī gives no further proof here.)
  74:11/q10/p1 Some exegetes said that it was abrogated. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī records their sword-verse claim but calls it unsound.
  74:11/q10/p2 The abrogation claim is unsound. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī rejects the reported claim.
74:11/q11 What inward reading is given to the solitary man? [* · inward]
  74:11/q11/p1 Al-Walīd figures the solitary evil soul, whose worldly following and traits are overcome by spiritual light. — reasons: The Najmī allusion reported by Bursawī makes wealth, bad traits, followers and prestige symbols of sins and dark natural powers. It makes Abū Bakr the hidden, ʿUmar the spirit, ʿUthmān the secret and ʿAlī the heart, whose light erases the self's darkness, defeats its works and following, and ends its dominion.
74:11/q12 Does calling al-Walīd 'unique' affirm his boast? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether an epithet asserts a true quality or can function as a name, quotation, or ironic condemnation.)
  74:11/q12/p1 Taking it as praise would wrongly affirm his claim of unequaled status. — reasons: Wāḥidī rejects that meaning on this ground; Rāzī reports that many later scholars make the same objection.
  74:11/q12/p2 The verse can invoke his title without endorsing the boast. — reasons: Rāzī replies that a proper name need not assert the quality, that uniqueness may be only in al-Walīd's belief, or that he is unique in evil and lowliness; he cites 44:49. Qurṭubī likewise says God can repeat a boast without endorsing it.
74:11/q13 What specific wealth is reported for al-Walīd? [* · reports]
  74:11/q13/p1 He had ten slaves, each trading with a thousand dinars. — reasons: Al-Qummī's report says this of al-Walīd; it adds that a qinṭār could mean a bullhide filled with gold.
