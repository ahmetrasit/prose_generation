455/r3] WAHIDI-BASIT, d. 468 · author | reports | An objection cites pre-Islamic poetry using al-Raḥmān to argue that Arabs knew the name before Islam.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v1p455/r4] WAHIDI-BASIT, d. 468 · أبو العباس | reports | Abū al-ʿAbbās replies that most pre-Islamic Arabs did not know the name; Quraysh said they knew al-Raḥmān only as a man in al-Yamāma, and a few poets learned it from scriptural communities.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v1p455/r5] WAHIDI-BASIT, d. 468 · أبو العباس | holds | His intended distinction is that al-Raḥmān circulated across Hebrew and Arabic speech, while al-Raḥīm was distinctively Arabic and so clarified the pairing.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v1p455/r6] WAHIDI-BASIT, d. 468 · author | prefers | The author instead holds that al-Raḥmān is an Arabic name derived from mercy, supported by Arabic patterns such as laḥfān, nadmān, and ghaḍbān.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v1p455/r7] WAHIDI-BASIT, d. 468 · الليث | reports | Al-Layth treats al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm as two names both derived from mercy.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v1p455/r8] WAHIDI-BASIT, d. 468 · أبو عبيدة | reports | Abū ʿUbayda treats the two words as divine attributes meaning possessor of mercy.
[WAHIDI-BASIT:v1p455/r9] WAHIDI-BASIT, d. 468 · author | holds | The question What is al-Raḥmān? in 25:60 asks about the attribute, not the person's identity, so it does not prove the name was unknown; it may also express irreverence. | mentions 25:60
[WAHIDI-WAJIZ:1:1-4/r4] WAHIDI-WAJIZ, d. 468 · author | holds | The two divine attributes mean possessing mercy, an abiding divine quality understood as willing good; the names do not differ in meaning, like nadmān and nadīm.
[JISHUMI:v1p200/r10] JISHUMI, d. 494 · author | holds | Al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm derive from mercy, whose basis is benefaction to the needy; both are intensive forms, with faʿlān more emphatic than faʿīl because it departs further from the base pattern.
[JISHUMI:v1p203/r10] JISHUMI, d. 494 · author | reports | A further distinction assigns al-Raḥmān to creation and al-Raḥīm to provision.
[JISHUMI:v1p203/r11] JISHUMI, d. 494 · author | reports | The pairing intensifies the attribution of mercy and teaches that all blessings come from God.
[JISHUMI:v1p203/r12] JISHUMI, d. 494 · author | reports | Because Arabs commonly knew Allah but not al-Raḥmān, the names are paired to show that Allah, al-Raḥmān, and al-Raḥīm all name and describe Him.
[JISHUMI:v1p203/r13] JISHUMI, d. 494 · author | reports | Another explanation says al-Raḥmān is more frequent in the Torah, al-Raḥīm in the Gospel, and Allah in the Qur’an, so the pairing shows all refer to God.
[JISHUMI:v1p203/r14] JISHUMI, d. 494 · author | holds | Al-Raḥmān precedes al-Raḥīm because its stronger intensity and exclusive use make it like a proper name, and the more definite name comes first.
[JISHUMI:v1p203/r6] JISHUMI, d. 494 · author | reports | One view treats al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm as synonymous names for the merciful, like nadmān and nadīm.
[JISHUMI:v1p203/r7] JISHUMI, d. 494 · author | reports | Another view distinguishes the names because other beings may be called raḥīm but not raḥmān.
[JISHUMI:v1p203/r8] JISHUMI, d. 494 · author | reports | One distinction makes al-Raḥmān the provider for all creatures and al-Raḥīm the forgiver of all believers.
[JISHUMI:v1p203/r9] JISHUMI, d. 494 · author | reports | Another makes al-Raḥmān the giver of foundational blessings only God can create, while al-Raḥīm denotes one possessing mercy.
[JISHUMI:v1p206/r7] JISHUMI, d. 494 · author | holds | Al-Raḥmān gives the blessings of worldly life and religion; al-Raḥīm has expansive mercy, and the whole praise belongs to the One who creates, owns, blesses, and deserves worship.
[JISHUMI:v1p206/r8] JISHUMI, d. 494 · author | reports | Explanations for repeating al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm: the first occurrence is outside the sūra, it first seeks aid and the second assigns praise to God, or the repetition emphasizes mercy.
[JISHUMI:v1p206/r9] JISHUMI, d. 494 · علي بن عيسى | reports | ʿAlī b. ʿĪsā denies mere repetition: the first occurrence links servitude to blessings warranting worship, the second links praise to blessings warranting praise.

[RAZI:1:1/r1] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Razi sketches the sūra’s sequence: seek refuge from sins, begin good works in God’s name, praise and recognize his lordship and mercy, heed judgment, worship and seek help, ask guidance, and avoid ignorance and error.
[RAZI:1:3/r1] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Mercy means both rescuing people from kinds of affliction and bringing benefits to those in need.
[RAZI:1:3/r2] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Knowing how God rescues people requires knowing the many afflictions; medical works reveal only some possible diseases in each organ and body part.
[RAZI:1:3/r3] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Reflection on how God guided human minds to identify foods and medicines from minerals, plants and animals opens an ocean of knowledge without shore.
[RAZI:1:3/r4] RAZI, d. 606 · جالينوس | reports | Razi recounts Galen’s story that, while writing about the eye’s benefits, he withheld his account of the divine wisdom in the two hollow optic nerves meeting at one place; an angel in a dream rebuked him, so he wrote the account.
[RAZI:1:3/r5] RAZI, d. 606 · جالينوس | reports | Galen said his thickened spleen resisted every treatment he knew; in a vision an angel directed him to bleed the vein between the little and ring fingers, which cured it.
[RAZI:1:3/r6] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Razi says many early medical signs trace to promptings and inspirations like Galen’s experiences, and reflection on them shows that divine mercy exceeds measurement and enumeration.
