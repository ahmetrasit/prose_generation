[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r11] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Isti‘ana means asking for aid or assistance; giving help is i‘ana, and mutual assistance is ta‘awun, so the terms must be distinguished.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r12] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The exclusive restriction here concerns asking for help; the omitted complement permits three readings: aid itself, aid in every matter, or aid in a specific matter.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r13] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The first and universal readings converge in meaning, though the first is more eloquent and the second more explicit.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r14] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | rejects | Although worship could suggest a specific request for aid, the verse's monotheistic emphasis and context of God's exclusive praise require a general meaning.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r15] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · İbn Abbas | reports | Ibn Abbas's transmitted view supports the general scope of seeking aid, in worship and all other affairs.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r16] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The resulting meaning is that believers ask God alone for help both in worship and every other matter, unlike disbelievers who seek help elsewhere.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r17] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Beyzâvî | reports | Beydawi distinguishes necessary aid, without which an act cannot occur, from nonessential aid that makes it easier; necessary aid includes ability, conception, instruments, and material.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r18] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Legal theory calls these two kinds of aid enabling power and facilitating power.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | God alone controls fear, hope, pleasure, pain, body, soul, and the inner capacities of perception and volition; these gifts should be devoted to His command with gratitude.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | reports | The proud objector asks whether worship's humility is degrading for a soul that wants to rise or believes it has already risen.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | rejects | Wanting to rise admits one's own need and the value of a higher state, which worship recognizes most fully.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | rejects | Claiming to have risen already falsely treats progress as finite and decline as impossible; ascent is limitless and the danger of falling remains, so worship is pride's remedy.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Humility and reverence toward God provide the highest possible elevation for the human conscience through affiliation with Him.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r7] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The imagined worshipper pledges freedom from all but God, obedience to His commands, moral effort, and even sacrifice of life and worlds in God's path.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Such faithful affiliation is the greatest human power and nobility; the Fatiha asks the speaker to say 'we,' making the pledge communal.
[ELMALILI:1:5#3/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The first Muslims lived this spirit and spread God's exaltation across the world, whereas those who lost it bowed to worldly power.
[ELMALILI:1:5#4/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | All enabling aid belongs to God, but the request is ours; capability arises when human seeking and divine assistance coincide with the act, which is called acquisition or partial will.
[ELMALILI:1:5#4/r10] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The limits of understanding human willing reflect our inability to comprehend God's reality.
[ELMALILI:1:5#4/r11] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The verse itself assigns the capacity to seek to us and assistance and power to God, excluding both Jabriyya compulsion and Qadariyya denial of destiny.
[ELMALILI:1:5#4/r12] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | rejects | It is mistaken to infer that every partial will succeeds; when people fail, they may wrongly swing toward compulsion by treating willing as creation and assuming God cannot intervene.
[ELMALILI:1:5#4/r13] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | God can create outcomes that match or diverge from our seeking, grant extra favor to those on His path, or let others face loss, punishment, or reminders.

[ELMALILI:1:5#4/r15] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | reports | A hadith says that when God wills to enact His decree, He removes the reason of people of understanding; this concerns good or bad seeking, not compulsion.

[ELMALILI:1:5#4/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | In voluntary acts we are the proximate cause through our request, but not the complete cause, since we depend on necessary aid; the Creator makes the total causal set.
[ELMALILI:1:5#4/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Voluntary action expresses a relation between a requesting agent and a creating agent; bodies are inherently passive, while the soul is inherently desirous and God's action is creative.
[ELMALILI:1:5#4/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The engine analogy distinguishes the machine as the locus of motion, the operator as the one who wants and initiates it, and God as the creator of the motion from nonexistence.
[ELMALILI:1:5#4/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | rejects | The account rejects both Jabriyya or materialist views that reduce people to soulless machines and the idea that humans create like God.

[ELMALILI:1:5#4/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The request of existence is not existence itself: seeking is a relation among existents, not a created existent, so it entails neither infinite regress nor compulsion nor creation.
[ELMALILI:1:5#4/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Universal will is created, while partial will, seeking, and choice called kasb are an uncreated relation belonging to us.
