2:206/q21 Why call Hell a wretched ٱلْمِهَادُ? [وَلَبِئْسَ ٱلْمِهَادُ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the bed image is prepared recompense, physical contact, ironic replacement, or settled permanence.)
  2:206/q21/p1 It is the bed or abode he has prepared for himself by his deeds. — reasons: Tabari attributes his bed to hypocrisy, immorality and rebellion; Mujahid and Ibn Abbas call it what its occupants prepared; Biqa'i connects the bed to his chosen corruption. Razi cites 30:44 on preparing for oneself.
  2:206/q21/p2 It is the bed on which he is laid or which contacts his sides and back. — reasons: Razi's scholars picture a sleeper laid upon fire; Ibn Ashur says those thrown in meet it with their sides and backs.
  2:206/q21/p3 It is ironic substitution: Hell replaces the comforting bed he expected. — reasons: Wahidi compares glad tidings of painful punishment at 84:24, Tabrisi at 31:7 and Qurtubi at 3:21; Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi cite a 'greeting' consisting of a painful blow. Samin calls it derisive irony; Alusi says it is used mockingly.
  2:206/q21/p4 It emphasizes Hell as his permanent settled place. — reasons: Al-Hasan sees a resting place as something enduring beneath its occupant; Qurtubi calls it the unbelievers' settled abode; al-Harali describes a long or eternal afterlife stay.
2:206/q22 How is وَلَبِئْسَ ٱلْمِهَادُ parsed? [وَلَبِئْسَ ٱلْمِهَادُ · grammar] (turns on: How the omitted مخصوص, the blame verb, and the implied oath are syntactically related.)
  2:206/q22/p1 It answers an implied oath, with Jahannam understood as the omitted specific object of blame. — reasons: Baydawi and Bursavi say the lām carries an implied oath; Abu Hayyan and Samin supply Jahannam or hiya as the omitted مخصوص. Alusi also notes the implied oath and obvious ellipsis.
  2:206/q22/p2 Biʾsa is a frozen verb with al-mihād its subject and the specified object a topic, placed before or after. — reasons: Abu Hayyan follows Basran grammar and Sibawayh; he says treating the omitted specific object as one word preserves a single clause regardless of its position.
  2:206/q22/p3 The omitted specified object is a subject whose praise/blame clause is its predicate. — reasons: Samin reports this construal, while Abu Hayyan and Samin reject analyses deleting an entire unsupported sentence or leaving an unconnected clause.
  2:206/q22/p4 Hell is appositional to al-mihād, receiving the same name. — reasons: Jishumi reports this alternative construction.
2:206/q23 Why omit the specific object after بئس? [وَلَبِئْسَ ٱلْمِهَادُ · rhetoric]
  2:206/q23/p1 It is already clear as Jahannam, and omission lets the verse end on al-mihād. — reasons: Abu Hayyan says Quranic verse endings often omit the object for cadence; Samin says the verse ending makes omission apt.
2:206/q24 What other verse parallels the reaction to admonition? [* · links]
  2:206/q24/p1 The anger resembles disbelievers' reaction to clear revelation at 22:72. — reasons: Ibn Kathir draws this explicit comparison.
2:206/q25 How does this verse relate to 2:204–207? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the sequence is read through reported combat, a moral character contrast, or its broader sura structure.)
  2:206/q25/p1 It is the arrogant corrupt figure contrasted with one who gives himself for God; the two are pictured as fighting. — reasons: Ali says 'the two fought' when reading into 2:207; Ibn Abbas depicts one man admonishing the other, then risking himself in combat, an explanation Umar praised. Tabari places the hypocrites' mockery of al-Raji martyrs against their reward; Tha'labi recounts Khubayb's faithful conduct.
  2:206/q25/p2 The admonition tests his contentious character and leads to the stated fate. — reasons: Biqa'i says refusal distinguishes him from those who stop under correction; Abu Hayyan says the preceding call to fear God is recalled by the punishment that follows.
  2:206/q25/p3 The surrounding five traits are a moral portrait rather than proof of formal hypocrisy. — reasons: Razi says they can characterize show-offs and other sinners, and the admonition response alone does not establish hypocrisy.
  2:206/q25/p4 The portrait contrasts hypocrisy with sincerity after verses contrasting worldly and otherworldly aims. — reasons: Tabataba'i links it to the Hajj sequence, including 2:200.
2:206/q26 What broader lesson does the passage teach about outward appearances? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The practical lesson drawn from the verse's portrait.)
  2:206/q26/p1 Sweet speech can hide corruption; test conduct and inner sincerity before trusting appearances or entrusting responsibility. — reasons: Razi says the traits encourage sound conduct of heart and limbs and recall that God sees hidden affairs; Elmalılı urges evaluating responsibility by deeds. Ibn Ashur warns against pleasing words and looks concealing plots; Kur'an Yolu describes an attractive-speaking corrupter.
  2:206/q26/p2 Taqwa and freedom from egoistic pride protect against hypocrisy and enable honest response to truth. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu links reverence to avoiding hypocrisy, ostentation, discord and corruption, and to considering justified warnings. Qushayri says mercy lets a person recognize faults and accept counsel.
2:206/q27 What theological conclusions are drawn from the rebuke and threat? [* · theology] (turns on: Different theological inferences drawn from the same censure.)
  2:206/q27/p1 Human agents are responsible for their own acts. — reasons: Jishumi argues that rebuke, describing sinful pride, condemnation and threatened punishment would be senseless if human deeds were not their own.
  2:206/q27/p2 There is no punishment beyond Hellfire. — reasons: Jishumi infers this from saying Hell is sufficient.
2:206/q28 Why is punishment deferred to the hereafter? [فَحَسْبُهُۥ جَهَنَّمُ · interpretation]
  2:206/q28/p1 The community receives respite out of regard for its Prophet, while evildoers face a long or eternal afterlife stay. — reasons: Al-Harali says immediate worldly punishment of believers would leave worldly life to disbelievers while believers receive the next life.
2:206/q29 Where does the form وَلَبِئْسَ occur? [وَلَبِئْسَ · links]
  2:206/q29/p1 Wa-la-biʾsa occurs twice in al-Baqara and once each in al-Hajj and al-Nur; fa-la-biʾsa occurs only in al-Nahl. — reasons: Burhan cites 16:29 for the latter form.
2:206/q30 What does interpreting this Arabic idiom require? [أَخَذَتْهُ ٱلْعِزَّةُ بِٱلْإِثْمِ · meaning]
  2:206/q30/p1 One must understand Arabic vocabulary, grammar and idiomatic construction beyond a paraphrase. — reasons: Wahidi cites a poem where a woman's tears seize the speaker's heart and says its paraphrase does not explain its structure; sayings about a contradictory excuse, composure and seasoned leadership show idiom cannot always be reconstructed word by word. He cites 3:175 and 25:71 when insisting on Arabic foundations.
