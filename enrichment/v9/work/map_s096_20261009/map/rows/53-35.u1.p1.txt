53:35/q11 Why did the person withhold his giving? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether his restraint reflects fear of dwindling wealth or reliance on a paid bearer of punishment.)
  53:35/q11/p1 Greed, stinginess, and fear of becoming poor led him to stop giving. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr says he withheld charity, kindness, good works, and family ties. He cites the Prophet's instruction to Bilāl to spend without fearing poverty from the Lord of the Throne and 34:39, which promises replacement of what is spent. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reads the supposed unseen concern as exhaustion of wealth; the Study Quran also mentions fear for one's wealth.
  53:35/q11/p2 He believed a payment would transfer the punishment for his turning away. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says he acted as though hiring a bearer of his afterlife burden would avert punishment, presuming that abandoning Islam was a crime and sins could be transferred. The reported transactions in Ibn Zayd and the exegetes' al-Walīd account give different incident details.
53:35/q12 Can another person bear his punishment? [* · theology]
  53:35/q12/p1 He supposed his burden could be borne by another, but the next verse denies transfer of another's burden. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says the man is unaware that each carries only his own burden, citing 53:38. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the next statement answers this claim. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the man assumed both that turning away was culpable and that its punishment could be contracted away.
53:35/q13 What does the rebuking question expose? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The notes emphasize either unwarranted private knowledge and self-sufficiency or the ironic confidence of those claiming heavenly status.)
  53:35/q13/p1 It exposes his ignorance, need for guidance, and unjustified confidence in unseen judgment. — reasons: Al-Rāzī calls turning away ugly while hidden matters still require a guide; only direct sight at a time when faith no longer helps could excuse it. His alternative says the man thinks his judgment sufficient without guide or warner. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the verse a repudiation of his mistaken confidence, and the Study Quran extends the criticism to people acting as if they know better than God, comparing 52:41 and 68:47.
  53:35/q13/p2 It uses sarcastic irony toward misers who presume Paradise is already visible to them. — reasons: Islahi contrasts their grudging gifts with their confidence in Paradise's luxuries; he also criticizes the assumption that Abrahamic or Mosaic descent guarantees Paradise instead of attending to those prophets' teachings and sacrifices.
53:35/q14 How does this question connect to its surrounding passage? [* · links] (turns on: The sources connect the verse to different parts of its context: prior limited knowledge, the opening rebuke, the later rule about burdens, or the larger scriptural section.)
  53:35/q14/p1 It answers the earlier description of limited worldly knowledge. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says 53:35 answers 53:30: the man's worldly knowledge is no knowledge of the unseen or afterlife.
  53:35/q14/p2 It restarts the explanation of the one who turned away. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says this explanatory restart answers the astonishment in 'Have you seen the one who turned away?'
  53:35/q14/p3 Its assumption about transferable sins is answered by the next statement. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī points to 53:38, 'no bearer carries another's burden'; al-Rāzī also invokes that statement when identifying the implied object.
  53:35/q14/p4 The broader section moves from reflection on creation to truths in the scriptures of Moses and Abraham. — reasons: The Study Quran describes the third section this way. Bursevī's alternative invokes the content of Moses' scrolls, understood as Torah books; Islahi says ancestry from Abraham and Moses does not replace their teachings and sacrifices.
53:35/q15 What is said about the Quran's inimitability and unseen knowledge? [* · theology] (turns on: Al-Māwardī lists distinct bases for inimitability and prefers their combined force; the two sarfa versions differ over retained ability to compose a rival.)
  53:35/q15/p1 Truthful forecasts of unseen future events are one aspect of its inimitability. — reasons: Al-Māwardī names this the sixth proposed aspect. As examples, he cites the Jews' failure to wish for death after 2:94–95 challenged them and the fulfilled prediction that Quraysh would not produce a comparable surah.
  53:35/q15/p2 Its collection of knowledge beyond the Arabs and other scholars is another aspect. — reasons: Al-Māwardī calls this the seventh aspect, supported by 6:38 and 16:89 on the Book's completeness. He reports, without grading, a Prophetic description of the Quran as containing news of the past and future, being truth rather than jest, and leading seekers of other guidance astray.
  53:35/q15/p3 God's diversion of people from answering the challenge is an eighth proposed aspect, called sarfa. — reasons: Al-Māwardī reports two versions: their ability to rival the Quran was removed, so an attempt would fail; or their resolve was diverted although they retained ability and might have succeeded if they tried.
  53:35/q15/p4 All eight proposed aspects jointly make it inimitable; none is uniquely preferable. — reasons: Al-Māwardī says their combination heightens the Quran's eloquence and concision.
53:35/q16 How should readers approach the Quran's inimitable wording? [* · interpretation]
  53:35/q16/p1 They should reflect carefully and persistently because meanings become clear or subtle at different times. — reasons: Al-Māwardī grounds this in the Quran's inimitable form and meanings and warns against relying on first impressions.
53:35/q17 What is inferred from Quranic singular and plural forms of wind? [* · meaning]
  53:35/q17/p1 Plural winds indicate mercy, while singular wind indicates punishment. — reasons: Al-Itqān states the pattern and reports it from Ubayy ibn Kaʿb through Ibn Abī Ḥātim and others. It cites a Prophetic prayer seeking plural winds rather than singular wind, explaining that merciful winds vary and temper each other while punitive wind comes unopposed from one direction.
