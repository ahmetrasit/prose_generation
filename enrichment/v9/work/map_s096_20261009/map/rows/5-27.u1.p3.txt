5:27/q34 What does “God accepts only from the God-fearing” imply for deeds and sinners? [إِنَّمَا يَتَقَبَّلُ ٱللَّهُ مِنَ ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ · theology] (turns on: How exclusive the condition is, what acceptance means, and whether a sinner's good act can be accepted.)
  5:27/q34/p1 Acceptance of obedience requires belief and piety; the verse warns against outward deeds without inward reverence. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf calls it a warning to many who labor; al-Rāzī cites 22:37, where piety rather than flesh or blood reaches God. A report says God does not accept until pleased with the servant. Al-Ṭabrisī defines acceptance as obligating reward; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says assurance of mercy is learned through revelation, not rational necessity. ʿĀmir wept at death over this verse; Abū al-Dardāʾ and Ibn ʿUmar valued certainty of one accepted prayer, prostration or dirham above the world, Faḍāla said the same of a mustard seed of deeds, ʿAlī denied that a pious deed can be small, and Muṭarrif prayed over an accepted fast.
  5:27/q34/p2 A sinner can still have a sincerely obedient deed accepted. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī rejects the inference that every act of a sinner must fail: reward is due for an obedient act done as obedience. Ibn ʿAṭiyya distinguishes basic monotheistic acceptance from highest acceptance through avoiding sins. Ibn ʿĀshūr says Islam accepts a believer's good acts despite other lapses.
  5:27/q34/p3 The restriction may concern complete acceptance, sacrifices alone, or correction of Cain's mistaken expectation. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr compares complete guidance and afterlife in 2:2 and 43:35 and offers a sacrifice-only scope. Ṭabāṭabāʾī distinguishes exclusion of impious persons from reversing Cain's expected preference. Al-Samīn says the object may be their offerings/deeds or intentionally left unspecified, comparing 92:5.
5:27/q35 What broad moral does the brothers' story teach? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The sources develop different aspects of the lesson.)
  5:27/q35/p1 Envy and oppression lead to fratricide, ruin, and accountability; readers should follow Abel. — reasons: Muqātil and the Prophetic report present the better son as a model. Ibn Kathīr says the victim gained Paradise while Cain lost this world and the next. Elmalılı stresses the first bloodshed; Ṭabāṭabāʾī sees just cosmic recompense; the Study Quran sees fallen humanity's propensity for violence. Reports of the Prophet describe envy and hatred as a disease that cuts religion; ʿUmar's report links sudden wealth to discord.
  5:27/q35/p2 Piety and justice govern acceptance and reward, regardless of ancestry or outward offering. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī applies this to claims of inherited divine favor; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the brothers knew one Lord, afterlife, prophethood, law, and the duty to avoid injustice. Al-Sulamī says greed deprives, pride humiliates and envy forsakes. Kuranyolu draws a universal human-rights principle from 5:32. Ṭabāṭabāʾī reads the crow's burial lesson as gradual human learning through senses and reason.
5:27/q36 Why does this account follow the preceding discussion of Israel and its covenants? [* · links] (turns on: Sources offer several complementary links through envy, violence, ancestry, revealed knowledge and contrasting conduct.)
  5:27/q36/p1 It rebukes Jewish envy, violence and broken covenants while consoling and vindicating Muhammad. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says the Jews plotted against the Prophet; Jishumī's al-Aṣamm and ʿAlī b. ʿĪsā warn that injustice returns on perpetrators. Al-Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān connect 5:11, 5:15, 5:18 and the story's attempted violence and disclosed scriptural truth; the Study Quran cites 5:11's hand-stretching idiom and other envy passages. Ṭabāṭabāʾī connects rejection of Muhammad to Cain's fruitless envy. Jishumī also says obedience and disobedience retain the same consequences from Adam's sons onward; al-Ṭabrisī compares Cain's punishment with covenant-breaking Jews.
  5:27/q36/p2 Revealing a hidden account from earlier scripture proves Muhammad's prophethood. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās says he had not read books; Abū Muslim in Jishumī says disclose hidden reports to the People of the Book. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān likewise say revelation alone gave access to these obscurities; al-Samīn makes conformity to scripture proof.
  5:27/q36/p3 The story contrasts Israel's cowardice before the giants with Cain's audacity in murder. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān ties it to 5:24; al-Ālūsī notes both acts are sins. Al-Biqāʿī contrasts Moses and Aaron's compassion with Cain's deed, compares rejected spoils, Cain's exile with Israel's wandering, and proposes a stronger link to the Holy Land promise in 5:26. Al-Rāzī also sees Israel's regret after refusing the giants mirrored in the brothers' story. Bursawī's connected Joshua account says Moses lived 120 years and described death as flaying; after forty years Joshua besieged Jericho six months, its walls fell at horn blasts, and a Friday sun was held for victory over thirty-one kings. The offering of spoils burned only after a thief produced a jeweled golden bull's head; Joshua died at 126 after twenty-seven years' leadership. A Persian couplet urges attachment to the Creator rather than the transient world.
  5:27/q36/p4 The story introduces Israel's law of retaliation and the moral in 5:32. — reasons: Mujāhid connects the killing with 5:32 and 4:93; al-Rāzī connects concealed Torah law to 5:15; Asad says Bible followers concealed the moral that 5:32 states; Kuranyolu calls it a universal principle.
  5:27/q36/p5 Adam's son shows that descent from prophets does not guarantee God's favor. — reasons: Abū Ḥāmid in Jishumī, al-Rāzī and al-Biqāʿī answer the claim in 5:18 that Jews are God's children and beloved; even a son reportedly born in Paradise could lose favor. The brothers shared a law, and obedience decided their fates.
  5:27/q36/p6 The story warns later Muslims against envy after wealth and victory. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī imagines Israel after entering the land and Muslims after the Prophet's death; prosperity can renew hatred. He quotes Prophetic reports with graded chains and ʿUmar's concern over war spoils.
5:27/q37 How does an inward reading map the story onto the faculties of the soul? [* · inward]
  5:27/q37/p1 Abel is intellect, Cain estimation; their twins are practical reason and imagination. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī says the heart pairs faculties to discipline estimation and use imagination for knowledge. Intellect's offering is a valid universal conclusion corresponding to reality; estimation offers a fallacy or partial image, so envy arises and it tries to stop intellect's activity and spiritual guidance.
5:27/q38 What other recitation techniques are cited near this verse? [* · readings] (turns on: The notes address distinct phonetic rules and report their transmission.)
  5:27/q38/p1 A slight pause (sakt) may occur on a vowelless consonant before hamza, including the article lām. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī distinguishes same-word and cross-word settings and long vowels from other consonants; reports routes through Ḥamza, Ibn Dhakwān, Ḥafṣ, Ruways and Idrīs. A route through Khalaf and Khallād pauses on article lām and shayʾ; al-Dānī instead gives madd on shayʾ and sakt on lām only.
  5:27/q38/p2 Ikhfāʾ of nūn or tanwīn occurs before fifteen specified letters, with degrees of nasal concealment. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī lists tāʾ through kāf, while al-Dānī defines it between clear articulation and assimilation; proximity strengthens it. Abū Jaʿfar extends nasal articulation before ghayn and khāʾ, whereas other readers treat them as throat letters.
  5:27/q38/p3 An anomalous report of showing feminine tāʾ before dāl is rejected. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī rejects al-Mubhij's attribution to routes of Qālūn and Ibn Buwayyān; showing it before ṭāʾ is also very weak.
5:27/q39 How do reason, piety and revelation guide judgment in this story? [* · theology]
  5:27/q39/p1 Innate reason recognizes broad truth and morality, while detailed religious law comes through revelation and must be approached with piety. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites 30:30 for innate cognition, argues that objections to reason and appeals to experiment depend on rational premises, and says analogies cannot establish revealed laws. He cites 17:36, 2:168, 2:213, 10:32 and 16:37 against speculation and unjust means; 2:232, 2:193 and 29:45 pair legal teaching with moral cultivation.
5:27/q40 What accounts for the expressive force of Qur'anic wording? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether excellence is mere sound or composed meaning.)
  5:27/q40/p1 Eloquence comes from fitting expression, crafted images and precise word choice. — reasons: Al-Jurjānī, explaining al-Jāḥiẓ, says ordinary meanings gain new form through arrangement, like fashioned jewelry; he rejects reducing excellence to sound alone. Al-Sāmarrāʾī calls the Qur'an's fit between expression and context its inimitability and contrasts a verbal noun for giving with ʿaṭāʾ, which also names a good gift.
