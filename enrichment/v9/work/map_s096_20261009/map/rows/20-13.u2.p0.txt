## THE CURRENT MAP
20:13/q01 How is 'I chose you' read? [وَأَنَا ٱخْتَرْتُكَ · readings] (turns on: Singular versus majestic plural, light versus doubled nūn, and open versus broken initial hamza; the singular is supported by the written form and nearby direct address, while the plural has the 17:1–2 parallel.)
  20:13/q01/p1 Wa-anā ikhtartuka: a light nūn and singular 'I chose you.' — reasons: Ibn Mujāhid names Ibn Kathīr, Nāfiʿ, ʿĀṣim, Abū ʿAmr, Ibn ʿĀmir and al-Kisāʾī; al-Naḥḥās and Qurṭubī name the Medinans, Abū ʿAmr, ʿĀṣim and al-Kisāʾī. Ibn Khālawayh parses anā as subject, ikhtartu as predicate, its tāʾ as agent and kāf as object. Al-Naḥḥās and Qurṭubī favor the written form and direct-address sequence; al-Fārisī favors agreement with 'I am your Lord' and Ubayy's reported text, though he acknowledges the plural parallel in 17:1–2. Ibn ʿAṭiyya likewise notes harmony with 'I am your Lord.'
  20:13/q01/p2 Wa-annā ikhtarnāka: a doubled nūn and majestic plural 'We chose you,' with an open hamza. — reasons: Ibn Mujāhid and many later sources attribute it to Ḥamza; Ibn al-Jawzī also names al-Mufaḍḍal, al-Samīn says Ḥamza and others, and al-Ālūsī names Ṭalḥa, Ibn Abī Laylā, Ḥamza, Khalaf and one transmission from al-Aʿmash. Ibn Khālawayh explains the plural as the royal 'we' of rulers; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Ṭabrisī cite the singular-to-plural shift in 17:1–2. Al-Naḥḥās says its sense equals the singular. Some reports describe Ḥamza's initial form as wa-innā rather than wa-annā. Ibn ʿĀshūr attributes the majestic-plural reading to Ḥamza alone.
  20:13/q01/p3 Wa-innā ikhtarnāka: the plural with a kasra on the initial hamza. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya attributes it to a group; al-Samīn names al-Sulamī, al-Aʿmash and Ibn Hurmuz; al-Ālūsī names those readers but distinguishes one of two transmissions from al-Aʿmash. Ibn Khālawayh explains the doubled nūn as assimilation of two nūns, and al-Zajjāj permits an independent or quoted clause.
  20:13/q01/p4 Ubayy's reported singular wa-annī or wa-innī ikhtartuka. — reasons: Al-Fārisī reports Ubayy's codex as wa-annī ikhtartuka and uses it to support the singular; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Samīn report wa-annī, whereas al-Rāzī reports wa-innī. Al-Samīn says its open hamza follows the coordination of Ḥamza's reading. Abū Ḥayyān also mentions Ubayy's yāʾ reading.
  20:13/q01/p5 A proposed open yāʾ in Abū Jaʿfar's reading is inferred, without explicit transmission. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī says Abū Jaʿfar's general rule would open yāʾ where he cuts the hamza, but he found no explicit report for this inference.
20:13/q02 How does the clause 'and I chose you' connect grammatically to the preceding speech? [وَأَنَا ٱخْتَرْتُكَ · grammar] (turns on: The vowel of the initial hamza and whether the clause coordinates with the preceding call, resumes speech, or supplies an implied cause.)
  20:13/q02/p1 It is coordinated with the preceding declaration of God's lordship or call to Moses. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ attaches the singular clause to nūdiya; Ibn Khālawayh continues the open-hamza plural from 'I am your Lord' in 20:12. Al-Samīn prefers coordination with the preceding 'I am your Lord' and says the open or broken hamza follows the preceding reading; al-Ālūsī allows coordination whether the earlier clause attaches to nūdiya or an implied 'know.' The Qirāʾāt Encyclopedia glosses anna as subordinate to the preceding verb, 'and that I chose you.'
  20:13/q02/p2 The kasra form begins a new, independent clause or quoted speech. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ calls it a new sentence; al-Zajjāj permits resumption or a quotation of what Moses was told, 'We chose you.' Ibn Khālawayh treats the doubled-nūn particle as opening the clause. Al-Ālūsī also reports an analysis treating the clause as an independent address with no syntactic governor.
  20:13/q02/p3 The open-hamza clause can depend on an implied 'know' or on the preceding command to remove the sandals. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports both analyses, and says the open clause can coordinate with the earlier 'I am your Lord' under either nūdiya or implied aʿlam; he ascribes an implied aʿlam to the open plural reading.
  20:13/q02/p4 An implied 'because' makes the clause the reason to listen. — reasons: Abū al-Baqāʾ proposes 'because I chose you, so listen,' linking it to istamiʿ; al-Ālūsī reports and dismisses this implied-lām construction.
20:13/q03 What does 'I chose you' mean? [ٱخْتَرْتُكَ · meaning]
  20:13/q03/p1 God selected Moses; choice selects the one best suited to its purpose. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī and Ibn al-Jawzī gloss ikhtartuka as 'I selected you.' Ṭabāṭabāʾī derives choice from khayr: an agent weighs alternatives and selects the best for a purpose, and God found Moses best suited to receive prophecy and mission. Ibn ʿĀshūr explains ikhtiyār as making an effort to seek what is good; its form here expresses excellent pursuit of that good.
20:13/q04 For what did God choose Moses? [ٱخْتَرْتُكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the choice confers the prophetic mission here or marks an earlier stage in an inward sequence.)
  20:13/q04/p1 For prophethood and messengership, including the privilege of divine speech. — reasons: Muqātil, Yaḥyā, al-Kalbī, al-Baghawī, al-Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr and the Study Quran explicitly include the message, speech, or both; the Study Quran connects direct address with Moses' title Kalīm Allāh. Ibn Kathīr and the Study Quran cite 7:144. Al-Wāḥidī reports al-Kalbī's purpose: Moses is to carry out God's command. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says God chose Moses as suited to prophecy and mission; al-Iṣlāḥī describes the appointment as honor and great responsibility. Ibn ʿĀshūr specifies that Moses is selected to receive what God will reveal.
  20:13/q04/p2 In the inward reading, this is an election before prophetic commissioning by revelation. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī distinguishes disclosure of attributes before Essence, says Moses is sent at this stage without commissioning by revelation, and interprets the order as spiritual discipline, presence and vigilance.
20:13/q05 From among whom was Moses chosen? [ٱخْتَرْتُكَ · referent] (turns on: The unstated comparison group: all humanity, Moses' people, or those alive in his time.)
  20:13/q05/p1 From humanity at large. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī gives humanity as one scope of God's selection.
  20:13/q05/p2 From his own people. — reasons: Al-Samīn says a second object of ikhtartuka is omitted, yielding 'I chose you from among your people'; al-Ālūsī also gives this narrower scope.
  20:13/q05/p3 Above all people alive in Moses' time. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr grounds the temporal scope and distinction in 7:144, where God selects Moses above the people by His messages and words.
20:13/q06 Was Moses' selection granted at this moment or reported as already complete? [ٱخْتَرْتُكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the clause performs a new appointment or describes a stage in a prior and later sequence of election.)
  20:13/q06/p1 The utterance itself confers prophecy and mission at this moment. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī reads the clause as a performative appointment, not a report of an earlier choice; the command to listen follows from this appointment.
  20:13/q06/p2 This choice is an intermediate inward election near the original election in 20:122. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī places it between the original election of 20:122 and a later selection, within his account of Moses' self-disclosure and awakening.
20:13/q07 What does Moses' selection teach about the source of prophethood? [ٱخْتَرْتُكَ · theology]
  20:13/q07/p1 Prophethood comes from God's initial choice, not human entitlement. — reasons: Al-Rāzī infers this from God's choosing Moses for prophethood and privileged speech.
20:13/q08 Why was Moses singled out for direct speech? [ٱخْتَرْتُكَ · reports]
  20:13/q08/p1 He humbled himself before God more than anyone else. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr transmits an unnamed dialogue in which God asks Moses the reason, Moses says he does not know, and God gives Moses' unique humility as the answer. The Study Quran reports Abū Sulaymān al-Dārānī's statement that Moses' heart was the most humble among Adam's children.
20:13/q09 What inward effect does God's choice have on Moses or the chosen servant? [ٱخْتَرْتُكَ · inward] (turns on: Different accounts of what selection does inwardly: preparation, purification and effacement, or awakening after self-disclosure.)
  20:13/q09/p1 The chosen servant is nurtured for revelation, loved, articulate and made steadfast. — reasons: Al-Sulamī reports al-Wāsiṭī: God's choice nurtures and selects the servant, makes God's servants love him, enables clear speech, and eases his affairs so he can stand before revelation.
  20:13/q09/p2 Choice purifies Moses of illusion and effaces his self so that he belongs to God and acts through Him. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī says God's choice is made with knowledge of Moses and strips away what clouds clarity; he also reports Moses' annihilation after selection.
  20:13/q09/p3 Choice follows the collapse of Moses' separate existence and awakening through true existence. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī reads 7:143–144 as Moses' mountain-like existence crumbling in annihilation, his stunned fall, and subsequent awakening; he calls this disclosure of attributes before Essence.
20:13/q10 Why does the command to listen follow the announcement of selection? [فَٱسْتَمِعْ · rhetoric]
  20:13/q10/p1 Selection gives the reason for listening: Moses must attend to the revelation of his new calling. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says the fāʾ makes listening follow from selection. Al-Ṭabrisī says the instruction to hear revelation follows the announcement of prophethood; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the command follows the conferral of prophecy and mission.
