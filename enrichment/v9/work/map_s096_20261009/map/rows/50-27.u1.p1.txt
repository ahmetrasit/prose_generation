50:27/q10 Why does the companion invoke the person's prior distant error? [وَلَٰكِن كَانَ فِى ضَلَٰلٍۭ بَعِيدٍۢ · interpretation]
  50:27/q10/p1 The person was already deeply astray and freely receptive to evil, so the companion's prompting did not compel or originate his transgression. — reasons: Tabari and Nahhas describe a path away from guidance and truth. Wahidi reports Muqatil's detail that he was forsaken by God. Baghawi says he would not return; Baydawi and Bursevi say temptation works on unstable judgment inclined to wickedness. Ibn Kathir says he welcomed falsehood and resisted truth. Biqai says his pervasive disposition made him rush toward what angers God without the slightest prompting. Ibn Ashur reads كان as original and enduring, compares the disavowal in 2:166, and argues a mere follower is not so firmly established as an independent investigator. Tabatabai and Study Quran assign responsibility to the human who accepted suggestion. Asad instead applies the point to the conscious mind's departure from moral truth, explaining 50:24–25. Bursevi quotes Attar's Persian couplet of unsatisfied hunger and the unrewarded sieve as an illustration.
50:27/q11 What does كان convey about the person's misguidance? [كَانَ · rhetoric]
  50:27/q11/p1 The misguidance was original and enduring in the person's makeup. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says the perfect verb establishes this duration; his reading of بعيد reinforces it.
50:27/q12 What does ضلال بعيد convey? [ضَلَٰلٍۭ بَعِيدٍۢ · meaning] (turns on: Whether distance is from guidance and truth or, in the inward reading, from unity and innate light.)
  50:27/q12/p1 Error far from the path of guidance or truth. — reasons: Tabari describes departure from guidance; Nahhas and Baghawi from truth; Ibn al-Jawzi and others gloss the phrase as far from guidance. Muqatil associates it with prolonged worldly loss. Ibn Ashur says distance is a metaphor like a distant place or time and echoes 4:116.
  50:27/q12/p2 Distance from unity and original nature, with the soul turned to the lower realm and veiled in darkness. — reasons: Qashani's inward account says this condition makes satanic whispering preferable to angelic inspiration; he compares 14:22.
50:27/q13 What does بعيد add to the description of misguidance? [بَعِيدٍۢ · rhetoric]
  50:27/q13/p1 It intensifies the error as extreme, entrenched, and difficult to reverse. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says return to guidance was difficult. Ibn Ashur treats distance as metaphorical, compares 4:116, and says it reinforces the enduring force of كان.
50:27/q14 What does the companion's denial teach about responsibility and causation? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether denial of compulsion also excludes Satan's causal role through suggestion, and how that role relates to divine creation.)
  50:27/q14/p1 Each person answers for accepting evil; Satan could invite, but could not force the choice. — reasons: Tabarsi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Baydawi, Ibn Kathir, Bursevi, and Tabatabai make the distinction between invitation and acceptance; 14:22 repeatedly supplies the parallel. Study Quran says people would not heed Satan unless already astray. Jishumi says God justly holds each accountable and wrongs no one by disputing on the basis of what He knows. Bursevi's cited hadith says even the messenger cannot guide everyone by his own power, and Iblis only adorns.
  50:27/q14/p2 Satan did cause transgression by whispering and beautifying evil, while God created it under His prior decree. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says Satan's denial is false if made general; God's creation by decree is just and admits no other lord.
50:27/q15 What does the quarrel show about companions at judgment? [* · interpretation]
  50:27/q15/p1 Companions disown one another and try to shift blame when judgment comes. — reasons: Tabari states the lesson and reports Ibn Zayd and Abu Imran explaining the companion's words as disavowal. Baghawi and Ibn Kathir say Satan disowns the unbeliever. Study Quran reports Tabari's innocence interpretation and compares Satan's disavowal and fear of God in 59:16. Ibn Ashur compares 2:166. Ibn Qutayba links pairing wrongdoers and companions to Arab practice of pairing camels and to 37:22 and 44:54; Wahidi compares the mutual blame in 37:27–33. Tabatabai recalls the gathering in 37:22.
50:27/q16 What does the exchange signify in an inward reading? [* · inward]
  50:27/q16/p1 It depicts conflict among inner faculties, especially estimative and intellectual powers, and opposing impulses such as anger and desire. — reasons: Qashani says the dialogue is an imaginative form that fixes its meaning in the heart. The powers cooperate when satisfied, but blame one another in loss because each seeks its own benefit and is veiled from unity. Distance from innate light and attraction to the lower realm make satanic whispering effective instead of angelic inspiration; he cites 14:22 and regards the sinner's acquired kinship with the devil as his fault.
50:27/q17 What reports are cited to illuminate companions and the quarrel? [* · reports] (turns on: The reports address different aspects: the companion's identity and mutual attack at judgment.)
  50:27/q17/p1 Everyone has a jinn companion; God helped the Prophet against his, and it submitted and urged only good. — reasons: Abd al-Razzaq and al-Durr transmit the report from the Prophet.
  50:27/q17/p2 Haritha saw the people of Hell attacking one another, and the Prophet approved his observation. — reasons: Qashani transmits Haritha's report and says the Prophet also approved the devil's assertion that the man was already far astray.
50:27/q18 How does this scene fit the surrounding passage? [* · links] (turns on: The notes emphasize different features of the same surrounding judgment passage.)
  50:27/q18/p1 It belongs to the account of resurrection and recompense from the trumpet blast to God's promise of more. — reasons: Ibn Ashur locates the account from 50:20 through 50:35.
  50:27/q18/p2 It helps vividly contrast the dreadful fate of disbelievers with the good fate of believers. — reasons: Islahi describes the passage's judgment scene and its contrast.
