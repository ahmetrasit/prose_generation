[IBNASHUR:96:3/r11] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The relative clause is placed between subject and predicate to answer the Prophet's fear that he could not read because he could not write: God can teach him reading.
[IBNASHUR:96:3/r2] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The sentence “your Lord is the Most Generous” begins an explanatory answer to how the Prophet can read when he lacks literacy; the One who taught by the pen can teach him.
[IBNASHUR:96:3/r3] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Read alongside ʿĀʾishah's revelation report and Gabriel's repeated command, the sentence answers the Prophet's “I cannot read”: knowledge can come through dictation, instruction, or inspiration.
[IBNASHUR:96:3/r4] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | God taught Adam the names even though Adam was not a reader, illustrating knowledge imparted without literacy.
[IBNASHUR:96:3/r5] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The expected pronoun is replaced by rabbuka to emphasize divine care and to keep the Lord-title connected to the command to read in His name.
[IBNASHUR:96:3/r6] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Al-akram intensifies generosity rather than comparing God with others; karam is giving the recipient beneficial gifts, including existence, creation, and sustenance.
[IBNASHUR:96:3/r7] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The opening five verses gather divine attributes: Lordship entails existence and oneness; creation and teaching entail action; the Most Generous conveys perfection and freedom from defect.
[IBNASHUR:96:3/r8] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The two objects of “taught by the pen” are omitted and understood as teaching writers or people how to write.
[IBNASHUR:96:3/r9] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · أبو حية النميري | reports | Abū Ḥayyah al-Numayrī's verse uses a Jew's hand writing as evidence for the association of writing with the People of the Book.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p435/r3] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · عائشة | reports | Aisha narrates in the two Sahihs that Gabriel came to Muhammad in Hira, commanded him three times, embraced him forcefully each time, then recited the opening through 'what he did not know.'
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p435/r4] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | Aisha's narration is directly from the Prophet; her account that he returned trembling with the passage means he retained and recited what had been dictated.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p435/r5] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | Khadija's request that Waraqa hear him further indicates Muhammad recited the revealed passage immediately; Gabriel's repeated command repeated God's wording to accustom him to reading.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p439/r7] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | The opening five verses gather divine attributes: lordship implies existence and oneness, creation and teaching indicate acts, and al-Akram entails perfection and freedom from deficiency.
[IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p441/r6] IBNASHUR-FULL, d. 1393 · author | holds | These are the final five verses of the passage first revealed at Hira; the following discussion on verses 6–10 begins a new subject.
[TABATABAI:96:1-7/r5] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The second command to read visibly reinforces the first.
[TABATABAI:96:1-7/r6] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · قيل | reports | One view distinguishes the commands: the first tells the Prophet to read privately and the second to recite to the people in proclamation.
[TABATABAI:96:1-7/r7] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · قيل | reports | Another view takes both commands as orders to recite the Qur’an publicly.
[TABATABAI:96:1-7/r8] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | rejects | Neither alternative about public recitation is apparent from the wording.
[TABATABAI:96:1-7/r9] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The Most Generous Lord gives more than anyone else without prior entitlement; every blessing ultimately comes from Him.
[BINTSHATI:v2p13/r2] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | She presents the surah’s opening as the first revelation, noting that Ibn Ishaq and al-Tabari report no disagreement about that account.
[BINTSHATI:v2p13/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · عائشة أم المؤمنين | reports | Aisha recounts Muhammad’s true dreams, retreat to Hira, Gabriel’s command and three embraces, and the recitation of the opening five verses, which remained fixed in his heart; no grading is stated.
[BINTSHATI:v2p13/r4] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · عائشة أم المؤمنين | reports | Aisha continues that Muhammad saw Gabriel in the sky, returned to Khadija, and was reassured because his character included kinship, truthfulness, trustworthiness, hospitality, and helping in adversity; no grading is stated.
[BINTSHATI:v2p14/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الفخر الرازي | reports | Al-Razi also transmits that only al-Alaq’s first five verses came at the start; the remainder came after public opposition and were joined to the surah by God’s command.
[BINTSHATI:v2p14/r4] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · أبو حيان | reports | Al-Bahr al-Muhit says the surah is Meccan and its opening was the first revelation at Hira, as established in Bukhari and elsewhere; it judges the claims that al-Muddaththir or the Fatiha came first unsound.
[BINTSHATI:v2p15/r1] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | The reports in al-Tabari and the Prophetic biography stop the first-revelation account at verse five, supporting the view that the opening alone was first revealed.
[BINTSHATI:v2p20/r1] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · بعض المفسرين | reports | Some exegetes, as al-Razi transmits, say the first اقرأ is for the Prophet himself and the second for proclamation to others.
[BINTSHATI:v2p20/r2] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · بعض المفسرين | reports | A second reported interpretation makes the first command to read for learning and the second to teach.
[BINTSHATI:v2p20/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · بعض المفسرين | reports | A third reported interpretation says the first recitation is in prayer and the second outside prayer.
[BINTSHATI:v2p20/r4] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | prefers | She prefers the straightforward context: the command to recite is reiterated, and revelation specifies what the Prophet should read in his Lord’s name.
[BINTSHATI:v2p20/r5] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | In Arabic, karam is the opposite of baseness and suggests honor; ikram is the opposite of humiliation and degradation.
[BINTSHATI:v2p20/r6] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | The Quran applies karim to God, His throne, a messenger, an angel, scripture, and the promised provision, reward, abode, and station of the righteous.
[BINTSHATI:v2p20/r7] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | In a threatening, ironic address to the sinner, the Quran calls him العزيز الكريم as he is led to Hell. | mentions 44:47
[BINTSHATI:v2p21/r1] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | God’s honoring of the righteous is contrasted with His humiliating whom He wills; in al-Hujurat, akramakum is restricted to the addressed human community. | mentions 22:18, 49:13
[BINTSHATI:v2p21/r2] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الزمخشري | reports | Al-Zamakhshari explains al-Akram as God’s unsurpassed generosity: He gives countless gifts, is patient with disbelief, accepts repentance, and forgives grave sins.
[BINTSHATI:v2p21/r3] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الفخر الرازي | reports | Al-Razi says God not only forbears when people sin but increases His kindness afterward; a cited poem makes the point that greater failure brings greater favor.
[BINTSHATI:v2p21/r4] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الفخر الرازي | reports | Al-Razi’s second explanation contrasts Muhammad’s generosity with the greater generosity of his Lord.
[BINTSHATI:v2p21/r5] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الفخر الرازي | reports | Al-Razi also says God originates every generosity and kindness, and His generosity is free of any shortcoming.
[BINTSHATI:v2p21/r6] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الفخر الرازي | reports | One further reading urges recitation by promising ten rewards for each Quranic letter.
[BINTSHATI:v2p21/r7] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · الفخر الرازي | reports | Alternatively, al-Razi reads al-Akram as reassurance to proclaim the message without fear because God will support the Prophet in this strenuous mission.
[BINTSHATI:v2p21/r8] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | rejects | She rejects limiting al-Akram to comparison among finite beings: the definite, unqualified form claims absolute, unrestricted supremacy for God, unlike the human comparison in al-Hujurat. | mentions 49:13, 87:1, 92:20
[BINTSHATI:v2p22/r1] BINTSHATI, d. 1419 · author | holds | An Arabic superlative made definite with al and left unspecified conveys an absolute general sense; with a complement or specification, comparison remains limited to that scope. | mentions 87:1, 92:20
[BINTSHATI-IJAZ:p272/r1] BINTSHATI-IJAZ, d. 1419 · الملاحدة | reports | Heretics argued, as al-Razi notes, that calling God “the Highest” would imply another, lesser lord by the logic of comparison. | mentions 87:1
[BINTSHATI-IJAZ:p272/r2] BINTSHATI-IJAZ, d. 1419 · author | rejects | Bint al-Shati rejects that comparative reading: an unqualified form such as “the Highest” expresses the utmost degree of height without limit or comparison. | mentions 87:1
[BINTSHATI-IJAZ:p272/r3] BINTSHATI-IJAZ, d. 1419 · مفسرون | rejects | Some commentators treated al-akram as a comparison with karīm and offered several grounds for God’s greater generosity; Bint al-Shati rejects this and says the form is not chosen merely for rhyme.
[BINTSHATI-IJAZ:p272/r4] BINTSHATI-IJAZ, d. 1419 · author | holds | The forms afʿal and fuʿlā express an unrestricted maximum; comparison is specified when a comparator is named, such as with an explicit مِنْ or a stated object. | mentions 2:191
[BINTSHATI-IJAZ:p272/r5] BINTSHATI-IJAZ, d. 1419 · author | holds | “God is the best of plotters” is genuinely comparative in context, because it answers the plotting of human opponents, including Thamud and the disbelievers among the Israelites. | mentions 27:50, 3:54
