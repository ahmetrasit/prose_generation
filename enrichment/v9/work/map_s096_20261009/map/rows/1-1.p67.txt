ELMALILI:1:1#5/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | reports | ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib invoked God at the Kaaba during the Elephant event with laahumma, illustrating pre-Islamic use of the name; the author points readers to Sūrat al-Fīl. | mentions 105:1
[ELMALILI:1:1#5/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Ebû Yezîd-i Belhî | reports | Abū Yazīd al-Balkhī regards laah as foreign because Jews and Christians say laha, and some relate it to Hebrew Elohim; the author says chronology cannot establish borrowing and cognate forms may share an older source.
[ELMALILI:1:1#5/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Kûfiyyûn | reports | The Kufans derive Allah from al-ilāh, with the article becoming integral and its hamza compensating for the elided hamza of ilāh; ilāh is linked to worship, awe, tranquility, or release from fear.
[ELMALILI:1:1#5/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Zemahşerî, Kādî Beyzâvî | reports | Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Bayḍāwī, and other investigators accept the al-ilāh analysis; its meanings are gathered in the true object of worship, and the plural pattern āliha supports the proposed root.
[ELMALILI:1:1#5/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | reports | The two derivational analyses both preserve an original Arabic meaning in the name, though some posit a foreign origin followed by complete Arabization.
[ELMALILI:1:1#5/r7] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Ebû Hayyân-ı Endelüsî | reports | Abū Ḥayyān says the majority consider Allah an un-derived, primordial proper name for the true God.
[ELMALILI:1:1#5/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · İmam Fahreddin Râzî | reports | Al-Rāzī’s preferred position is that Allah is God’s proper name and not originally derived; al-Khalīl, Sībawayh, many legal theorists, and jurists agree.
[ELMALILI:1:1#5/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | prefers | The author concludes that Allah is neither derived nor transferred but was assigned primordially as a proper name; God precedes all names and attributes, and His worship-worthiness derives from Himself rather than from being worshipped.
[ELMALILI:1:1#6/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rahmān is an exclusive divine name and a dominant adjective of essence rather than action; it can occur with or without its described noun, but does not take a preposition as a verb would.
[ELMALILI:1:1#6/r10] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | reports | Commentators distinguish Rahmān as looking to pre-eternity and Rahīm to endless duration: creatures benefit from the former through an initial relation and from the latter through actual action at the end.
[ELMALILI:1:1#6/r11] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | reports | A transmitted explanation calls God the Rahmān of this world and Rahīm of the afterlife, or the Rahmān of believers and unbelievers but Rahīm only of believers; the author clarifies that God is both names in both realms. | mentions 33:43
[ELMALILI:1:1#6/r12] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Because Rahmān is God’s exclusive name, its kind of mercy and beneficence cannot issue from creatures; Rahīm is not exclusive and can describe mercy and generosity from creatures.
[ELMALILI:1:1#6/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Derived from raḥm and raḥma, Rahmān is an intensive adjective meaning possessing very abundant and enduring mercy; although analogically applicable to anyone, actual use is reserved for God.
[ELMALILI:1:1#6/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | reports | A flattering poet once called Musaylima rahmāna without the article, but the author says this was wrong even linguistically and all the more so religiously.
[ELMALILI:1:1#6/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rahmān can be explained as very merciful but cannot be translated as a proper divine name; esirgeyici wrongly suggests withholding or jealousy, while acıyıcı lacks the positive sense of mercy.
[ELMALILI:1:1#6/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Older Turkish speakers used yarlığayıcı for mercy’s gracious result, but this remains an adjective; divine mercy itself is not an emotional inclination but the will of good or unlimited bestowal.
[ELMALILI:1:1#6/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rahmān also cannot be translated as bağışlayıcı, since that confuses it with names such as Vehhāb or ʿAfūvv, which are not exclusive to God.
[ELMALILI:1:1#6/r7] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | prefers | Rahīm is a second divine attribute, best understood here as an intensive active participle meaning very merciful or much showing mercy.
[ELMALILI:1:1#6/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Unlike Rahmān, Rahīm is a pure adjective, requires a described noun, can be used of others, functions verbally, and has a definite article of reference.
[ELMALILI:1:1#6/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The phrase moves from the pure name Allah to the name-and-attribute Rahmān and then the pure attribute Rahīm, gradually unfolding God’s name in the heart as the first manifestation of divine beauty.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rahman is a divine attribute exclusive to God whose relation is not a particular act; its primordial grace extends universally without condition.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r10] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rahim is the grace through which ethical people compete in compassion and good works.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r11] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rahman's first mercy brings everyone into existence and sustains them regardless of effort.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r12] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rahim's further mercy grants the goals achieved by those who work.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r13] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rahman gives absolute hope and prevents universal despair; without it humans would lack existence and essential natural gifts.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r14] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rahim answers particular despair, sustains specific aims and effort, and rewards responsibility.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r15] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Under Rahman's universal grace believers and unbelievers, and this world and the next, are equal; Rahim's grace sharply distinguishes them, as the two groups in 42:7 show. | mentions 42:7
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r16] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Thus the inherited formulations call God Rahman of this world and the next but Rahim of the next, or Rahman of believer and unbeliever but Rahim of the believer.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r17] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Şeyh Abduh | rejects | Abduh neglects the inherited distinction because he assumes the lexicon does not support it; Elmalılı says this is mistaken.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r18] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Lexically Rahman is a predominant attribute specific to God, without an active relation and suggestive of eternity and the beginning; Rahim has an active relation and continues without end.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r19] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rahman's mercy is an essential attribute of willing good at the outset, while Rahim's is an active attribute of doing good at the end.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Every creature's original gifts and existence arise from Rahman's grace, received without anyone's earned choice.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r20] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rahman and Rahim are not repeated merely for emphasis: each has its own foundational meaning and distinct mode of intensification.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r21] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rahman is more emphatic in encompassing every creature, while Rahim is more emphatic in actual beneficence, which also operates through servants.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r22] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Bazı tefsirler | reports | Some commentaries associate Rahman's mercy with great blessings and Rahim's with the subtleties of blessings.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r23] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The grammatical pattern distinguishes them: Rahman's subject is restricted while its object is general; Rahim's subject is general while its object is restricted.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r24] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Together these attributes replace pessimism with boundless optimism, the spirit of faith; the first benefit received from the basmala is this hope, affirmed by 28:83. | mentions 28:83
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Primordial creation is compelled and bestowed: stones, trees, and humans have their kinds through this unchosen mercy.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | All realms and beings, from obedient and believing to rebellious and idolatrous, are immersed in Rahman's mercy and freed from fear in that respect.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | If this universal grace were all, distinctions between knowledge and ignorance, life and death, effort and idleness, obedience and disobedience, and faith and unbelief would disappear.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Without a further mercy enabling agency, people would be determinists and compelled beings, unable to act on what they know or desire.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r7] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | God's wisdom requires that He display His own will and create creatures who possess will, rather than make all action compulsory.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | God advances creatures according to good choices and grants enjoyment of mercy, while bad choices bring deprivation, pain, and punishment; this harmonizes their wills with His.
[ELMALILI:1:1#7/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rahman is the instinctive grace seen in mother birds beating their wings over their young.
[ELMALILI:1:1#8/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The basmala has an apparent structure of one genitive relation, two adjectival relations, and an initial attachment; its hidden relation makes it a complete, concise statement.
[ELMALILI:1:1#8/r10] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | This fronting makes the beginning exclusive to Allah's name, rejecting the pagan practice of starting with Lat or Uzza and expressing tawhid.
