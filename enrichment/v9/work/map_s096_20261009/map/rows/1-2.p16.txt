[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2#4/r4] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · الطبري | reports | Al-Tabari says God’s praise of Himself implicitly commands people to praise Him; Arabic can omit the understood imperative, as in the cited poem.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2#4/r5] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · ابن أبي عبلة | reports | Ibn Abi ʿAbla reads the dāl and lām with ḍamma for vowel harmony, a common Arabic tendency also illustrated by other readings and poetic examples.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2#5/r1] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · الحسن بن أبي الحسن | reports | Hasan al-Basri and Zayd ibn ʿAli read the dāl with kasra by vowel assimilation.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2#5/r10] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · author | reports | Rabb may derive from nurture: God manages and raises creation; stepdaughters are called rabāʾib because a husband raises them, making Rabb an attribute of action, while owner or master is an attribute of essence. | mentions 4:23
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2#5/r11] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · author | holds | With the definite article, Rabb belongs exclusively to God; without it, people may be called a household’s lord. God alone creates and provides, owns both owners and owned things, and cannot lose His dominion.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2#5/r2] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · author | reports | A poem’s “wayl ummiha” derives from “waylun li-ummiha”: the first lām was dropped and the hamza’s ḍamma shifted after kasra proved heavy.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2#5/r3] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · author | holds | Rabb means owner; lexically it is a divine name, used of others only in a possessive construction, though pre-Islamic Arabs also used it for a king.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2#5/r4] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · author | reports | Rabb can mean master, as in Joseph’s “your lord,” and the hadith that a slave woman gives birth to her mistress. | mentions 12:42
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2#5/r5] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · الهروي | reports | Rabb also means one who repairs, manages, restores, or takes care of something; rabbani scholars are named for tending to scripture.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2#5/r6] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · author | reports | A hadith asks whether someone has a blessing he tends for another, using rabbaha for tending and improving it.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2#5/r7] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · author | reports | Rabb can also mean the one worshipped; the cited poet mocks a fox worshipped after it soils an idol’s head.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2#5/r8] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · author | reports | The verb has intensive forms and can mean raising a child; al-Nahhas records rabbahu, rabbabahu, and rabbatahu as forms.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2#5/r9] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · بعض العلماء | reports | Some consider Rabb God’s greatest name because supplicants frequently call upon Him by it and the name conveys the relation of care, mercy, and dependence.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2/r1] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Abu Hurayra and Abu Saʿid report that when a servant says “Praise belongs to God,” God affirms the servant and says the praise belongs to Him.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2/r2] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Anas reports that God is pleased when a servant praises Him after eating a meal or drinking.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2/r3] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · الحسن | reports | Hasan says that no blessing is better than saying “Praise belongs to God.”
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2/r4] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Anas reports that praising God for a blessing is met with a gift greater than what the servant received.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2/r5] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | A report from Anas says that if someone owned the whole world and then said alhamdulillah, the praise would be better than it.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2/r6] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · أبو عبد الله | reports | Abu ʿAbd Allah explains that the enduring word is better because the world perishes, while praise remains among the righteous deeds; both world and praise ultimately come from God. | mentions 18:46
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2/r7] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · author | reports | A variant says the gift was greater than what was received; this treats praise as the servant’s gift and the world as God’s, though both are from God, who enriches with the world and honors with praise in the afterlife.
[QURTUBI-FULL:1:2/r8] QURTUBI-FULL, d. 671 · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Ibn ʿUmar reports that angels could not record a servant’s praise “as befits” God’s majesty, so God told them to write it as spoken and reward him when they meet.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r1] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | holds | Ḥamd praises chosen goodness, whether a blessing or otherwise; madh praises goodness without qualification, so one praises knowledge and generosity but only commends beauty.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r10] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | holds | If the article is exhaustive, all praise belongs to God because He bestows every good directly or indirectly; this implies He is living, powerful, willing, and knowing. | mentions 16:53
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r11] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | reports | Readings assimilate dāl and lām in opposite directions, treating the letters as one word because they are used together.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r12] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | reports | Rabb originally means tarbiya, bringing something to perfection little by little, then became an intensive description like ṣawm and ʿadl.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r13] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | reports | Another derivation makes rabb a descriptive form from rabba-yarubbuhu; it then means owner because an owner preserves and nurtures what is owned.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r14] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | holds | Rabb is unqualified for God alone, though it can be qualified for others, as in “return to your lord.” | mentions 12:50
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r15] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | holds | ʿĀlam means what makes something known, like a signet or mold; it came to denote all besides God, substances and accidents, whose contingency indicates the necessary Creator.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r16] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | holds | The plural encompasses diverse classes and uses the masculine rational plural because rational beings predominate among them.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r17] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | reports | Another view restricts al-ʿālamīn to rational beings—angels and the human and jinn races—with others included secondarily.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r18] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | reports | A further view takes it here to mean people: each person contains counterparts of the cosmos that reveal the Creator, as in the verse about looking within oneself. | mentions 51:21
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r19] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | reports | The accusative rabb al-ʿālamīn is analyzed as praise, a vocative, or an implied verb indicated by “praise.”
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r2] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | reports | Some consider ḥamd and madh synonymous or close companions.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r20] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | holds | Contingent things need an originator at coming-to-be and a sustainer for as long as they exist.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r3] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | holds | Shukr responds to a blessing through speech, action, and belief; a poem names hand, tongue, and hidden heart as the three responses.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r4] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | holds | Shukr is broader than ḥamd and madh in one respect and narrower in another; praise is a branch of gratitude and better signals blessings than hidden belief or ambiguous bodily acts, so it is gratitude's chief element.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r5] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · النبي | reports | The Prophet is quoted saying praise is the head of gratitude and that whoever does not praise God has not thanked Him.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r6] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | holds | Blame is the opposite of praise, and ingratitude the opposite of shukr.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r7] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | holds | Al-ḥamdu is nominative as subject with “to God” as predicate, though an accusative reading is also reported.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r8] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | holds | The nominative conveys universal and permanent praise belonging to God rather than newly arising praise.
[BAYDAWI:1:2/r9] BAYDAWI, d. 685 · author | holds | Ḥamd is a verbal noun normally governed by an implicit, rarely used verb; its article may denote the genus or point to the familiar idea of praise.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:1:2#2/r1] BAYDAWI-FULL, d. 685 · author | holds | Rabb originally means gradual nurturing toward completion, then became an intensive description; another derivation treats it as a noun for the owner who preserves and develops what he owns.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:1:2#2/r2] BAYDAWI-FULL, d. 685 · author | holds | Rabb is used of others only in a qualified phrase, whereas al-ʿālam names what makes something known and came to mean everything besides God.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:1:2#2/r3] BAYDAWI-FULL, d. 685 · author | holds | The worlds comprise all substances and accidents, whose possibility and need for a necessary cause show God's existence; the plural gathers different genera, with rational beings predominant in its form.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:1:2#2/r4] BAYDAWI-FULL, d. 685 · author | reports | An alternative restricts the term to knowledgeable beings—angels, humans, and jinn—with other creatures included only secondarily.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:1:2#2/r5] BAYDAWI-FULL, d. 685 · author | reports | Another view means human beings here: each person contains counterparts of the greater world's substances and accidents, through which the Creator is known, as 51:21 invites people to observe themselves. | mentions 51:21
[BAYDAWI-FULL:1:2#2/r6] BAYDAWI-FULL, d. 685 · author | reports | An accusative reading treats رب العالمين as praise, direct address, or an implied action indicated by الحمد.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:1:2#2/r7] BAYDAWI-FULL, d. 685 · author | holds | Possible things need God not only to originate them but also to keep them in existence.
[BAYDAWI-FULL:1:2/r1] BAYDAWI-FULL, d. 685 · author | holds | Ḥamd is praise for chosen excellence, including a blessing, while madḥ is praise for any excellence; one praises knowledge but merely commends beauty.
