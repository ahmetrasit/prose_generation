20:53/q14 Does water itself have a causal role in plant growth? [بِهِۦٓ · theology] (turns on: Whether the preposition gives water divinely granted causal efficacy or only customary accompaniment.)
  20:53/q14/p1 God grants water properties by which growth follows, while God remains the true effective cause. — reasons: Razi says this does not violate Islamic principles; Alusi reports Maturidis and earlier authorities that properties work only with God's permission; Tabatabai takes 'by it' as affirming causal links.
  20:53/q14/p2 Water has no inherent causal effect; God creates growth when water is present. — reasons: Razi reports earlier theologians' denial of water's effect; Alusi describes the Ashʿari reading of 'by it' as 'at it,' comparing water's quenching and fire's burning, while calling the charge against causal powers extreme.
20:53/q15 What does أَزْوَٰجًۭ mean here? [أَزْوَٰجًۭ · meaning] (turns on: Whether 'pairs' denotes associated species or actual sexual pairs.)
  20:53/q15/p1 It means kinds or varieties of vegetation. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and Mujahid give varieties; paired means associated or mutually resembling kinds, such as palms and grapes. Farra and al-Aʿsha's brocade verse support a sense of color; Asad cites 13:3.
  20:53/q15/p2 It may mean actual male and female plant pairs. — reasons: Tabatabai proposes this alternative as a reality drawn to attention by the Qur'an; Bursevi includes sexual and other paired relations within the word's broad sense.
20:53/q16 What range does نَبَاتٍ have? [نَبَاتٍۢ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the note concerns the semantic extent of vegetation or the collective morphology of the noun.)
  20:53/q16/p1 It denotes vegetation broadly, including trees and stemless plants; ordinary usage may narrow it to fodder. — reasons: Al-Raghib defines every growing, feeding body as vegetation; Bursevi says the word can cover all growing things, including animals and humans in its widest sense.
  20:53/q16/p2 It is a verbal or collective noun used for plants in singular and plural. — reasons: Razi and Nasafi say its form serves singular and plural; Ibn al-Anbari calls it collective without an ordinary singular, and Abu al-Baqa permits agreement with plural shattā.
20:53/q17 What does شَتَّىٰ mean? [شَتَّىٰ · meaning]
  20:53/q17/p1 It means diverse, separated or scattered. — reasons: Wahidi and Qurtubi derive it from shatta and list related forms; Ruʾba's camel verse illustrates scattering. Samīn and Ibn Ashur call it plural of shatīt; Bint al-Shati distinguishes shattā's difference from ashtāt's dispersion, citing 59:14, 99:6 and 24:61.
20:53/q18 What does شَتَّىٰ modify, and how is it parsed? [أَزْوَٰجًۭ نَبَاتٍۢ شَتَّىٰ · grammar] (turns on: Whether shattā describes the kinds, collective vegetation, or functions adverbially as a circumstantial accusative.)
  20:53/q18/p1 It is an adjective describing azwāj, the diverse kinds. — reasons: Akhfash allows the reading; Abu Hayyan and Samīn prefer it, the latter for direct noun-adjective relation. Tabrisi makes min nabāt and shattā successive adjectives; Ibn al-Anbari allows 'varied varieties of plants.'
  20:53/q18/p2 It describes nabāt, the vegetation collectively. — reasons: Akhfash accepts this; al-Zamakhshari and Abu al-Baqa use nabāt as a verbal noun or collective whose form permits the plural description. Bursevi prefers this for the verse ending's rhyme.
  20:53/q18/p3 It is a circumstantial accusative linked to azwāj or to the preceding adjective's subject pronoun. — reasons: Samīn says the indefinite azwāj is specified by min nabāt, licensing the first ḥāl; he gives a third parse attaching it to the raised subject pronoun. Tabrisi also allows an accusative verbal noun.
20:53/q19 How do the plants differ, and whom do they serve? [أَزْوَٰجًۭ نَبَاتٍۢ شَتَّىٰ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the emphasis falls on diverse kinds and uses or on their paired counterparts.)
  20:53/q19/p1 They vary in kinds, colors, tastes, scents, shapes, benefits and uses for people and animals. — reasons: Al-Kalbi lists white, red, green and yellow; Farra adds tastes; Ibn Kathir mentions crops, fruits, sour and sweet, fresh and dry fodder. Kashshaf, Nasafi and Alusi say livestock eat vegetation people cannot. Biqai adds size and nature.
  20:53/q19/p2 Each plant kind or color has a pair or counterpart. — reasons: Yahya, Thaʿlabi and Baghawi say each kind is paired; Tabrisi calls each color a pair; Biqai says none is wholly without a counterpart.
20:53/q20 What proof or teaching do earth, paths, rain and plants provide? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether the signs are read chiefly as proof of monotheistic lordship or as instances of universal guidance.)
  20:53/q20/p1 They display God's exclusive lordship, unity, wise provision and power over creation. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says these concrete acts could not be claimed by Pharaoh; Razi calls them specific proofs after a general one. Biqai argues varied ordered terrain cannot arise from a uniform natural cause, and links complete provision to God's lack of error or forgetfulness. Islahi addresses Quraysh's worship and judgment.
  20:53/q20/p2 The provision and earth's pathways guide creatures to sustain life and humans toward God. — reasons: Tabatabai links bodily food and grazing with universal guidance and lordship, while human earthly life prepares for the heavenly destination.
20:53/q21 How does this verse connect to its narrative and other passages? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the sentences continue Moses' proof or interrupt the story as a direct divine address.)
  20:53/q21/p1 It resumes or extends the Moses–Pharaoh exchange with concrete proofs of the Lord. — reasons: Razi says Pharaoh diverted Moses with old generations, and Moses resumed proof; Ibn Kathir says the earth and water description completes Moses' answer after an intervening clause. Al-Tabari recalls grace in rain after the answer and cites 2:22.
  20:53/q21/p2 It is a Quranic insertion that renews attention and addresses the present audience. — reasons: Ibn Ashur describes three sentences interrupting the story; Islahi says four verses directly address Quraysh; al-Jurjani explains that an unjoined 'he said' can answer a hearer's implicit next question, comparing 51:24–28 and 26:23–29.
  20:53/q21/p3 Its signs continue the preceding discussion of God's universal guidance and recur in other creation and rain passages. — reasons: Tabatabai links the earlier exchange to guidance; Study Quran compares 43:10–11, 6:99 and 13:4. Ibn Ashur says earth leads to vegetation and indirectly recalls heaven, citing 80:25–26; Burhan contrasts 'salaka' here with 'jaʿala' at 43:10.
20:53/q22 What inward sense is given to the earth, paths, water and plants? [* · inward]
  20:53/q22/p1 They signify the bodily faculties, their organs, spiritual perception and their varied states. — reasons: Qashani treats earth as bodily realm, paths as eye, ear, nose and other organs, heaven as spirit, rain as perception and aid, and plants as perceptions, actions, properties, states and dispositions.
20:53/q23 What attribution is given for the poem challenging Pharaoh with creation signs? [* · reports] (turns on: The reported poet's identity.)
  20:53/q23/p1 Ibn Ishaq attributes it to Zayd ibn Amr ibn Nufayl. — reasons: Biqai reports Ibn Kathir's citation; the poem challenges Pharaoh to claim he made earth without supports, raised sky without pillars, made light and sun, and grew crops.
  20:53/q23/p2 Ibn Hisham attributes it to Umayya ibn Abi al-Salt. — reasons: Biqai notes the competing attribution of the cited poem.
20:53/q24 What background is reported about Abu Amr, a reader of mihādan? [مَهْدًۭا · reports] (turns on: The varying biographical transmissions.)
  20:53/q24/p1 His personal name and lineage are reported in several forms. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid transmits Ibn Ammar ibn al-Aryan, Zabban, al-Aryan, al-Aryan ibn al-Ala, and the claim he had only a kunya. Lineage reaches Mazin with clientage among Banu al-Anbar; his brother Abu Sufyan also lacked another name.
  20:53/q24/p2 He was portrayed as an early, leading transmitted reader and Arabist. — reasons: Abu Amr says he led while al-Hasan al-Basri lived, with a report placing the latter's death at 110 AH. Saʿid ibn Jubayr urged him to sit with elders; Ibn Mujahid describes modesty and Hijazi reading practice.
20:53/q25 What methodological caution about explaining the Qur'an accompanies these notes? [* · reports]
  20:53/q25/p1 Explanation by unsupported personal opinion is blameworthy even if it happens to reach the right answer. — reasons: Ibn Kathir cites Jundub's prophetic warning, with Tirmidhi calling the report gharīb and criticism of Suhayl; a version from Tirmidhi, Abu Dawud and Nasa'i says an accidental right answer is still error. He compares 24:13 and cites Abu Bakr's caution.
20:53/q26 What codex and writing background accompanies the reading notes? [* · sciences] (turns on: The reported history and differing scholarly rules for codex presentation.)
  20:53/q26/p1 Accounts differ on the spread of writing into Mecca and Arabia. — reasons: Hisham al-Kalbi traces it from al-Anbar through Bishr, Harb and Sufyan, with Umar and Muawiya learning from them; another account begins with Tayy people from Baqqa, while al-Shaʿbi traces Muhajirun through Hira to al-Anbar.
  20:53/q26/p2 Early script and codex spelling developed through scribal practice. — reasons: Ibn Kathir calls al-maktufa the early script, credits Ibn Muqla and Ibn al-Bawwab with refinement, and says early codex word placement differences were orthographic rather than semantic; Abu Ubayd and Ibn Abi Dawud catalogued orthography.
