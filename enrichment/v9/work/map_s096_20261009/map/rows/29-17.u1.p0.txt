## THE CURRENT MAP
29:17/q01 What are the awthān worshipped here? [أَوْثَٰنًۭا · meaning] (turns on: Whether the terms wathan and ṣanam are synonyms or distinguished by material, bodily form, or the breadth of objects worshipped.)
  29:17/q01/p1 They are idols, images or statues worshipped besides God. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba calls wathan a stone or plaster idol; al-Ṭabarī calls them likenesses, Qatāda statues, and Bursevī manufactured images. Al-Jawharī equates wathan and ṣanam; Biqāʿī reports lexicographers who agree. Qatāda calls them an idol or stone. Abū Ḥātim notes Christian images of Christ and Mary as objects of prostration.
  29:17/q01/p2 A wathan is specifically a carving of wood or stone, unlike a metal ṣanam. — reasons: Abū ʿUbayda contrasts wooden or stone awthān with gold, silver or copper aṣnām; Qurtubī transmits a variant assigning plaster or stone to wathan.
  29:17/q01/p3 A wathan is a bodily object set up for worship, unlike a bodiless ṣanam image. — reasons: Biqāʿī reports Abū Manṣūr through al-Qazzāz. Ibn ʿArafa instead includes any plaster, stone or similar image; Abū Ḥātim calls a wall image wathan but a stone statue ṣanam, showing the distinction varies.
  29:17/q01/p4 A wathan is a nonhuman stone image, unlike a human shaped ṣanam of wood or metal. — reasons: Bursevī reports this distinction from some authorities.
  29:17/q01/p5 Awthān covers any worshipped object, more broadly than aṣnām. — reasons: Elmalılı includes stone images, fetishes, standing stones and crosses.
29:17/q02 What does the root of awthān evoke in this rebuke? [أَوْثَٰنًۭا · meaning]
  29:17/q02/p1 Its associated senses of increase, dispersal and weakness expose idols as unfit for divinity. — reasons: Biqāʿī traces permutations of وثن through abundance, scattered reports, weakness and fixed immobility, and takes the plural as scattered objects dividing attention.
29:17/q03 How does إنما govern the clause ending in أوثانا? [إِنَّمَا أَوْثَٰنًۭا · grammar] (turns on: Whether mā merely blocks inna or is its relative noun; the actual accusative awthānan bears on that choice.)
  29:17/q03/p1 Mā blocks inna, making innamā one restrictive particle; awthānan is the object of taʿbudūna. — reasons: Naḥḥās says inna cannot govern a verb and that mā still blocks it before a noun. ʿAlī ibn ʿĪsā objects that a relative mā would require nominative awthān. Al-Farrāʾ treats innamā as one particle. Jishumī transmits the restrictive analysis.
  29:17/q03/p2 Mā can be relative, yielding nominative awthān as inna's predicate in a hypothetical construction. — reasons: Some grammarians allow the relative analysis before a noun. Naḥḥās and Qurtubī allow it outside the Qurʾānic reading, with taʿbudūna the relative clause and an omitted return pronoun; Jishumī also reports the analysis. ʿAlī ibn ʿĪsā rejects applying it to the actual accusative reading.
29:17/q04 What force does calling their objects only awthān have? [إِنَّمَا تَعْبُدُونَ أَوْثَٰنًۭا · rhetoric]
  29:17/q04/p1 It strips their objects of divine standing and confines their worship to mere manufactured idols. — reasons: Biqāʿī says restriction reverses the claim to divinity, while plurality signals division and weakness; Ālūsī stresses that statues have no quality beyond manufacture. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the indefinite plural belittles empty claims and sets true worship with God. Biqāʿī contrasts God, without equal or minister, with the idols.
29:17/q05 What does khalq or تخلقون mean in Arabic usage? [وَتَخْلُقُونَ · meaning] (turns on: The root ranges from measuring and shaping to fabrication and divine origination; context determines its force here.)
  29:17/q05/p1 Here it can mean inventing or fabricating a lie, especially in speech. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba compares 26:137 and 38:7 and links ikhtilāq to invention. Abū ʿUbayda groups khalaqa, ikhtalaqa, kharaqa, ikhtaraqa and iftarā. Ibn Jawzī cites exegetes and 26:137. Al-Rāghib, reported by Bursevī and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ, distinguishes human fabrication from God's origination; al-Qāḍī Abū Bakr invokes God's sole creation at 6:102 and 39:62 to read this as lying.
  29:17/q05/p2 It can mean forming, shaping or fashioning an object. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba cites the clay bird in 5:110; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ traces the sense to measuring and shaping. Al-Rāghib permits carving by hand. The Itqān glosses the verse's verb as making or fashioning.
  29:17/q05/p3 Its underlying sense is measuring or determining. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba illustrates the leather cutter's measured pattern with Zuhayr's verse; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ also begins from measuring or shaping.
  29:17/q05/p4 It can mean originating creation, a sense assigned to God. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba cites 7:189; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says divine usage is origination without precedent; al-Rāghib reserves this sense to God.
  29:17/q05/p5 Khalq can mean religion in other verses. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba reads God's khalq in 30:30 as religion, and reports that changing it in 4:119 can mean changing religion or bodily alterations such as castration and slitting ears.
29:17/q06 How is وتخلقون read? [وَتَخْلُقُونَ · readings] (turns on: Vocalization, gemination and an added or elided tāʾ distinguish the transmitted verb forms.)
  29:17/q06/p1 The common reading is the simple form تخلقون, meaning make or fabricate. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī accepts the regional readers' light khāʾ and ḍamma on lām because the authoritative readers agree; Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it the majority reading. Al-Samīn describes it as from خلق. Al-Zajjāj and Qurtubī record a form spelled like the verse.
  29:17/q06/p2 A doubled-lām form from تخلّق or تتخلّق is read, conveying emphatic invention or lying. — reasons: Al-Sulamī, Zayd, ʿAlī, Qatāda, ʿAwn, Ibn Abī Laylā, ʿUbāda and Ibn al-Zubayr appear in varying transmitter lists. Ibn Mujāhid says one tāʾ drops from تتخلقون; Ibn Jinnī compares تكذبون. Some allow the basic فعل sense. The reports describe the initial tāʾ as open.
  29:17/q06/p3 An intensive doubled-lām form from خلق is read, suggesting repeated or abundant making or lying. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī and Ālūsī give the intensive implication; al-Nasafī names Abū Ḥudhayfa and al-Sulamī, and Ālūsī reports Zayd ibn ʿAlī, with lying and slander as its sense.
  29:17/q06/p4 The verb is read تختلقون with an added tāʾ. — reasons: Ibn al-Sumayfiʿ and Abū al-Mutawakkil are named by Ibn al-Jawzī.
  29:17/q06/p5 A doubled-lām reading also has ḍamma on the initial tāʾ. — reasons: Al-Samīn reports this separately from Zayd ibn ʿAlī.
29:17/q07 What does إفكا mean here? [إِفْكًا · meaning]
  29:17/q07/p1 It means a lie, falsehood or perversion of truth. — reasons: Jishumī calls it a lie; Bursevī calls it the worst kind of lie. Biqāʿī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī trace it to being turned from proper truth, for a statement or deed. Mujāhid gives the same basic sense; Asad renders the phrase literally as creating a lie.
29:17/q08 What are they doing when they 'create falsehood'? [وَتَخْلُقُونَ إِفْكًا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the falsehood is primarily their spoken divine claims, the manufactured idols themselves, or a sarcastic characterization; some notes combine carving with the claim.)
  29:17/q08/p1 They invent lies by calling idols gods, partners or intercessors. — reasons: Al-Suddī specifies spoken claims, while reports from Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid say uttering or making lies; Mujāhid also extends lies beyond idols. Muqātil joins hand manufacture to the claim of divinity, citing 37:96. Al-Kashshāf, al-Bayḍāwī, Bursevī and others mention partners or intercession. Ibn Kathīr reports invented divine names; Elmalılı compares a portrait to a living person and calls worship of an imagined image slander. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the true God is one.
  29:17/q08/p2 They carve or manufacture idols, which are themselves called falsehood. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan, Qatāda, Ibn Zayd and Ibn ʿAbbās are variously reported; Wahidī also gives Mujāhid and al-Kalbī. The things are handmade yet falsely called gods; Ibn ʿAbbās explains the name falsehood by this false claim. Yaḥyā cites 37:95 and Muqātil 37:96. Biqāʿī notes that the present tense evokes continual remaking and the inversion of created artifact into creator. Al-Bayḍāwī and Ālūsī describe making them for falsehood, with Ālūsī's lām of outcome rather than intention. Al-Ṭabarī prefers speech fabrication over merely carving; Ibn Kathīr's report says Ibn Jarīr chooses carving, an attribution that differs from the extant Tabari note.
  29:17/q08/p3 The phrase is a sarcastic description of their supposed creating. — reasons: Ālūsī reports this as a further possibility without specifying its target beyond the phrase.
29:17/q09 How is إفكا analyzed grammatically and read? [إِفْكًا · readings] (turns on: Whether the form is a verbal noun, an adjective, or a shortened participle, and whether the initial hamza is read with fatḥa.)
  29:17/q09/p1 The written ifkan is a verbal noun meaning lying or a false act. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf compares kadhib and laʿib. Ibn Jinnī allows a verbal noun and al-Nasafī treats ifk as a shortened form of afk; al-Samīn says the verbal noun analysis may require an omitted noun.
  29:17/q09/p2 The written ifkan is an adjective describing a false creation. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf, al-Bayḍāwī and al-Samīn report this analysis; al-Samīn says an omitted noun is unnecessary for the adjective, opposing the proposed ellipsis. Ibn Jinnī allows an adjective replacing an omitted noun.
  29:17/q09/p3 Afkan with open hamza is read as a verbal noun or adjective. — reasons: Fuḍayl and Ibn al-Zubayr are named; Ibn Jinnī also permits a shortened active participle of āfik. Qurtubī and Nasafī give its relation to lighter ifk. Ālūsī specifies fatḥa on hamza and kasra on fā. ʿĀṣim is reported to read its letters lightly in a connected recitation.
