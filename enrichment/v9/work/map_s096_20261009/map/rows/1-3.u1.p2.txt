  1:3/q19/p3 The order of lordship, mercy, and judgment is the best rational or rhetorical sequence. — reasons: Rāzī calls it unsurpassable. Al-Taftāzānī maps first creation to rabb, preservation to mercy, second creation to judgment, and afterlife preservation to subsequent worship. Al-Ālūsī lists inherent perfection, past benefit, future kindness and feared power; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the names legislative mercy; Sāmarrāʾī ties the guidance plea to lordship, mercy and reliance.

1:3/q20 Why do the mercy names recur after the basmala? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the basmala belongs to the sūra, or each occurrence has a distinct rhetorical task.)
  1:3/q20/p1 The basmala and this verse serve distinct functions, so recurrence is purposeful. — reasons: ʿAlī b. ʿĪsā and al-Rummānī say the first ties servitude to blessings and the second praise to blessings. Al-Ālūsī says the basmala explains beginning in God's name while this verse explains His right to praise. Al-Bayḍāwī calls it causal explanation; Abū Ḥayyān says, if the basmala is in the sūra, attributes gain reinforcement.
  1:3/q20/p2 The recurrence deliberately emphasizes the importance and breadth of mercy. — reasons: Jishumī lists emphasis as one explanation; al-Rāzī says Qur'anic repetition is common and mercy deserves special stress before judgment (40:3). Study Quran says the names intensify mercy, while Biqāʿī invokes al-Ghazālī's principle that no Qur'anic repetition is redundant.
  1:3/q20/p3 The apparent repetition indicates the basmala is outside the Fātiḥa. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says adjacent complete verses would repeat without real separation and cites Jarīr and 18:1-2 for fronted semantic order; Makkī and al-Nasafī say al-ḥamdu does not truly separate them. Ibn ʿĀshūr rejects appeals to mere emphasis and al-Bayḍāwī's praise explanation.
  1:3/q20/p4 The two names were already explained under the basmala. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr and Kur'an Yolu refer readers to earlier commentary; Abū Ḥayyān calls them praise epithets following the unique name.
  1:3/q20/p5 There is no repeated basmala in recitations that omit it; the names introduce a title. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum notes rites without a basmala, so this verse emphatically introduces the mercy title.
  1:3/q20/p6 The names' first occurrence seeks aid; their second assigns praise. — reasons: Jishumī reports this distinction alongside the outside-sūra and emphasis explanations.
1:3/q21 How does the verse's order relate to nearby praise and sovereignty? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · links] (turns on: Whether to retain the recited order or move mercy beside praise and kingship beside lordship.)
  1:3/q21/p1 The recited order builds from lordship through mercy to judgment and worship. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān rejects Makkī's reordered analysis, arguing that lordship and mercy precede dominion and worship. Rāzī calls the order rationally optimal; Biqāʿī says mercy completes lordship; al-Ālūsī says descriptions explain prior praise and prepare prayer.
  1:3/q21/p2 Mercy belongs semantically beside praise and kingship beside lordship. — reasons: An exegetical group cited by al-Ṭabarī and Makkī in Abū Ḥayyān place mālik next to rabb and mercy next to al-ḥamdu; al-Ṭabarī says Arabic permits such fronting and cites Jarīr and 18:1-2.
1:3/q22 What response does God give when this verse is recited in prayer? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · reports]
  1:3/q22/p1 God calls the recitation an act of extolling or magnifying Him. — reasons: Abū Hurayra's prophetic and non-prophetic versions, Jābir's divine saying, and reports through Ibn Abī Ḥātim, al-Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī, al-Rāzī, al-Biqāʿī, al-Durr, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, Abū Dāwūd and Study Quran vary in whether the basmala glorifies, whether Mālik glorifies or entrusts, and how the rest is divided. Baghawī grades his version sound; al-Biqāʿī cites Muslim and the four Sunan. The central answer is praise for al-ḥamdu, extolling for the mercy names, and a granted petition.
1:3/q23 What do these names imply about God's relation to creatures? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · interpretation]
  1:3/q23/p1 God's bond with creatures is comprehensive mercy and care, eliciting praise, trust and affection. — reasons: Quṭb contrasts Greek and Babel portrayals of capricious gods and calls mercy a foundational worldview principle. Rāzī speaks of complete generosity before and after death; Sāmarrāʾī reads the names as total mercy in both worlds; al-Bayḍāwī says invocation seeks assistance and mercy for righteous action.
1:3/q24 What devotional uses of these words are reported? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · reports] (turns on: Hope in frequent recitation versus supplication for rain.)
  1:3/q24/p1 Their frequent recitation gives hope of divine rescue. — reasons: Rāzī compares Mary's rescue from slander through one mercy (19:21) with worshippers reciting the names 34 times daily.
  1:3/q24/p2 They were used in a drought prayer for sustaining rain. — reasons: A report from ʿĀʾisha names God Lord of the worlds, Merciful and Master of Judgment; Abū Dāwūd calls it rare with a good chain and says it supports the Medinan no-alif reading.
1:3/q25 What commentary do the divine names require? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · interpretation]
  1:3/q25/p1 They call for explanation of meaning, majesty, divine attributes, and placement. — reasons: Ibn Abī Jamra says a commentary must explain the two names, all divine names and attributes, and why these occur here.
1:3/q26 Is the basmala counted as a Fātiḥa verse? [* · sciences] (turns on: How recitation, verse division, and repetition bear on the first verse.)
  1:3/q26/p1 Yes, it is counted as verse one. — reasons: Umm Salama and a separate attribution to ʿĀʾisha count the basmala first, the mercy names third, and Mālik fourth. Ibn ʿĀshūr assigns this division to Mecca and Kufa; al-Ḥasan separately counts both debated segments.
  1:3/q26/p2 No, it is outside the Fātiḥa. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī and al-Nasafī infer this from close repetition. Ibn ʿĀshūr gives Medina's count, which instead separates 'those You favored,' and notes the opening revelation report's omission of the formula (96:1).
1:3/q27 What does the Fātiḥa's prayer dialogue teach about worship? [* · interpretation]
  1:3/q27/p1 The opening is God's share of praise; worship and aid mediate; petitions are the servant's granted share. — reasons: Al-Harālī says God teaches creatures praise they could not formulate and accepts it. Rāzī says the two halves join knowledge of lordship with servitude to fulfill the covenant, citing 17:7, 51:56, 76:2 and 2:40. The reports give variant distributions of basmala and Mālik.
1:3/q28 Where and when was the Fātiḥa revealed? [* · sciences] (turns on: Meccan majority and evidence versus the Medinan attribution and twice-revealed explanation.)
  1:3/q28/p1 It was revealed early in Mecca. — reasons: Most authorities, al-Wāḥidī, Quṭb and Study Quran say so. Abū Maysara tells of Waraqa counseling the fleeing Prophet to hear a caller, who commanded testimony and recitation; ʿAlī calls it a treasure beneath the Throne; Ibn ʿAbbās reports Quraysh's insult. Al-Wāḥidī cites Meccan 15:87 and the implausibility of years of prayer without it.
  1:3/q28/p2 It was revealed in Medina. — reasons: Mujāhid alone is cited; al-Ḥusayn b. al-Faḍl calls this an isolated lapse, and Study Quran reports the majority's prayer-based objection.
  1:3/q28/p3 It was revealed twice, in Mecca and Medina. — reasons: Bursevī records this as one explanation of the title 'oft repeated.'
1:3/q29 Why is the Fātiḥa called the Opening or Mother of the Book? [* · sciences] (turns on: Whether the titles refer to placement, prayer, or a synopsis of the Qur'an.)
  1:3/q29/p1 Mother names its gathering of the Qur'an's major purposes. — reasons: Rāzī lists theology, resurrection, prophecy, decree; praise, service and requests; lordship and servitude; theology, duties and purification. Bursevī names divinity, prophecy and decree. Ibn ʿĀshūr lists praise, commands and prohibitions, promise and warning, and also theoretical and practical wisdom. Study Quran connects the title to 3:7 and the celestial archetype.
  1:3/q29/p2 Opening names its place at the start of codex, recitation, teaching, prayer, or speech. — reasons: Rāzī adds first descent as a proposal; Bursevī adds worldly aims and Paradise's gates. Ibn ʿĀshūr debates whether the title is descriptive annexation or true annexation to the rest of the Book; Study Quran includes opening the heart to faith.
  1:3/q29/p3 Other titles name its praise, prayer, cure, sufficiency, thanks and supplication. — reasons: Rāzī explains Sūrat al-Ḥamd by its first praise word. Biqāʿī links many titles to watchfulness (murāqaba); Bursevī lists prayer, healing, cure, foundation, sufficient, complete, praise, supplication, thanks, invocation, and treasure beneath the Throne. Study Quran links cure to body and soul and foundation to the Qur'an.
