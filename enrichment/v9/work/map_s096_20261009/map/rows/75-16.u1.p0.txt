## THE CURRENT MAP
75:16/q01 What does moving the tongue mean here? [تُحَرِّكْ لِسَانَكَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the phrase denotes the organ's motion or the recitation accomplished with it.)
  75:16/q01/p1 It is the physical motion of the speech organ in reciting revelation. — reasons: Al-Jishumī and al-Ṭabrisī define taḥrīk as moving something between directions; al-Jishumī places motion and rest among spatial states. Al-Ṭabrisī calls the tongue the instrument of speech. Ibn al-Jawzī gives lisān as the organ, lasin as skilled speech, and lasan as eloquence; he also reports the simple sense 'tongue itself'.
  75:16/q01/p2 Moving the tongue stands for reciting or rehearsing the words. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī explicitly glosses tongue movement as recitation to preserve the revelation; al-Ṭabrisī gives the same metonymy.
75:16/q02 Which broader facts about the articulation and form of speech are cited? [لِسَانَكَ · grammar] (turns on: Which articulatory or morphological environment governs the cited speech forms.)
  75:16/q02/p1 Identical consonants may assimilate, conceal, or remain distinct according to movement, word boundaries, and word structure. — reasons: Sībawayh favors assimilation when at least five moving segments would follow, citing poetic meter; clear Hijazi articulation remains good. More movements strengthen the preference, and a preceding long vowel counts as movement, as his rhyme example with yāʾ shows. A preceding matching moving consonant and following quiescent consonant also favor assimilation. Across words, distinct articulation remains possible; a quiescent predecessor bars silencing, though concealment with moving weight is possible. Ibn Nūḥ and ism Mūsā preserve word form, unlike intra-word رد and قل. Qurādd/lahāmm cannot be silenced; poems of an unnamed poet and Ghaylān b. Ḥurayth witness concealment. Meccans reportedly do not fully pronounce both tāʾs in 58:9; thawb Bakr favors clarity. Ṣuwaym permits concealment or silence. Final long wāw/yāʾ remain unassimilated before matching initials; speakers can conceal or articulate but cannot silence without losing length, as rhyme confirms. Imperative ikhshi/ikhshū can assimilate because their glides are not long vowels. Two hamzas are normally distinct, although Ibn Abī Isḥāq's assimilation is called poor but permissible. Iftaʿala tāʾs can be shown or concealed, unlike fixed iḥmarra; some Arabs quiesce the first or move the predecessor. Al-Ḥasan's 37:10 reading and Meccan murdifīn reported by al-Khalīl and Hārūn attest alternatives; muqtalīn is rarer and radif dialectal. Initial alif is retained in ألْحمر as hamzat qaṭʿ-like. Assimilation in iqtatalū is acceptable like jaʿal laka. اردد permits no concealment between two still letters.
  75:16/q02/p2 Hamza at pause permits several realizations determined by its preceding sound and case. — reasons: Sībawayh records hinted or applied case vowels. Tamīm and Asad often move the predecessor to clarify faint hamza; some Tamīm avoid a faʿl sequence in al-radiʾ. Other Arabs replace final hamza by wāw, yāʾ, or alif by case, as in al-kalʾ/al-wathʾ. The choice should remain consistent. Once the predecessor moves, ordinary pause rules apply; final hamza is not doubled. Hijazi lightening after fatḥa yields alif without ishām; after ḍamma/kasra yields wāw/yāʾ. After a still consonant, softened hamza is deleted and its vowel transferred.
  75:16/q02/p3 Weak letters change form according to position, vowel, suffix, and pattern. — reasons: Sībawayh cites taʿayyaṭat and rajaz ʿawṭaṭ for quadriliteral yāʾ becoming wāw. Final radicals are weakest because endings bear inflection and suffixes. Wāw-final imperfects use yafʿulu, yāʾ-final yafʿilu; faʿula occurs with wāw, not yāʾ. Final wāw after ḍamma and yāʾ after kasra resist heavy endings but accept fatḥa. After fatḥa they become alif; before consonantal suffixes they retain their original identity. Declensional final wāw after ḍamma shifts to yāʾ/kasra; consonant-preceded glides behave like sound radicals. Some plurals prefer yāʾ, while wāw after kasra always becomes yāʾ, with ʿAbd Yaghūth's verse cited. An added alif hamzates a final weak radical. Dialectal ghazi/shaqi retain yāʾ; raḍiyū/sarūw depend on vowel retention and two-sukūn avoidance. Lightened hamzated jīʾ/jūyi turns to jī. Deleted weak radicals remain deleted in singular jussives; dual long-vowel markers remain while jussive nūn drops.
  75:16/q02/p4 Weak-medial noun plurals vary by vowel, number, gender, and analogy. — reasons: Sībawayh gives afʿāl for small counts of wāw-medial faʿl to avoid heavy afʿul, and fiʿāl/fiʿlān for larger counts; qaws/aqwus and rajaz athwāb preserve rarer afʿul. Yāʾ-medial faʿl likewise takes afʿāl in small counts, often fuʿūl in larger ones. Hollow qāʿ/tāj/jār favor afʿāl and fiʿlān. Yūnus reports feminine dār/sāq/nār with afʿul, but Sībawayh doubts gender alone explains it; dār/sāq also take fuʿul, dīrān, diyār. Nīb avoids yāʾ plus ḍamma. Fīl nouns use afʿāl/fuʿūl, sometimes -ah. Arwāḥ/riyāḥ preserve wāw class patterns. Feminine weak-medial nouns can take -āt or sound-like larger plurals, while nūbah/jawbah/dawlah/qaryah take broken fuʿul. Ḍayʿah/khaymah have nonregular fuʿul by analogy. Qīmah/rībah/dīmah permit sound and broken patterns; nāqah-type favors fiʿāl with fuʿul also; ʾaynūq/qiyam and the poetic tārāt/tīr are exceptional.
  75:16/q02/p5 First-person suffix and pausal pronunciation depend on host structure and articulatory pressure. — reasons: Sībawayh distinguishes verb-object -ni from genitive -ī; frequent particles may drop nūn, especially laʿallī, whereas Zayd al-Khayl's poetic laytī is exceptional. ʿAnnī/qadnī/qaṭnī/minnī/ladunnī preserve the nūn-yāʾ marker; ilā/ladā/ʿalā cannot add nūn because their endings cannot move; qaṭ/ʿan/min are fixed like verbs. Some Arabs move a prefinal consonant to avoid adjacent still letters at pause; a Saʿdī poet's al-naqri witnesses this. Vowel harmony or pattern avoidance explains ʿudl/fisl, al-busr, and al-ʿukm; long vowels in Zayd/ʿawn differ. Qalqala qāf/jīm/ṭāʾ/dāl/bāʾ release audibly, sometimes strongly; zāy/ẓāʾ/dhāl/ḍād release softer breath, and other voiceless sounds breathe. Lām/nūn/mīm/ʿayn/ghayn/hamza lack this release. Connected speech suppresses pausal releases, illustrated by ayqiẓ ʿumayran and aḥriz mālan.
75:16/q03 Who is addressed by the prohibition? [لِسَانَكَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the scene is Gabriel delivering revelation, the resurrection record, an individual's inward knowledge, or inquiry about Judgment.)
  75:16/q03/p1 The Prophet Muhammad is addressed while receiving revelation. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī and many commentators connect the command to Gabriel's delivery. Abū Ḥayyān calls this the apparent account supported by the sound occasion report. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says it is etiquette during revelation.
  75:16/q03/p2 A person confronted with a record of deeds at resurrection is addressed. — reasons: Al-Qaffāl ties this to 75:13–15 and 17:14: God gathers and reads deeds, then commands confession and explains punishment. Al-Balkhī appeals to the surrounding judgment passage. Al-Jishumī says this fits what a person sent ahead better, though he says the worldly address is more apparent. Al-Suyūṭī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reject the account because of the sound revelation report and 20:114 parallel.
  75:16/q03/p3 Humanity generally is addressed about its inward knowledge or conscience. — reasons: Elmalılı reports this possible universal reading, based on 75:14–15 and the implied inner knowledge; he prefers the specific prophetic address because Qurʾān is later named.
  75:16/q03/p4 The addressee is a person asking prematurely about Judgment Day. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports this proposal: God will gather those destined for Judgment, reveal its description, and clarify it with the trumpet blast; he rejects it.
  75:16/q03/p5 The instruction extends to every believer studying the Qurʾān. — reasons: Asad extends it to reading, listening, and studying; Study Quran says students should reflect before drawing conclusions.
75:16/q04 What does the pronoun “it” refer to? [بِهِۦ بِهِۦٓ · referent] (turns on: The unstated antecedent, identified from the revelation occasion or the surrounding resurrection passage.)
  75:16/q04/p1 The Qurʾān or revelation, though its antecedent is implicit. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī compares the unintroduced pronoun with 97:1; Ibn ʿAṭiyya compares 38:32. Al-Rāzī allows revelation, its descent, or Qurʾān. Elmalılı prefers this because Qurʾān is named later. Al-Ālūsī likewise cites 97:1. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says concision suits the sūra's haste motif. Asad specifies the contents of revelation; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports a narrower reading of what had already been recited.
  75:16/q04/p2 The person's record of deeds or the deeds read from it. — reasons: Al-Qaffāl links the pronoun to 75:13–15 and 17:14; al-Jishumī and al-Bayḍāwī describe an anxious reader. Al-Suyūṭī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reject the interpretation by the revelation report and parallel 20:114.
  75:16/q04/p3 What is within a person, namely conscience. — reasons: Elmalılı says this follows if the address is universal and the implicit antecedent derives from the preceding self-witness passage; he prefers the Qurʾān reading.
  75:16/q04/p4 The coming Judgment and its description. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports this interpretation and calls it untenable.
75:16/q05 What action does the verse prohibit? [لَا تُحَرِّكْ بِهِۦ لِسَانَكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether tongue movement is simultaneous recitation, later review, premature delivery, or a resurrection scene.)
  75:16/q05/p1 Reciting along with Gabriel before his revelation is complete. — reasons: The Prophet should listen with heart and ear and repeat only after Gabriel finishes. Al-Ṭabarī prefers Ibn ʿAbbās via Saʿīd: review for memory would be after collection, so the prohibition concerns premature recitation. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī compares a listener finishing a speaker's words
