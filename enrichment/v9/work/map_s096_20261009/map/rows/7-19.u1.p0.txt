## THE CURRENT MAP
7:19/q01 How is Adam's address connected to the preceding speech? [وَيَٰٓـَٔادَمُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the wāw continues the expulsion speech or introduces an understood new saying, with 2:35 supplying that wording.)
  7:19/q01/p1 An unspoken divine saying introduces the address. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī supplies 'And We said'; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī infer it from 2:35. Al-Ālūsī favors connection to the earlier angelic command and favors to humanity; the direct call gives the command weight. Al-Kashshāf simply expands the call as God's address to Adam.
  7:19/q01/p2 The address continues the same quoted speech as Iblis's expulsion. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says wāw joins the speech of 7:18 and the speaker turns to Adam without repeating 'We said'; his prophetic legal-case example and 12:28–29 illustrate successive addressees. This juxtaposition humiliates Iblis and honors Adam. Al-Sāmarrāʾī contrasts this shared saying with the explicit 'We said' in 2:35. Al-Ālūsī reports an argument for this connection but finds it against the apparent reading.
7:19/q02 What does the call to Adam convey? [يَٰٓـَٔادَمُ · rhetoric]
  7:19/q02/p1 It draws attention to Adam and honors him. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says direct address makes the command important and singles out Adam's priority; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the vocative honors him before the gathered company and sharpens Iblis's humiliation. Study Quran contrasts this intimate direct address with God's later call from a distance, signaling alienation.
7:19/q03 What does the command to dwell mean for Adam? [ٱسْكُنْ · interpretation]
  7:19/q03/p1 Take up or continue residence in the Garden. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī and al-Nasafī distinguish residence from mere stillness; al-Ālūsī explicitly rejects the sense of immobility. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says a command to continue an existing action means persistence. Ibn ʿĀshūr says if Adam was created there it means remain, and if elsewhere it grants entry as an honor; either taunts Iblis. Al-Bursevī adds that the residence honors the pair and permits their stay.
7:19/q04 Why is the independent pronoun 'you' stated? [أَنتَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether coordination requires both nouns to be independently eligible for the same verb form.)
  7:19/q04/p1 It reinforces Adam as the subject and enables coordination with his wife. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj prefers the pronoun because 'go and Zayd' without one is awkward. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the hidden subject of 'dwell' particularly needs reinforcement; al-Burhān says a connected subject is conventionally reinforced before a coordinated noun. Al-Biqāʿī adds that it confirms Adam himself, rather than a metonymic associate, is addressed. Ibn ʿĀshūr sees an indirect contrast with Iblis, applicable also to 2:35, while denying that pronouns inherently carry contrast.
  7:19/q04/p2 The wife's mention represents a separate, omitted command to dwell. — reasons: Ibn ʿUmarūn and Ibn Mālik require both coordinated nouns to be eligible for the same grammatical role. Abū Ḥayyān counters with 'Hind and Zayd rise': masculine Zayd may be coordinated after a feminine verb although he could not independently be its subject.
7:19/q05 Who is Adam's spouse, and what is reported of her origin? [وَزَوْجُكَ · referent] (turns on: The reported account of Eve's origin: formed from Adam or already among the Garden's women.)
  7:19/q05/p1 She is Eve, created from Adam. — reasons: Al-Māwardī, al-Jishumī, al-Bursevī, and Study Quran identify her as Ḥawwāʾ/Eve; Study Quran notes the Quran never names her. Al-Suddī reports that Adam awoke to a companion made from his rib, named Ḥawwāʾ because she came from living matter. Study Quran says the rib account appears in Hadith and many read 4:1 as alluding to her origin; it notes that the Quran omits the origin and that primordial Adam represents male and female humanity. Kuranyolu says Adam was made on earth from special soil and his mate from him.
  7:19/q05/p2 Ḥawwāʾ was one of the Garden's women. — reasons: Ashʿath al-Ḥaddānī adds that her pregnancy was so transparent the child's sex could be seen.
7:19/q06 Why is the wife described as Adam's 'zawj'? [وَزَوْجُكَ · rhetoric]
  7:19/q06/p1 The expression concisely conveys their paired relationship and shared address. — reasons: Al-Bursevī says zawj names one counterpart and zawjān the pair. Al-Jishumī and al-Ṭabrisī say the possessive relation makes a feminine form redundant. Al-Ālūsī says Eve follows Adam in the residence command, whereas both are addressed equally in eating and prohibition; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī likewise says the ruling includes her though Adam alone is directly addressed.
7:19/q07 Which Garden did Adam and his wife inhabit? [ٱلْجَنَّةَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the setting is final recompense, another heavenly test garden, an earthly garden, or specifically Eden; accounts weigh departure, prohibition, sleep, and Iblis's presence differently.)
  7:19/q07/p1 The Garden of Eternity or final reward. — reasons: Al-Māwardī says leaving it was possible because their stay was not permanent reward; al-Jishumī attributes the choice to ʿAlī ibn ʿĪsā. Al-Bursevī calls this the majority view. Kuranyolu prefers it after considering parallel verses: the pair were created on earth, then placed in a Paradise unlike earthly life.
  7:19/q07/p2 A heavenly garden distinct from the final Garden. — reasons: Al-Jishumī attributes a heavenly-gardens view to Abū ʿAlī and Abū Hāshim. Al-Bursevī reports a test garden in heaven as one alternative; its advocates cite prohibitions, sleep, expulsion, and Iblis's entry as unlike eternal reward. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī specifies an intermediate heavenly garden before earthly life and legal responsibility, revealing general human disposition.
  7:19/q07/p3 An earthly garden. — reasons: Al-Māwardī reports an earthly garden without duties, although Adam was obligated. Al-Bursevī prefers earth: a report says Adam's sons sought Garden grapes at his death and angels buried him; he also interprets the Nile's Garden source as earthly. Kuranyolu gives the lexical earthly-garden view. Other test-garden advocates mention an earthly elevated garden and objections to identifying the site with final reward.
  7:19/q07/p4 The Garden of Eden, distinct from Eternity. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās identifies the place of Adam and expelled Iblis as Eden. Elmalılı glosses adn with unvowelled dāl as residence, linked to maʿdin, and suggests Eden was the first afterlife garden on the way to Eternity, where beginning and return meet, citing 43:72.
7:19/q08 When were Adam and his wife settled in the Garden? [ٱسْكُنْ ٱلْجَنَّةَ · reports] (turns on: Different reports supply relative sequence and a specific day; the details are not mutually exclusive.)
  7:19/q08/p1 After Iblis was expelled; Adam had previously been alone there. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Nasafī, and al-Bursevī place the divine address after Iblis's expulsion. Al-Suddī says Adam first lived alone, then awoke to his wife.
  7:19/q08/p2 Adam was created and placed in Firdaws on Friday. — reasons: Abū al-ʿĀliya reports both events on that day.
7:19/q09 How does 'then eat' relate to dwelling, compared with 2:35? [فَكُلَا · rhetoric]
  7:19/q09/p1 Fāʾ marks eating as following residence; 2:35's wāw gives broader conjunction. — reasons: Al-Rāzī and Tafsīr al-Kabīr say succession is a special case of conjunction, resolving the variation. Al-Biqāʿī says eating accompanies settlement. Al-Itqān says taking residence comes first and min ḥaythu is narrower. Ibn ʿĀshūr says fāʾ conveys prompt, complete favor and distress to Iblis; his general principle allows Quranic variation where both formulations fit. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says wāw leaves timing and choice open while fāʾ orders eating immediately; his Lamāsāt cites 45:24, 42:3, and 5:6 to show wāw can indicate order without requiring it.
7:19/q10 How is the imperative 'eat' formed? [فَكُلَا · grammar]
  7:19/q10/p1 It comes from akala with its first root letter omitted in the imperative. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya also mentions avoidance of a difficult vowel on a weak letter.
7:19/q11 What does eating here mean? [فَكُلَا · meaning]
  7:19/q11/p1 Actual eating. — reasons: Al-Dāmghānī adduces the parallel in 20:121.
7:19/q12 How far does the permission to eat extend? [فَكُلَا مِنْ حَيْثُ شِئْتُمَا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the locution primarily ranges over places or over the fruits available there; both remain bounded by the exception.)
  7:19/q12/p1 It permits broad, free use of the Garden's food, except the singled-out tree. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī mentions fruit wherever they wish; Mujāhid says no accounting for where they eat. Al-Jishumī calls it permission to enjoy blessings. Al-Ṭabrisī allows any place or manner; al-Nasafī, al-Bursevī, al-Ālūsī, al-Biqāʿī, al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, al-Iṣlāḥī and Study Quran include the exception. Elmalılı insists this bounded license grants neither unlimited freedom nor ownership; the tree marks a spatial and practical limit.
  7:19/q12/p2 It permits eating anywhere in the Garden. — reasons: Al-Māwardī records this spatial reading as one of two ways to take the permission.
  7:19/q12/p3 It permits eating any of the Garden's fruits. — reasons: Al-Māwardī supports this alternative because the prohibited tree bore fruit.
7:19/q13 What is the force of 'from wherever you wish' here compared with 2:35? [مِنْ حَيْثُ شِئْتُمَا · rhetoric]
  7:19/q13/p1 It qualifies eating alone and is narrower than 2:35's wording. — reasons: Al-Itqān contrasts min ḥaythu with the broader ḥaythu shiʾtumā. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says the latter can govern both dwelling and eating, whereas this phrase governs eating only; he connects the wider Baqara wording to its honorific setting.
