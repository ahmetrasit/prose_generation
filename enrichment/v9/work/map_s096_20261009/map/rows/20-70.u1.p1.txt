20:70/q14 Why is Aaron named before Moses here? [هَٰرُونَ وَمُوسَىٰ · rhetoric] (turns on: The notes weigh cadence, age, Pharaoh's claim, rank, contextual meaning, and whether the two accounts quote one speech, two groups, or a narrator's retelling.)
  20:70/q14/p1 Aaron comes first to suit this passage's verse endings. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya compares the delayed shatta in 20:53. Nasafi says waw does not require temporal order, unlike the wording at 26:48. Abu Hayyan adds that Moses is the staff miracle's agent; Burhan and al-Sakkaki note Moses otherwise has stronger precedence or merit and cite 7:122 and 26:48. Burhan's author gives the default order of subject before object, topic before predicate, and possessor before circumstantial phrase unless a reason favors change. Baydawi, Bursevi, Alusi, and Ibn Ashur also offer cadence; Ibn Ashur says a narrator could select this order without reproducing the original speech. Samarrai says the order also fits the scene's meaning: Ta-Ha ends in Musa's alif, its opening letters end in ha, Harun's first letter, while Ta-Sin-Mim ends in Musa's mim; neither opening has the other's initial.
  20:70/q14/p2 Aaron comes first because he was the elder brother. — reasons: Baydawi and Abu Hayyan report this. Alusi says it may reflect the actual spoken order and rejects al-Sayyid's assertion that Moses was older. Ibn Ashur says this seniority could explain one of two formulas the magicians uttered.
  20:70/q14/p3 Naming Aaron first prevents 'Moses' Lord' from being mistaken for Pharaoh, who raised Moses. — reasons: Razi relates Pharaoh's boast about raising Moses to 26:18 and his lordship claims to 79:24 and 28:38. Baydawi, Abu Hayyan, and Bursevi report the same concern. Alusi says the al-Araf order may be the narrator's arrangement for this reason and cites 7:122.
  20:70/q14/p4 Aaron's precedence serves this sura's meaning, guiding proud people to humble support of the Prophet. — reasons: Biqai says the order foreshadows the Qur'an's guiding of supporters despite their former pride.
  20:70/q14/p5 The order traces a meaningful progression through the minister and translator to the one who sent him. — reasons: Biqai argues this is the order's substantive point, rejecting an explanation merely by rhyme.
  20:70/q14/p6 Moses is placed second as the more honored figure and primary bearer of the mission. — reasons: Alusi says the miracle came through Moses and the order also preserves rhyme.
  20:70/q14/p7 Different groups of magicians voiced the brothers' names in opposite orders, and both statements can be attributed to all. — reasons: Abu Hayyan proposes this to reconcile 7:122 with this verse; Alusi explicitly prefers it for this sura.
  20:70/q14/p8 The magicians themselves may have uttered both orders for two different merits. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says Aaron could lead for age, Moses for his greater prophetic rank and divine speech, citing 7:121–122 and 26:47–48.
  20:70/q14/p9 A narrator may select different wording and order when recounting the same declaration. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says rhyme may govern the narrator's choice rather than the magicians' exact words; Burhan reports al-Qadi's view that varied retelling preserves meaning and displays inimitable eloquence.
20:70/q15 Does this order imply that Aaron outranks Moses? [هَٰرُونَ وَمُوسَىٰ · grammar]
  20:70/q15/p1 No; the coordinating waw includes both without establishing sequence or rank. — reasons: Nasafi contrasts the order at 26:48; Abu Hayyan says coordinated names do not require sequence. Ibn Ashur contrasts 7:121–122 and says neither ordering implies relative rank.
20:70/q16 Does the name order exemplify Qur'anic saj or rhyme driven wording? [هَٰرُونَ وَمُوسَىٰ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether cadence governs the wording or merely accompanies an independently intended meaning; the sources dispute whether the Qur'anic pattern should be called saj.)
  20:70/q16/p1 Yes; the reversal shows deliberate cadence and the Qur'an's frequent saj is a rhetorical excellence. — reasons: Burhan reports opponents citing the reversed names and defining saj as successive speech on one meter; unlike poetry's necessarily intended meter, Qur'anic saj is too frequent to be accidental. Al-Sakkaki and Burhan's author also describe this order as fitting the verse endings.
  20:70/q16/p2 No; Qur'anic expression follows intended meaning, while saj would make meaning follow rhyme. — reasons: Burhan reports al-Qadi that varying story wording and order without changing meaning is difficult eloquence proving imitators' inability. Biqai explains that completed meaning may incidentally resemble saj, with rhyme only decorating it, and refers to his discussion at 9:129. He cites the Prophet's criticism of contrived soothsayer-like rhyme; Razi as quoted by Abu Hayyan says no Qur'anic order is changed for rhyme because meaning and wording together are inimitable. Biqai reports al-Baqillani's school and al-Ash'ari opposing the label. Biqai's own alternative reads a progression through minister and translator to sender.
20:70/q17 How does this account relate to the other Qur'anic tellings? [* · links] (turns on: The notes compare different emphases and wording across the retellings; they do not present them as incompatible.)
  20:70/q17/p1 Other passages supply the defeat, confession, and varying order of the brothers' names. — reasons: Wahidi Basit points to al-Araf for the episode; Qurtubi for the staff's swallowing. Abu Hayyan notes the name order differs from 7:122. Burhan notes the reversal between Ta-Ha and al-Shu'ara without resolving it in one note. Asad points to 7:117–119 for defeat and 7:120 for their response. Study Quran points to 7:119–122. Kuranyolu suggests comparing the Qur'anic account at 7:103–126 with the Biblical episode. Samarrai Asrar Muh and Samarrai Lamasat raise the order and verse-ending question without answering it in those segments.
  20:70/q17/p2 The al-Araf account adds 'Lord of all worlds'; this account need give only wording suited to its context. — reasons: Ibn Ashur cites 7:121 and says a report need not reproduce every word spoken.
20:70/q18 What change did the magicians undergo that day? [* · interpretation]
  20:70/q18/p1 They moved rapidly from sorcery and defiance to faith, righteous witness, and martyrdom. — reasons: Qushayri contrasts oaths by Pharaoh's might with oaths by God. Ibn Kathir transmits Ibn Abbas and Ubayd ibn Umayr that they began as sorcerers and ended righteous martyrs; an Ibn Abbas report specifies seventy men. Biqai says they ended as righteous witnesses. Islahi says the declaration went beyond the customary acknowledgment of superior skill. Kuranyolu says after tasting faith they valued the afterlife above worldly reward or even being spared and spoke openly.
20:70/q19 How did Pharaoh respond to their public faith? [* · interpretation]
  20:70/q19/p1 He feared others would follow them and answered their public profession with a verbal attack. — reasons: Razi says Pharaoh feared imitation after witnessing their prostration and confession. Islahi says his attack aimed to influence the crowd. Kuranyolu describes political anxiety over the Israelites in Egypt and Moses' plan to lead them home as a setting for psychological warfare; he contrasts Pharaoh's persistent unbelief with the magicians' recognition.
20:70/q20 What inward change accompanied their prostration? [* · inward]
  20:70/q20/p1 Divine illumination and devotion drew them from ego and imitation to inward sight, sincere faith, and prostration of the heart. — reasons: Qushayri describes inward suns of gnosis and lights of divine care, through which they saw God inwardly. Bursevi transmits a Sufi authority that devotion removes ego's claims and brings sincerity, certainty, and divine lights; the magicians believed by proof, while Pharaoh saw no proof of his claimed lordship.
20:70/q21 What does their defeat teach about miracles, saintly gifts, and sorcery? [* · theology]
  20:70/q21/p1 A prophet's miracle or saint's gift draws on higher divine orientation, whereas sorcery weakens through attachment to nature. — reasons: Qashani says extraordinary acts can arise through material combinations, prepared images attracting celestial forces, or soul-power; a prophetic miracle comes with challenge and opposition, unlike a non-prophet saint's gift. He says the sorcerer may first exert strong influence but then weakens by turning from the higher source, while prophet or saint strengthens; a defeated sorcerer often accepts the prophet unless natural inclination has erased his capacity.
