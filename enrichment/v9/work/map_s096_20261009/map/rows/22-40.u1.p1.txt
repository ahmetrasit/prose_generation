22:40/q16 How does God repel some people through others? [دَفْعُ ٱللَّهِ ٱلنَّاسَ بَعْضَهُم بِبَعْضٍۢ · interpretation] (turns on: The major distinction is fighting in this context versus an unrestricted principle including law, testimony, piety, and providence.)
  22:40/q16/p1 Through believers' fighting and jihad against aggressors or polytheists. — reasons: Tabari reports Ibn Jurayj and Ibn Zayd; Razi prefers the fitting context, as do Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan and Alusi. Jishumi says God strengthens hearts because fighting alone is insufficient; Elmalili says all sanctuaries would otherwise fall. Bursevi makes the defense every-age empowerment; Razi calls it God's established way of preserving religion. Ibn Ashur treats attribution of defense to God as figurative causation through His permission.
  22:40/q16/p2 Through truthful witnesses securing rights and restraining injustice. — reasons: Mujahid adds testimony, justice and legal retaliation; Razi says retaliation protects lives.
  22:40/q16/p3 Through righteous worshippers or their prayers, averting punishment from others. — reasons: Abu al-Darda contrasts mosque-goers with outsiders and fighters with nonfighters; Ibn Abbas names prayer, alms and pilgrimage; a Prophetic report says one righteous Muslim protects a hundred relatives and neighbors, without grading. Ibn Atiyya rejects this as lacking actual repelling.
  22:40/q16/p4 God checks people generally by every means, including fighting, rulers, witnesses, and rights. — reasons: Tabari prefers the general wording because neither reason nor binding report narrows it; Kuran Yolu includes governance and testimony. Tabataba'i describes innate mutual defense of life's interests, with combat a last resort.
  22:40/q16/p5 Through just rulers and enforcement of legal limits. — reasons: Tabari includes rulers; Tha'labi and Baghawi mention legal penalties. Qutrub says retaliation restrains violence.
  22:40/q16/p6 Through the Companions protecting later generations. — reasons: Ali says the Companions checked disbelievers who threatened the Successors; Qurtubi accepts the general principle but favors fighting in this verse.
  22:40/q16/p7 Through Muslim protection of treaty communities' worship places. — reasons: Al-Hasan and Ibn Abbas say Islam's security preserves non-Muslim sanctuaries; Alusi finds a narrow present-community account unpersuasive.
  22:40/q16/p8 Through prophets protecting believers. — reasons: Al-Kalbi names prophets; Alusi finds this alternative weak in context.
  22:40/q16/p9 Through virtues, good acts, and divine good displacing evil. — reasons: Qutrub says virtues protect; Mawardi adds good repels evil. Qushayri says God spares lesser people for the elect.
22:40/q17 Who are 'the people' in the repelling clause? [ٱلنَّاسَ · referent] (turns on: A general human principle versus the immediate combatants.)
  22:40/q17/p1 The people are generic across times and communities. — reasons: Tabari refuses a restriction; Ibn Ashur permits the historical sense and recalls David, Goliath, Solomon and Nebuchadnezzar.
  22:40/q17/p2 The people in context are Muslims and Meccan polytheists. — reasons: Ibn Ashur ties this to expulsion and Muslim self-defense; Kashshaf speaks of believers overcoming unbelievers.
22:40/q18 What legal duty follows from God's protecting worship? [وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ ٱللَّهِ ٱلنَّاسَ بَعْضَهُم بِبَعْضٍۢ · law] (turns on: Whether the verse directly establishes defensive fighting, religious liberty, and wider moral duties.)
  22:40/q18/p1 Believers may fight defensively to protect worship and freedom of religion. — reasons: Qurtubi says earlier communities also used jihad; Asad calls defense of religious freedom a foremost reason for arms, citing 2:193 and 2:251; Kuran Yolu cites passages on defensive preparations.
  22:40/q18/p2 The verse also indicates a duty to command right and forbid wrong. — reasons: Jishumi infers the obligation; Uthman's report links 22:40–41 to later command of right.
22:40/q19 What inward meaning is given to the repelling and the worship places? [وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ ٱللَّهِ ٱلنَّاسَ بَعْضَهُم بِبَعْضٍۢ · inward] (turns on: The inward readings assign the conflict to faculties, the ego and spiritual aspiration.)
  22:40/q19/p1 Divine help lets hearts resist the ego so sacred law, spiritual manners, reality and remembrance endure. — reasons: Qushayri says promptings corrupt hearts and God defeats them by turning and unveiling; Bursevi describes mosques of the heart.
  22:40/q19/p2 God counters psychic forces through spirit and intellect; the four sites mark secret, heart, breast, and spirit. — reasons: Qashani sets desire against anger, spirit against psyche and intellect against fancy; remembrance culminates in divine character and annihilation.
  22:40/q19/p3 The saints may seem humiliated, yet God avenges or later empowers them. — reasons: Qushayri cites 27:52 and says truth outlasts falsehood.
  22:40/q19/p4 God overcomes self-exalting claims of independent existence with His light. — reasons: Qashani applies divine victory inwardly.
22:40/q20 How is 'would have been demolished' read? [لَّهُدِّمَتْ · readings] (turns on: Light Form I versus doubled Form II, with the latter commonly conveying repeated or extensive demolition.)
  22:40/q20/p1 The dāl is light: lahudimat, a simple demolition that may include little or much. — reasons: Ibn Kathir, Nafi, Abu Ja'far and others are reported with this reading; Farisi and Tabrisi say the light form can describe much as well as little.
  22:40/q20/p2 The dāl is doubled: lahuddimat, emphasizing numerous or repeated demolitions. — reasons: Tabari prefers it for repeated unbelievers' attacks; Abu Ali cites 12:23 and a poem of repeated door opening; Ibn Atiyya cites plural sanctuaries and contrasts 4:78, 22:45, 12:43 and 38:50. Ibn Ashur says rage might leave no trace.
22:40/q21 Is the tāʾ in 'lahuddimat ṣawāmiʿ' assimilated? [لَّهُدِّمَتْ صَوَٰمِعُ · readings] (turns on: Transmission routes differ on idghām before ṣād.)
  22:40/q21/p1 The tāʾ assimilates to the following ṣād. — reasons: Jishumi lists the remaining readers; Nashr details Abu Amr, Hamza, Kisa'i and route-specific variants. Nashr rejects Abu al-Ala's unique report about al-Suri before ḍād.
  22:40/q21/p2 The tāʾ is clearly pronounced. — reasons: Jishumi lists Abu Ja'far, Nafi, Ibn Kathir, Asim and Ya'qub; Nashr details al-Hulwani's display, Ibn Dhakwan's routes and exceptions.
22:40/q22 What does hadm mean here? [لَّهُدِّمَتْ · meaning] (turns on: Literal structural ruin or broader disabling of worship.)
  22:40/q22/p1 Hadm is the physical demolition and collapse of buildings. — reasons: Wahidi says the noun may also mean what has been demolished; Ibn Ashur says it brings structures down.
  22:40/q22/p2 The threatened demolition signifies broad social corruption and disruption. — reasons: Alusi calls it hyperbole; Tabataba'i says visible worship sites stand for religion's broader existence.
22:40/q23 What are the ṣawāmiʿ in this verse? [صَوَٰمِعُ · referent] (turns on: The buildings are assigned variously to monks, Sabians, Magians, roadside worship, or broader communities.)
  22:40/q23/p1 Monks' hermitages or monasteries, usually Christian. — reasons: Mujahid and Ibn Zayd name monks; Ibn Ashur describes their upper chambers, stairs and lamps; Qatada says the term later extended to Muslim minarets. Mawardi cites a report, 'the believer's monastery is his house.' Mawardi quotes a Prophetic report calling a believer's house his monastery.
  22:40/q23/p2 Temples or hermitages of the Sabians. — reasons: Qatada is repeatedly cited; Alusi objects that Sabian worship was not a true dispensation. Ibn Qutayba transmits Qatada's account of six religions and the Sabians.
  22:40/q23/p3 Names for worship places shared among scriptural communities. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya prefers a broad rhetorical list, except biyaʿ specifically Christian; al-Hasan treats all four as places of believers.
  22:40/q23/p4 Roadside houses or worship structures. — reasons: Muqatil ibn Hayyan identifies buildings beside roads; Mujahid describes a roadside sequence of worship places.
  22:40/q23/p5 Magian temples. — reasons: Ibn Kathir transmits another report from Qatada.
  22:40/q23/p6 Small built cells without fixed communal assignment. — reasons: Al-Dahhak says people build small ṣawāmiʿ.
22:40/q24 What is the form and original sense of ṣawmaʿa? [صَوَٰمِعُ · meaning] (turns on: Compression of the sides versus a sharp or tapering top, with related Arabic usages.)
  22:40/q24/p1 It denotes a tall, narrow, often isolated structure with a pointed or compressed top. — reasons: Mawardi derives it from sides pressed together; Azhari and Qurtubi from sharpening a dish top. Tabarsi quotes Abu Dhu'ayb for 'compressed'; Ibn Ashur quotes Imru' al-Qays on a lantern tower.
  22:40/q24/p2 The root also denotes sharp wit or speech and small close-set ears. — reasons: The sources connect aṣmaʿ to keen intellect or an ear close to the head; the noun follows fawʿala.
  22:40/q24/p3 The word's later use includes Muslim minarets. — reasons: Qatada and later authors note the semantic extension.
22:40/q25 What are the biyaʿ? [بِيَعٌۭ · referent] (turns on: Christian churches are the customary Arabic sense, but some reports name Jewish synagogues or broader temples.)
  22:40/q25/p1 Christian churches and congregational prayer houses. — reasons: Akhfash and others distinguish larger communal churches from monks' retreats; Ibn Kathir says more people worship there. Tabari's Arabic usage favors this.
  22:40/q25/p2 Jewish synagogues or churches. — reasons: Mujahid and Ibn Zayd are cited; Ibn Kathir questions whether Mujahid actually specified Jewish churches. Tha'labi transmits Mujahid and Ibn Zayd.
