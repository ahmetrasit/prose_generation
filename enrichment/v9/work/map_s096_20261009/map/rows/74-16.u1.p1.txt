sons: Al-Jishumi says abundant favors make disobedience more serious and further increase can corrupt. Al-Elmali says ingratitude withdraws rather than increases gifts. Bursevi says the abundance amounted to gradual entrapment (istidrāj) when met by defiance. Islahi says the favors were unearned gifts testing gratitude, a test the man failed. Kuran Yolu grounds gratitude to the giver in reason and justice and calls failure to thank God for life, material and spiritual gifts grave ingratitude, especially when joined to denial, polytheism, and injustice. Abu Hayyan points to his greedy love of worldly life. Bursevi quotes an incomplete Persian admonition about not heeding loved ones' counsel.
74:16/q14 Can an unbeliever receive God's favor in the sense at issue here? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether favors granted in this life count as God's favor to an unbeliever in the disputed sense.)
  74:16/q14/p1 Yes, an unbeliever may receive God's favor. — reasons: Ibn Ashur reports this as the view of al-Maturidi and the Mu'tazilites.
  74:16/q14/p2 No, an unbeliever does not receive God's favor in this sense. — reasons: Ibn Ashur reports al-Ash'ari's differing view.
  74:16/q14/p3 The disagreement over favor to unbelievers is verbal. — reasons: Ibn Ashur follows careful scholars who frame the difference as one of wording.
74:16/q15 What does the opponents' response reveal about the Qur'an's inimitability? [* · interpretation]
  74:16/q15/p1 Their resort to insults and fighting, despite intense opposition, shows they could not meet the Qur'an's challenge. — reasons: Al-Jishumi uses the enemies' failure to rebut the challenge as evidence of inimitability.
74:16/q16 Can reflection or thought be bad? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether intent to invalidate truth can make reflection bad.)
  74:16/q16/p1 Yes: thought aimed at invalidating truth can be bad. — reasons: Al-Jishumi says describing unbelief as an attempt to invalidate truth supports Abu Hashim and argues against Abu Ali.
  74:16/q16/p2 Abu Ali's opposed view denies that reflection can be bad in this way. — reasons: Al-Jishumi names Abu Ali as the opponent of Abu Hashim's account, but supplies no separate argument for him.
74:16/q17 Who performs the thinking and grimacing described in this passage? [* · theology]
  74:16/q17/p1 The human servant performs these acts. — reasons: Al-Jishumi explicitly attributes the thinking and grimacing to the servant.
74:16/q18 How does al-Muddaththir relate to al-Muzzammil? [* · links]
  74:16/q18/p1 It is the preceding surah's counterpart, with essentially the same central themes and a similarly meaningful name. — reasons: Islahi draws the connection between their themes and names.
74:16/q19 What is the warning of verses 11–17? [* · links]
  74:16/q19/p1 Judgment will be severe for disbelievers who assume wealth and rank promise further gain. — reasons: Islahi says the affluent man's expected increase is false; opposing the Qur'an instead brings severe penalty.
74:16/q20 How do other verses portray Meccan opposition to the Qur'an? [* · links]
  74:16/q20/p1 They record repeated allegations that it was human fabrication, ancient lore, or copied writings, and argue against those claims. — reasons: Bint al-Shati cites 68:10–15, 44–47; 25:4–10; 17:87–96; 6:7–9, 25–26; and 34:43–45 as parallels and rebuttals.
