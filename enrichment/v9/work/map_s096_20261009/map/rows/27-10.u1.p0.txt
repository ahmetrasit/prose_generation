## THE CURRENT MAP
27:10/q01 What was Moses' staff? [عَصَاكَ · reports]
  27:10/q01/p1 An angel gave it to Moses on his way to Midian. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās says it lit the night, brought up plants on struck ground, and shook leaves for sheep.
  27:10/q01/p2 It was named Masha. — reasons: Saʿīd ibn Jubayr names it; al-Māwardī's Ibn ʿAbbās report repeats the name.
  27:10/q01/p3 It was made of boxthorn. — reasons: Ibn Jubayr identifies ʿawsaj as its wood.
27:10/q02 How did Moses take the command to cast his staff? [وَأَلْقِ عَصَاكَ · reports]
  27:10/q02/p1 He thought he was to abandon it. — reasons: Wahb says he threw it as if rejecting it; al-Qurṭubī transmits the same understanding.
27:10/q03 Why was Moses told to cast the staff? [وَأَلْقِ عَصَاكَ · interpretation]
  27:10/q03/p1 To show a sign, establish his commission and train him for its use. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī says the familiar staff yields certainty; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Nasafī stress training and reassurance; Ibn Kathīr sees God's free power; al-Biqāʿī, might and wisdom; al-Qurṭubī says each prophet needs a personal sign; Iṣlāḥī calls it the main sign among nine.
  27:10/q03/p2 To teach reliance on God alone. — reasons: Elmalılı says Moses should no longer rely on another support at this station.
27:10/q04 How is 'cast your staff' connected to the preceding speech? [وَأَلْقِ عَصَاكَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether unlike declarative and imperative clauses coordinate, and how the parallel أن bears on it.)
  27:10/q04/p1 It coordinates with the blessing announcement as part of the divine call. — reasons: Al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī and al-Nasafī cite repeated explanatory أن in al-Qaṣaṣ. Al-Zamakhsharī supplies 'he was told.' Al-Fārisī and Abū al-Baqāʾ alternatively construe būrika as prayer so both clauses are performative. Al-Ālūsī replies that renewed 'O Moses' can continue the call or be parenthetic.
  27:10/q04/p2 It coordinates directly with the declaration 'I am God'. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn cite Sībawayh's permission to coordinate unlike sentence forms; al-Samīn rejects a parallel-form requirement. Study Quran links the opening to verse 9. Al-Naḥḥās says the utterance to Moses ends here.
  27:10/q04/p3 An unspoken 'he was told' connects the declaration and imperative. — reasons: The Shaykh objects that Zamakhsharī's explanation needs this extra speech clause. Al-Ālūsī says an omitted command would favor fa-alqi.
27:10/q05 Why is the command direct here but introduced by 'wa-an' in al-Qaṣaṣ? [وَأَلْقِ عَصَاكَ · rhetoric]
  27:10/q05/p1 The direct imperative gives God's utterance and honors Moses; al-Qaṣaṣ gives the call's sense. — reasons: Al-Burhān notes the wording difference. Sāmarrāʾī links direct address, command and ladayya to divine nearness.
27:10/q06 What events are omitted between the command and seeing the staff move? [فَلَمَّا رَءَاهَا تَهْتَزُّ · rhetoric]
  27:10/q06/p1 Moses cast it and it became a moving serpent. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī says al-Aʿrāf and al-Shuʿarāʾ state the steps; al-Bursawī, al-Ālūsī and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī say fa compresses a quick sequence. Abū Ḥayyān differently locates the omitted casting after trembling.
27:10/q07 How is 'raʾāhā' read? [رَءَاهَا · readings] (turns on: The placement of imāla.)
  27:10/q07/p1 Abū Bakr from ʿĀṣim, al-Kisāʾī and Ḥamza incline both vowels. — reasons: Al-Fārisī reports kasra on rāʾ and hamza and points to earlier grounds for imāla; Ibn ʿAṭiyya mentions imāla.
  27:10/q07/p2 Abū ʿAmr opens rāʾ and inclines the hamza. — reasons: Al-Fārisī reports this throughout.
  27:10/q07/p3 Ḥafṣ, Ibn ʿĀmir, Ibn Kathīr and Nāfiʿ open both. — reasons: Al-Fārisī contrasts Ḥafṣ with Abū Bakr from ʿĀṣim.
27:10/q08 What does 'tahtazzu' mean? [تَهْتَزُّ · meaning]
  27:10/q08/p1 It moves, shakes or writhes vigorously. — reasons: Muqātil and al-Wāḥidī say it moves; Ibn Jurayj says it had become a moving serpent; al-Bursawī says it darted violently; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī defines ihtizāz as vigorous motion.
27:10/q09 What is the grammatical role of 'tahtazzu'? [تَهْتَزُّ · grammar]
  27:10/q09/p1 It is a circumstantial clause from the seen object pronoun. — reasons: Al-Nasafī, al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī attach it to raʾāhā; the latter two specify visual seeing.
27:10/q10 What does 'jānn' mean? [جَآنٌّ · meaning] (turns on: Lexical usage, size and the force of the simile.)
  27:10/q10/p1 A small, light, swift snake. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba says not large; al-Layth says white, Ibn Shumayl smooth and harmless, and the house-snake hadith uses plural jinān. Al-Māwardī, al-Jishumī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Rāzī derive the name from concealment. Al-Qāmūs gives dark-eyed and harmless; Elmalılı notes doubled nūn.
  27:10/q10/p2 A snake between small and large. — reasons: Al-Kalbī and al-Farrāʾ give the intermediate size.
  27:10/q10/p3 A large serpent. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says a large snake of this kind, citing a rajaz poet's jinnān; al-Naḥḥās invokes Arab usage. Al-Zajjāj calls thuʿbān the great snake.
  27:10/q10/p4 A devil of the jinn, likened for its frightfulness. — reasons: Al-Māwardī cites 37:65; Sāmarrāʾī says jinn are hidden yet may appear in forms, citing Abū Hurayra.
27:10/q11 How is 'jānn' read? [جَآنٌّ · readings] (turns on: How the dialect avoids adjacent sukūns.)
  27:10/q11/p1 Al-Ḥasan, al-Zuhrī and ʿAmr ibn ʿUbayd replace alif with hamza. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī explain avoidance of adjacent sukūns, with 1:7 and daʾba/shaʾba parallels.
  27:10/q11/p2 Al-Ḥasan omits tanwīn. — reasons: Al-Rāzī attributes it to a dialect avoiding two sukūns, citing shaʾba and daʾba.
27:10/q12 How is the jān comparison reconciled with the great serpent elsewhere? [كَأَنَّهَا جَآنٌّ · interpretation] (turns on: One body and motion, successive changes, or distinct occasions.)
  27:10/q12/p1 One great serpent moved with a small snake's speed and lightness. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj, Abū Isḥāq and Thaʿlab distinguish shape from motion; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says small snakes move faster. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites 7:107 and 26:32. Al-Biqāʿī describes twisting and speed.
  27:10/q12/p2 It first appeared as a small snake, then grew into a great serpent. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī says it swelled from staff size; al-Qurṭubī gives a variant with an intermediate female serpent before a male thuʿbān. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says staging fails to explain the comparison.
  27:10/q12/p3 It was a small jān at the mountain and a great serpent before Pharaoh. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī and Abū al-Layth distinguish the occasions; Sāmarrāʾī says jān fits private fear and thuʿbān public might. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī objects that this fails to resolve the simile.
27:10/q13 What happened to the staff in the transformation? [عَصَاكَ كَأَنَّهَا جَآنٌّ · theology] (turns on: The nature of the change and the witnessed form.)
  27:10/q13/p1 God made it alive and changed its attributes while preserving its substance. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya says God created life and changed properties; Abū Ḥayyān denies destruction and recreation of the staff's substance.
  27:10/q13/p2 It became a huge terrifying serpent. — reasons: Wahb says it swallowed a boulder, tore up a tree, and had fiery eyes, hair-like ridges and fangs; Ibn Kathīr stresses immediate change.
27:10/q14 How does 'kaʾannahā jānn' function grammatically? [كَأَنَّهَا جَآنٌّ · grammar]
  27:10/q14/p1 It is a circumstantial clause, either second from the object or nested under 'tahtazzu'. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās identifies a ḥāl; al-Nasafī attaches it to the pronoun in tahtazzu; al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī give both parses.
27:10/q15 What does 'wallā mudbiran' describe? [وَلَّىٰ مُدْبِرًۭا · interpretation]
  27:10/q15/p1 Moses turned his back and fled from the serpent. — reasons: Yaḥyā says in fright; al-Ṭabarī and al-Ṭabrisī describe retreat, and Kashf al-asrār says he put the snake behind him. Al-Itqān says mudbiran adds meaning, since merely turning need not be flight, citing the command to turn toward the Sacred Mosque.
  27:10/q15/p2 Inwardly, Moses turned toward God and away from the self. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī takes the serpent as the sanctified self.
27:10/q16 What is the grammatical role of 'mudbiran'? [مُدْبِرًۭا · grammar]
  27:10/q16/p1 It is a circumstantial accusative. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās marks it as another ḥāl.
27:10/q17 Why did Moses flee? [وَلَّىٰ مُدْبِرًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: Natural fright, a feared divine threat, or initial unfamiliarity.)
  27:10/q17/p1 He naturally feared harm from the terrifying serpent. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān stress normal human reaction without advance reassurance. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says his only staff had turned frightening, leaving him unable to repel danger; flight was not blameworthy. Ibn Kathīr stresses its size and speed.
  27:10/q17/p2 He feared that God might have directed the portent against him. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī says God could punish as He wished; al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī and al-Zamakhsharī infer this from reassurance. Al-Bursawī specifies fear of death.
  27:10/q17/p3 The unfamiliar first prophetic experience frightened him until God trained him. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says God gradually made such experiences familiar; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says taking the serpent became habitual. Ibn Zayd says he turned only after restoration to a staff. Wahb reports that Moses ran far, stopped in shame on remembering God, and was called back.
27:10/q18 What does 'lam yuʿaqqib' mean? [وَلَمْ يُعَقِّبْ · meaning] (turns on: Whether it describes return, looking, lingering, or a further step.)
  27:10/q18/p1 He did not return or retrace his steps. — reasons: Mujāhid supplies the common gloss; al-Ṭabarī derives it from returning to the start. Al-Zajjāj cites Labīd and a fighter's renewed charge; al-Wāḥidī cites Shammar for every returner as muʿaqqib. Some derive it from the heel; al-Muḥkam and al-Qazzāz confirm return after departure. Several also add that he never looked back.
