[IBNASHUR:1:3/r36] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | This ranking raises a rhetorical problem: why append al-Raḥīm after al-Raḥmān when eloquence normally progresses from general to specific and from strong to stronger?
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r37] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | Most exegetes resolve the sequence by treating al-Raḥmān as more specific, so al-Raḥīm broadens the scope after a restricted description; al-Raḥmān is exclusive to God.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r38] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Al-Raḥīm signifies mercy with many objects, so it can describe someone other than God, as 9:128 calls the Messenger raʾūf and raḥīm; the two attributes are not redundant. | mentions 9:128
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r39] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Al-Raḥmān precedes al-Raḥīm because an attribute of essential character comes before one indicating the many recipients of that attribute.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r4] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | rejects | Ibn ʿĀshūr says al-Mubarrad offers no proof; Arabic and Hebrew are sister languages, and Arabic may be older, so al-Raḥmān could be Arabic.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r40] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · قطرب | reports | Quṭrub says al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm are equivalent ṣifa mushabbaha forms and that their pairing is verbal emphasis; al-Zajjāj leaned toward this view.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r41] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | rejects | Ibn ʿĀshūr rejects Quṭrub’s emphasis analysis as weak: emphasis is contrary to the default preference for adding a new meaning, and the context does not call for emphasis; other proposed solutions also fail to persuade.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r42] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · جمهور الأئمة | reports | The majority of leading scholars say al-Raḥmān was not used in pre-Islamic Arabic; the Qur’an introduced it as God’s attribute, so it became exclusive to Him, and some call it a proper name rather than an adjective.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r43] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · جمهور الأئمة | reports | The Qur’an’s report that people asked “What is al-Raḥmān?” and said they disbelieved in Him (25:60; 13:30) supports the claim that the name was unknown or rejected. | mentions 25:60, 13:30

[IBNASHUR:1:3/r45] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The evidence suggests the epithet had fallen out of common use, or that people denied it as a name of God.

[IBNASHUR:1:3/r47] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The Qur’an says “al-Raḥmān” in the Makkan 67:19 but “Allah” in 16:79, a portion revealed in Medina, underscoring the Makkan effort to establish this divine name. | mentions 67:19, 16:79
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r48] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Following “Lord of the worlds” (1:2) with these names is apt: God governs and brings worlds to bodily and spiritual perfection, while weak creatures naturally need and hope for mercy. | mentions 1:2
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r49] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Al-Raḥmān names mercy as God’s intrinsic attribute whose effects issue universally and consistently; this especially suits Him as Lord of weak creatures. | mentions 1:2
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r5] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The polytheists’ question “What is al-Raḥmān?” (25:60) may have prompted the claim that Arabs did not know this divine name. | mentions 25:60
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r50] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Although lordship entails gradual growth to perfection and all blessings, al-Raḥmān clarifies that this care reaches perfection gently, in ways suited to each creature’s capacity.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r51] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Lordly blessings may involve hardship or pain; al-Raḥmān signals that they arrive through gentleness, ease, and removal of hardship, including in religious commands so far as their purpose allows.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r52] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Some of God’s governance mercies are obvious: He enables people to live on earth and makes its benefits accessible.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r53] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Other mercies appear in duties that serve people, such as purification and the cultivation of good character, with ease observed as far as possible.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r54] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Some duties benefit the public; mercy for the community extends to everyone through social order, as in zakāt.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r55] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Grammarians disagree whether bare (indefinite) raḥmān declines or is diptote; the Kāfiya gives two criteria for diptote faʿlān adjectives, yielding a dispute over this form.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r56] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · بنو أسد | reports | Banū Asad decline every faʿlān adjective because they give each a feminine form in faʿlānah.

[IBNASHUR:1:3/r6] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Some Arabs in Yemen say رَخِم or رَخْمَة with khāʾ, which bears on the proposed Arabic or Hebrew etymology.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r7] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | In Arabic, mercy is a tender inward feeling and inclination toward a living being that prompts gentleness, beneficence, harm prevention, and help through hardship.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r8] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | As a psychological quality, mercy is a passive affect; its effects are active deeds prompted by that affect and limited by the person’s capacity.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r9] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Calling someone merciful describes an inward affect, while saying that they have mercy on someone means that effects of mercy issue from them; the transitive verb names those effects.
