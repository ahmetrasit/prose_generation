18:42/q11 Is there another reading of يُقَلِّبُ كَفَّيْهِ? [يُقَلِّبُ كَفَّيْهِ · readings]
  18:42/q11/p1 تُقَلَّبُ كَفَّاهُ: his two hands themselves are turned over. — reasons: Al-Samin reports the passive variant and gives its meaning; al-Alusi also notes the variant and says its mā can be verbal or relative.
18:42/q12 How does عَلَىٰ مَآ أَنفَقَ attach and express regret? [عَلَىٰ مَآ أَنفَقَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the prepositional phrase attaches through an implied sense of regret or functions as a state description.)
  18:42/q12/p1 It attaches to يُقَلِّبُ because the verb implies regretting what was spent. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf says regret takes 'on'; al-Baydawi parses it as 'regretted what he spent'; Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi derive 'over' from the implied sense of remorse. Al-Alusi says this makes it grief over expenditure.
  18:42/q12/p2 The phrase can be a settled circumstantial modifier: he turns his hands while grieving. — reasons: Al-Alusi reports this alternative; treating the modifier as emphatic requires equating grief with regret, whereas some distinguish grief as narrower. Al-Samin reports Abu al-Baqa's omitted-phrase paraphrase of a regretful subject but says its formal parse is an unrestricted state.
18:42/q13 What expense does مَآ أَنفَقَ recall? [مَآ أَنفَقَ · referent]
  18:42/q13/p1 What he spent constructing, cultivating and improving the garden. — reasons: Al-Damghani specifies construction or cultivation; al-Kashshaf building, al-Nasafi cultivation, al-Biqa'i cultivation and improvement. Bursevi says regret over voluntary spending stands for grief over the acquired garden. Al-Alusi says the owner regretted this expenditure because he could have saved or used the money elsewhere, after trusting the garden would endure. Al-Razi says the loss is severe after a life spent seeking worldly gain at religion's expense.
18:42/q14 Does فِيهَا mean 'in' or 'on' the garden? [فِيهَا · meaning] (turns on: Whether fī is taken as a direct equivalent of ʿalā here or as a contextual extension of its locative sense.)
  18:42/q14/p1 Here fī means 'on' or 'upon' the garden. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi and al-Wahidi compare the Qur'anic wording for crucifying upon palm trunks (5:33 or 20:71); Ibn al-Jawzi's Nuzha calls this a recognized usage and notes 34:15; Ibn al-Jawzi's Zad states the equivalence here.
  18:42/q14/p2 The underlying sense is location within, extended metaphorically by context. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi's Nuzha states the base locative meaning and reports Abu Zakariyya on metaphorical uses such as being 'in' knowledge or work and possession pictured as being in the hand. Al-Wahidi notes that Arabic may speak of spending 'in' a house or garment as well as 'on' it.
18:42/q15 What can مَآ in مَآ أَنفَقَ mean? [مَآ أَنفَقَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether mā nominalizes the act of spending or refers to the thing spent.)
  18:42/q15/p1 A relative pronoun: what he spent. — reasons: Al-Alusi allows this on the variant reading where his hands are turned; the ordinary explanations also identify the lost money.
  18:42/q15/p2 A verbal noun: his spending. — reasons: Al-Alusi explicitly allows this alongside the relative sense on that variant reading.
18:42/q16 How did the garden lie خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا? [خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether ʿurūsh are vine frameworks or building roofs, and whether the walls also fell or remained standing.)
  18:42/q16/p1 The vine trellises fell and the vines or garden growth collapsed over them. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf, al-Nasafi, al-Baydawi and Bursevi identify grape supports and vines; al-Biqa'i says supports fell first, then what they carried. Al-Wahidi includes vine frameworks and building roofs. Al-Alusi defines ʿurūsh as vine supports and khāwiya as collapsed, and al-Qurtubi says both crop and framework perished. Al-Tabari and Muqatil describe fallen supports; al-Mawardi says foundation and produce both died.
  18:42/q16/p2 The roofs fell first, and the walls or structure collapsed over them. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and al-Tabrisi explicitly sequence roof then wall; al-Razi reports roofs and walls as an alternative to vine arbors. Yahya reports the garden overturned onto roofs, and al-Mawardi says the garden was overturned. The phrase marks complete structural ruin.
  18:42/q16/p3 The walls remained standing but the roofs fell inside, leaving roofless buildings. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj describes roofless walls; al-Asamm in al-Jishumi gives this among roofless, dry and upside-down possibilities. Ibn al-Jawzi's Zad explicitly retains the walls while roofs have collapsed within; al-Jishumi says the remaining walls are useless.
  18:42/q16/p4 The site is empty of its plants, with bare soil or abandoned houses left. — reasons: Al-Hasan in Yahya says soil remained after plants vanished; al-Majaz glosses ʿurūsh as houses. Al-Tha'labi says plantings and buildings are gone; al-Tabrisi also reports empty houses after trees vanished. Ibn Qutayba calls khāwiya ruined and ʿurūsh roofs; al-Qurtubi notes usage for barren land, setting stars and collapsed houses. Al-Jishumi includes dryness.
  18:42/q16/p5 It broadly depicts an empty, overturned and ruined garden, without fixing the architecture. — reasons: Al-Farra allows fallen or empty on its roofs; al-Tabari, al-Tha'labi, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, al-Biqa'i, Elmalili and Tabataba'i describe full collapse. Al-Razi says either arbor or roof interpretation yields complete ruin; Tabataba'i allows the root to mean falling or emptiness. The owner in Islahi finds the fences collapsed.
18:42/q17 Why are the vine trellises prominent in the ruin? [عُرُوشِهَا · rhetoric]
  18:42/q17/p1 The vines were the main planting, their ruin entailed the rest, or they cost the most to grow. — reasons: Al-Alusi reports these three explanations for singling out grapevines; Bursevi says the grape garden is singled out as the principal asset.
18:42/q18 How is وَيَقُولُ connected grammatically? [وَيَقُولُ · grammar] (turns on: Coordination versus a circumstantial wāw, whose use here al-Alusi calls rare.)
  18:42/q18/p1 It coordinates with يُقَلِّبُ. — reasons: Al-Samin and al-Alusi give this direct coordination.
  18:42/q18/p2 It is a circumstantial clause describing him while he turns his hands. — reasons: Al-Samin allows the state analysis; al-Alusi says it requires an implicit pronoun and notes the rarity of an affirmative present verb with circumstantial wāw.
18:42/q19 When does the owner say يَٰلَيْتَنِى لَمْ أُشْرِكْ? [وَيَقُولُ يَٰلَيْتَنِى · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the present narrative reports a speech immediately after worldly loss or anticipates a resurrection-day lament.)
  18:42/q19/p1 He says it in this life after the garden's destruction. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya allows worldly repentance after the calamity as a warning to Quraysh and others; Abu Hayyan prefers this apparent sense and the warning against faith delayed until punishment. Al-Wahidi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Qurtubi and al-Nasafi say the regret is too late to help. Ibn Ashur sees the present tense as repeated regret after punishment.
  18:42/q19/p2 He says it in the Hereafter or on the Day of Resurrection. — reasons: Yahya places the speech in the Hereafter as a wish about his earlier life; Ibn Atiyya, Ibn al-Jawzi, Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi report this position. Al-Alusi notes that faith newly adopted on Judgment Day is unanimously useless.
18:42/q20 Is the wish sincere repentance or regret tied to lost wealth? [يَٰلَيْتَنِى لَمْ أُشْرِكْ بِرَبِّىٓ أَحَدًۭا · theology] (turns on: Whether he regrets shirk as sin and resolves to change, or wishes merely that monotheism had protected his wealth.)
  18:42/q20/p1 It is regret for lost worldly blessings, without accepted repentance or true faith. — reasons: Al-Jishumi says he wanted wealth preserved and that sincere repentance would have helped. Al-Razi says he wanted monotheism only to save the garden and God did not accept it. Al-Biqa'i says his thought remained confined to visible things; Bursevi likewise rejects sincerity. Al-Alusi rejects the faith interpretation: regretting disbelief alone is insufficient, he regretted the garden rather than the sin, and later context still denies resurrection. Al-Alusi cites al-Mawaqif that repentance requires resolve against returning and remorse for sin as sin. Al-Wahidi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Qurtubi and al-Nasafi call the regret too late. Al-Razi says pursuit of worldly gain left him both material and religious loss.
  18:42/q20/p2 It may be repentance from shirk, remorse for past conduct and entry into faith. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf, al-Tabrisi, al-Nasafi, al-Baydawi and al-Alusi report this possibility; Ibn Atiyya allows worldly repentance. Ibn Ashur says the lament implies he now believes in God's oneness. Al-Alusi says regret over past shirk might imply present belief but argues against its adequacy.
18:42/q21 What does his admission of أُشْرِكْ mean? [أُشْرِكْ بِرَبِّىٓ أَحَدًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the confession is expressed as associationism generally or specified as dependence on created causes and personal achievement.)
