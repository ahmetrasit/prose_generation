[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f11] RIFAI-KHULI · محمد أحمد خلف الله | reports | Khalafallah defines a literary story as imagined or reshaped events, including reordered, omitted, added, or exaggerated details that transform historical figures into fictional characters.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f12] RIFAI-KHULI · محمد الخضر حسين | rejects | Khidr Husayn rejects applying that definition to Qur’anic stories: it suits human works for artistry or pleasure, whereas divine narratives are lessons, examples, and wisdom, not imaginative writing mixing truth with falsehood.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f13] RIFAI-KHULI · أحمد أمين | reports | Ahmad Amin’s report characterizes the dissertation as arguing that Qur’anic stories are artistic creation unconstrained by historical accuracy and that Muhammad was an artist in this sense.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f14] RIFAI-KHULI · author | holds | The author calls Ahmad Amin’s report severe and inflammatory, says it catered to official religious opinion and public taste, and attributes a major role in the dissertation’s rejection to it.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f15] RIFAI-KHULI · أمين الخولي | reports | Al-Khuli stood by and praised Khalafallah after the doctorate was withheld; in the third-edition preface he said public prejudice had punished the thesis, which later became a rich contribution to Qur’anic inimitability, and commended its author’s sincerity.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f16] RIFAI-KHULI · أمين الخولي | reports | Al-Khuli said he would declare Khalafallah’s thesis true even if no one else in Egypt or the East supported it, accepting the threat of being cast into the fire to clear his conscience.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f17] RIFAI-KHULI · طه حسين | reports | Taha Hussein had earlier argued that Abraham and Ishmael’s appearance in the Torah and Qur’an does not by itself establish their historical existence.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f18] RIFAI-KHULI · محمد عبده | reports | Muhammad Abduh held that Qur’anic stories serve admonition rather than history or belief in every ancient report; they recount good and bad beliefs, traditions, and customs so the narrative’s form can signal approval or condemnation.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f19] RIFAI-KHULI · محمد أحمد خلف الله | reports | To defend his approach, Khalafallah says Muhammad Abduh had already advanced it and cites al-Manar’s account of stories as lessons and signs of God’s ways, not detailed histories of peoples or places.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f2] RIFAI-KHULI · نصر حامد أبو زيد | reports | Abu Zayd said he aimed to reconnect Qur’anic studies with literary and critical studies after modern conditions had separated them from heritage and its scientific methods.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f20] RIFAI-KHULI · author | reports | Some writers rejected Khalafallah’s attribution of this position to Muhammad Abduh and Rashid Rida, accusing him of ignorance and misunderstanding.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f21] RIFAI-KHULI · محمد إقبال | reports | Iqbal reads Adam’s descent as an account of humanity’s moral development from instinctual desire to awareness of a free self capable of doubt and disobedience, not the appearance of the first human on earth.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f22] RIFAI-KHULI · محمد أحمد خلف الله | reports | Khalafallah calls for reading sacred narratives through the symbolic meaning of the language of religion: their purpose is guidance, not a historical account.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f23] RIFAI-KHULI · author | holds | The author says this approach remains largely rejected: Qur’anic studies still rely on inherited sciences and interpretive rules shaped by past worldviews, without opening to modern human and social sciences that al-Khuli urged scholars to use.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f24] RIFAI-KHULI · author | holds | The book pioneered a new concept of religious language in Qur’anic studies, yet scholars, including al-Khuli’s students, remained wary; Aisha Abd al-Rahman did not follow its path and stayed within a closed, repetitive historical approach.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f25] RIFAI-KHULI · author | holds | The author presents Aisha Abd al-Rahman, known as Bint al-Shati, as the finest portraitist of al-Khuli the human in her dedication to him.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f26] RIFAI-KHULI · عائشة عبد الرحمن | reports | In that dedication, Bint al-Shati describes al-Khuli as embodying human grandeur and frailty, and says he sharpened her sense of humanity’s story from its beginning to its end.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f3] RIFAI-KHULI · author | reports | Both books stirred controversy, and their authors were accused of apostasy while public opinion was mobilized against them.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f4] RIFAI-KHULI · author | reports | The author places Khalafallah’s 1947 dissertation among three especially controversial Arabic books of the period; the committee rejected it and withheld his doctorate.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f5] RIFAI-KHULI · محمد أحمد خلف الله | reports | Khalafallah argues that Qur’anic narratives should be studied as religious and literary texts, not historical documents: the Qur’an usually obscures time and place, since its aim is religious and social guidance that moves hearts and minds.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f6] RIFAI-KHULI · محمد أحمد خلف الله | reports | He says Qur’anic stories aim at admonition, not teaching history or establishing historical facts; readers should not infer what the Qur’an did not intend.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f7] RIFAI-KHULI · محمد أحمد خلف الله | reports | Khalafallah allows that some Qur’anic stories may draw on myths without compromising revelation: the Qur’an rejects myths as proof of Muhammad’s authorship, and he surveys nine occurrences of asāṭīr.
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f8] RIFAI-KHULI · محمد أحمد خلف الله | reports | He treats Qur’anic narratives as parables and adduces the story of the town’s messengers and the wives of Noah and Lot as examples. | mentions 36:13, 66:10
[RIFAI-KHULI:c7/f9] RIFAI-KHULI · الفخر الرازي | reports | Al-Razi defines narrative as discourse that guides to religion, truth, and salvation; “truth” points to proofs for justice and prophethood. | mentions 3:62, 11:120
