## THE CURRENT MAP
72:20/q01 How is the opening word read? [قُلْ · readings] (turns on: Whether the opening is a direct command, fitting following commands, or a past report continuing the preceding narration; the attributed readers and manuscript observation are also cited.)
  72:20/q01/p1 Qul, an imperative commanding the Prophet to say the declaration. — reasons: Farrāʾ reports al-Aʿmash, ʿĀṣim, and most Medinan readers with this form. Ibn Khālawayh says dropping the alif preserves God's direct command. Fārisī connects it to repeated commands in 72:21–22; Wāḥidī, Bayḍāwī, and Ālūsī also cite what follows. Wāḥidī cites Muqātil's report of revelation in response to the Meccans. Reported reader lists vary: ʿĀṣim and Ḥamzah recur; Abū Jaʿfar appears in Jishumī, Baghawī, and Ibn ʿĀshūr; Abū ʿAmr is reported by Fārisī, Abū Ḥayyān, and Ālūsī in one transmission. Ibn ʿĀshūr makes it a fresh part of the revelation, with an implied 'it was revealed to me ... then say.' Samīn notes parallels at 17:93, 21:4, and 23:118.
  72:20/q01/p2 Qāla, a past-tense report that he said the declaration. — reasons: Farrāʾ transmits this from ʿAlī. Fārisī says it continues the preceding third-person narration; Ibn Khālawayh says retaining the alif reports that the Prophet first obeyed and then told of it. Wāḥidī, Baghawī, and Ibn ʿĀshūr take the speaker as the Messenger or servant; Wāḥidī says it answers the opponents' demand. Most readers or the remaining readers are assigned this form by several sources; Samīn reports al-Jaḥdarī's observation that the manuscript has qāla. Qirāʾāt-ER says the quoted content stays the same.
72:20/q02 How does the declaration connect syntactically to the preceding and following clauses? [قُلْ إِنَّمَآ · grammar] (turns on: Naḥḥās accepts both constructions; Ibn ʿĀshūr connects the explanatory or fresh construction to the past or imperative reading.)
  72:20/q02/p1 It can continue and explain the preceding report, with the following clause coordinated. — reasons: Naḥḥās allows a connected construction and coordination of the next clause. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the past-tense reading explains the servant's preceding act of calling upon God.
  72:20/q02/p2 It can begin a fresh clause, and the following clause can also stand independently. — reasons: Naḥḥās explicitly allows independence for both. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the imperative reading starts a new revealed instruction, supplied as 'it was revealed to me ... then say.'
72:20/q03 What account of a negative use of inna is reported? [إِنَّمَآ · grammar]
  72:20/q03/p1 Some deny negative inna and supply an omitted mā in comparable expressions. — reasons: Burhān cites the example mā in al-kāfirūn illā fī ghurūr and a line of poetry in support of omission.
72:20/q04 What does 'I call upon my Lord' mean? [أَدْعُوا۟ رَبِّى · interpretation]
  72:20/q04/p1 The Prophet invokes and worships his Lord alone, relying on Him and seeking His protection. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyyah explicitly glosses adʿū as 'I worship.' Qurṭubī and Nasafī explain exclusive invocation and worship. Ibn Kathīr adds seeking refuge and relying on God alone. Elmālili adds that God is his Creator and Lord and that he neither hopes nor fears in another. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the restriction excludes worship of anyone besides God. Biqāʿī names Wadd, Suwāʿ, and Yaghūth among excluded partners, whether idol or living being.
72:20/q05 What point does this declaration make to those who opposed or wondered at him? [أَدْعُوا۟ رَبِّى · interpretation] (turns on: The notes give different emphases for the audience's reaction: hostility to exclusive worship and suspicion of worldly designs.)
  72:20/q05/p1 Worship of God alone is ordinary and gives no cause for amazement, disgust, blame, or united hostility. — reasons: Kashshāf says the Prophet does nothing strange; Bayḍāwī, Bursawī, and Ālūsī likewise deny that exclusive worship warrants wonder or collective opposition. Nasafī applies this to the jinn's crowding and amazement. Abū Ḥayyān reports the hostile crowd explanation, with amazed jinn as another audience. Ibn Kathīr describes opponents joining to defeat the truth; Bursawī adds that others should adopt this state. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the audience was bewildered by worship unlike what it had seen; worship of one's recognized Lord deserves no blame. Elmālili and Iṣlāḥī mention hostile opponents' surprise or disgust.
  72:20/q05/p2 He disavows the worldly schemes and tricks that his audience suspects. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī says his sole purpose is to worship his Lord.
72:20/q06 How is the yāʾ of 'my Lord' pronounced? [رَبِّى · readings] (turns on: The vowel or lack of vowel on the yāʾ.)
  72:20/q06/p1 The yāʾ is opened. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyyah records the difference without naming readers here.
  72:20/q06/p2 The yāʾ is quiescent. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyyah records the difference without naming readers here.
72:20/q07 What does the denial of association exclude? [وَلَآ أُشْرِكُ بِهِۦٓ أَحَدًۭا · interpretation]
  72:20/q07/p1 It excludes every partner in worship alongside God. — reasons: Qurṭubī and Nasafī state the denial directly. Biqāʿī includes Wadd, Suwāʿ, and Yaghūth, and says a partner could be an idol or living being. Iṣlāḥī says opponents assign partners a share with God, but the Prophet refuses them.
72:20/q08 Why add an explicit denial of association after the exclusive call? [وَلَآ أُشْرِكُ بِهِۦٓ أَحَدًۭا · rhetoric]
  72:20/q08/p1 It reinforces the restriction and gives the denial special emphasis. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the restriction already means worship of God alone; separate wording stresses the exclusion of any partner.
72:20/q09 How is a moving hamza after yāʾ or wāw handled here? [بِهِۦٓ أَحَدًۭا · readings] (turns on: Whether yāʾ or wāw is intrinsic or added for lengthening or liaison, and whether a long letter can be ignored in easing the hamza.)
  72:20/q09/p1 Transfer and assimilation are both transmitted even when the preceding long letter is additional. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī says readers who ease the corresponding category after alif apply both treatments after yāʾ or wāw, intrinsic or added, including liaison forms; he says analogy would require only assimilation.
  72:20/q09/p2 Transfer is used with yāʾ and wāw generally, but added lengthening or liaison letters take assimilation only. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī prefers this distinction, following Abū ʿAbd Allāh al-Ṣāʾigh.
  72:20/q09/p3 The hamza can be eased as if it followed a vowel, ignoring the preceding long letter. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī attributes this extension to Abū al-ʿAlāʾ alone and says reciters and grammarians do not know it.
72:20/q10 What inward practice does exclusive calling entail? [أَدْعُوا۟ رَبِّى · inward]
  72:20/q10/p1 Affirm God's oneness and turn away from everything else to avoid association. — reasons: Qāshānī gives this as the inner sense of calling only the Lord.
72:20/q11 Who speaks the declaration, and to whom? [* · referent] (turns on: The identity of the crowd and the antecedent of kādū in the preceding verse, alongside the opening reading.)
  72:20/q11/p1 The Prophet speaks to the hostile Meccan or Quraysh crowd. — reasons: Kashshāf refers to people gathered against him. Wāḥidī and Baghawī connect the answer to the Meccans' demand. Ṭabrisī says the reply supports Quraysh as the preceding crowd. Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī report this as a reading of the past form; Abū Ḥayyān says the imperative can address polytheists. Ibn Kathīr calls them those who harmed and opposed him. Study Quran says it answers Quraysh objections.
  72:20/q11/p2 The Prophet speaks to jinn gathered in wonder. — reasons: Kashshāf says the surprising act would be invoking someone beside God, rather than worshipping Him alone. Nasafī connects the words to the jinn's amazement and crowding. Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī report this alternative; Abū Ḥayyān says the imperative can also address jinn.
  72:20/q11/p3 The jinn quote the Prophet's declaration to their own people. — reasons: Kashshāf, Abū Ḥayyān, and Ālūsī report this possibility. Abū Ḥayyān says it depends on the antecedent of kādū.
72:20/q12 What event prompted this declaration? [* · reports]
  72:20/q12/p1 Meccan opponents demanded that Muhammad abandon his unprecedented monotheistic message, with some reports adding an offer of protection. — reasons: Muqātil says Quraysh called the message unprecedented and said it made Muhammad enemies, then offered protection if he left it. Wāḥidī, Baghawī, Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī, and Qurṭubī repeat variants: they call the message extraordinary or grave and demand return to the old religion. Kuranyolu repeats the protection offer. Study Quran says they accused him of infringing on people and demanded he abandon the message. Wāḥidī treats this report as support for the imperative reading; the verse gives the answer.
72:20/q13 Who is the servant in the preceding verse, in light of this one? [* · referent] (turns on: Which opening reading better fits the servant mentioned in the preceding verse; Ibn ʿAṭiyyah says either identity remains possible.)
  72:20/q13/p1 Muhammad. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyyah says the imperative qul supports this identity while allowing either.
  72:20/q13/p2 Noah. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyyah says the qāla reading, used by most of the Seven and attributed to ʿAlī, supports this identity.
72:20/q14 How does this declaration fit the movement of the surrounding passage? [* · links]
  72:20/q14/p1 It concludes the account of the servant's calling and leads into renewed instruction to the Prophet. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says it explains the preceding description of calling; verses 21–23 then begin a fresh address after hidden matters about followers and rejecters, instructing the Prophet how to answer opponents. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the jinn story yields the Prophet's mission, divine oneness, and resurrection, then closes by indicating the messenger's protection from error.
