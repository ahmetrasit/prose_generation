## THE CURRENT MAP
17:14/q01 How is the command to read introduced in the sentence? [ٱقْرَأْ كِتَٰبَكَ · grammar]
  17:14/q01/p1 An omitted saying introduces direct speech to the person. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ and al-Kisāʾī compare omitted speech in 40:46 and 3:106. Al-Ṭabarī identifies God as speaker and the angels' deed-register as object; al-Rāzī says God speaks through angels. Al-Samīn allows the clause to be adjectival or circumstantial; al-Ālūsī also allows an independent sentence. Ibn ʿĀshūr says context makes the speaker plain.
17:14/q02 How is the imperative ٱقْرَأْ formed or pronounced? [ٱقْرَأْ · readings]
  17:14/q02/p1 Its imperative drops the hamza's ḍamma; a light or replaced hamza is also reported. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās adduces Arab replacement of hamza and Zuhayr's line وإلا يبد بالظلم يظلم; Ibn al-Jawzī reports Abū Jaʿfar's light hamza.
17:14/q03 Can someone who was unable to read in life read the record then? [ٱقْرَأْ · interpretation]
  17:14/q03/p1 Yes; everyone reads the record at resurrection, including the formerly illiterate. — reasons: Qatāda is repeatedly transmitted; al-Ḥasan says literate and illiterate alike. Al-Biqāʿī says God grants the ability and each reads personally. Ibn ʿĀshūr allows miraculous understanding of inscriptions.
17:14/q04 What does reading one's book mean here? [ٱقْرَأْ كِتَٰبَكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether reading concerns a displayed register or the soul's disclosed knowledge.)
  17:14/q04/p1 The person reads or understands the displayed account of deeds. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī calls it the angels' counted register; Ibn ʿĀshūr says reading can mean knowledge of recorded deeds or miraculous understanding of writing. Al-Biqāʿī stresses personal reading rather than another reader. Al-Ṭabrisī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī report that the person recalls every deed as if performing it then, explaining 18:49.
  17:14/q04/p2 The intellect reads the soul's exposed impressions when bodily attachment ends. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says each deed leaves a lasting effect, like water drops boring stone; conventional letters signify sounds by agreement, whereas soul impressions are intrinsic. He cites 50:22 and affirms outward reports as true too. Al-Ālūsī calls this remote from the plain sense; Asad reports al-Rāzī's allegory of complete recollection.
17:14/q05 Why command the person to read the record? [ٱقْرَأْ كِتَٰبَكَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the imperative primarily elicits acknowledgment or sharpens rebuke.)
  17:14/q05/p1 It establishes proof by making the person recognize and confess the deeds, leaving no excuse. — reasons: Al-Māwardī says reading makes one acknowledge the deeds. Al-Jishumī says it completes proof and shows no injustice. Al-Ṭabrisī says every deed and recompense is seen. Ibn al-Jawzī adds that Hell follows sin while Paradise is grace. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it a compelled challenge and says the person already knows the deeds.
  17:14/q05/p2 It heightens reproach and rebuke. — reasons: Al-Māwardī offers this as another purpose of making the person read.
17:14/q06 What is the person's book? [كِتَٰبَكَ · referent] (turns on: Whether 'book' denotes the written register, deeds themselves, or a figurative record within the person.)
  17:14/q06/p1 It is the complete record of the person's deeds, displayed at resurrection. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says recording angels keep and count it; al-Ḥasan's report says two noble angels write it, roll it at death, and place it around the neck until resurrection. Study Quran cites 82:10–12 and lists parallels 17:71, 18:49, 69:19, 69:25, 84:7, 84:10. Al-Biqāʿī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī say no deed is omitted; the latter says it was already prepared but is brought out from heedlessness.
  17:14/q06/p2 The book is the actual deeds and their revealed realities, rather than ordinary written signs. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites 3:30 and 50:22: deeds themselves appear with consequences, and the clinging 'bird' and book name the same deed. Their exposure confirms unavoidable recompense and removes concealment.
  17:14/q06/p3 The book can be the soul inscribed with enduring traces of deeds. — reasons: Al-Rāzī likens habitual acts to drops wearing stone; soul impressions outlast conventional letters and become apparent on separation from the body. Study Quran reports this and cites bodily testimony at 24:24, 36:65, 41:20–22. Al-Ālūsī calls the reading remote but reports the view that outward and inward senses coexist. Al-Rāzī says the impressions reveal felicity or misery and explicitly affirms this spiritual sense alongside outward narrated events.
  17:14/q06/p4 The book can represent the person and full recollection of the past life. — reasons: Some commentators in Study Quran picture tongue as pen, saliva as ink, limbs as page and memory as dictated record; al-Qurṭubī gives tongue, saliva and limbs as a pious saying. Asad transmits al-Rāzī's allegory of complete comprehension, citing 37:19, 39:68 and 50:22.
17:14/q07 What does a report say happens when a believer receives and reads the book? [كِتَٰبَكَ · reports]
  17:14/q07/p1 A believer sees sins within and good deeds outside, then learns the sins were forgiven and rejoices. — reasons: Ubayy says the person led many to good, fears sins, finds them forgiven and good deeds doubled; his face brightens, he receives the book in his right hand, a crown, two garments, ornaments at every joint and Adam's sixty-cubit stature, then announces the reward to companions with 69:19–23. Bakr says the visible good deeds draw envy, and private forgiveness brings joy, citing 69:19 and, in al-Rāzī, 80:38–39. Al-Ālūsī's version begins with feared sins, then forgiven sins and increasing light and joy, citing 69:19–20.
17:14/q08 What does a report say happens when an unbeliever reads the book? [كِتَٰبَكَ · reports]
  17:14/q08/p1 The unbeliever sees good deeds rejected and then sins, with darkness and lament. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī's report cites 25:23 for rejected deeds and 69:25–26 for the lament.
17:14/q09 What is the grammatical subject of كَفَىٰ and the function of ب in بِنَفْسِكَ? [كَفَىٰ بِنَفْسِكَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the bāʾ introduces the overt subject or belongs to a different analysis of kafā.)
  17:14/q09/p1 Nafsika is the subject in nominative position; bāʾ is added for emphasis. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ says the bāʾ can be omitted with a raised noun in praise or blame, unlike ordinary verbs, and adduces كفى الهدي. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī cite poetry without bāʾ; Ibn ʿĀshūr compares 4:79. Al-Ālūsī calls this the majority parse.
  17:14/q09/p2 The subject is an implicit notion of sufficiency, so bāʾ is not extra. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn mention this analysis; al-Ālūsī includes it among alternatives. Al-Burhān supplies kāna after kafā, with bi-nafsika its predicate, or a pronoun explained by accusative ḥasīban as in نعم رجلا زيد.
  17:14/q09/p3 Kafā is an imperative-like form with an implicit addressee, and bāʾ is not extra. — reasons: Al-Samīn finds the analysis weak because kafā accepts verbal inflections; al-Ālūsī says the majority prefers the ordinary past-verb parse. Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports the indeclinable imperative-like meaning اكتف.
17:14/q10 Why is كَفَىٰ masculine although نَفْسِكَ is grammatically feminine? [كَفَىٰ بِنَفْسِكَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether masculine agreement is allowed with the feminine noun nafs.)
  17:14/q10/p1 Masculine agreement is permitted because nafs is grammatically, not naturally, feminine. — reasons: Al-Samīn cites Qur'anic feminine agreement at 21:6 and 6:4 yet allows masculine for figurative gender; Ṭabāṭabāʾī makes the same point. Al-Ālūsī notes the difficulty and analogous verbs that do agree in feminine.
  17:14/q10/p2 The lack of feminine agreement argues that bāʾ is not extra and nafs is not the subject. — reasons: Al-Burhān reasons that if bāʾ were extra, kafā would agree with normally feminine nafs; he offers omitted kāna or an explanatory pronoun as parses.
17:14/q11 What does بِنَفْسِكَ refer to as the sufficient witness? [بِنَفْسِكَ · referent] (turns on: Whether self-witnessing is the person's direct knowledge or testimony of bodily members.)
  17:14/q11/p1 It means the person's own self and knowledge of the deeds. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān calls this the apparent sense and notes Abū ʿUbayda's emphasis on what the person knows; Ibn Kathīr says the person remembers and knows no injustice or alien deed was recorded. Al-Ālūsī calls the bodily-limbs reading contrary to the apparent meaning.
  17:14/q11/p2 It means the person's bodily limbs testifying if the person denies. — reasons: Muqātil compares the denial in 6:23 and testimony in 75:14, specifying self, tongue, hands and feet. Al-Biqāʿī says the tongue may deny but limbs confirm the precise record. Abū Ḥayyān transmits Muqātil; al-Ālūsī objects that this departs from the apparent meaning.
17:14/q12 What does ٱلْيَوْمَ modify? [ٱلْيَوْمَ · grammar]
  17:14/q12/p1 It is the time adverb governed by kafā. — reasons: Al-Samīn gives the attachment; al-Bursawī and al-Ālūsī call it the adverb.
17:14/q13 What does حَسِيبًا mean? [حَسِيبًا · meaning] (turns on: Whether ḥasīb is understood from counting and judgment or from sufficiency and testimony.)
  17:14/q13/p1 An accountant or reckoner who audits and calls to account. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba gives the active sense beside sufficient; al-Wāḥidī compares God's epithet in 33:39. Al-Zamakhsharī likens the form to occupational nouns; al-Kalbī and others give accountant or intensive reckoner. Al-Qurṭubī's pious saying pictures tongue as pen, saliva as ink and limbs as witnesses.
  17:14/q13/p2 A witness against oneself. — reasons: Muqātil says no better witness than oneself can be found; al-Zajjāj says the self suffices as witness. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Nasafī explain that a witness suffices a claimant in the matter at issue.
  17:14/q13/p3 One who suffices, standing in place of a witness or accountant. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba offers sufficient as an alternative to recko
