## THE CURRENT MAP
39:71/q01 Who are those who disbelieved? [ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوٓا۟ · referent] (turns on: Whether the reference is all deniers or the idolaters specifically.)
  39:71/q01/p1 They are those who reject God's oneness or signs. — reasons: Muqatil specifies denial of God's oneness. Biqa'i says they cover the light of intellect and become confused. Tabataba'i associates them with obstinate rejection of God's signs.
  39:71/q01/p2 They are the idolaters addressed in this passage. — reasons: Islahi says associating partners with God is disbelief because only pure monotheism is acceptable.
39:71/q02 What does their being driven to Hell convey? [وَسِيقَ إِلَىٰ جَهَنَّمَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether sawq denotes coercion, general conveyance, or the inward force of deeds.)
  39:71/q02/p1 They are urged or forced toward Hell, in humiliation. — reasons: Tabataba'i cites al-Majmaʿ for sawq as urging onward. Thalabi says they are dragged on their faces; Ibn Zaydan cites 52:13, 107:2 and 19:86 for pushing. Nasafi compares prisoners or rebels led to confinement or execution. Ibn Kathir adds thirst, threats, deafness, dumbness, blindness and some walking on their faces (17:97; 19:85–86). Ibn Ashur explains a driver goes ahead and beside one forced along, comparing 8:6. Biqa'i says God's command moves them swiftly after judgment; Bursevi adds the Persian gloss, their disgrace, and Hell's scowl. Study Quran connects heavy and light scales (23:102–103; 101:6–9) and 52:13. Abu Hayyan says driving normally implies urging and force.
  39:71/q02/p2 All souls are driven; their destinations distinguish them. — reasons: Samarra'i compares 50:21, where every soul comes with a driver and witness: unbelievers go to Hell and the God-fearing to Paradise.
  39:71/q02/p3 Inwardly, their deeds and lower selves drive them. — reasons: Qashani reads the veiled as guided by desire and downward inclination.
39:71/q03 What are the zumar, and how are the groups formed? [زُمَرًا · meaning] (turns on: Whether the word denotes succession, group membership or size, derives from sound, or evokes a sounding form of driving.)
  39:71/q03/p1 Separate groups follow one another. — reasons: Majaz cites al-Akhtal; Mawrdi cites Khuffaf ibn Nudba; Qurtubi cites two unnamed poets and notes zumrah as singular, with a pattern comparison. Abu Hayyan and Samin cite a line on group after group. Nasafi and Samin call zumar circumstantial accusative. Ibn Ashur says succession is intrinsic, rather than one isolated group. Muqatil and others add that each nation's unbelievers may be separate.
  39:71/q03/p2 The groups are distinct communities or peoples. — reasons: Al-Kalbi calls them communities; Muqatil and Thalabi say each nation's unbelievers are gathered separately.
  39:71/q03/p3 They are classes arranged by kind or degree of error and sin. — reasons: Biqa'i reports Abu Ubayd: like people are gathered with like. Baydawi, Bursevi, Alusi and Ibn Ashur distinguish degrees of misguidance or wickedness. Ibn Ashur specifies Quraysh's treatment of Muslims and the Prophet, or polytheists' kinds of shirk; Islahi says the gates receive groups classified by sins.
  39:71/q03/p4 A zumrah is a small group. — reasons: Al-Raghib relates the sense to sparse hair and scant manliness; Baydawi and Samin report the same derivation. Samin also mentions an ostrich's cry and derived music and wanton-woman terms.
  39:71/q03/p5 The word is connected with a group's sound. — reasons: Baydawi, Biqa'i, Bursevi and Alusi connect zamr to the sound or noise a gathering makes; Biqa'i compares a flute, and Samin reports al-Raghib's related sound words.
  39:71/q03/p6 It can suggest being pushed and rebuked with a pipe-like sound. — reasons: Mawrdi compares the phrase about David's pipes; Qurtubi records a gloss connecting the sound to mizmar.
39:71/q04 How are the passive vowels of sīqa and jīʾa read? [وَسِيقَ جَآءُوهَا · readings] (turns on: The transmitted passive-vowel pronunciations.)
  39:71/q04/p1 The initial letters are pronounced with kasra. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya attributes this to the majority.
  39:71/q04/p2 The vowels incline toward ḍamma. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya names al-Hasan, Ibn Waththab, Asim and al-Aʿmash; Qiraat-ER says the vowel texture changes without changing the consonantal verb or procession.
39:71/q05 When and how do Hell's gates open? [حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَا جَآءُوهَا فُتِحَتْ أَبْوَٰبُهَا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the scene is explained as a physical prison-like opening or an inward account of Hell's affinity.)
  39:71/q05/p1 They stay shut until the disbelievers arrive, then open for entry. — reasons: Muqatil adds that folded records are spread. Nahhas contrasts Paradise's already-open gates. Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, Biqa'i, Bursevi, Alusi and Samarra'i compare a prison: the wait humiliates, terrifies or spiritually punishes them; Abu Hayyan and Alusi say the doors close after entry. Ibn Kathir says opening on arrival starts punishment at once; Biqa'i says heat and flames rush out. Study Quran compares 16:29. Bursevi cites 15:44 for the seven gates.
  39:71/q05/p2 Inwardly, Hell opens because it longs for those suited to it. — reasons: Qashani interprets Hell's eagerness as the reason for opening at arrival.
39:71/q06 How many gates does Hell have? [أَبْوَٰبُهَا · interpretation]
  39:71/q06/p1 Hell has seven gates. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and Bursevi cite 15:44; Qurtubi refers to discussion in Surat al-Hijr. Tabataba'i says the seven gates open for entry.
39:71/q07 What are the seven gates or levels called and ordered? [أَبْوَٰبُهَا · reports] (turns on: The reports reverse the order of the named levels.)
  39:71/q07/p1 From lowest upward: Jahannam, Lahza, Hutama, Saqar, Jahim, Sa'ir, Hāwiya. — reasons: Tabataba'i reports this from Ali, describing seven stacked gates or levels.
  39:71/q07/p2 Hāwiya is lowest and Jahannam highest. — reasons: Tabataba'i reports this reversed order from al-Kalbi.
39:71/q08 What do Hell's seven gates signify inwardly? [أَبْوَٰبُهَا · inward]
  39:71/q08/p1 They signify seven blameworthy traits through which entrants pass. — reasons: Bursevi lists pride, stinginess, greed, lust, envy, anger and rancor; the soul should be purified of them.
39:71/q09 How does the construction with ḥattā and idhā work? [حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَا جَآءُوهَا فُتِحَتْ أَبْوَٰبُهَا · grammar] (turns on: Whether the expressed opening completes the conditional directly or an implied repeated arrival does.)
  39:71/q09/p1 The answer to idhā is 'its gates were opened'. — reasons: Nahhas contrasts Paradise's omitted answer. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan say the explicit answer makes arrival the condition of opening; Baydawi says ḥattā introduces and depicts the following sentence. Bursevi calls it a narrative clause; Ibn Ashur calls ḥattā a new start and idhā a future time adverb with conditional force; Samarra'i says omitting mā presents an ordinary arrival-opening sequence, unlike 41:20.
  39:71/q09/p2 The full implied construction repeats 'they arrived' before the doors opened. — reasons: Burhan says ḥattā requires completion, so an implied repetition of jāʾūhā completes it.
39:71/q10 How is futiḥat read, and what does the form suggest? [فُتِحَتْ · readings] (turns on: Whether the form is geminated to mark repeated or emphatic action, or light for a single opening.)
  39:71/q10/p1 The tāʾ is doubled, conveying repeated, abundant or intensified opening. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid and Ibn al-Jawzi name Ibn Kathir, Nafiʿ, Abu ʿAmr and Ibn ʿAmir; Ibn Atiyya calls this the majority. Ibn Khalawayh says each door's opening may recur and cites 12:23 and 38:50; Thalabi and Baghawi describe repeated or abundant action. Qiraat-ER allows multiple gate access or emphatic opening.
  39:71/q10/p2 The tāʾ is light, describing a single opening. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid and Ibn al-Jawzi name Asim, Hamza and al-Kisa'i; Ibn Atiyya also lists Talha and al-Aʿmash. Thalabi, Baghawi, Baydawi and Nasafi call it the Kufan reading. Ibn Khalawayh prefers it because this action is not repeated.
39:71/q11 Why is there no waw before Hell's gate opening, unlike Paradise's? [فُتِحَتْ أَبْوَٰبُهَا · rhetoric] (turns on: The grammatical force of waw and whether the gates stand open before arrival.)
  39:71/q11/p1 Hell's arrival triggers opening; Paradise's gates are already open. — reasons: Nahhas reports the Basran view that waw is meaningful coordination, unlike the Kufan claim that it is extra. Ibn Atiyya compares open homes ready for joyful visitors. Abu Ali rejects the waw-of-eight account, taking Paradise's waw as circumstantial. Samarra'i compares open Paradise with a closed prison and cites 38:50.
  39:71/q11/p2 The Paradise waw is extra and the answer is its gates opening. — reasons: Jishumi reports the construction joining arrival and opening; Nahhas assigns the extra-waw account to Kufans and says Basrans reject it.
  39:71/q11/p3 Paradise has eight gates, Hell seven; the waw is a waw of eight. — reasons: Jishumi reports this proposed explanation. Abu Ali explicitly rejects it.
  39:71/q11/p4 Paradise's answer is omitted because its blessings exceed expression. — reasons: Nahhas states the Paradise answer is omitted. Samarra'i says language cannot encompass the blessings and the answer lies within Paradise.
  39:71/q11/p5 The Paradise waw can coordinate an appositional expression. — reasons: Jishumi adduces 21:48, where Moses and Aaron's Criterion and light exemplify conjunction understood as apposition.
39:71/q12 Who are Hell's keepers? [خَزَنَتُهَآ · referent] (turns on: Literal angelic custodians and Qashani's symbolic account.)
  39:71/q12/p1 They are angels appointed to guard and punish Hell's people. — reasons: Nasafi and Bursevi specify their punitive charge; Ibn Kathir calls them the harsh and powerful Zabāniya. Qurtubi gives khāzin as the singular of khazana, like sādin and sadana. Study Quran says there are nineteen, citing 74:30; Tabataba'i calls them angels.
  39:71/q12/p2 Inward
