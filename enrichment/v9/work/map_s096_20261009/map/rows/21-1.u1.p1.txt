21:1/q09 What does ḥisāb mean? [حِسَابُهُمْ · meaning] (turns on: Whether ḥisāb names the accounting process, the resulting punishment, a counted quantity, or a promised event.)
  21:1/q09/p1 It is God's accounting of people's deeds and blessings, with recompense for good or evil. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī specifies inquiry into deeds, blessings and obedience; al-Būrsawī says it discloses what is for and against someone so recompense follows. Al-Biqāʿī includes enumerating and requital. Al-Jishumī equates ḥisāb and muḥāsaba as verbal nouns, with an Arabic cognate example.
  21:1/q09/p2 It can mean punishment or recompense. — reasons: ʿAṭāʾ from Ibn ʿAbbās argues that whoever has an account examined is punished; al-Ḍaḥḥāk identifies Mecca's Badr punishment. Al-Biqāʿī allows worldly requital at Badr and the Conquest.
  21:1/q09/p3 It can mean reckoning an amount or the amount reckoned. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān gives these two lexical senses while explaining the approach of the Hour.
  21:1/q09/p4 It means what has been promised. — reasons: Ibn Jurayj's report gives this gloss without further detail.
21:1/q10 Why name the Resurrection by the account? [حِسَابُهُمْ · rhetoric] (turns on: Which feature of the Day the wording aims to foreground.)
  21:1/q10/p1 Accounting exposes people's state and is the most consequential, fear inducing part of the Day. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says the name makes the warning more frightening; al-Būrsawī calls it the event weighing most on hearts. Al-Ālūsī says naming the account suffices to evoke the Hour's consequences and that even a precursor should deter denial.
  21:1/q10/p2 It suits the ensuing criticism of heedlessness toward reminders. — reasons: Abū al-Suʿūd says the discourse immediately turns to heedlessness and turning away; al-Ālūsī questions whether this fully explains the wording.
  21:1/q10/p3 It makes accountability for deeds, rather than a spectacle of terror, the warning's focus. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the context calls people to heed the divine reminder seriously; if terrors were intended, 'Day of Reckoning' or its time would be more fitting.
21:1/q11 What does 'their' in 'their account' emphasize? [حِسَابُهُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the pronoun is read as general human responsibility or a targeted Meccan threat.)
  21:1/q11/p1 The addressees themselves must answer for deeds and divine blessings. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says they will answer for blessings and obedience; Ibn al-Zubayr connects 20:131 and 102:8 with the previous warning against coveting worldly goods; Kur'an Yolu frames life, death, and blessings as a test calling for gratitude and obedience.
  21:1/q11/p2 The account of sinful Meccan polytheists is at issue. — reasons: Al-Būrsawī links their evil deeds to resurrection and al-Biqāʿī to possible Badr reprisal.
21:1/q12 What is ghafla? [غَفْلَةٍ · meaning] (turns on: Whether ghafla means bare inattention, ignorance of the outcome, or deliberate neglect.)
  21:1/q12/p1 It is inattention or lack of vigilance and alertness. — reasons: Al-Jishumī calls it loss of a meaning from the mind, opposite to alertness; al-Būrsawī traces it to insufficient vigilance.
  21:1/q12/p2 It is ignorance of the reckoning and what God will do. — reasons: Al-Kalbī glosses it as ignorance; exegetes say people do not know what awaits them. Al-Ālūsī reports either ignorance confined to the account or ignorance extending to God, revelation, messengers, resurrection and recompense.
  21:1/q12/p3 It can mean neglect or excessive immersion. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī cites Arabic usage and 17:67 for the intense sense, and 23:17 for neglect, which avoids a conflict with deliberate turning away.
21:1/q13 What kind of heedlessness is condemned here? [غَفْلَةٍ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the description is denial, worldly absorption, sinful failure to prepare, or inward absorption in God.)
  21:1/q13/p1 They fail to contemplate or prepare for the final account. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says worldly life keeps them from reflecting on its severe trials. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī add that reason points to requital of good and evil. Al-Būrsawī stresses outright denial, beyond simple carelessness; al-Ālūsī warns that heedlessness about requital produces every error. Al-Biqāʿī says resurrection or deadly events expose their heedlessness and humble survivors, who then discern truth from falsehood.
  21:1/q13/p2 They are engrossed in worldly life and desire, ignoring the Hereafter. — reasons: Reports attributed to the Prophet identify worldly life; al-Qushayrī says this alienation ends only at death. Al-Māwardī and al-Qurṭubī pair worldly absorption with turning from the afterlife; Study Quran reports the same.
  21:1/q13/p3 They are heedless through erroneous beliefs and turn from guidance. — reasons: Al-Māwardī states this alternative; al-Būrsawī says deniers shut out faith, signs and warnings.
  21:1/q13/p4 Believers can be heedless when they believe in the afterlife but fail to act. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya includes sinful believers in the warning; Kur'an Yolu names neglected repentance, worship, good works and avoidance of sin.
  21:1/q13/p5 Heedlessness can be absorption in God, beyond concern for world or even Paradise and Hell. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī contrasts this union with alienated worldly heedlessness: those effaced in divine existence do not return from ecstatic absence. Al-Nasafī says such people awaken only at the vision of the Lord; Study Quran transmits the Sufi distinction.
21:1/q14 Who is described as heedless and turning away? [وَهُمْ غَفْلَةٍ مُّعْرِضُونَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the ensuing traits restrict the general noun to polytheists or can also characterize believers.)
  21:1/q14/p1 The description singles out unbelievers, especially Meccan polytheists. — reasons: The following verses describe disbelievers' play and accusations; Ibn al-Jawzī says the account's approach is universal while this description is specific. Al-Ālūsī calls extending the description to monotheist sinners strained.
  21:1/q14/p2 It may apply to sinful believers as well as the disbelievers it first addresses. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya gives sinful believers a share; al-Ālūsī reports an argument that the traits need not restrict a generic 'people', but judges it far from the apparent sense. Kur'an Yolu distinguishes a believer's failure to prepare from a disbeliever's denial.
  21:1/q14/p3 It describes humanity generally while the later pronouns can narrow to Meccan polytheists. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports the grammatical possibility of generic nās with subsequent unannounced reference to Meccans.
21:1/q15 What are they turning away from? [مُّعْرِضُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Which warning or object their aversion concerns.)
  21:1/q15/p1 They turn from preparing for and thinking about their reckoning. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī and other commentators pair heedlessness with lack of preparation; al-Zamakhsharī says they shut their ears to warnings once roused.
  21:1/q15/p2 They turn from faith, the Qur'an, the Prophet's message, signs and warnings. — reasons: Yaḥyā says they turn from the Qur'an; ʿAṭāʾ from Ibn ʿAbbās says from what Muḥammad brought. Al-Būrsawī and al-Ālūsī specify faith and signs that should awaken them; Kur'an Yolu cites prophets and revelation.
  21:1/q15/p3 They turn away from the Hereafter or from guidance. — reasons: Al-Māwardī and al-Qurṭubī contrast their worldly involvement with aversion to the afterlife; another explanation pairs error with rejection of guidance.
  21:1/q15/p4 They are obstinate in heedlessness. — reasons: Asad renders the phrase literally this way, emphasizing persistence.
21:1/q16 What does muʿriḍūn imply lexically? [مُّعْرِضُونَ · meaning]
  21:1/q16/p1 Aʿraḍa is to turn aside while exposing one's side. — reasons: Al-Būrsawī gives the physical source of the figurative aversion.
21:1/q17 How can they be both heedless and turning away? [غَفْلَةٍ مُّعْرِضُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the phrases describe successive responses, a state beside an action, or a broader sense of neglect.)
  21:1/q17/p1 They initially fail to think of the outcome, then deliberately ignore warnings that awaken them. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī explain the sequence; al-Kashf distinguishes persistent neglect of rational proofs from rejection when scriptural proofs are presented. Al-Biqāʿī reverses the causal direction: turning from warnings produces heedlessness. Al-Ālūsī says even a remote chance of the warning's truth can unsettle them.
  21:1/q17/p2 Heedlessness is a settled state while turning away is a renewed act. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī contrasts the nominal prepositional clause with the participle, and considers treating them as one state weaker.
  21:1/q17/p3 Ghafla can mean neglect, so deliberate turning away is compatible with it. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī cites 23:17 and a usage of excessive heedlessness; these senses remove the apparent contradiction.
21:1/q18 How is the closing clause parsed? [وَهُمْ فِى غَفْلَةٍۢ مُّعْرِضُونَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the inner prepositional phrase is a predicate or a circumstance, and how the whole clause attaches.)
  21:1/q18/p1 The wāw introduces a circumstantial clause describing the people. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī and al-Jishumī identify the wāw; Sībawayh equates it to idh and cites 8:11. Al-Samīn says the whole nominal clause is a circumstance from lil-nās. Al-Qurṭubī calls it an independent report and allows an accusative circumstantial form outside the Qur'anic reading.
  21:1/q18/p2 Both 'in heedlessness' and 'turning away' are predicates of hum. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī offers this, Abū al-Baqāʾ treats the phrase as a second predicate, and al-Samīn notes it is actually first in order. Al-Būrsawī and al-Ālūsī likewise call turning away the second predicate.
