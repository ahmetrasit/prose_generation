  1:3/q12/p5 The second name makes small needs approachable after the exclusive majestic first. — reasons: Rāzī says humans might hesitate to ask the great Lord for a sandal strap or salt; a report has God invite Moses to request salt and fodder.
1:3/q13 Why does al-Raḥmān precede al-Raḥīm? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether form strength, divine exclusivity, intrinsic priority, or immediacy determines order.)
  1:3/q13/p1 Its stronger, more exclusive, name-like form comes first. — reasons: Jishumī says stronger intensity and exclusivity make it more definite; Biqāʿī orders existence, general mercy, then worship-specific mercy. Ibn ʿĀshūr records most scholars' stronger faʿlān view, while noting the expected rhetorical increase from weaker to stronger. Al-Suyūṭī gives raʾūf then raḥīm as another instance of a less emphatic term following a stronger one.
  1:3/q13/p2 An intrinsic attribute comes before its outward effects among recipients. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr makes essential character prior to the many objects of its mercy; 9:128 shows that the latter is also applied to the Messenger.
  1:3/q13/p3 Immediate renewed mercy is mentioned first because people desire what comes soon. — reasons: Sāmarrāʾī cites 75:20 and the contrast between faʿlān's renewed fullness and faʿīl's stability.
1:3/q14 How are the two names read and parsed in this verse? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · readings] (turns on: Case endings and whether the names continue the preceding construction or detach as praise.)
  1:3/q14/p1 Both are accusative as detached praise. — reasons: Abū al-ʿĀliya, Ibn al-Sumayfaʿ and ʿĪsā b. ʿUmar are named by Ibn al-Jawzī and Abū Ḥayyān; al-Ālūsī also names Zayd. Al-Naḥḥās allows an implied praising verb.
  1:3/q14/p2 Both are nominative as detached praise or an implied subject. — reasons: Abū Razīn al-ʿUqaylī, al-Rabīʿ b. Khuthaym and Abū ʿImrān al-Jawnī are named by Ibn al-Jawzī, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī.
  1:3/q14/p3 Both are genitive, attached as adjectives or apposition. — reasons: The majority connect the names to preceding divine epithets; Abū Ḥayyān allows adjectival, appositional and explanatory appositional parses. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says joining the alif of al-ḥamdu may resolve adjacent consonants.
  1:3/q14/p4 Mixed case endings are grammatically possible. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās allows one nominative and one accusative, or first genitive then second nominative or accusative.
  1:3/q14/p5 An opened final mīm joined to an alif was heard, but not established as a transmitted reading. — reasons: Al-Kisāʾī cites an Arab's pronunciation; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says he knows no reading transmitter for it.
1:3/q15 What does mercy mean when attributed to God? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · theology] (turns on: Whether it names essential willing, acts of benefit, both, or humanlike feeling.)
  1:3/q15/p1 It means God's willing good and bringing benefit, without human emotional change. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī calls willing good an essential attribute. Elmalılı prefers both essential will and beneficent acts and distinguishes Turkish rahmet from merhamet. Ibn ʿĀshūr distinguishes affect from effective outcome, invokes 42:11 and 7:156, and says common use makes the divine sense clear, not ambiguous under 3:7; al-Ghazālī says mercy unable to produce its effect is incomplete.
  1:3/q15/p2 It means the effect of mercy: rescue, gentleness and benefaction. — reasons: Exegetes reported by Elmalılı take the lexical transfer as an action attribute. Ibn ʿĀshūr says scripture directs ordinary language toward gentle action and aid rather than creaturely passion. Rāzī joins rescue from affliction and delivery of benefits.
  1:3/q15/p3 The names are truly divine but their ultimate reality exceeds human knowledge. — reasons: Sulamī invokes 20:110 against claims to comprehend God's essence; Elmalılı says divine predication is literally true, though not identical to human feeling. Ibn ʿĀshūr says scriptural language expresses exalted meaning as ordinary language permits.
  1:3/q15/p4 Divine mercy is the same tender emotion as human compassion. — reasons: Elmalılı and Ibn ʿĀshūr describe this human sense in order to rule it out for God, citing divine transcendence.
  1:3/q15/p5 It means prior willing of good, an essential attribute. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī and some exegetes in Elmalılı's account distinguish divine will from human tenderness; Elmalılı says willing is choice, not emotional affect.
1:3/q16 What instances display the mercy named here? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · interpretation] (turns on: Which observed benefits, rescue, and apparent harms demonstrate its reach.)
  1:3/q16/p1 Mercy also guides ordinary need, communal welfare, and free striving. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr points to accessible earth, purification, good character, and zakāt benefiting society. Elmalılı describes life's provision, choice, a just balance and a hoped-for eternal good; 20:5 depicts encompassing sovereignty and 10:26 promises more to those who do good.
  1:3/q16/p2 Creation, health, provision, insight, rescue, and even beneficial trials display mercy. — reasons: Rāzī cites medical discoveries, Galen's optic-nerve dream and vein cure, a raven feeding a bound man, a scorpion saving a youth, and gnats feeding a nestling. He argues schooling or amputation may hurt yet help, as al-Khiḍr's boat, boy and wall hide wisdom (18:79); worship disciplines the soul and Hell directs the wicked (17:7; 51:50). He says good generally exceeds evil and every atom testifies to mercy.
  1:3/q16/p3 Mercy remains greater than even a loving mother or the Prophet's compassion. — reasons: Rāzī recounts the dying youth reconciled with his mother, asks how God could burn a long-believing servant, and contrasts the Prophet's finite compassion at the broken tooth, debt funeral and slander with God's limitless mercy.
1:3/q17 How can divine mercy coexist with evil, punishment, and human choice? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · theology] (turns on: Whether the names fit created disbelief and Hell, and how God and humans share agency.)
  1:3/q17/p1 Trials and punishment may serve a wise mercy judged by their ultimate effect. — reasons: Rāzī distinguishes appearance from reality: slight harm can enable greater good, religious duties purify, and al-Khiḍr's acts seemed objectionable to Moses. Elmalılı says harm follows nonexistence, divided wills and false partnership; mercy orders a society otherwise ruined and keeps recompense just.
  1:3/q17/p2 God creates human capacities while people choose their striving under His continuing rule. — reasons: Elmalılı rejects both absolute determinism and autonomous creation. His examples are sudden paralysis, former Pharaohs' helplessness, and the limited, borrowed character of human authority; necessity witnesses divine power and choice divine will.
  1:3/q17/p3 The Qadariyya question how mercy fits eternal Hell, created unbelief, and blocked faith. — reasons: Rāzī reports their objections without resolving them in that note.
  1:3/q17/p4 The Jabriyya say God must create faith, the greatest mercy. — reasons: Rāzī reports their argument that if humans created faith, they would deserve these divine mercy names.
  1:3/q17/p5 Humans have absolute future control after God delegates creation's course. — reasons: Elmalılı raises this idea only to reject it as confusing temporary agency with independent ownership and as the root of injustice.
1:3/q18 What inward distinctions are drawn between the two names? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · inward] (turns on: How the two names address saints, creatures, disclosure, governance, or modes of nurture.)
  1:3/q18/p1 Al-Raḥmān nurtures through intermediaries, sometimes with painful medicine; al-Raḥīm nurtures directly through pure divine words. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī interprets the names as unpacking general lordship: the latter pure favor belongs to complete felicity.
  1:3/q18/p2 Al-Raḥmān addresses saints' secrets and prophets' spirits; al-Raḥīm sustains creatures in this life. — reasons: Sulamī includes good and wicked among the creatures sustained.
  1:3/q18/p3 Al-Raḥmān gives divine disclosure; al-Raḥīm governs. — reasons: Sulamī transmits this unnamed inward distinction.
  1:3/q18/p4 Al-Raḥmān reveals lights; al-Raḥīm preserves entrusted secrets. — reasons: Sulamī transmits this distinct account.
  1:3/q18/p5 Al-Raḥmān provides outer and inner sustenance. — reasons: Jaʿfar lists food, drink, health, intellect, knowledge, understanding, senses and aspiration.
1:3/q19 Why do the mercy names follow 'Lord of the worlds'? [ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ · links] (turns on: How lordship, mercy, fear, praise, and worship cohere in the sequence.)
  1:3/q19/p1 Mercy softens the awe of lordship and gives hope that supports obedience. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī cites 15:49-50 and 40:3 and a Muslim report that full knowledge of punishment or mercy would overwhelm hope or despair. Abū Ḥayyān says God's control makes mercy apt, and rejects alternatives 'constant one' or 'companion' for rabb; Sāmarrāʾī says a real nurturer should be merciful, citing 9:128.
  1:3/q19/p2 Mercy explains and completes lordly nurture, giving reason to continue praise. — reasons: Biqāʿī says lordship lacks full wellbeing without mercy and the names detail praise twice over. Ibn ʿĀshūr says weak creatures need gentle completion; benefits and duties can be arduous, so the name assures easing and public good. Al-Ālū
