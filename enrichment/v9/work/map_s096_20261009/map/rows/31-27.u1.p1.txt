and abḥur? [سَبْعَةُ أَبْحُرٍۢ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether seven is a limit or a sign of abundance.)
  31:27/q14/p1 Seven means great abundance and does not limit further supply. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says even a thousand would not change the point; Ibn Kathīr invokes 18:109's repeated supply. Al-Ālūsī cites heavens, planets, regions and days and the believer-one/unbeliever-seven saying; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it idiomatic.
  31:27/q14/p2 Abḥur is a paucity plural suited to the numeral seven. — reasons: Burhān, Abū Ḥayyān, Biqāʿī and al-Ālūsī note grammatical matching even where the image indicates abundance.
  31:27/q14/p3 Seven is a customary complete count with symbolic extensions. — reasons: Al-Rāzī derives it from seven climes, days and planets, three as minimal completeness and seven as the larger part of ten; he compares ritual threes, Hell's seven gates, Paradise's eight and al-Farrāʾ's 'wāw of eight.'
31:27/q15 What action is left implicit? [أَقْلَٰمٌۭ وَٱلْبَحْرُ كَلِمَٰتُ ٱللَّهِ · rhetoric]
  31:27/q15/p1 The pens and seas write God's words as ink and writing materials, then run out first. — reasons: Abū ʿUbaydah calls it compressed; al-Ṭabarī says pens break and ink dries. Al-Fārisī, al-Wāḥidī and al-Ṭabrisī compare other omitted actions at 26:63, 2:196 and 27:28–29; al-Biqāʿī and Islahi note omitted ink.
31:27/q16 What is the response to law? [مَّا نَفِدَتْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether mā naf idat is itself the answer.)
  31:27/q16/p1 Mā naf idat is the expressed answer. — reasons: Burhān says denial amid great supply proves denial with less; al-Ālūsī calls it the jawāb.
  31:27/q16/p2 The answer about exhausted materials is omitted before the stated denial. — reasons: Burhān offers this as an alternative, with possible omitted conjunction.
31:27/q17 What does naf idat mean? [مَّا نَفِدَتْ · meaning]
  31:27/q17/p1 To reach an end through exhaustion over time. — reasons: Al-Māwardī contrasts it with perishing all at once; al-Zajjāj applies the denial to God's words.
31:27/q18 What are God's kalimāt? [كَلِمَٰتُ ٱللَّهِ · referent] (turns on: Whether the term refers to knowledge, creative acts, eternal speech, revelation or promises.)
  31:27/q18/p1 God's decrees, possible creations, signs and wonders brought about by Be. — reasons: Al-Rāzī argues cause-for-effect naming and cites Jesus as a word; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites 36:82 and 4:171; Islahi says signs manifest the creative command. Al-Ālūsī's Imam prefers this connection to 31:26.
  31:27/q18/p2 God's limitless knowledge, known realities and wisdom. — reasons: Muqātil and al-Suddī include wonders; al-Naḥḥās details kinds, parts, qualities and change; al-Wāḥidī reasons limitless knowledge entails limitless expressing words. Abū ʿAlī distinguishes still-possible things from those already existing.
  31:27/q18/p3 God's eternal speech and meanings, including utterances, revelation and creative commands. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī contrasts finite writers with eternal word; Ibn ʿĀshūr includes speech to angels and prophets and the command Be. Al-Rāzī distinguishes created letters and arrangement from divine attributes.
  31:27/q18/p4 God's favors for the obedient, or promised reward and threatened punishment. — reasons: Al-Māwardī reports Paradise favors; Abū Muslim extends this to unseen reward and punishment.
  31:27/q18/p5 The Qur'an and further revelation to Muhammad. — reasons: Qatādah's reported occasion answers a prediction that recitation will soon end; Ibn ʿĀshūr says this reading implies much more revelation.
  31:27/q18/p6 Kalimāt has other Quranic senses outside this verse. — reasons: Al-Dāmghānī lists rites, Adam's received words, tawḥīd, creative wonders, Jesus, religion and the Quran.
31:27/q19 Why use the plural kalimāt? [كَلِمَٰتُ ٱللَّهِ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the small plural has force here.)
  31:27/q19/p1 Even a small number of God's words exceeds these supplies, all the more His total speech. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī, Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Biqāʿī, Bursevī and al-Ālūsī make the a fortiori claim; al-Samīn invokes Ḥassān's jufanāt.
  31:27/q19/p2 A sound plural in a general construction need not mean a small number. — reasons: Al-Shaykh objects to the strict paucity reading; al-Samīn notes debate about whether the article marks generality.
31:27/q20 What does the image establish? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the comparison asserts a precise quantity or simply proves inexhaustibility.)
  31:27/q20/p1 Finite materials, pages, writers and lives cannot exhaust divine words, knowledge, decrees or signs. — reasons: Muqātil says all creation's pens would fail; al-Ḥasan says the writer dies; Ibn ʿAṭiyyah says adding more cannot establish a limit. Burhān says non-exhaustion holds before and after ink is spent; Islahi says science has only found a drop.
  31:27/q20/p2 The wording denies exhaustion without asserting a precise limit on the imagined materials. — reasons: Burhān reports careful scholars using al-Khiḍr's bird-and-sea analogy; Ibn ʿAṭiyyah also rejects a bound beyond the stated seas.
31:27/q21 What follows about divine speech? [كَلِمَٰتُ ٱللَّهِ · theology] (turns on: Whether kalimāt must denote eternal speech or can denote limitless referents.)
  31:27/q21/p1 God's speech is eternal and uncreated, so its meanings do not end. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī infers uncreatedness; al-Qurṭubī prefers eternal speech on contextual grounds; Bursevī infers Quranic pre-eternity; al-Rāzī distinguishes divine attribute from created letters.
  31:27/q21/p2 Limitless knowledge and possibilities can be called words without treating them as eternal speech. — reasons: Abū ʿAlī confines some referents to future possibilities; Ibn ʿAṭiyyah observes this tends toward Muʿtazilī created-speech doctrine; al-Fārisī reports a wisdom-and-workmanship reading.
31:27/q22 What do Mighty and Wise contribute? [عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌۭ · interpretation]
  31:27/q22/p1 God cannot be thwarted and nothing escapes His knowledge and wise governance, so His words do not end. — reasons: Muqātil says mighty in kingdom and wise in command; Ibn Kathīr extends wisdom through creation, law and speech. Ibn ʿĀshūr reads the closure against hopes of prophetic failure; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī links it to God's sole governance; Islahi links unity, prophethood and resurrection.
31:27/q23 What occasion is reported? [* · reports] (turns on: Who challenged the message and on what issue.)
  31:27/q23/p1 Jewish scholars challenged little human knowledge by citing the Torah and great wisdom. — reasons: Versions place direct inquiry in Medina or through Quraysh in Mecca, sometimes starting with the spirit at 17:85 or wisdom at 2:269. One names Ḥuyayy ibn Akhṭab; another brings Torah stoning and 5:43. The Prophet says Torah knowledge is much for people yet little beside God's. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah prefers this occasion.
  31:27/q23/p2 Polytheists or Quraysh predicted Muhammad's speech would end. — reasons: Qatādah and others give this report; al-Suddī varies it to a complaint that Muhammad spoke abundantly. Al-Ālūsī calls its contextual connection weak.
  31:27/q23/p3 Some Jews predicted the recited words themselves would cease. — reasons: Al-Jishumī reports this distinct Jewish version.
31:27/q24 Where was the verse revealed? [* · sciences] (turns on: Whether the Jewish exchange happened after migration or through Meccan intermediaries.)
  31:27/q24/p1 In Medina, as an exception to the Meccan sūra. — reasons: ʿAṭāʾ and Ibn ʿAbbās are cited; reports vary between two and three exceptional verses beginning here. Ibn ʿAṭiyyah prefers Medina. Ibn ʿĀshūr says prophetic direction then fixed its place.
  31:27/q24/p2 In Mecca, whether as part of the sūra or after Jews instructed Quraysh. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr prefers the familiar Meccan classification despite Medinan reports; Ibn ʿĀshūr says a Meccan account fits its revealed sequence.
31:27/q25 How does it connect to the sūra? [* · links] (turns on: Which surrounding argument it advances.)
  31:27/q25/p1 It extends claims of divine ownership, knowledge and sole governance to limitless dominion and creative commands. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says 31:26 could seem finite; Ibn ʿĀshūr links hidden knowledge at 31:16 and 31:23; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī links exclusive governance; Biqāʿī connects resurrection wisdom.
  31:27/q25/p2 It supports the sūra's argument for revelation against Quranic opponents. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites the sūra's opening and 31:6–7; Ibn ʿĀshūr follows the theme to 31:34; Islahi says abundant signs answer demands for more.
  31:27/q25/p3 It offers signs of power and unity against polytheism. — reasons: Al-Qaffāl and al-Qurṭubī read the wonders as proofs; Islahi says their abundance leaves no excuse for idol worship.
31:27/q26 How does it relate to 18:109? [* · links]
  31:27/q26/p1 The sea-as-ink image there likewise states inexhaustibility. — reasons: Many commentators cite 18:109; Ibn Kathīr reads its 'another like it' as unlimited repeated supply. Ibn ʿĀshūr links Luqmān with Cave and Dhū l-Qarnayn narratives, and distinguishes completion of words in 6:115 as fulfillment.
31:27/q27 How much is human knowledge beside God's? [* · interpretation]
  31:27/q27/p1 Much by a human measure, yet little beside God's knowledge. — reasons: ʿIkrimah says Torah wisdom suffices for salvation; al-Rabīʿ compares human knowledge to a drop beside seas. Ibn ʿĀshūr says God wisely discloses some knowledge and withholds other knowledge.
31:27/q28 What inward lesson is drawn? [* · inward]
  31:27/q28/p1 Wisdom can flow without end from God's inexhaustible treasury. — reasons: Abū Saʿīd al-Kharrāz describes sages' speech; Bursevī adds knowers receiving insight in a flash and finite pages unable to contain eternal meanings.
  31:27/q28/p2 God's pre-eternal words invite the servant to love and felicity. — reasons: Al-Junayd links continuous blessings with love, divine choosing and future felicity.
