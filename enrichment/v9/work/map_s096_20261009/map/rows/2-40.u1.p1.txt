2:40/q15 How is the yāʾ of biʿahdī read? [بِعَهْدِىٓ · readings]
  2:40/q15/p1 It remains quiescent before the following alif. — reasons: Ibn Mujahid records unmoved forms, Nafi's general vocalization rule and exceptions, Ibn Jammaz and Ismail ibn Ja'far's still yāʾ; Nashr says readers agree here because of the many letters.
2:40/q16 How is the relative phrase constructed? [ٱلَّتِىٓ أَنْعَمْتُ عَلَيْكُمْ · grammar]
  2:40/q16/p1 Allatī modifies niʿmah; the relative clause omits a resumptive object. — reasons: Nahhas, Abu Hayyan, Samin and Alusi supply 'I bestowed it'; Samin cites a 9:69 reading for deletion after preposition loss.
  2:40/q16/p2 The clause clarifies and widens the favor. — reasons: Harali interprets allatī as initially vague; Samin says ʿalaykum conveys comprehensive reach.
2:40/q17 What does ʿahd mean? [بِعَهْدِىٓ بِعَهْدِكُمْ · meaning]
  2:40/q17/p1 A pledge, testament or undertaking to preserve and fulfill. — reasons: Mawardi cites scripture and oath; Jishumi says command/testament; Tabatabai derives preservation; Biqai's Harali, Isfahani and Khalil describe prior private undertaking and continual tending; Kur'an Yolu lists compact, promise and oath.
  2:40/q17/p2 It can name the Mosaic law and the many divine duties. — reasons: Ibn Ashur notes Israel's scripture uses covenant for law; Kur'an Yolu relates Old and New Testaments to covenant obligations.
2:40/q18 What covenant must Israel fulfill? [وَأَوْفُوا۟ بِعَهْدِىٓ · interpretation] (turns on: Unspecified covenant: all obligations or a specific historical or inward pledge.)
  2:40/q18/p1 All commands, prohibitions, faith and obedience, including Muhammad. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and the majority give the broad view. Ibn Ashur cites 5:12-13, 5:70-71, 2:84-85 and Jewish scriptures: worship, prayer, alms, support for messengers, no bloodshed and fidelity to all revelation.
  2:40/q18/p2 Believe and follow Muhammad as foretold, disclosing his description. — reasons: Tabari cites 5:12 and 7:157; Kalbi reports the unlettered prophet of Ishmael's line with double reward; Zajjaj cites 3:187; Kashshaf points to 2:41. Razi debates explicitness and concealment.
  2:40/q18/p3 Keep the specific 5:12 pledge with twelve chiefs. — reasons: It requires prayer, alms, belief in messengers and a good loan; it promises forgiveness and gardens. Muqatil, Qatada, Mujahid and Ibn Jurayj identify it.
  2:40/q18/p4 Hold fast to Torah under the Sinai pledge. — reasons: Hasan links it to the raised mountain and 2:63; others cite 2:93.
  2:40/q18/p5 Worship God alone and follow Islam. — reasons: Abu al-Aliya calls the covenant Islam; Muqatil and Thalabi cite 2:83.
  2:40/q18/p6 Thank God for disclosed favors. — reasons: Tabrisi and Razi report that disclosure binds them to gratitude; Study Quran generalizes it.
  2:40/q18/p7 Do not flee battle. — reasons: Ismail ibn Ziyad cites 8:15-16 and 33:15; Abu Hayyan adds Paradise as reward.
  2:40/q18/p8 Keep the primordial pledge of assent and service. — reasons: Sulami's Baghdadis and Bursevi recall pretemporal assent; Qushayri pairs acceptance with fulfillment at return.
  2:40/q18/p9 Guard trusts, secrets, manners, self, reliance and sincerity inwardly. — reasons: Sulami gives distinct reciprocal pairs: trusts/treasuries, duties/acceptance, struggle/help, manners/adornment, reliance/sufficiency, worship/stations, service/vision. Qushayri and Abu Hayyan add knowledge, love, contentment and proximity; Qurtubi reports such readings.
2:40/q19 What does God promise in return? [أُوفِ بِعَهْدِكُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: The promised outcome varies with the proposed human commitment.)
  2:40/q19/p1 Acceptance, forgiveness, reward and Paradise. — reasons: Muqatil and Tabari cite 5:12; Ibn Abbas adds divine pleasure; Qurtubi says obedience signals rather than causes gracious reward; a Prophetic report promises Paradise.
  2:40/q19/p2 Lift burdens and fetters when Israel follows Muhammad. — reasons: Ibn Abbas says prior burdens followed sins; Tabari, Baydawi, Ibn Kathir and Elmali link release to prophetic belief.
  2:40/q19/p3 Give double reward to People of Scripture who follow earlier prophets and Muhammad. — reasons: Kalbi transmits forgiveness, Paradise and two rewards; Razi cites 28:52-54, 57:28 and a hadith on three double-reward groups.
  2:40/q19/p4 Give worldly security, sufficiency and victory. — reasons: Biqai cites Torah language of livelihood and freedom from fear; Elmali relates 2:38; Sulami and Qushayri pair reliance with sufficiency.
  2:40/q19/p5 Give inward help, nearness, vision and communion. — reasons: Sulami, Qushayri, Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi and Alusi describe levels from confession and protection to unity and encounter.
2:40/q20 How are the two covenants annexed? [بِعَهْدِىٓ بِعَهْدِكُمْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether annexation names maker or beneficiary, and whether the promise is causal.)
  2:40/q20/p1 Either party may be named as maker or beneficiary. — reasons: Kashshaf, Abu Hayyan and Samin discuss subject/object annexation; Baydawi and Alusi prefer God's initiating command and promised reward; Alusi notes a caveat to both as object genitives.
  2:40/q20/p2 Reciprocal language expresses command and promised reward. — reasons: Razi says God cannot be commanded, so divine 'covenant' differs from human obligation, as in reciprocal plotting/deceiving language.
  2:40/q20/p3 Human fulfillment signals, rather than causes, God's gracious act. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi reject a causal obligation upon God.
2:40/q21 What do awfū and awfi mean? [وَأَوْفُوا۟ أُوفِ · meaning] (turns on: Whether doubling intensifies or simply varies the form.)
  2:40/q21/p1 Awfā, wafā and waffā can all mean fulfilling a covenant. — reasons: Zajjaj prefers Qur'anic awfā; Ibn Awf's verse combines forms; Farra distinguishes Hijaz and Najd; Ibn Qutayba allows both for covenants but only awfā for fully measuring; Abu al-Haytham rejects Shammar's distinction.
  2:40/q21/p2 Awfā can also mean rise or ascend. — reasons: Abu Hayyan, Samin and Alusi cite a poem; Shammar distinguishes rights fully paid from mere completion.
  2:40/q21/p3 A doubled verb can intensify repeated or abundant fulfillment. — reasons: Ibn Jinni says doubled form is emphatic, comparing 6:160, while his light-form discussion of yudhabbihun says a generic verbal noun may itself indicate repeated action; he cites Arabic poets and morphological variation.
  2:40/q21/p4 The doubled form can be simply synonymous. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and Tha'labi permit that account.
2:40/q22 Which doubled fulfillment reading is transmitted? [وَأَوْفُوا۟ بِعَهْدِىٓ أُوفِ بِعَهْدِكُمْ · readings] (turns on: Transmission and morphological force.)
  2:40/q22/p1 Al-Zuhrī reads doubled fāʾ, sometimes in both clauses. — reasons: Nahhas, Ibn Jinni and later writers report it, differing on the first clause and vowel detail; Baydawi reports a doubled wāw.
  2:40/q22/p2 The common light form remains the basis of comparison. — reasons: The doubled form can imply amplification rather than a new obligation.
2:40/q23 Why is awfi jussive? [وَأَوْفُوا۟ بِعَهْدِىٓ أُوفِ بِعَهْدِكُمْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the command or an elided condition governs.)
  2:40/q23/p1 The imperative itself carries conditional force and licenses the response. — reasons: Nahhas and al-Khalil describe it; Abu al-Hasan ibn Khuruf and one Sibawayh account support direct licensing.
  2:40/q23/p2 An elided if-clause governs the jussive. — reasons: Al-Farisi and al-Sirafi supply 'if you fulfill'; Abu Hayyan reports Sibawayh citing al-Khalil for this account.
2:40/q24 What does rahbah mean? [فَٱرْهَبُونِ · meaning] (turns on: Whether caution is intrinsic to rahbah.)
  2:40/q24/p1 Fear, dread or apprehension, often with caution. — reasons: Jishumi cites the fear/mercy proverb; Tabrisi contrasts desire; Abu Hayyan records vocalizations and a breastbone derivation; Baydawi and Alusi distinguish wary fear from taqwa.
  2:40/q24/p2 Fear rests on belief about harm and benefit. — reasons: Jishumi says perceived harm prompts avoidance and perceived benefit prompts action.
2:40/q25 What are they to fear? [وَإِيَّٰىَ فَٱرْهَبُونِ · interpretation] (turns on: The feared consequence and scope of the immediate covenant.)
  2:40/q25/p1 God alone, especially when breaking covenant or denying Muhammad. — reasons: Muqatil warns of Hell for denial; Tabari warns of punishment unless they repent; Wahidi, Baghawi and Tabrisi focus on breach; Baydawi and Alusi extend fear to all deeds.
  2:40/q25/p2 Ancestral calamities, including transformation. — reasons: Ibn Abbas' warning recurs in Tabari, Ibn Abi Hatim, Abu Hayyan, Ibn Kathir and Alusi.
  2:40/q25/p3 Future punishment under certain or uncertain accountability. — reasons: Razi says prophets and angels may fear the future (16:50); uncertainty about past obedience creates further fear.
  2:40/q25/p4 Fear rabbis no more; follow the promised messenger. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says leaders obstructed covenant keeping; Elmali distinguishes common people and elites in 2:40-41.
