22:40/q26 How is biyaʿ formed or derived? [بِيَعٌۭ · meaning] (turns on: An Arabic plural, a foreign loan, or a suggested Arabic allusion.)
  22:40/q26/p1 Biyaʿ is the plural of bīʿa, a church. — reasons: The grammarians specify the singular.
  22:40/q26/p2 Bīʿa is a borrowed word, possibly Persian; its exact origin is unknown. — reasons: Mawardi calls it adapted; Jawaliqi reports Persian as one proposal.
  22:40/q26/p3 If Arabic, its name may allude to God's purchase of believers' lives. — reasons: Al-Raghib invokes 9:111.
22:40/q27 How do Arabic weak-root forms illuminate words in this verse? [بِيَعٌۭ · grammar] (turns on: Preservation of a weak root's original form versus vowel transfer and weakening.)
  22:40/q27/p1 Weak triliteral nouns can preserve an original shape but usually follow the weakening of corresponding verbs. — reasons: Sibawayh compares qawd, khawna, nawma, sawla, ḥawl, ṣayr, bayʿ and dīm; al-Khalil analyzes khāfa, māla and rāḥa. Wāw with ḍamma may be made still to avoid heaviness; poetry can restore heavy forms.
  22:40/q27/p2 Augmented weak verbs transfer vowels or retain weak letters according to pattern and analogy. — reasons: Sibawayh contrasts iftaʿala with fāʿala and tafāʿala; al-Khalil explains ijtawarū by its reciprocal sense. He discusses ikhtāra, iʿtāda, shawaytu and ḥayītu.
22:40/q28 What are the ṣalawāt? [وَصَلَوَٰتٌۭ · referent] (turns on: Jewish synagogues are the prevalent assignment; alternatives include Christian, Sabian, shared, and literal prayers.)
  22:40/q28/p1 Jewish synagogues or prayer houses. — reasons: Dahhak, Qatada and Ibn Abbas often name synagogues, with a borrowed ṣalōtā/ṣəlōṯā label. Tabari prefers the established Arabic mapping. Tabrisi transmits Ibn Abbas, Dahhak and Qatada.
  22:40/q28/p2 Sabian temples or places of worship. — reasons: Abu al-Aliya is a recurrent authority.
  22:40/q28/p3 Acts of prayer, especially Muslim prayers, which enemies would stop. — reasons: Ibn Zayd says invasion would interrupt Muslim prayer; al-Hasan says disabling prayer means killing performers or preventing worship.
  22:40/q28/p4 Christian churches, chapels, or traveler prayer houses. — reasons: Ibn Abbas has a Christian-church report; Abu Ubayda describes wilderness houses for travelers, Qutrub small monasteries; Ibn Jinni reports Christian burial houses near Mosul.
  22:40/q28/p5 Roadside worship places shared by scriptural communities and Muslims. — reasons: Mujahid describes sites along roads; Ibn Atiyya treats the list as an abundance of sites.
22:40/q29 Where does the word ṣalawāt come from? [وَصَلَوَٰتٌۭ · meaning] (turns on: Arabic plural or metonymic place-name versus a borrowed Hebrew, Syriac, or Aramaic term.)
  22:40/q29/p1 It is the Arabic plural of ṣalāt, and a prayer site can be named for the prayer held there. — reasons: Tabarsi says the plural is rare but analogous to variant plurals of ḥujra; Razi compares 'ask the town' in 12:82; Tabrisi and Tabataba'i cite 4:43 for prayer meaning a place. Ibn Atiyya understands each community's customary prayers; Samin says the practices vary.
  22:40/q29/p2 It is adapted from the Jewish ṣalōtā/ṣəlōṯā, described as Hebrew, Aramaic, or Syriac. — reasons: Reports vary in language assignment and spelling. Samin says foreign source forms explain many readings; Ibn Ashur notes lexicographers' surprising omission of the word.
  22:40/q29/p3 It can mean worship or mercy in general, but here denotes worship houses. — reasons: Burhan and Ibn Faris distinguish this contextual use.
22:40/q30 How is ṣalawāt read? [وَصَلَوَٰتٌۭ · readings] (turns on: The common prayer-word spelling contrasts with many vocalic and consonantal forms, some treated as foreign names or as crosses.)
  22:40/q30/p1 Forms with thāʾ or altered endings include ṣalūth, ṣalūthā and ṣalwaythā. — reasons: The transmitters include Dahhak, Kalbi, Mujahid, Ikrima and Jahdari; Ibn Atiyya counts nine, Abu Hayyan thirteen, and Samin fourteen forms.
  22:40/q30/p2 The common reading is ṣalawāt, with fatḥa on ṣād and lām. — reasons: Samin calls this the only well-known form; the majority read the Arabic prayer plural. Alusi says the word has unusually many variants.
  22:40/q30/p3 The ṣād and lām vowels vary, including two ḍammas, a still lām, and other vocalizations. — reasons: Reports name Ja'far, al-Jahdari, Abu al-Aliya and others; Samin enumerates the forms.
  22:40/q30/p4 Ṣulūb is read as an irregular plural of ṣalīb, 'cross'. — reasons: Al-Hajjaj and al-Jahdari are cited; Samin compares kaʿūb.
  22:40/q30/p5 A form without tanwīn treats the word as an indeclinable foreign name. — reasons: Abu Amr's report prompts discussion whether it is a generic proper name; Alusi doubts a specific place-name.
22:40/q31 If ṣalawāt means prayer, how can it be demolished? [لَّهُدِّمَتْ صَوَٰمِعُ وَبِيَعٌۭ وَصَلَوَٰتٌۭ · grammar] (turns on: Omitted place noun, omitted second verb, figurative disabling, or coordination of unlike objects.)
  22:40/q31/p1 Understand demolition figuratively as interruption or disabling of prayer. — reasons: Enemy entry stops worship; al-Hasan says the worshippers might be killed or prevented from praying. Alusi rejects this literal-prayer construction as unpersuasive.
  22:40/q31/p2 Supply an omitted head noun: places or houses of prayer would be demolished. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba notes Arabic omission of a possessive head; Razi compares 'ask the town' (12:82).
  22:40/q31/p3 Supply a second verb: buildings would be demolished and prayers abandoned. — reasons: Akhfash and Basran grammarians say prayer itself cannot be torn down.
  22:40/q31/p4 Coordinate an undemolishable activity with demolished buildings without a supplied noun. — reasons: Razi compares carrying a sword and a spear in a single expression.
22:40/q32 What are masājid here? [وَمَسَٰجِدُ · referent] (turns on: Specifically Muslim mosques versus a generic term embracing earlier worship sites.)
  22:40/q32/p1 Muslim mosques, places of prostration in Muhammad's community. — reasons: Ibn Ashur places Quba and Medina mosque building at the Hijra; Alusi says the name honors closeness to God through prostration. Alusi rejects making prostration exclusive to Muslims, citing Mary's prostration in 3:43.
  22:40/q32/p2 Masājid can generically include the preceding Jewish and Christian worship sites. — reasons: Study Quran cites 72:18; Islahi notes broader Qur'anic usage; al-Hasan calls all four mosques.
22:40/q33 Why is masājid a diptote? [وَمَسَٰجِدُ · grammar]
  22:40/q33/p1 It follows the terminal plural pattern mafāʿil, so it has no nunation or ordinary declension. — reasons: Sibawayh contrasts terminal mafāʿil/mafāʿīl with singular-like or further-pluralizable forms; suffixes or diminutives can change declension. His examples include maqātil, ṣayāqila, madāʾinī, and the Qur'anic 16:66. Nahhas applies the maximal-plural rule to both ṣawāmiʿ and masājid.
22:40/q34 Which sanctuaries would God's defense preserve, and in which era? [صَوَٰمِعُ وَبِيَعٌۭ وَصَلَوَٰتٌۭ وَمَسَٰجِدُ · interpretation] (turns on: Successive prophetic dispensations, contemporary protected communities, or a broad shared set of monotheist worship places.)
  22:40/q34/p1 Each prophetic community's lawful worship places were protected in its own era. — reasons: Wahidi prefers this because sanctuaries were protected before dhimma and because earlier laws were later superseded. Sources vary in assigning Moses' era churches or synagogues, and Jesus' era cloisters and churches. Tabrisi varies which era gets churches; Razi says remembered places differ from idol worship; Islahi says earlier houses originally stood like mosques.
  22:40/q34/p2 Muslim victory now protects both Muslim and protected non-Muslim sanctuaries. — reasons: Kashshaf says polytheists would overpower Muslims and People of the Book; Wahidi reports al-Hasan's dhimma reading but prefers historical succession. Dahhak and Biqa'i describe threatened present destruction and seizure of pilgrimage sites.
  22:40/q34/p3 The four names broadly amplify worship institutions across scriptural communities. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says Arabic usage makes only biyaʿ specifically Christian; Magian and polytheist sites are omitted because they lack protected revealed worship. Kuran Yolu notes disagreement over which communities own the names.
  22:40/q34/p4 All four terms designate the worship places of believers, or even mosques. — reasons: Al-Hasan says Muslims can worship in the named structures and that prayer and mosques are preserved.
22:40/q35 Why are Magian and polytheist sanctuaries absent? [صَوَٰمِعُ وَبِيَعٌۭ وَصَلَوَٰتٌۭ وَمَسَٰجِدُ · interpretation]
  22:40/q35/p1 They lack the protected revealed worship and divine remembrance relevant to this list. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi exclude their institutions on those grounds; Alusi's objection to Sabian identification follows a similar premise.
  22:40/q35/p2 The verse is about preserving visible signs of religion against erasure, not only buildings. — reasons: Tabataba'i says buildings remind people of religion, while Alusi sees threatened ruin as a figure for wider corruption.
