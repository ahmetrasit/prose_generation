## THE CURRENT MAP
20:49/q01 What encounter is understood before Pharaoh says this? [قَالَ · interpretation]
  20:49/q01/p1 Moses and Aaron came to Pharaoh and delivered the message they had been commanded to deliver; the narrative omits this understood step. — reasons: Abū Isḥāq in al-Wāḥidī infers their arrival and speech from Pharaoh's reply. Al-Zajjāj in Ibn al-Jawzī supplies 'they went and delivered the message.' Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī say their obedience is understood; al-Ālūsī calls the compression brevity and the question an explanatory resumption. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the omitted steps a standard dialogue style (cf. 2:30). Ṭabāṭabāʾī says their invitation to monotheism and request to release Israel are implied; Bursevī/Kāshifī mentions their call to worship God.
20:49/q02 What does the prefixed فَ connect Pharaoh's question to? [فَمَن · grammar]
  20:49/q02/p1 It follows the brothers' assertion that they are messengers of their Lord, prompting Pharaoh to ask who sent them. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī explicitly grounds this in the prefixed fāʾ; Bursevī's reported explanation likewise treats the question as following their claim to be messengers.
20:49/q03 What is the function of مَن here? [مَن · grammar]
  20:49/q03/p1 It is an interrogative meaning 'who.'
20:49/q04 What is Pharaoh asking about their Lord, and with what intent? [فَمَن رَّبُّكُمَا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether Pharaoh seeks the sender's identity or God's essence, and whether his challenge is sincere, evasive, or mocking.)
  20:49/q04/p1 He asks for the identity of the deity who sent and commissioned Moses and Aaron. — reasons: Al-Jishumī reports this as 'who sent them'; al-Baghawī and Ibn Kathīr identify the sought deity as their sender. Al-Ālūsī relates it to the brothers' claim to be messengers. Iṣlāḥī says Pharaoh challenges whose messengers they can be.
  20:49/q04/p2 He asks what sort of being their Lord is, raising the question of divine essence or modality. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī says Moses answers through God's acts because God has no genus. Al-Rāzī's reported argument distinguishes asking essence from disputing existence, citing Pharaoh's response to Moses' description in 26:23-27. Study Quran says that, if sincere, the question concerns how God is.
  20:49/q04/p3 He asks in denial, mockery, or confrontation rather than as a sincere inquiry. — reasons: Al-Jishumī reports denial of the message. Ibn Kathīr connects the question to Pharaoh's pretense that he knows no other deity. Iṣlāḥī says it is sarcastic because Pharaoh controlled Israel while their God appeared absent, despite their presentation of a living God who commands and warns. Kuranyolu calls the question mocking; Study Quran reports other commentators' confrontational reading of 26:23-24.
  20:49/q04/p4 The initial 'who' can begin a serious theological inquiry that later moves to 'what' in 26:23. — reasons: Study Quran attributes this to al-Rāzī. Al-Rāzī prefers a sequence in which Pharaoh first asks identity and, after God's existence becomes undeniable, turns to the harder question of essence.
20:49/q05 Why does Pharaoh say 'Lord' rather than 'God'? [رَّبُّكُمَا · rhetoric]
  20:49/q05/p1 He exploits the two senses of lordship: he had raised Moses and so claims to be his lord, while Moses speaks of divine Lordship. — reasons: Al-Rāzī and Bursevī's Imam cite Pharaoh's boast about raising Moses (26:18); al-Ālūsī calls it a possible taunt at Moses as his dependent. Al-Rāzī compares the equivocation to Nimrod's verbal counterclaim to Abraham about giving life and death (2:258).
20:49/q06 Why does Pharaoh ask about 'your Lord' rather than acknowledge Him as his own? [رَّبُّكُمَا · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the wording is explained by Pharaoh's calculated refusal, the prior message's scope, or pagan conventions for naming divine beings.)
  20:49/q06/p1 He avoids conceding that God is his Lord and turns the claim back against the brothers. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says he deflects fair consideration to humiliate them; Bursevī links it to arrogance. Al-Ālūsī cites extreme tyranny; Ibn ʿĀshūr says he feared his followers might think he doubted his deity or admitted a lord. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says he feigns not to hear their calling God Pharaoh's Lord. Iṣlāḥī says he implies his own lordship; Ibn ʿĀshūr ties the dual pronoun to their statement in 20:47.
  20:49/q06/p2 Their prior declaration already included universal or Pharaoh's lordship, so referring only to the brothers' Lord served the immediate exchange. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports this explanation and cites 26:16.
  20:49/q06/p3 In pagan usage, 'my lord' would name an intermediary deity, whereas the creator was understood as Lord of lords. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī says pagans worshipped created intermediaries for nearness and intercession because the creator was too transcendent for direct worship; their idiom did not use 'god' or 'lord' for the supreme creator in the same way.
20:49/q07 What did Pharaoh believe or claim about divine lordship? [رَّبُّكُمَا · theology] (turns on: How to reconcile Pharaoh's god-king claim with his knowledge of a creator and the force of his question.)
  20:49/q07/p1 He knew of the creator but denied Him publicly through arrogance or opposition. — reasons: Al-Rāzī reports six arguments: 17:102 and 27:14 say he knew or inwardly recognized the signs; a rational accountable person knows his own dependence; 28:39 is read as denial of return rather than creation; his power stopped at Egypt and Moses escaped to Midian (28:25); his question in 26:23-27 concerns essence, not existence. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the question denies neither the creator nor Pharaoh's having a deity.
  20:49/q07/p2 He was ignorant of God, possibly a materialist who denied an effective cause. — reasons: Al-Rāzī reports this view but says its advocates still concede a rational person could not believe himself creator of the cosmos or of himself; materialism is one proposed form of ignorance.
  20:49/q07/p3 He may have been a philosopher affirming a necessary causal principle. — reasons: Al-Rāzī reports this as one proposed form of his belief.
  20:49/q07/p4 He may have worshipped the stars. — reasons: Al-Rāzī reports this possibility.
  20:49/q07/p5 He may have belonged to an incarnationist corporealist sect. — reasons: Al-Rāzī reports this possibility.
  20:49/q07/p6 His claim to lordship demanded his subjects' obedience and submission. — reasons: Al-Rāzī explains his claim as political obedience. Ṭabāṭabāʾī points to Pharaoh's boasts and threats (79:24). Iṣlāḥī describes the Egyptian god-king as a solar avatar with statues and idols worshipped throughout the land.
20:49/q08 How does the dual address to Moses and Aaron become a vocative to Moses alone? [رَّبُّكُمَا يَٰمُوسَىٰ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the singular vocative follows Moses' leadership or personal tie, poetic endings, Pharaoh's desire to exploit his speech, ordinary representative address, or an omitted conjunction.)
  20:49/q08/p1 Moses is singled out as the principal messenger and bearer of the message and signs, with Aaron his supporter or minister. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya adds the distinguished signs; Ibn ʿĀshūr says Moses had palace access and likely spoke while Aaron affirmed him. Al-Qurṭubī says Moses alone carried the speech and sign. Al-Samīn says leading with Moses matters more than a possible Aaron-first alternative. Al-Qushayrī calls this Moses' precedence.
  20:49/q08/p2 Aaron's name is omitted for brevity or to preserve the verse endings. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Qushayrī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Jishumī, al-Ṭabrisī and al-Qurṭubī mention rhyme; al-Wāḥidī also says Moses' name suffices for both. Al-Samīn concedes rhyme could suit a proposed ellipsis but denies that ellipsis is needed.
  20:49/q08/p3 Pharaoh targets Moses' speech impediment and avoids Aaron's greater eloquence. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī cite Pharaoh's jibe in 43:52; al-Samīn also cites Aaron's eloquence in 28:34. Al-Burhān says Pharaoh feared Aaron's sharp answer; al-Ālūsī notes the explanation assumes Moses' impediment persisted.
  20:49/q08/p4 The dual refers to both brothers, while Moses alone is the immediate addressee and can answer for them. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba cites comparable shifts of addressee and the mention of Adam as humanity's father. Al-Ṭabarī compares one person's answering for a group to the lost-fish story (18:61, 18:63). Al-Wāḥidī explicitly distinguishes the dual 'your Lord' from the singular address. Al-Ṭabrisī calls it grammatical dominance of the dual. Al-Burhān calls it al-mutalawwin or iltifāt (also 33:1), and al-Itqān surveys singular, dual and plural address shifts (26:16; 65:1; 20:117; 10:87; 9:112).
  20:49/q08/p5 The singular vocative reflects Moses' special relationship to Pharaoh through his upbringing. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya, as reported in al-Itqān, connects the direct address to their upbringing relationship; Ibn ʿĀshūr also notes Moses' earlier palace access.
  20:49/q08/p6 An omitted conjunction makes the vocative mean 'O Moses and Aaron.' — reasons: Abū al-Baqāʾ proposes ellipsis of the conjunction. Al-Samīn replies that the ellipsis is unnecessary, though it could serve the verse ending; an Aaron-first wording is grammatically possible but Moses' precedence counts more.
20:49/q09 What does the dual pronoun show about Aaron's part in the mission? [رَّبُّكُمَا · interpretation]
  20:49/q09/p1 Both brothers took part in delivering the commission, with Moses speaking and Aaron present or supporting him. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī says one speaker can discharge a jointly entrusted task while the other supports him, both meriting reward; commands to both and Pharaoh's dual address show both performed it. Al-Ālūsī says Aaron was apparently present despite Wahb's omission. Ibn ʿĀshūr says Aaron likely affirmed Moses. Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls him Moses' minister. Al-Sāmarrāʾī cites their joint sending, fear, reassurance, delivery and address
