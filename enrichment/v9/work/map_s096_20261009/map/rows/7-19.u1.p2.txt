  7:19/q26/p4 They forgot the prohibition itself. — reasons: Al-Jishumī reports this and notes that a forgotten prohibition is not binding, using the point in support of Abū ʿAlī's explanation.
  7:19/q26/p5 God allowed a lapse so its lesson and proof would emerge. — reasons: Al-Kharrāz says even Paradise's command and prohibition unsettled Adam; God allowed the lapse, expelled and reproached him, and 20:115 is cited.
7:19/q27 What default ruling applies to acts or new cases without an explicit ruling? [فَكُلَا وَلَا تَقْرَبَا · law] (turns on: How silence in revelation bears on a new act and whether reasons based on divine or another's property can establish a prior rule.)
  7:19/q27/p1 Default to prohibition and caution. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports jurists who say silence does not license people to legislate permission for a new case without text, Prophetic report, or consensus. He reports a rationalist form: use of another's property is encroachment, especially God's property.
  7:19/q27/p2 Default to permission until a ban arrives. — reasons: Jurists argue that God completed religion and would not hide an intended prohibition. A rationalist version permits harmless use of another's property, such as shade from a wall, especially when God suffers no harm. Some exempt unavoidable breathing and movement from a ban; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says necessity could permit them.
  7:19/q27/p3 Suspend judgment while seeking a general or specific revealed ruling. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls this the clearest juristic response; cases still require revealed authority and further inquiry.
7:19/q28 Can reason alone establish a binding moral ruling before revelation? [فَكُلَا وَلَا تَقْرَبَا · theology] (turns on: Whether moral obligation comes from reason independently or from revelation, and whether a person without revelation is a meaningful case.)
  7:19/q28/p1 No; binding judgments arise through revelation. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya distinguishes jurists' derivation from revelation from rational good and evil. He says their new-case debate is not a denial of this principle and rejects Ibn al-Bāqillānī's accusation that jurists unwittingly adopted a Muʿtazilī argument. The people of truth and Sunna state the principle expressly.
  7:19/q28/p2 Reason can judge good and evil in a hypothetical absence of revelation. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya attributes this framing to Muʿtazila and like-minded thinkers, who imagine a person or period without revelation or Adam before earthly commands. Ibn al-Bāqillānī says jurists borrowed this argument without grasping its implications; Ibn ʿAṭiyya objects to that charge.
  7:19/q28/p3 The supposed revelation-free case never arises. — reasons: Some argue reason is never without revelation; Adam received Paradise commands, including praise after sneezing and directions to dwell, eat, and avoid the tree.
7:19/q29 Can believers' sins be placed on unbelievers? [ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · theology] (turns on: How to reconcile 6:164's individual burden with 29:13's additional burdens.)
  7:19/q29/p1 God may forgive believers and place their sins on Jews and Christians while punishing unbelievers for their own sins. — reasons: Al-Bursevī cites a report of mountain-sized forgiven sins and interprets it as grace to believers and justice to sinners. Against a Muʿtazilī appeal to 6:164, he cites 29:13 on unbelievers bearing their own and added burdens.
  7:19/q29/p2 No; 6:164 bars transferring another's burden. — reasons: Al-Bursevī reports the Muʿtazilī objection but rejects its application by citing 29:13.
7:19/q30 How does this telling fit the story and audience of al-Aʿrāf? [* · links] (turns on: The notes offer complementary explanations: immediate sūra audience and the broader function of repeated narration.)
  7:19/q30/p1 It instructs humanity through Adam's favored beginning, temptation, and fall, with special force for al-Aʿrāf's audience. — reasons: Elmalılı says humanity's first station was elevated: angels prostrated before Adam's Garden residence. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the scene honors Adam in front of punished Iblis and warns polytheists who chose Satan as patron; 2:35 addresses Israelites wary of Satan and recalls favor, sin, repentance. Asad says appetite, vanity, and false self-interest expose human weakness and the need for continuing prophetic guidance. Study Quran describes 7:11–27 as creation, temptation, and expulsion.
  7:19/q30/p2 The recurring story reaches different listeners and uses varied wording to teach. — reasons: Al-Jishumī says revelation over twenty-odd years amid changing visitors required retelling; distinct eloquent formulations also demonstrate Quranic inimitability. Ibn ʿĀshūr says variation refreshes repeated material and distributes favors for moral instruction.
7:19/q31 How does the rest of Adam's account differ between al-Aʿrāf and al-Baqara? [* · links]
  7:19/q31/p1 Al-Baqara's wording honors and softens Adam's case; al-Aʿrāf's wording emphasizes reproach and the consequences of deception. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī says 2:36 calls the offense a slip, whereas al-Aʿrāf describes being lowered; reproach appears in 7:22 and answers the descendants' ingratitude at 7:10. Baqara omits explicit confession and reports accepted repentance; Aʿrāf gives the plea in 7:23 without acceptance, echoing the punished peoples' confessions at 7:4–5. Adam's verbal 'we wronged' signals a passing slip, unlike descendants' nominal 'we were wrongdoers' and settled persistence. Baqara's 2:38 offers freedom from fear and grief in the afterlife; Ṭā Hā's 20:123 uses an intensive 'follow' for guidance through worldly struggle and afterlife success, matching its third-person attribution and 20:117's suffering, whereas Baqara's lighter verb suits direct divine attribution and recurs there especially often. Lamāsāt reiterates the slip/lowering contrast.
7:19/q32 Where do commentators direct readers for the fuller explanation of this verse? [* · links]
  7:19/q32/p1 To the parallel at 2:35, sometimes also 20:120. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī's two commentaries and al-Qurṭubī refer back to al-Baqara; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī likewise send readers to their earlier Garden discussions. Al-Rāzī lists ten deferred questions: dwelling as worship or permission, Eve's creation, Garden location, eating permission, the ban's force, tree identity and kind, sin gravity, injustice and condemnation, and whether Adam was already a prophet, mentioning 11:18. Asad adds 20:120. Study Quran notes the 2:35 parallel.
