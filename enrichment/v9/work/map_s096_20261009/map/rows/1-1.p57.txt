[ALUSI:1:1/r142] ALUSI, d. 1270 · البيضاوي | reports | Al-Bayḍāwī’s ordering moves from God’s universal outpouring of existence to each creature’s particular share and perfection; al-Ālūsī questions the distinction.

[ALUSI:1:1/r145] ALUSI, d. 1270 · ابن الحاجب والزمخشري والرضي وابن مالك والبيضاوي والسعد | reports | Grammarians dispute whether raḥmān declines: most prefer diptote treatment by analogy with the predominant faʿlā feminine pattern, while al-Saʿd allows both.
[ALUSI:1:1/r146] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | Banū Ḥanīfa called Musaylima “the Merciful of Yamāma”; al-Ālūsī treats this as blasphemous excess, not evidence that the sacred name is ordinarily shared.
[ALUSI:1:1/r147] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | The basmala opens God’s discourse because it summarizes what follows and is a profound blessing; analyzing even its brief wording draws on many disciplines.
[ALUSI:1:1/r148] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Its study ranges from syntax, morphology, rhetoric, language, and script to logic, theology, jurisprudence, hadith method, ethics, and esoteric knowledge.
[ALUSI:1:1/r149] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | Al-Ālūsī finds layered triads in its articulation, omitted letters, written and spoken forms, word classes, genitives, divine names, grammatical roles, and connections.
[ALUSI:1:1/r15] ALUSI, d. 1270 · ابن كيسان والسيرافي وجمع | reports | Ibn Kaysān, al-Sīrāfī, and others treat the part-to-whole addition as partitive, with some grammarians extending that analysis to all such constructions.
[ALUSI:1:1/r150] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | A second reason for opening with the basmala is to teach people how to begin: invoking God declares dependence on His power in every undertaking.
[ALUSI:1:1/r151] ALUSI, d. 1270 · أبو هريرة | reports | Abū Hurayra transmits the Prophet’s maxim that an important matter begun without God’s name is incomplete; al-Ālūsī interprets it broadly to include trivial matters.
[ALUSI:1:1/r152] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Al-Ālūsī takes dhū bāl as comprehensive—anything that enters the mind—rather than restricting it to dignified matters, since God’s aid matters in small acts too. | mentions 6:38
[ALUSI:1:1/r153] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | Reports urge asking God even for a sandal strap, and God tells Moses to ask for salt and a shoe strap; these support invocation in minor needs.
[ALUSI:1:1/r154] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Reciting the basmala over forbidden acts is itself forbidden without being disbelief according to the sound view; over disliked acts it is disliked, unless contempt makes it disbelief.
[ALUSI:1:1/r155] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | The maxim’s variants include beginning with praise, “praise of God,” or another remembrance, and incomplete versus severed; al-Ālūsī calls it unstable in wording and chain but strengthened in meaning.
[ALUSI:1:1/r156] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Al-Ālūsī reconciles variants by taking the intended opening as remembrance that manifests divine perfection; matters may be outwardly complete yet deficient in meaning without it.
[ALUSI:1:1/r157] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Since the basmala is a kind of praise, it fittingly precedes the comprehensive praise that follows. | mentions 1:2
[ALUSI:1:1/r16] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Fātiḥat al-Kitāb means the opening of the Qurʾān in its received order, not the first lesson taught or the first sūra revealed.
[ALUSI:1:1/r17] ALUSI, d. 1270 · المرسي | reports | Al-Mursī says it was named because it was the first sūra written on the Preserved Tablet; al-Ālūsī says this needs transmission and sequence of writing is uncertain.
[ALUSI:1:1/r18] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Umm al-Kitāb and Umm al-Qurʾān reflect its priority, like a mother preceding what follows, a banner leading an army, or its encompassing the Qurʾān’s aims.
[ALUSI:1:1/r19] ALUSI, d. 1270 · الحسن البصري | reports | The alleged prophetic prohibition on calling it Umm al-Kitāb has no basis; sound reports establish that title, and al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī also used it.
[ALUSI:1:1/r2] ALUSI, d. 1270 · علي وابن عباس وقتادة وأكثر الصحابة | reports | Reports from ʿAlī, Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatāda, and most Companions support its Meccan origin.
[ALUSI:1:1/r20] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Al-Ālūsī says al-Fātiḥa gathers praise, worship, and promise or warning; these summarize the guidance sent through messengers and revealed books for worldly and final welfare. | mentions 1:2, 1:4, 1:7
[ALUSI:1:1/r21] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Praise appears in al-ḥamdu lillāh; worship is taught by its implied command, guidance-seeking, or the declaration of worship, while command entails prohibition. | mentions 1:2, 1:5, 1:6
[ALUSI:1:1/r22] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | He answers the Muʿtazilī objection that worship has no prior command: monotheism is worship’s foundation, and Muhammad’s prior charge to proclaim it suffices. | mentions 1:1
[ALUSI:1:1/r23] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Promise and warning appear in the favored and the angered or astray, and in the Day of Recompense understood as reward and punishment. | mentions 1:4, 1:7
[ALUSI:1:1/r24] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | Al-Ālūsī offers a second map: theoretical knowledge is covered through the Day of Judgment, practical conduct by worship and guidance, and human outcomes by the final verse. | mentions 1:2, 1:4, 1:5, 1:6, 1:7
[ALUSI:1:1/r25] ALUSI, d. 1270 · البيضاوي | reports | Al-Bayḍāwī’s threefold scheme may hint, in line with Muʿtazilī doctrine, that rational gratitude to the benefactor is obligatory; al-Ālūsī notes this implication.
[ALUSI:1:1/r26] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | A four-discipline scheme maps theology to Lordship and mercy, prophecy to those favored, and the afterlife to the Day of Judgment. | mentions 1:2, 1:3, 1:4, 1:7
