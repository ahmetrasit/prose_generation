9:103/q28 May the blessing formula be used for people besides the Prophet? [وَصَلِّ عَلَيْهِمْ · law] (turns on: Whether Prophetic donor prayers establish a general formula or his standing makes it exclusive.)
  9:103/q28/p1 Prayer and blessing for donors or other Muslims are permitted. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī says the Prophet is an exemplar, citing donor and Jābir reports. Al-Qāḍī cites the taught blessing on Muḥammad's family as including ʿAlī. The Shīʿa invoke blessings for ʿAlī and descendants, arguing 9:103 permits blessing payers and returned greetings are accepted. Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī are reported by the Study Quran to reject exclusivity.
  9:103/q28/p2 The formula 'God's blessings upon him' is reserved for the Prophet. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to prohibit ṣalāt for anyone else; al-Rāzī's school and some commentators restrict it. Al-Qurṭubī reports appeal to the Prophet's special standing in 24:63. The Study Quran says Muslims invoke blessings and peace on Muḥammad but only peace on other prophets.
9:103/q29 How is the greeting of peace analyzed in the discussion of blessings? [وَصَلِّ عَلَيْهِمْ · grammar] (turns on: The grammatical structure, word order, and purpose of the greeting.)
  9:103/q29/p1 'Peace be upon you' can have an indefinite subject with 'upon you' as predicate. — reasons: Al-Rāzī says the indefinite is qualified by complete peace, citing 2:221 and 2:96; alternatively 'upon you' qualifies the subject and the predicate is omitted for grandeur. He says indefinite salām has fuller qualities than definite al-salām, with Qurʾanic comparisons and Yaḥyā as an exception.
  9:103/q29/p2 The reversed reply 'and upon you, peace' foregrounds what concerns the respondent. — reasons: Sībawayh says speakers put the most important element first; al-Rāzī adds that it promises more and reads 4:86 as support.
  9:103/q29/p3 Greeting with peace reassures a person amid pervasive potential harm and creates a spiritual bond. — reasons: Al-Rāzī argues restraint of harm preserves safety more generally than adding benefit; unknown creatures naturally elicit flight. The plural can include guardian angels and different spirit classes. Mutual spiritual reflection explains a student's greeting and prayer for a teacher.
9:103/q30 What hermeneutic principle governs a legal and an ordinary sense of a word here? [وَصَلِّ عَلَيْهِمْ · interpretation]
  9:103/q30/p1 When ordinary linguistic and legal senses are equally plausible, the legal sense takes priority absent contextual evidence. — reasons: Al-Burhān orders legal over customary over linguistic use. Al-Itqān says ijtihād clarifies general wording, ambiguous points, and legal rulings; scholars must use evidence rather than mere opinion, and this verse's prayer serves as an example of the legal-versus-linguistic choice.
9:103/q31 What does the prayer command imply for funeral prayer? [وَصَلِّ عَلَيْهِمْ · law] (turns on: Whether 9:103's prayer includes funeral practice and how that practice is historically grounded.)
  9:103/q31/p1 Funeral prayer is prescribed as a communal obligation, alongside washing and burial duties. — reasons: Al-Bursevī cites al-Kāfī's use of the verse and a report to pray over righteous and sinful dead; he says it became obligatory in year one after the Prophet prayed four takbīrs at Barāʾ ibn Maʿrūr's grave. Al-Qunya treats denial of its obligation as unbelief. Washing is communal duty; intention is not required for validity, though it discharges duty. He says death makes the body impure and washing honors it, even if found in water.
  9:103/q31/p2 Adam's funeral establishes ancient precedent for washing, shrouding, prayer, burial and laḥd. — reasons: One report has angels wash and pray; another has Seth lead thirty takbīrs after Gabriel declined. Al-Bursevī says pre-Islamic Arabs may have washed and shrouded bodies and recited virtues, reconciling reports that funeral prayer is unique to this community.
9:103/q32 How is the reply in al-Aʿshā's cited prayer verse parsed? [وَصَلِّ عَلَيْهِمْ · grammar] (turns on: The case assigned to the reply in the poetic citation.)
  9:103/q32/p1 The reply can be nominative like a greeting returned with 'peace be upon you'. — reasons: Abū ʿUbayda's citation treats the answer as a nominal expression.
  9:103/q32/p2 The reply can be accusative as an imperative to repeat the same words and pray. — reasons: Al-Majāz reports another reading of the poetic line.
9:103/q33 What does God's ṣalāt mean when attributed to Him? [وَصَلِّ عَلَيْهِمْ · theology] (turns on: Whether God's ṣalāt is described as an idiom of desert or as mercy.)
  9:103/q33/p1 It describes persons deserving divine blessing, not God supplicating for them. — reasons: Sībawayh's idiom compares 'woe to the deniers' in 52:11 and 37:12 and 'woe to the defrauders' in 83:1; al-Ṭabrisī applies this account to God's prayer on His Messenger.
  9:103/q33/p2 God's prayer signifies mercy, while the Prophet's prayer is supplication. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr distinguishes the subjects and compares 9:99.
9:103/q34 Why does the verse state that his prayer is a comfort after commanding it? [إِنَّ صَلَوٰتَكَ سَكَنٌۭ لَّهُمْ · rhetoric] (turns on: The causal and emphatic functions of inna.)
  9:103/q34/p1 The inna-clause gives the reason for the command and answers an implied 'why?'. — reasons: Al-Burhān says explicit causes strengthen and help transmit rules, and causal fāʾ could replace inna while losing the question-answer effect; al-Itqān cites Ibn Jinnī's 'because' sense. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the clause explanation of the imperative.
  9:103/q34/p2 The emphatic clause confirms the significance of the preceding commands. — reasons: ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī compares other inna confirmations, including Luqmān's counsel and passages in 22:1, 31:17, 11:37 and 12:53.
9:103/q35 How does the pronoun of the affair interact with emphatic inna? [إِنَّ · grammar]
  9:103/q35/p1 In conditional clauses, a pronoun of the affair is scarcely used without inna. — reasons: ʿAbd al-Qāhir cites 12:90, 9:63, 6:54, 23:117 and 22:46; even if Abū al-Ḥasan's forward reference to al-abṣār in 22:46 is allowed, he rejects a version lacking inna. He notes 112:1's exceptional ungoverned pronoun and possible non-affair parse.
9:103/q36 How is 'your prayer' read? [صَلَوٰتَكَ · readings] (turns on: The transmitted forms and whether singular or plural better conveys generic prayer or many prayers.)
  9:103/q36/p1 The plural ṣalawātuka is read, indicating repeated supplications or multiple recipients. — reasons: Medinan readers and most named canonical readers including Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr, Abū ʿAmr, Ibn ʿĀmir, Abū Bakr from ʿĀṣim, plus Abū Jaʿfar and Yaʿqūb, read the plural, with kasra on tāʾ. Abū Ḥātim prefers it and denies feminine plural implies few, citing 18:109 and 31:27; al-Jishumī favors its transmission by most readers and the two sanctuaries. Al-Wāḥidī also cites muṣḥaf wāw here and in Hūd and al-Muʾminūn. Al-Fārisī says distinct kinds can pluralize a verbal noun.
  9:103/q36/p2 The singular ṣalātuka is read, expressing prayer generically or collectively. — reasons: Iraqi and some Meccan readers, notably Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī, Ḥafṣ and Khalaf, use singular with fatḥa; Ḥafṣ differs in al-Muʾminūn. Al-Ṭabarī prefers singular because the point is reassurance, not a count of prayers; Abū ʿUbayd or Abū ʿUbayda prefers its frequent collective use and argues plural denotes few. Al-Fārisī says a verbal noun can be singular with collective reference. Al-Ṭabrisī rejects the number argument.
  9:103/q36/p3 Both readings convey the Prophet's supplication generally. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Ibn ʿĀshūr treat singular and plural as verbal-noun forms, the plural enumerating instances and singular implying them. Al-Fārisī cites 8:35 and 31:19. Other occurrences in 6:92 and 70:23, 34 are reported as undisputed.
9:103/q37 What does 'sakan for them' signify? [سَكَنٌۭ لَّهُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether sakan names emotional assurance, mercy, dignity, nearness, purification, or inner stillness.)
  9:103/q37/p1 The Prophet's prayer gives calm, reassurance, security, and confidence that God accepted repentance or charity. — reasons: Muqātil, al-Farrāʾ, al-Ṭabarī, al-Kalbī and many later readers connect repose to accepted repentance or charity; some call it safety or strengthening of hearts. Ibn ʿĀshūr defines sakan as what one rests in, figurative mental calm, comparing night, home and spouse in 6:96 and 16:80; he also says it inclines the soul to righteous action, comparing 9:45 and 13:28. Al-Ālūsī prefers making prayer itself tranquility by hyperbole over likening resort to it to a home; al-Rāzī's soul-transmission account is rejected as philosophical by Ibn al-Naqīb.
  9:103/q37/p2 It is mercy for them. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās repeatedly gives this gloss; al-Thaʿlabī, al-Ṭabrisī and others report it. Some connect it to God's response to the prayer.
  9:103/q37/p3 It gives dignity, composure, gravitas, or honor. — reasons: Qatāda consistently gives this gloss; al-Ṭabarī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya and others preserve it.
  9:103/q37/p4 It is nearness to God. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās through al-Ḍaḥḥāk gives this in al-Māwardī and Ibn al-Jawzī; Abū Ḥayyān includes nearness.
  9:103/q37/p5 It purifies them. — reasons: Muʿādh is cited in al-Thaʿlabī, repeated by Ibn al-Jawzī.
  9:103/q37/p6 In an inward reading it is spiritual tranquility from Prophetic attention. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī describes a settled light strengthening certainty and freeing the heart from egoic and satanic agitation; al-Sulamī says it cuts worldly attachment and turns one to the afterlife.
