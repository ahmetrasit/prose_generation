## THE CURRENT MAP
30:11/q01 Why is God's name placed before the verb? [ٱللَّهُ يَبْدَؤُا۟ · rhetoric]
  30:11/q01/p1 The initial divine name emphasizes that God originates creation. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the fronting of the divine name before the verbal predicate serves emphasis alone.
30:11/q02 What does يَبْدَؤُا۟ mean? [يَبْدَؤُا۟ · meaning]
  30:11/q02/p1 It means beginning an action. — reasons: Jishumī contrasts badʾ with ending and also with returning.
30:11/q03 How is يَبْدَؤُا۟ read? [يَبْدَؤُا۟ · readings] (turns on: Whether the prefix has ḍamma and the dāl kasra or the dāl has the usual open vowel.)
  30:11/q03/p1 The usual reading opens the latter vowel. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says most readers open the dāl in contrast to the reported kasra.
  30:11/q03/p2 The variant yubdiʾu has ḍamma on the yāʾ and kasra on the dāl. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya attributes it to Ṭalḥah and Ibn Masʿūd; Abū Ḥayyān names Ibn ʿAbd Allāh and Ṭalḥah; Ālūsī names ʿAbd Allāh and Ṭalḥah.
30:11/q04 What is the creation God begins? [ٱلْخَلْقَ · referent] (turns on: Whether al-khalq names all created beings, human creation, or the historical change in the two empires' fortunes.)
  30:11/q04/p1 Created beings generally, brought into existence by God alone. — reasons: Ṭabarī says creation comes from nothing by God's power, without partner or helper; Kuran Yolu reports the same from Ṭabarī and adds no prior material. Nasafī says created beings come into existence. Biqāʿī says God continually begins whatever creation He wills, as people observe. Ibn ʿĀshūr says even the polytheists must concede that God alone originated creation, citing 13:16. The Study Quran gives this as an alternative to a specifically human reading and cites 10:4, 10:34, 27:64, and 30:27. Ibn al-Jawzī says al-khalq means created beings; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the return pronoun refers to them.
  30:11/q04/p2 Human beings in their first worldly life. — reasons: Muqātil, reported also by Wāḥidī, says God first created people. Baghawī and Ibn al-Jawzī likewise identify people. Bursevī specifies the human's creation from a drop of fluid. The Study Quran's human reading specifies first composing them from dust and cites 10:4, 10:34, 27:64, and 30:27.
  30:11/q04/p3 The beginning is the Persians' victory over the Romans. — reasons: Qāshānī reads the first creation in the setting of the two empires' fortunes.
30:11/q05 What does God's repeating or restoring creation mean? [يُعِيدُهُۥ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether repetition denotes resurrection, creation after annihilation, observable renewal, both renewal and resurrection, or the historical reversal of power.)
  30:11/q05/p1 God revives human beings after death for resurrection. — reasons: Muqātil and Wāḥidī say they return alive as they were. Yaḥyā ibn Sallām, Baghawī, Ibn al-Jawzī, Nasafī, Bursevī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, and Asad explain the return as resurrection; Bursevī distinguishes worldly and afterlife creation. Bayḍāwī and Ālūsī also call the restoration resurrection. The Study Quran describes a human body first composed from dust and then resurrected, citing 10:4, 10:34, 27:64, and 30:27. Rāzī and Iṣlāḥī say initial creation demonstrates this human restoration.
  30:11/q05/p2 God restores creation after it perishes or is annihilated. — reasons: Ṭabarī says God annihilates creation and makes it anew, complete as at first. Jishumī says God alone can restore after annihilation. Biqāʿī says creation perishes before God restores it; Kuran Yolu reports Ṭabarī's destruction and recreation. Ṭabāṭabāʾī summarizes the verse as God originating and repeating creation.
  30:11/q05/p3 God continually renews creation in nature. — reasons: Kuran Yolu offers nature's ongoing renewal as one possible sense of repeated creation.
  30:11/q05/p4 The phrase embraces both nature's continual renewal and resurrection after Judgment Day. — reasons: Kuran Yolu explicitly permits both senses together, as well as each separately.
  30:11/q05/p5 God reverses the imperial outcome by making the Romans prevail over the Persians. — reasons: Qāshānī pairs this reversal with the earlier Persian victory.
30:11/q06 What does restoration mean as a term? [يُعِيدُهُۥ · meaning]
  30:11/q06/p1 It is doing something a second time, also called return or second creation. — reasons: Jishumī says repetition is a second doing; restoration, return, and second creation are equivalent. He says return is literal for bodies and enduring accidents but figurative for speech, and does not apply literally to sounds.
30:11/q07 What does the singular pronoun in يُعِيدُهُۥ refer to? [ٱلْخَلْقَ يُعِيدُهُۥ · grammar]
  30:11/q07/p1 It refers back to al-khalq in its singular grammatical form. — reasons: Baghawī notes that the verse says He restores it rather than explicitly He restores them. Ibn al-Jawzī says al-khalq means created beings, though the singular object follows the word's form. Wāḥidī likewise contrasts the singular form here with the plural sense followed by the last verb.
30:11/q08 Must creation perish before it can be restored? [يُعِيدُهُۥ · theology] (turns on: Whether restoration requires the body's prior annihilation or can consist in regathering and reviving bodies that never perish.)
  30:11/q08/p1 Yes; restoration follows prior annihilation or perishing. — reasons: Jishumī explicitly argues from the word restores that annihilation must precede it. Ṭabarī describes annihilation before a new creation, while Biqāʿī and the account of Ṭabarī in Kuran Yolu also place perishing or destruction before restoration.
  30:11/q08/p2 No; bodies need not perish, since God can regather and revive them. — reasons: Jishumī reports al-Jāḥiẓ's view that bodies do not perish and restoration consists in gathering and reviving them.
30:11/q09 How are bodies said to be annihilated? [يُعِيدُهُۥ · theology] (turns on: Whether annihilation occurs through a created opposite, direct annihilation of bodies, a nonenduring state, or a divine command alone.)
  30:11/q09/p1 God creates an opposite called annihilation, while bodies and their accidents remain. — reasons: Jishumī reports this as the view of Abū ʿAlī, Abū Hāshim, and their followers.
  30:11/q09/p2 The bodies themselves are annihilated. — reasons: Jishumī attributes this view to Abū al-Ḥusayn al-Khayyāṭ.
  30:11/q09/p3 God creates a nonenduring state in bodies, then no subsequent state, so they cease to exist. — reasons: Jishumī introduces this as a further reported view.
  30:11/q09/p4 God merely commands the bodies to perish and they perish. — reasons: Jishumī calls this claim a weak argument.
30:11/q10 What conditions make a thing restorable? [يُعِيدُهُۥ · theology] (turns on: Whether divine power must be exclusive and whether non-generation is absolute or restricted to a nonpersistent cause.)
  30:11/q10/p1 It can persist, is not generated, and lies within the Creator's power. — reasons: Jishumī reports these three conditions from the Qāḍī.
  30:11/q10/p2 It lies within God's exclusive power, can persist, and belongs to a kind only God can restore. — reasons: Jishumī reports these three conditions from Abū ʿAlī.
  30:11/q10/p3 It lies within God's power, can persist, and is not generated from a cause that does not persist. — reasons: Jishumī reports this as another formulation, distinguishing it from the Qāḍī's unrestricted condition that the thing not be generated.
30:11/q11 How does the verse argue that God can restore creation? [ٱللَّهُ يَبْدَؤُا۟ ٱلْخَلْقَ ثُمَّ يُعِيدُهُۥ · theology]
  30:11/q11/p1 God's power to originate creation establishes His power to repeat it. — reasons: Rāzī says the first act was by power and will, so the resurrection claim has proof. Ibn Kathīr likewise infers capacity to restore from capacity to originate. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls repetition an a fortiori inference from what polytheists already concede; Biqāʿī calls ability to repeat one's own creation self-evident. Iṣlāḥī says repeating a task is easier and answers deniers who deem resurrection far-fetched. Kuran Yolu reports Ṭabarī's argument from God's unique power without partners or prior material. Asad links new creation to 10:4 and 30:27; the Study Quran cites 10:4, 10:34, 27:64, and 30:27. Kuran Yolu also invites reflection on the wisdom of creating and recreating.
30:11/q12 Whose restoration does reason or revelation require? [يُعِيدُهُۥ · theology]
  30:11/q12/p1 Reason requires restoration for those owed reward or unpaid compensation; revelation promises restoration of every living being. — reasons: Jishumī says punishment is God's right and could be omitted, while revelation includes accountable and unaccountable living beings in the promise of restoration.
30:11/q13 What does the second ثُمَّ convey? [ثُمَّ إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ · rhetoric]
  30:11/q13/p1 It marks a rise in rank, not simply later time. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says return to God is greater than recreation because it is the ultimate purpose of creation and resurrection.
30:11/q14 What does returning to God mean here? [إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the return is reckoning at resurrection, the universal destination of creatures, an inward realization alongside bodily return, or annihilation in God.)
  30:11/q14/p1 People return at resurrection for God's judgment and recompense. — reasons: Muqātil and Wāḥidī say recompense follows deeds. Yaḥyā ibn Sallām says this occurs on Judgment Day. Ṭabarī adds gathering and cites 2:28; Baghawī, Ibn al-Jawzī, Ibn Kathīr, Ālūsī, Bayḍāwī, and Bursevī also identify reckoning or recompense. Zamakhsharī and Abū Ḥayyān specify reward or punishment. Jishumī says the return establishes resurrection. Kuran Yolu stresses inevitable appearance for judgment.
