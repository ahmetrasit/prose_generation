[ELMALILI:1:2#2/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Fatiha proceeds as instruction; the command to read in the name of the Lord is a key contextual sign of this teaching purpose. | mentions 96:1
[ELMALILI:1:2#2/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · bazı müfessirîn | reports | Some exegetes posit an implicit command meaning say this at the start of Fatiha.
[ELMALILI:1:2#2/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · muhakkıkīn | prefers | The discerning exegetes hold that the teaching is clear from the style itself, without supplying an omitted command.
[ELMALILI:1:2#2/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The opening three verses speak about God in the unseen; reflection makes God present to the worshipper, who can then turn to direct address and pledge worship and need.
[ELMALILI:1:2#2/r7] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The shift from unseen narration to direct address is an eloquent turn of address that compresses extensive meanings and contributes to the Qur’an’s inimitability. | mentions 42:51
[ELMALILI:1:2#2/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Fatiha’s successive clauses unfold the opening praise and return to alhamdu lillah; the surah is the root and comprehensive representative of the other Qur’anic surahs.
[ELMALILI:1:2#2/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The basmala is the crown, the Qur’an a perfect body, Fatiha its head, and alhamdu lillah its face; mercy, guidance, worship, and divine unity express its spiritual life.
[ELMALILI:1:2#3/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Hamd is beautiful verbal praise that honors an agent for a voluntary benefaction or its source, goodness; it combines aspects of praise and gratitude and lacks a single Turkish equivalent.
[ELMALILI:1:2#3/r10] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Gustave Le Bon | reports | Le Bon and other thinkers conclude that imagination rules people more strongly than reality.
[ELMALILI:1:2#3/r11] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | rejects | Elmalılı rejects Le Bon’s conclusion: imagination affects people because it can represent reality, so reality remains the true source of its force.
[ELMALILI:1:2#3/r12] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Resûl-i Ekrem | reports | The Prophet calls hamd the head of gratitude and says that whoever fails to praise God has not thanked Him.
[ELMALILI:1:2#3/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Medh can praise living or inanimate things and can precede or follow a favor; hamd is directed to a choosing benefactor after a favor, though the benefit need not reach the praiser.
[ELMALILI:1:2#3/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Shukr responds in speech, action, or heart to a blessing actually received; spoken gratitude is also hamd and medh, while inward or bodily thanks need not be either.
[ELMALILI:1:2#3/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Every hamd is medh but not every medh is hamd; hamd is broader than gratitude in one respect and narrower in another, and unlike empty flattery both hamd and shukr affirm truth.
[ELMALILI:1:2#3/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Hamd springs from justified hope or experienced enjoyment and leans toward the future, whereas shukr celebrates an accomplished gift and its received fulfillment.
[ELMALILI:1:2#3/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Resûl-i Ekrem | reports | The Prophet commands that dust be thrown in the faces of professional eulogists, illustrating the moral danger of medh that can become flattery.
[ELMALILI:1:2#3/r7] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Resûl-i Ekrem | reports | The Prophet says that one who does not praise people does not praise God, making hamd generally commendable.
[ELMALILI:1:2#3/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Gratitude is a distinctive mark of people of truth, yet only a few servants attain it. | mentions 34:13
[ELMALILI:1:2#3/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Hazret-i Ömeru’l-Fârûk | reports | Umar’s remark that truth left him no friend conveys how difficult it is to remain faithful to truth.
[ELMALILI:1:2#4/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Verbal expressions of reverence count as hamd; announcing a blessing is a moral virtue that praises its free agent and rightly rejoices in the happiness received.
[ELMALILI:1:2#4/r10] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The lām in lillah denotes entitlement, particularity, and ownership; the whole genus and every form of hamd belongs to God.
[ELMALILI:1:2#4/r11] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Alhamdu lillah informs believers, implies reverent praise, and uses a timeless nominal sentence; the four following clauses explain it, making Fatiha one sentence in substance.
[ELMALILI:1:2#4/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Human happiness depends on recognizing and tasting a blessing, not merely possessing it; the recipient’s expressed hamd lets the giver witness that the gift reached its fitting recipient.
[ELMALILI:1:2#4/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Giving stands above receiving, so the station of the praised giver is the highest rank; the joy of this rank culminates in further hamd and increased gifts. | mentions 14:7
[ELMALILI:1:2#4/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Hamd advances through four ranks: praising, being praised, uniting both, then receiving praise while praising.
[ELMALILI:1:2#4/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The final rank manifests the divine names Hamid, Mahmud, and Hamid together; the Prophet also bears the related names Ahmed and Muhammad.
[ELMALILI:1:2#4/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The promised station of praise belongs especially to the Seal of the Prophets and is the station of the great intercession, where he receives the banner of praise.
[ELMALILI:1:2#4/r7] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Through the Prophet’s intercession, the community gathered beneath the banner of praise shares in divine hamd, and the people of Paradise conclude their plea with this verse. | mentions 10:10
[ELMALILI:1:2#4/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Hamd’s fullest nature speaks through the Prophet’s Ahmediyyah and Muhammadiyyah; its ultimate reality belongs at its beginning and end to God alone.
[ELMALILI:1:2#4/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The definite article in alhamd can express a known instance, totality, kind, or essence; because no particular hamd is presupposed, it here gathers the entire genus and all its ranks.
[ELMALILI:1:2#5/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The sound plural al-alamin foregrounds rational beings; a concise Turkish rendering is difficult, but it means God alone is Lord of all realms and especially their rational inhabitants.
[ELMALILI:1:2#5/r10] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The sign in al-alamin, later clarified by al-Baqara’s description of the Qur’an as guidance for the Godfearing, signals the wisdom of revelation. | mentions 2:2
[ELMALILI:1:2#5/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The plural’s focus on rational consciousness, reinforced by the merciful and sovereign attributes, draws reason from unseen knowledge to witnessed presence and the direct covenant of worship.
[ELMALILI:1:2#5/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rab is a verbal noun for nurturing, applied emphatically to the nurturer; rububiyyah names this relation.
[ELMALILI:1:2#5/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Rab entails complete power to nurture, govern, guide, command, reward, and discipline, and also means owner; without a qualifying relation it is reserved for God.
[ELMALILI:1:2#5/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Nurturing is gradual movement toward perfection, visibly at work throughout the universe; contemplating the realms reveals the law of divine nurturing, while the realms themselves are known through their relation to God.
[ELMALILI:1:2#5/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · bir kısım felâsife | reports | Some philosophers say the whole cosmos came to be all at once, with or without causes, rather than through gradual development.
[ELMALILI:1:2#5/r7] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · bir kısım felâsife | reports | Another group denies origination by claiming that the present cosmic order has existed eternally; on this view there is no real progress, striving, or acquisition.
[ELMALILI:1:2#5/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | rejects | Daily observation and inference show that things arise gradually and disappear gradually; examples from geology, plants, animals, and human development contradict those denials.
[ELMALILI:1:2#5/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The changing stages of the cosmos and human life still disclose a continuous, powerful reality that binds them and gives the observer awareness of unity.
[ELMALILI:1:2#6/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Observation, experience, and reason jointly establish origination and development; science may affirm evolution, but it must recognize God as its complete first cause.
[ELMALILI:1:2#6/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | rejects | Some misuse evolutionary language by treating nature’s law as an uncaused absolute, displacing divine nurturing and making humanity the absolute summit of existence.
[ELMALILI:1:2#6/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Science depends on causality: what comes into existence after not existing must have a cause, since nothing can arise from nothing.
[ELMALILI:1:2#6/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Cause and effect must be proportionate: an effect cannot exceed its complete cause, and known effects disclose their cause; otherwise nonbeing would produce being.
[ELMALILI:1:2#6/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Consciousness, knowledge, and reason cannot have a blind force or blind nature as their complete cause; natural laws require an intelligent necessary being.
[ELMALILI:1:2#6/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The wheat seed’s multiplication illustrates that one cannot explain the many grains by the seed’s isolated capacity alone; each added stage receives perfection from beyond itself.
