## THE CURRENT MAP
20:70/q01 What happened between Moses' command and the magicians' fall? [فَأُلْقِىَ · links]
  20:70/q01/p1 Moses cast his staff; it became a serpent and swallowed the magicians' work, an event the account leaves implicit. — reasons: Muqatil says God then caused their fall. Wahidi says the omitted details occur in suras 7 and 26; Ibn Atiyya allows an equivalent event. Qurtubi says the swallowed ropes and sticks amounted to three hundred camel loads and the staff returned to normal, with only God knowing where the objects went. Bursevi adds the crowd fled and Moses caught the serpent. Biqai says the omission focuses this sura on God's power to soften hearts. Ibn Ashur compares the omitted result of the sea-striking command in 26:63 and cites 20:69. Asad points to 7:117–119 for the defeat.
20:70/q02 What does the opening fa connect to? [فَأُلْقِىَ · grammar]
  20:70/q02/p1 It follows omitted events: Moses cast the staff and it swallowed their devices. — reasons: Ibn Ashur calls it coordination with an omitted clause and compares 26:63; Alusi says fear ended before Moses cast the staff. Bursevi calls the fa explanatory, following the staff's swallowing and ensuing events.
20:70/q03 Why are the magicians described as being cast down? [فَأُلْقِىَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the passive chiefly marks their own action or the overwhelming force and speed of their response.)
  20:70/q03/p1 The passive depicts a sudden, powerful fall into prostration under the force of what they witnessed. — reasons: Al-Akhfash, reported by Nasafi, compares their earlier throwing of ropes in denial with throwing their heads in thankful worship. Kashshaf, Biqai, and Jar Allah as reported by Bursevi make the same contrast. Abu Hayyan and Alusi stress the miracle's irresistible effect; Alusi adds divine guidance from obstinate unbelief and stylistic correspondence with their casting of implements. Biqai stresses extraordinary speed and ease. Tabatabai emphasizes God's overwhelming power, leaving them no choice. Bursevi likens the wording to someone hurling them.
  20:70/q03/p2 The passive means that the magicians cast themselves down; nobody else threw them. — reasons: Tabrisi takes it as their own act. Ibn Ashur adds that their public prostration acknowledged Moses as God's messenger.
20:70/q04 To whom was the magicians' prostration directed? [سُجَّدًۭا · referent] (turns on: Whether the physical prostration was worship, an acknowledgment of Moses, or a precaution before their spoken confession.)
  20:70/q04/p1 They prostrated in worship of God. — reasons: Mawardi calls it obedience to God and affirmation of Moses; Wahidi Wajiz says it accompanied faith in the brothers' Lord. Baydawi and Alusi add repentance and reverence. Ibn Ashur offers this as one possible sense.
  20:70/q04/p2 They prostrated to honor Moses after his victory. — reasons: Ibn Ashur gives this as another possible sense; Islahi notes that prostration could signal acknowledgment of someone's mastery, while the magicians also publicly professed faith in God.
  20:70/q04/p3 They may have prostrated to Pharaoh as a precaution against his violence before declaring faith. — reasons: Ibn Ashur presents this as a possible sequence.
20:70/q05 What does sujjadan express? [سُجَّدًۭا · grammar]
  20:70/q05/p1 It is the plural of sajid and describes the magicians' state as they fell, with prostration intended at the moment of falling. — reasons: Ibn Ashur gives the plural form. Zajjaj parses it as an accusative circumstantial expression whose state is prospective: they fell intending to prostrate before actually doing so.
20:70/q06 Why did the magicians recognize Moses' sign and believe? [ٱلسَّحَرَةُ ءَامَنَّا · interpretation]
  20:70/q06/p1 Their expertise showed that the staff's transformation and swallowing of their devices was a divine miracle rather than sorcery, so they accepted Moses' mission. — reasons: Qushayri says they saw the sign, prostrated in faith, and left Pharaoh and his people defeated. Ibn Atiyya says the staff remained unaffected by sorcery, transformed, consumed the props, then returned to its former state. Baydawi calls the devices tricks. Ibn Kathir says experienced sorcerers recognized a feat possible only for the Creator who says 'Be,' citing 26:47–48. Bursevi's Persian narrative adds the fleeing crowd and Moses catching the restored staff. Bursevi and Alusi transmit the chief's argument: in their magic, tools remained after victory; their disappearance implied a powerful, knowing Creator. Alusi also infers the Creator from bodily transformation and allows total annihilation. Qashani stresses fair-minded recognition of a decisive proof. Ibn Ashur and Islahi stress their expertise; Islahi says they already suspected Moses' sign was not magic and were pressured to face him (20:73). Kuranyolu contrasts their belief with Pharaoh's continued unbelief.
20:70/q07 How many magicians were there? [ٱلسَّحَرَةُ · reports] (turns on: The transmitted estimates give different counts; the notes offer no criterion for choosing among them.)
  20:70/q07/p1 There were seventy-three magicians. — reasons: Muqatil also names their chief Sham'un.
  20:70/q07/p2 There were a little over thirty thousand. — reasons: Ibn Kathir transmits this estimate from al-Suddi.
  20:70/q07/p3 There were nineteen thousand. — reasons: Ibn Kathir transmits al-Thawri through Abd al-Aziz ibn Rafi from Abu Thumama.
  20:70/q07/p4 There were fifteen thousand. — reasons: Ibn Kathir transmits Muhammad ibn Ishaq's estimate.
  20:70/q07/p5 There were twelve thousand. — reasons: Ibn Kathir transmits Ka'b al-Ahbar's estimate.
  20:70/q07/p6 There were seventy men. — reasons: Ibn Kathir transmits Ibn Abbas through Ibn Abi Hatim; the seventy began as sorcerers and ended the day as martyrs.
  20:70/q07/p7 There were eighty thousand. — reasons: Ibn Kathir transmits Muhammad ibn Ka'b's estimate.
  20:70/q07/p8 There were seventy thousand. — reasons: Ibn Kathir transmits al-Qasim ibn Abi Bazza's estimate.
20:70/q08 Who was the magicians' chief? [ٱلسَّحَرَةُ · reports]
  20:70/q08/p1 Their chief was Sham'un. — reasons: Muqatil gives the name along with a count of seventy-three magicians; other notes quote an unnamed chief's argument about the disappearance of their tools.
20:70/q09 What did the magicians see while prostrating? [سُجَّدًۭا · reports] (turns on: The reports differ on whether they saw Paradise alone, both final realms, or their homes in construction.)
  20:70/q09/p1 They saw Paradise or their allotted places there. — reasons: Mawardi says they did not lift their heads until seeing Paradise and its people's reward. Ikrima, in Kashshaf and Alusi, says God showed their places; Baydawi and Nasafi report their homes and Nasafi says they then raised their heads. Ibn Kathir transmits al-Awza'i that Paradise was raised before them.
  20:70/q09/p2 They saw both Paradise and Hell, including the reward and punishment there. — reasons: Kashshaf says they stayed prostrate until seeing both realms and the inhabitants' rewards. Alusi likewise reports the Garden and Fire, reward and punishment.
  20:70/q09/p3 They saw their future abodes being built. — reasons: Ibn Kathir transmits this from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, and also from Ikrima and al-Qasim ibn Abi Bazza.
20:70/q10 Would seeing their Paradise stations have compelled their faith? [سُجَّدًۭا ءَامَنَّا · theology] (turns on: Whether the vision preceded the choice to believe, and whether the promised stations depended on their plea for forgiveness.)
  20:70/q10/p1 Yes; such a vision would compel belief and undermine moral responsibility. — reasons: Alusi reports al-Qadi's objection to Ikrima's account of God showing their stations.
  20:70/q10/p2 No; their faith came before the unveiling, so the vision did not compel it. — reasons: Alusi answers al-Qadi in this way. Al-Irshad, reported by Alusi, says seeing their stations accords with their later plea for forgiveness at 20:73: those stations were theirs conditional on that declaration.
20:70/q11 How does qalu relate syntactically to the prostration? [قَالُوٓا۟ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the speech starts a new explanatory sentence or is syntactically tied to the prostration.)
  20:70/q11/p1 It is an explanatory resumption stating what they said while prostrating. — reasons: Bursevi gives the content-of-speech explanation; Alusi calls it a resumption like earlier passages. Tabatabai says its lack of coordination answers an implied question about what they said. Ibn Ashur includes an independent opening of dialogue among possible analyses.
  20:70/q11/p2 It can be a circumstantial clause or an appositional specification of the prostration. — reasons: Ibn Ashur presents both syntactic alternatives as ways to explain what the prostration contained.
20:70/q12 What does 'we believe' mean here? [ءَامَنَّا · meaning]
  20:70/q12/p1 They affirm the truth of God as Aaron and Moses' Lord. — reasons: Muqatil glosses belief as affirmation. Biqai says their declaration was true belief and affirmation; Yahya, Wahidi Wajiz, and Baghawi state the public profession of faith. Qushayri contrasts their earlier oath by Pharaoh's might with their new oath by God.
20:70/q13 Whom do they identify as the Lord of Aaron and Moses? [بِرَبِّ هَٰرُونَ وَمُوسَىٰ · referent]
  20:70/q13/p1 They mean God, whom both messengers called people to worship, not Pharaoh or either brother. — reasons: Mawardi says He is Lord of the brothers and all creation.
