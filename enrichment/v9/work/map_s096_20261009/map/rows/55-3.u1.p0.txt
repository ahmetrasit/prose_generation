## THE CURRENT MAP
55:3/q01 What does God's creation of the human emphasize? [خَلَقَ · interpretation] (turns on: Which aspect of creation the source draws out: formation, benefaction and power, Adam's special honor, inward endowment, or the mercy evoked by the womb.)
  55:3/q01/p1 God forms the human person with a distinctive body and outward and inward faculties. — reasons: Al-Razi distinguishes the body's formation from the knowledge bestowed through expression. Al-Biqa'i describes a familiar form and structure distinct from inanimate and other growing things, showing God's creation of all existence. Bursevi and al-Alusi specify outward and inward faculties; al-Alusi says this is bringing the species into being.
  55:3/q01/p2 Human creation displays divine power and gives humanity a blessing and an ability to receive further blessings. — reasons: Ibn Ashur calls attention to God's power in perfected creation and the favors it gives. Al-Alusi says humanity is the recipient of the blessings mentioned after Qur'anic teaching. Kur'an Yolu identifies God as humanity's creator and expression of feelings and thoughts as the foremost gift.
  55:3/q01/p3 Adam's creation is marked by a special divine honor. — reasons: Al-Junayd, as transmitted by al-Sulami, cites creation by God's hand, the breathed spirit, and the angels' prostration.
  55:3/q01/p4 God places Qur'anic intellect in the human's original nature and manifests it in the human form. — reasons: Al-Qashani says the intellect is brought forth in this world in a wondrous human form.
  55:3/q01/p5 Human creation subtly evokes divine mercy and the obligations of kinship. — reasons: The Study Quran notes that raḥim, womb, shares the root r-ḥ-m with al-Raḥmān, and cites a hadith in which God promises to maintain relations with those maintaining kinship and sever them with those who break it; it also mentions 4:1.
55:3/q02 Who is the human whom God created? [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the singular is generic, names Adam as the first human, or names Muhammad in view of the Qur'anic teaching that precedes it; al-Tabari says the apparent wording allows Adam and humanity, and al-Razi argues its generality includes both individuals.)
  55:3/q02/p1 The human is humanity as a species, expressed by a generic singular. — reasons: Al-Tabari cites the generic singular of 103:2 and accepts both this and Adam as permitted by the apparent wording; al-Mawardi and the Study Quran call this the majority view. Al-Zajjaj, al-Qushayri, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, and Abu Hayyan connect the generic referent with human speech, language, writing, understanding, and distinction from animals; al-Qushayri says each people received its language. Al-Razi prefers the general wording because it includes Muhammad, Adam, and other prophets; Elmalılı also prefers humanity generally while allowing the perfect human as recipient of the Qur'an. Al-Biqa'i says Adam can be the foremost instance without excluding the species. Ibn Ashur says this referent prepares for 55:4. Al-Wahidi's brief commentary says Adam's child, a variant way of naming the generic human.
  55:3/q02/p2 The human is Adam, the first human and foremost instance of the species. — reasons: Muqatil pairs Adam with God's teaching of speech, the explanation of all things. Al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, and Bahr al-Ulum say Adam learned all names, with Bahr al-Ulum adding all languages. Al-Biqa'i says naming Adam does not exclude humanity generally. Al-Tabari allows Adam along with the generic reading; reports are attributed to Qatada, Ibn Abbas, al-Hasan, and others. Al-Sulami transmits al-Junayd's account of Adam's special honor in creation.
  55:3/q02/p3 The human is the Prophet Muhammad. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj relates this to the Prophet's being taught the Qur'an, which clarifies everything. Ata from Ibn Abbas and al-Wahidi's brief commentary connect it with eloquent Qur'anic speech. Ibn Kaysan says the Prophet was taught or disclosed past and future events, earlier and later peoples, and Judgment Day. Elmalılı reports the Qur'an context as a reason for the reading, while preferring humanity generally.
55:3/q03 How does creating the human relate to teaching expression? [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · links] (turns on: Whether the following teaching of expression is understood for humankind, Adam, or Muhammad, and whether its distinctive effect is communication or the reasoning it entails.)
  55:3/q03/p1 Creating humanity prepares people for the distinctive gift of expression, knowledge, and mutual communication. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj, al-Qushayri, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, and Abu Hayyan say expression sets humans apart from animals; the explanations mention speech, discernment, writing, understanding, communication, and each people's language. Al-Razi distinguishes bodily formation from the knowledge of expression. Kur'an Yolu emphasizes expression of feelings and thoughts, and Islahi says speech enables people to understand and teach each other. Muqatil calls Adam's speech the explanation of all things. Al-Biqa'i describes the human form and faculties as the created basis.
  55:3/q03/p2 Adam's creation is followed by teaching him the names of all things. — reasons: Al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, and Bahr al-Ulum report this; Bahr al-Ulum includes all languages, and Muqatil calls what Adam learned speech explaining everything.
  55:3/q03/p3 Muhammad's creation is followed by teaching him Qur'anic eloquence or disclosure of past and future. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj and Ata from Ibn Abbas identify the taught content as the Qur'an; Ibn Kaysan identifies knowledge or explanation of past and future, earlier and later peoples, and Judgment Day.
  55:3/q03/p4 Human speech entails intellect and the power to reason from particulars to principles. — reasons: Islahi says speech lets people infer, reason, and form opinions; he reports Aristotle's view that this capacity distinguishes humans from animals, without which a person is merely a two-legged animal.
55:3/q04 How does the clause connect to the preceding statements without a conjunction? [* · rhetoric]
  55:3/q04/p1 It is another independent assertion of God's benefaction, confirming the preceding assertion and rebuking deniers. — reasons: Al-Wahidi calls it a coordinated confirming assertion without a conjunction. Al-Zamakhshari makes al-Rahman the subject of successive predicates, each an independent rebuke enumerating favors; conjunctions resume where clauses suit and approach each other. His example lists Zayd's rescue from poverty, lowliness, and scarcity before asking which kindness could be denied. Ibn Ashur says pagans acknowledge God created humanity yet ignore its implication for exclusive worship, so this assertion is a second rebuke without conjunction.
55:3/q05 Why is Qur'anic teaching mentioned before human creation? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the order reflects rank, human purpose, a celestial-earthly pairing, teaching before human existence, the eternal origin of divine speech, an explanation of the teaching's recipient and means, or humanity's role as recipient of blessings; al-Samarra'i says the order need not be chronological.)
  55:3/q05/p1 The Qur'an is the greatest favor, so its teaching is named first by rank. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari ranks religion and Qur'anic revelation and teaching above other favors, calling it the summit, proof, and standard of heavenly books. Al-Razi and al-Alusi give the greatest-blessing explanation; Ibn Ashur reports al-Kashshaf's explanation and notes creation and expression follow as favors. Al-Samarra'i adds that order may follow merit and priority rather than chronology.
  55:3/q05/p2 Humanity was created for religion, revelation, and worship; the purpose is presented before its created recipient. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari says human creation aims at religion and knowing scripture. Abu Hayyan says humans, the intended learners, are named after teaching, with creation the means of Qur'anic instruction. Al-Alusi says the goal of human perfection in knowledge precedes its subject mentally, though not in external reality. Al-Samarra'i cites 51:56, worship as the purpose of human and jinn creation, and calls the Qur'an the book of worship. The Study Quran reports that humans were made to know revelation before worldly creation. Islahi says creation and speech fit people to learn through revelation.
  55:3/q05/p3 The order pairs a higher celestial matter with a lower earthly one, naming the celestial first. — reasons: Al-Razi pairs Qur'anic teaching above with teaching expression to humans below.
  55:3/q05/p4 The Qur'an was taught to angels before humans were created. — reasons: Al-Razi presents this conditional reading of Qur'anic teaching; he cites 56:77–80 about the preserved Qur'an touched only by the purified and sent down from the Lord of the worlds.
  55:3/q05/p5 Qur'anic teaching or the Qur'an has a prior, eternal origin, while human creation precedes the human capacity for expression. — reasons: Bursevi reports that receptive people had complete readiness in pre-eternity; Quran names speech in its unified origin and Furqan its differentiated end, citing 25:1, so Qur'anic teaching need not depend on human creation while expression does. Bursevi adopts this ordering distinction. Al-Samarra'i says the Qur'an, God's speech and knowledge, existed before humanity and was prior in existence as well as purpose.
  55:3/q05/p6 Creating the human and then teaching expression explains the recipient and means of Qur'anic teaching. — reasons: Al-Razi offers this as part of the greatest-favor explanation. Bursevi says creation identifies who is taught and how, as a person formed with outward and inward faculties.
  55:3/q05/p7 Humanity follows the Qur'an because humanity receives the blessings being enumerated. — reasons: Al-Alusi explicitly gives the human as recipient of blessings after the blessing of Qur'anic teaching.
