20:71/q23 What is Pharaoh trying to achieve with the threats? [فَلَأُقَطِّعَنَّ وَلَأُصَلِّبَنَّكُمْ وَلَتَعْلَمُنَّ · interpretation]
  20:71/q23/p1 He tries to intimidate the magicians and deter the crowd from their faith, publicly displaying his power. — reasons: Rāzī says the threat follows his attempt to discredit them and aims to stop imitators; Qushayrī lists killing, crosswise cutting, crucifixion and severe punishment. Ibn Kathīr says the public execution would make an example; Iṣlāḥī calls it an insurgent's punishment and Kur'an Yolu reports the severe torture threat. Qatāda says the magicians recognized God's sign and prostrated before the threat; Iṣlāḥī says their faith challenged Pharaoh's sovereignty, not merely his religious claim. Iṣlāḥī also says Pharaoh invokes fear that a magician's disobedient pupil might suffer his mentor's calamity, then claims his own punishment is worse. Ibn Kathīr says Pharaoh brazenly falsified what his eyes and the public had seen. Abū Ḥayyān reports al-Ṭabarī's view that the threat displays Pharaoh's domination and his accustomed torture of people, while mocking Moses.
20:71/q24 How do sources explain Pharaoh's bravado after the miracle? [وَلَتَعْلَمُنَّ أَيُّنَآ أَشَدُّ عَذَابًۭا · interpretation]
  20:71/q24/p1 He may have been terrified privately but feigned confidence to preserve his rule. — reasons: Rāzī recalls the staff's frightening serpent and Pharaoh's need for Moses' help; he calls the lie about magical instruction and denial of divine punishment calculated public bravado. Bursawī repeats the fearful ruler's pretense; Ālūsī says the serpent even pursued his pavilion. Al-Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya, reported by Bursawī, says Pharaoh knew worldly pain but was blind to afterlife punishment.
20:71/q25 What picture of Pharaoh's authority does the scene convey? [* · interpretation]
  20:71/q25/p1 He treats faith as subject to his sovereignty and bodily punishment as decisive, while the magicians recognize another authority and an afterlife. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī says Pharaoh sees himself as Egypt's sole lord and imagines truth must await his leave; his threat reveals a bodily, material idea of human life and happiness. Iṣlāḥī says belief in Moses rejects Pharaoh's divinity and rule. Qushayrī says the magicians changed from oaths by Pharaoh's might to oaths by God in one day; Kur'an Yolu says they valued afterlife happiness over worldly reward or survival. Ibn Kathīr sets their claim of guidance against Pharaoh's threatened punishment; Kur'an Yolu places the confrontation amid Egypt's political anxiety over Israel and Moses' effort to lead them home.
20:71/q26 Which parallel accounts illuminate this verse? [* · links]
  20:71/q26/p1 The confrontation recurs in al-Aʿrāf and al-Shuʿarāʾ, with useful differences in wording and narrative emphasis. — reasons: Study Quran points to 7:123–26 and 26:49; Ibn Khālawayh, Wāḥidī and Abū Ḥayyān refer to the al-Aʿrāf reading discussion. Abū Ḥayyān highlights bihi there versus lahu here; Ibn Kathīr and Ṭabāṭabāʾī link the conspiracy charge at 7:123. Kur'an Yolu invites comparison of the Qur'anic and Biblical accounts of the episode.
20:71/q27 What demeanor does Pharaoh's remark show? [قَالَ · interpretation]
  20:71/q27/p1 A degree of softening and submission. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya reads Pharaoh's remark this way.
