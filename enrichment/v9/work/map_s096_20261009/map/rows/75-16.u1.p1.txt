75:16/q06 What does “to hasten with it” mean? [لِتَعْجَلَ بِهِۦٓ · meaning] (turns on: What activity is premature and what 'it' denotes.)
  75:16/q06/p1 To try to take in or memorize revelation before its delivery is complete. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī distinguishes haste, action before its due time, from speed at the first due moment. Al-Wāḥidī and al-Rāzī gloss the phrase as hastening to receive it; 20:114 is their parallel. Al-Ālūsī says the bāʾ transitively marks what the tongue moves with or one hastens to learn.
  75:16/q06/p2 To deliver or speak the revelation prematurely out of eagerness. — reasons: Al-Shaʿbī gives eagerness to convey and sweetness of speech as the occasion; al-Jishumī's worldly reading calls for slow repetition to people.
  75:16/q06/p3 To hurry through a record of deeds, or to hurry a question about Judgment. — reasons: Al-Qaffāl links the former to the fearful reading of the record; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports and rejects the latter.
75:16/q07 How does the purpose phrase function? [لِتَعْجَلَ بِهِۦٓ · grammar]
  75:16/q07/p1 It explains why the Prophet moved his tongue; the bāʾ marks the object of his recitation or learning. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī says the purpose clause gives the cause of the motion and matches the sūra's atmosphere of haste. Al-Ālūsī calls the bāʾ transitive on both accounts, movement with it or haste to learn it.
75:16/q08 Why did the Prophet hasten? [لِتَعْجَلَ بِهِۦٓ · reports] (turns on: The reported motive: fear of loss, love and mission, questions about meaning, or the need to teach listeners.)
  75:16/q08/p1 He feared losing or forgetting the revelation and strained to secure its words. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās reports moving lips to catch a passage's beginning before its end, with the revelation weighing on him; Saʿīd demonstrates the movement in reports transmitted by Bukhārī and Muslim. Al-Tirmidhī grades one account ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ. Al-Biqāʿī adds reverence, while Farāhī adds fear of accountability for even one lost letter.
  75:16/q08/p2 He loved revelation, its sound, knowledge, and the duty of conveying it. — reasons: Al-Shaʿbī calls it love and sweetness on the tongue; al-Jishumī reports Ibn ʿAbbās, Saʿīd, and al-Ḍaḥḥāk likewise. Al-Biqāʿī compares Moses' haste to please God at 20:84 and calls Muhammad's motive praiseworthy though calm reception is a higher state. Iṣlāḥī compares his urgency to a hungry child, thirsty traveler, and separated lover receiving a letter. He needed answers to opponents, spiritual and intellectual guidance, and sound mission strategy; Farāhī says this concern coexisted with memory fear.
  75:16/q08/p3 He hurried to understand the meaning and ask questions while Gabriel was still reciting. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān reports al-Zamakhsharī's addition of questions about meaning to hurried memorization; divine preservation lets questions wait.
  75:16/q08/p4 In a worldly coherence reading, he was urged to repeat slowly so listeners could absorb the proof. — reasons: Al-Jishumī says listeners' attachment to worldly life made repeated reminders necessary.
75:16/q09 What happened after the instruction was revealed? [* · reports]
  75:16/q09/p1 The Prophet listened quietly to Gabriel, then recited the revelation as taught after Gabriel departed. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās and Saʿīd imitate the former lip movement. Bukhārī and Muslim transmit multiple routes; Bukhārī says he lowered his head when Gabriel arrived. One report adds that a sūra's end became known only when the basmala descended; another says he should follow the recitation and explain lawful and unlawful matters. Al-Biqāʿī gives no grading for his transmitted report.
  75:16/q09/p2 The revelation occasion is transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and several early interpreters. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr names al-Shaʿbī, al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, Qatādah, Mujāhid, and al-Ḍaḥḥāk; Wāḥidī names Muqātil, Mujāhid, Qatādah, and ʿAṭāʾ. Al-Tirmidhī's Ibn ʿAbbās report is graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ by al-Qurṭubī; Ibn ʿAṭiyyah and Abū Ḥayyān cite Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī.
75:16/q10 What does the instruction teach about receiving and preserving revelation? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the lesson is specific to the Prophet's reception or also extends to later Qurʾān students.)
  75:16/q10/p1 Listen until the reading is complete; God undertakes collection, preservation, recitation, and explanation. — reasons: Al-Khaṭṭābī says the unlettered Prophet should attend to understanding; al-Kashshāf adds heart and ear, then repetition and study. Ibn Kathīr identifies three stages: reception, recitation, explanation. Al-Sāmarrāʾī says inna causally grounds the prohibition in the promise, comparing 9:103. Iṣlāḥī adds arrangement and divinely appointed timing; 20:114–115 exhorts patience.
  75:16/q10/p2 Patient study should precede conclusions; students must attend to meaning rather than mere fluent sound. — reasons: Asad applies the instruction to every believer, linking 20:114 to hasty conclusions from isolated verses and urging study of the whole message. Study Quran likewise urges reflection on all teachings.
75:16/q11 Does the prohibition imply a prophetic sin? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether the act was permitted before the command, a sin, or a good act refined by instruction.)
  75:16/q11/p1 The earlier haste could have been permitted until this prohibition. — reasons: Al-Rāzī answers the sin argument by analogy with a ruling changed over time, as in abrogation.
  75:16/q11/p2 The haste was either unauthorized sin or inexplicable if already permitted. — reasons: Al-Rāzī attributes this dilemma to those who permit prophetic sin.
  75:16/q11/p3 The haste sprang from a praiseworthy motive, and the command moved him to a more complete calm. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī sees love of God and message as its motive and compares Moses' haste to please God at 20:84. Iṣlāḥī says noble motives still required patience under gradual revelation.
75:16/q12 What does this episode show about prophecy and divine agency? [* · theology]
  75:16/q12/p1 God fixed revelation in the Prophet without ordinary study, demonstrating divine preservation and authorship. — reasons: Al-Jishumī calls this a miracle contrary to ordinary experience. Al-Biqāʿī says the command unveils divine giving rather than Muhammad's composition and cites 76:30 for action under God's will.
  75:16/q12/p2 The Prophet had exceptional perception, yet receiving Gabriel's words still strained him. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says he was complete and continually advancing, not distracted from knowledge; he cites the Prophet's report of seeing companions behind his back, and describes sight in darkness and complete unveiling. Despite that, reverence and fear of loss prompted haste.
  75:16/q12/p3 The divine timing of revelation supplied the Prophet's mission, spiritual renewal, and answers to opponents. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says he depended on guidance at every step, while opponents challenged piecemeal revelation and demanded replies. Revelation strengthened resolve, soul, and intellect; the Prophet was to leave collection, arrangement, explanation, and timing to God.
75:16/q13 How is this instruction connected to other Qurʾānic passages? [* · links]
  75:16/q13/p1 It parallels 20:114, which forbids hastening the Qurʾān before revelation is complete. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī also cites 87:6. Al-Rāzī says 20:114 redirects reliance from repetition to God and asks Him for knowledge. Ibn Kathīr notes the instruction to ask for more knowledge. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the parallel supports reports of one-time as well as staged descent. Iṣlāḥī adds 20:115 for patience.
  75:16/q13/p2 Its divine guarantee is comparable to promises of preserving, reciting, or clarifying revelation. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī reports Abū ʿAlī's comparison to 20:114; 87:6 is also invoked for assured memory. Al-Sāmarrāʾī compares the causal use of inna with 9:103.
75:16/q14 How does the instruction fit the sūra's surrounding argument? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the command is an occasion-driven aside, a thematic transition, or part of the judgment scene.)
  75:16/q14/p1 It is a timely aside during revelation, after which the resurrection discourse resumes. — reasons: Al-Khaṭṭābī and al-Rāzī compare a teacher or speaker briefly correcting a distracted listener.
