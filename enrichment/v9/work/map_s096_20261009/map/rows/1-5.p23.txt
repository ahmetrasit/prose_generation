[ELMALILI:1:5#6/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | When a person shares another’s welfare and harm as their own, social feeling and brotherhood begin; practiced empathy strengthens community and reduces pride.
[ELMALILI:1:5#6/r10] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | As like-minded individuals meet, an actual congregation forms readily and follows an imam; its collective power corresponds to that imam.
[ELMALILI:1:5#6/r11] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Social spirit precedes the social body, so an individual can carry and represent the conscience of a community before it exists without circularity.
[ELMALILI:1:5#6/r12] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Islam is an unsurpassed social and civil bond; narrow-minded people seek small gods and think restricted brotherhood easier, yet cannot attain a Muslim's firmness and serenity.
[ELMALILI:1:5#6/r13] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The Muslim community's strength depends on the number of its members and the strength of their Islamic conscience; sustaining this universal spirit without an existing community is a prophetic station, especially that of the final Prophet, by divine support.
[ELMALILI:1:5#6/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The breadth and strength of shared feeling set the scale of brotherhood, society, and civilization; narrow particular affiliations fragment wider bonds.
[ELMALILI:1:5#6/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | A community needs both breadth in its common bond and strength in conscience: breadth without strength dissolves it, while strength without breadth prevents its growth.
[ELMALILI:1:5#6/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Social feeling is first a divine innate gift and second a reflection of one's surroundings, and it is shaped by both innate and acquired upbringing.
[ELMALILI:1:5#6/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Only God, without counterpart, equal, or partner, can encompass all human love and fear; devotion to Him grounds universal brotherhood and a worldwide community.
[ELMALILI:1:5#6/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | God trains and strengthens social feeling by taking a covenant from each rational individual in the plural, so each represents all humanity and the worlds.
[ELMALILI:1:5#6/r7] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The first-person plural is grammatically spoken by one individual who represents fellow believers; a congregation speaks through a representative rather than each member saying “I.”
[ELMALILI:1:5#6/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Saying “we worship” makes the individual represent those he feels as brothers, beginning with guardian angels and extending to present or envisaged congregations.
[ELMALILI:1:5#6/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | In the author's school, followers behind the prayer leader recite neither Fatiha nor anything else because the imam recites for them; a person praying alone recites as imam of a potential congregation.
[ELMALILI:1:5#7/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | God first established this covenant in Muhammad's prophetic heart through revelation; he is the complete, truthful servant and representative individual able fully to say the verse, and the model of servanthood.
[ELMALILI:1:5#7/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The faith declaration pairs God's oneness with Muhammad's being His servant and messenger; at Hira the Prophet's worship represented the universal spirit and his communal conscience became imam to all worlds.
[ELMALILI:1:5#7/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Communities sincerely following the Prophet's Sunna can collectively uphold the covenant, submit to no one besides God, and seek aid from no one else; this spirit drove early Islamic conquests and expansion.
[ELMALILI:1:5#7/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Replacing the plural with singular wording in prayer invalidates it because God requires a communal conscience covenant, not an individual-only promise; worshippers should consider whom they represent and on whose behalf they seek aid.
[ELMALILI:1:5#7/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | If no actual worshipping congregation suffices, a Muslim should mentally join the existing congregation to the potential one, including God's angels.
[ELMALILI:1:5#7/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | reports | Exegetes say the plural pronouns refer to the reciter together with guardian angels, the present congregation, or all monotheists.
[ELMALILI:1:5#8/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The verse entails both unity of Lordship, accepting God's unique real essence, and unity of worship, a pledge to form a unified community; the latter builds a relational unity on the former.
[ELMALILI:1:5#8/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The verse renews a witnessed testimony that there is no deity besides God and a commitment to act on it; collective Qur'anic pledges can distribute rights and duties to every individual (an individual obligation) or assign them to the collective body (a communal obligation).
[ELMALILI:1:5#8/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Here the individual sense is retained: each believer bears the individual obligation, while collective testimony is the aggregate of individual testimony; the two consciences mutually guarantee one another.
[ELMALILI:1:5#8/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The verse is pure monotheism and rejects every form of associating partners with God.
[ELMALILI:1:5#8/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · امام فخرالدین رازی | reports | Razi classifies shirk by corporeal versus incorporeal partners: lower or celestial bodies, simple or composite bodies, and material idols, plants, animals, humans, heavenly bodies, or spirits.
[ELMALILI:1:5#8/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · امام فخرالدین رازی | reports | Razi includes worshippers of fire, the sun, moon, stars, and astrologers who attribute fortune and misfortune to the stars, as well as Manichaean light-and-dark dualists.
[ELMALILI:1:5#8/r7] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · امام فخرالدین رازی | reports | Razi describes angel worshippers who assign cosmic and climatic governance to spirits and worship their images, and dualists who posit good and evil first principles such as Yazdan and Ahriman.
[ELMALILI:1:5#8/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | rejects | Two wholly disconnected first principles could not organize one world; if they share kinship or a higher principle, they cease to be first principles, which leads toward the Trinity and Christianity's developed form.
[ELMALILI:1:5#8/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Humanity wandered from awareness of one God into multiplicity, reduced it to two and then Trinity; Islam restores true oneness, and the verse refutes shirk, uniting people who enter Islam while departure fragments and humiliates them.
[ELMALILI:1:5#9/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | After the covenant, the prayer for guidance begins as the most comprehensive practical application of seeking divine aid; it answers the worshipper's need, after pausing at “we seek aid,” to know where and how to begin.
[ELMALILI:1:5#9/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Guidance is contained in seeking aid, which is contained in worship and praise; thus all of Fatiha unfolds as one complete الحمد لله sentence and unified discourse, so its first two pauses are complete but not obligatory.
[ELMALILI:1:5/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Elmalılı renders the verse as worshipping and serving God alone and seeking aid and care from Him alone.
[ELMALILI:1:5/r10] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Intention is a present, created will to obey God and draw near through the act; resolve comes before action, purpose accompanies it, and intention joins knowledge to the act.
[ELMALILI:1:5/r11] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Full awareness, containing both knowledge and will, belongs to the soul and heart.
[ELMALILI:1:5/r12] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Worship also requires an outwardly manifested act of obedience; inward reflection alone may be taat and qurbat but does not become worship.
[ELMALILI:1:5/r13] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Faith requires at least verbal confession as well as inward assent, while an unexpressed inner thought is insufficient as worship.
[ELMALILI:1:5/r14] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | External ritual acts without intention are not worship: sleep is not prayer, hunger is not fasting, giving is not alms, travel is not pilgrimage, and fighting is not jihad.
[ELMALILI:1:5/r15] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | A bad intention, meaning an aim other than obedience and nearness to God, prevents an act from counting as worship.
[ELMALILI:1:5/r16] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | rejects | Turkish tapmak and tapınmak suggest indiscriminate or unconscious obedience and are associated with idols and the cross, so translating worship that way debases the language; kulluk is more aware but weaker than worship.
[ELMALILI:1:5/r17] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Before later legal convention, the shared lexical root meaning of worship should be considered; its broadest sense appears in ubudet or abed and includes worshipper and object of worship.
[ELMALILI:1:5/r18] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Ubudet is the broadest term, while ibadat is narrower; ubudiyet displays humility and ibadat expresses its strongest form, including awe and reverence.
[ELMALILI:1:5/r19] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · cumhûr-i müfessirîn | reports | The majority of exegetes define worship as the utmost degree of humility, which entails complete obedience without asking the reason.
[ELMALILI:1:5/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The imperative meaning 'say this' is implicit; placing the independent pronoun before the verb expresses exclusivity: to You alone, not another.
[ELMALILI:1:5/r20] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · İbn Sikkît | reports | Ibn Sikkit is reported in Abu Hayyan's tafsir as deriving ibadat from the sense of tajrid, or detachment.
