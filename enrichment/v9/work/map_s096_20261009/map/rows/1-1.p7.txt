[MAWARDI:1:1/r41] MAWARDI, d. 450 · الكلبي | reports | Al-Kalbi offers an esoteric letter-by-letter gloss: b means beauty, blessing, kindness, and insight; s means radiance, loftiness, and sovereignty; m means glory, dominion, and favor.

[MAWARDI:1:1/r45] MAWARDI, d. 450 · author | holds | Saying bismillah is called basmala, a coined usage that also occurs in poetry by Umar ibn Abi Rabi‘a.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r46] MAWARDI, d. 450 · author | holds | Allah is God’s most exclusive name, since no other is called by it; the phrase asking whether He has a like is the broader interpretation because it covers names and deeds.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r47] MAWARDI, d. 450 · أبو حنيفة | reports | Abu Hanifa is reported to say Allah is God’s greatest name because no one shares it.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r48] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | One view treats Allah as an un-derived proper name for the divine essence, needed as the name to which attribute names attach.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r49] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | Another view derives Allah from aliha, with the hamza dropped and the word pronounced emphatically.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r5] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | The formula may be a declarative statement that the speaker is seeking refuge in God.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r50] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | One derivation of ilah is al-walah: servants flee to it in their affairs, just as people follow an imam.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r51] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | Another derives ilah from worship, citing Ru’ba’s verse using ta’alluh for worship and Ibn Abbas’s reading of 43:20 as worship. | mentions 43:20
[MAWARDI:1:1/r52] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | If ilah derives from the act of worship, it is not an eternal attribute, since worship began only after creation; on that view God was not ilah before creating creatures.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r53] MAWARDI, d. 450 · author | prefers | Al-Mawardi prefers deriving ilah from deserving worship, making it an eternal essential attribute; otherwise Jesus and idols would count as gods because they were worshipped.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r54] MAWARDI, d. 450 · author | holds | Al-Rahman and al-Rahim are divine names, and al-Rahim is a name derived from an attribute.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r55] MAWARDI, d. 450 · ثعلب | reports | Thalab says al-Rahman is a Hebraicized non-Arabic word; Quraysh’s reported failure to recognize it in 25:60 is his evidence, as is Jarir’s verse mentioning Rahman as an offering. | mentions 25:60
[MAWARDI:1:1/r56] MAWARDI, d. 450 · ثعلب | reports | On the non-Arabic-word view, al-Rahim would naturally precede al-Rahman as the Arabic name, but al-Rahman is placed first because it is more intensive and the pair removes ambiguity.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r57] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | The other view says al-Rahman is Arabic like al-Rahim, with their similar letters reflected in Arabic usage and Shanfarā’s verse.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r58] MAWARDI, d. 450 · author | holds | If Arabic, both names derive from mercy, defined as beneficence to someone in need; 21:107 calls the Prophet a mercy to the worlds, meaning a blessing to them. | mentions 21:107
[MAWARDI:1:1/r59] MAWARDI, d. 450 · author | holds | A blessing is called mercy because it arises from mercy.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r6] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | Alternatively, its declarative wording functions as a prayer asking the All-Hearing and All-Knowing God to protect the speaker, since He hears prayer and knows how to answer.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r60] MAWARDI, d. 450 · author | holds | Al-Rahman is more intensive than al-Rahim: its form and meaning extend, while al-Rahim’s meaning extends but its wording does not; people are named Rahim, but no one Rahman.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r61] MAWARDI, d. 450 · author | holds | Pre-Islamic Arabs called God al-Rahman, but Musaylima the Liar later took the divine name for himself.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r62] MAWARDI, d. 450 · عطاء | reports | Ata says al-Rahman is paired with al-Rahim to distinguish God’s name from anyone else’s, since no one bears both names.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r63] MAWARDI, d. 450 · أبو عبيدة | reports | Abu Ubayda distinguishes them by saying al-Rahman is the possessor of mercy and al-Rahim is the merciful one.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r64] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | One view derives both names from a single mercy, with al-Rahman as the more intensive form.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r65] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | Another view derives the names from two distinct mercies so the names and attributes differ.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r66] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | Under the two-mercy view, al-Rahman denotes mercy to all creation, while al-Rahim denotes mercy to the obedient.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r67] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | A second distinction makes al-Rahman’s mercy for people of this world and the afterlife, and al-Rahim’s for people of this world only.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r68] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | A third distinction reserves al-Rahman’s mercy to God alone and takes al-Rahim’s mercy as a kind also found among creatures.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r7] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | From Satan can mean from his whispering.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r8] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | From Satan can alternatively mean from his assistants.
[MAWARDI:1:1/r9] MAWARDI, d. 450 · unattributed | reports | Al-rajim may mean the one who pelts people with calamities and afflictions.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p47/r1] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · author | holds | The basmala is agreed to be Qur'an in 27:30, but scholars dispute its status at the start of al-Fatiha and each sura; al-Shafi'i's group affirms it and Abu Hanifa's group does not. | mentions 27:30
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p47/r10] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · author | holds | A name points to its referent, whereas an attribute conveys information about the one described; an attribute used as a name does both.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p47/r11] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · author | rejects | The claim that a name is identical with its referent and the spoken form is merely naming is unsound: it would make names of actions identical to the actions.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p47/r12] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · الفراء | reports | He derives ism from sima, a mark, because a name distinguishes its referent.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p47/r13] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · الخليل والزجاج | reports | They derive ism from elevation, because a name raises the person named above others.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p47/r2] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · أبو عبيدة | reports | He and others treat bi-sm as an extra connective, so the intended wording is Allah, the Merciful, the Compassionate.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p47/r3] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · لبيد | reports | His verse uses ism as an extra word, evidence for treating bi-sm as connective rather than essential.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p47/r4] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · قطرب | reports | On the extra-word reading, ism magnifies and honors God's mention, distinguishing it from mention of creatures.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p47/r5] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · الأخفش | reports | On the extra-word reading, ism shifts the phrase away from an oath and toward seeking blessing.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p47/r6] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · الجمهور | reports | Most scholars hold that bi-sm is an essential, intended part of the phrase.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p47/r7] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · الفراء | reports | The prefixed bāʾ has imperative force: begin in the name of God.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p47/r8] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · الزجاج | reports | The prefixed bāʾ has declarative force: I began in the name of God.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p47/r9] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · author | holds | The alif of connection is omitted in speech and writing through frequent use, as in al-Rahman; it remains written in 96:1 because that form is less frequent. | mentions 96:1
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p49/r1] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · عمرو بن معدي كرب | reports | The cited verse witnesses the derivation of ism from elevation through the wording سما لك or سموت له.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p49/r10] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · author | reports | One view treats Allah as a proper name for the divine essence, not derived from an attribute, since attribute names depend on an essence name.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p49/r11] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · author | reports | A second view derives Allah from ilāh: the hamza is dropped and the pronunciation intensified.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p49/r12] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · author | reports | One etymology derives ilāh from yearning: creatures flee to Him in their affairs, as followers turn to an imam.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p49/r13] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · author | reports | Another etymology derives ilāh from ulūhiyya, worship, as in the verb meaning to worship.
[MAWARDI-FULL:v1p49/r2] MAWARDI-FULL, d. 450 · الكلبي | reports | He assigns symbolic meanings to the letters: bāʾ signifies beauty, blessing, goodness, and insight; sīn splendor, eminence, and mastery; mīm glory, dominion, and beneficence.
