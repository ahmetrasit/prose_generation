  53:40/q14/p2 The 'most complete' reward may be an added reward beyond Paradise: seeing God. — reasons: Rāzī prefers this reading because an unrestricted superlative calls for what exceeds every otherwise complete reward; he compares 10:26.
53:40/q15 What does this promise teach about deeds and accountability? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the emphasis is comprehensive judgment or the worth of honest personal effort.)
  53:40/q15/p1 Every good and bad deed is accounted for and justly repaid, however small. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās says both kinds of deeds are seen and repaid; Ibn Kathīr specifies good for good and evil for evil. Islāhī says even tiny good deeds count and no small evil is ignored; Kur'an Yolu says every freely chosen deed is brought forward and judged with complete justice. Study Quran says deeds testify to a person's life.
  53:40/q15/p2 Honest effort and earning by one's own labor have value before God. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu draws this teaching from the verses.
53:40/q16 How do the neighboring statements address the fears and hopes of righteous people and sinners? [* · links]
  53:40/q16/p1 The wording leaves sinners hope of forgiveness and reassures the righteous about what they earn. — reasons: Rāzī says bearing no other's burden does not exclude God's forgiving one's own burden. For the righteous, 'only what he strives for' affirms receipt of what is earned; phrasing it as 'not what he did not strive for' would not. This balance expresses mercy preceding wrath.
53:40/q17 What inward lesson is drawn from a person seeing their striving? [سَعْيَهُۥ يُرَىٰ · inward] (turns on: Whether seeing effort exposes dependence on grace, removes self-credit, or discloses stages of the inward path.)
  53:40/q17/p1 One sees one's striving is unfit for God and survives only through divine grace. — reasons: Tustarī and Sahl say the servant learns what the striving merits; without the Lord's grace, it would perish or cause the servant to perish.
  53:40/q17/p2 Striving is not a means of earning reward. — reasons: Al-Wāsiṭī says this of the Prophet's striving in al-Sulamī, and Bursevī reports the principle without that limitation.
  53:40/q17/p3 Recognition of a work's defects makes one dislike credit for it. — reasons: Abū Ḥafṣ says one disowns attribution of deficient work to oneself and dislikes being recalled for it.
  53:40/q17/p4 The path of striving moves through beginning, middle, and end, with distinct gifts at the end. — reasons: Al-Warrāq says annihilation is rewarded with witnessing and subsistence with bodily nurture, including lawful provisions deferred on the path.
  53:40/q17/p5 Striving has four inward levels, each with its own result. — reasons: Bursevī describes the ego's purification from violations and natural conformity, yielding gardens; the heart's removal of darkness and veils, yielding abandonment of worldly desire, pleasure, adornment, wealth, and rank; the secret's polishing through divine attributes, yielding their manifestations; and the spirit's essential theophanies, yielding extinction of ego and subsistence in oneness.

53:40/q23 What do the cited literary examples show about figurative images? [* · rhetoric]
  53:40/q23/p1 A single image can support several different comparisons. — reasons: Al-Jurjānī says a sparking flint can suggest generosity, sharp intelligence, success, or reaching an aim; failure to spark can suggest miserliness, dullness, or fruitless effort. The moon can suggest fame, distinction, honor, elevation, completion, and decline. He cites Abū Tammām's growing noble child, al-Buḥturī's rising honor, an unnamed poet's life cycle, Ibn Bābak's vizier's partial royal authority after Fakhr al-Dawla appointed Abū ʿAlī, and Abū Bakr al-Khwārizmī's wealthy versus poor host.
53:40/q24 What determines whether a word sounds apt in a literary context? [* · rhetoric]
  53:40/q24/p1 A word's merit comes from its fit with neighboring words, not from its isolated sound or meaning. — reasons: Al-Jurjānī says أخدع works in al-Ḥamāsa and al-Buḥturī but burdens a line by Abū Tammām; شيء works in ʿUmar ibn Abī Rabīʿa and Abū Ḥayya but weakens al-Mutanabbī's line about the revolving heavens. If merit inhered in a word alone, its effect would not change between settings.
