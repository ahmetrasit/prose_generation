## THE CURRENT MAP
75:30/q01 What is meant by the journey being to your Lord? [إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the Lord is the endpoint Himself, His judgment or appointed abode, the agent doing the driving, or the object of a dying person's turn.)
  75:30/q01/p1 It is the inescapable return to God Himself, the final destination. — reasons: Muqātil says there is no departure from Him in the afterlife. Thaʿlabī and Wāḥidī call it the destination and return; Baghawī says servants are driven back; Qurṭubī glosses Lord as Creator and masāq as return. Ibn Kathīr describes the spirit ascending and returning to earth. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says this course begins at death and allows no choice or escape; he also reports that fronting can mark return to God alone. Samarrāʾī compares 75:12 and says there is no other destination; Iṣlāḥī stresses everyone's helpless journey. Al-Qummī and al-Bāqir call it the journey to the Lord of the worlds.
  75:30/q01/p2 The person is brought to God's judgment, reckoning, or recompense. — reasons: Jishumī says God decrees the person's fate. Ṭabrisī says God alone commands and forbids at the gathering place. Bursevī says no one else rules, and reports Kāshifī's gloss of return to the Lord's recompense. Ālūsī prefers an implied addition of ḥukm, God's judgment, and says no other destination exists. Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports the same supplied word. Elmalılı says the person is seized for reckoning and recompense; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the meeting with the Lord's recompense metaphorical driving. Kuran Yolu ties judgment to worldly deeds; Study Quran reports Final Judgment as the endpoint.
  75:30/q01/p3 It means the destination God appoints, Paradise or Hell, or the corresponding high or low station. — reasons: Nasafī says servants go where God commands, Paradise or Hell. Abū Ḥayyān names these two final destinations. Ṭabrisī's alternative has an angel carry a person of Paradise to ʿIlliyyūn and a person of Hell to Sijjīn; Qushayrī likewise names their degrees. Biqāʿī speaks of clear happiness or misery after death. Ālūsī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī report the alternative implicit addition of God's appointment.
  75:30/q01/p4 The phrase can mean that God alone is the one driving the person. — reasons: Rāzī contrasts God as driver with God as destination and says the driving is entrusted to Him. Ālūsī reports this alternative; Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports that return to God means He alone drives.
  75:30/q01/p5 The movement toward the Sustainer is belated repentance or an impulse to turn to Him. — reasons: Asad interprets the driving as belated repentance and says the person then feels impelled to turn toward his Sustainer.
75:30/q02 Who drives or carries the person or soul? [إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ ٱلْمَسَاقُ · referent] (turns on: Whether the verse assigns the act to angels generally, the recorder of evil deeds specifically, or God Himself.)
  75:30/q02/p1 Angels carry or drive the soul where God commands. — reasons: Thaʿlabī and Wāḥidī say angels take the spirit where God commands. Jishumī reports an angel doing so. Ṭabrisī specifies ʿIlliyyūn or Sijjīn according to the person's fate; Qushayrī also names these stations. Biqāʿī says angels drive each person after death toward happiness or misery.
  75:30/q02/p2 God Himself is the driver. — reasons: Rāzī says driving is entrusted to God; Ālūsī and Ṭabāṭabāʾī report this interpretation. Study Quran reports that God drives the soul out of the body at death or toward the Final Judgment, differing on the movement described.
  75:30/q02/p3 The angel who recorded the person's evil deeds drives him to God. — reasons: Qurṭubī reports this tafsīr without endorsing it.
75:30/q03 When is 'that day,' and when does this journey occur? [يَوْمَئِذٍ · referent] (turns on: Whether yawm points to death, the resurrection, or the transition through both.)
  75:30/q03/p1 It begins at death, when the soul leaves worldly life. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the forced course begins at death. Iṣlāḥī places the helpless beginning in death's agony. Samarrāʾī says fronting specifies the day of departure, and contrasts this movement with the later permanent abode. Study Quran reports God driving the soul out of the body at death. Asad renders the expression 'at that time,' while noting that its literal wording is 'on that day'; his note breaks off during discussion of yawm.
  75:30/q03/p2 It is the Resurrection or the Hereafter, including the Final Judgment. — reasons: Qurṭubī explicitly glosses 'that day' as Resurrection. Ibn Jurayj, as transmitted by al-Durr, places the procession in the Hereafter. Study Quran reports driving toward the Final Judgment.
  75:30/q03/p3 Death joins the first stage of the hereafter, with later resurrection hardship to follow. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās, via ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥa in Qurṭubī, says the last worldly day meets the first hereafter day, with death's agony meeting the encounter's terror unless God has mercy. Al-Bāqir's report describes the world entwining with the hereafter as the person dies. Qurṭubī reports another view in which a disbeliever's soul is punished on exit and then faces resurrection's hardship.
75:30/q04 Whose journey is described? [ٱلْمَسَاقُ · referent] (turns on: Whether the article in al-masāq is generic or stands for the course of the particular denying human.)
  75:30/q04/p1 It encompasses everyone, righteous and wicked. — reasons: Ṭabrisī says all creatures are driven to the gathering place. Bursevī says every person goes to God. Iṣlāḥī says none escapes. Ibn ʿĀshūr reads the article as generic, including the rejecting human. Ālūsī allows both the wicked and righteous and says each is driven suitably to the respective destination.
  75:30/q04/p2 The immediate subject is the rejecting or wicked human addressed in the passage. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr's alternative takes the article in masāq as replacing the man's genitive, identifying him as the one who asks when Resurrection will be in 75:6. Ālūsī explicitly allows a passage about only the wicked, while saying its driving fits their fate.
75:30/q05 What does al-masāq mean and how is it formed? [ٱلْمَسَاقُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether masāq names the action or journey, its endpoint, or several aspects together.)
  75:30/q05/p1 It is the act or course of being driven, a verbal noun from sāqa yasūqu. — reasons: Rāzī and Qurṭubī compare its pattern with maqāl from qāla yaqūlu. Nasafī derives it from sāqahu. Abū Ḥayyān derives it from sawq; Bursevī gives a Persian equivalent for being driven. Elmalılı and Ibn ʿĀshūr call it a mīm-form verbal noun. Ibn ʿĀshūr defines driving as leading someone ahead of oneself toward a destination, opposite to qawd, and says the meeting with recompense is a metaphorical use. Ṭabāṭabāʾī stresses its compulsory force.
  75:30/q05/p2 It is departure or the onward journey. — reasons: Al-Māwardī transmits Khārija's gloss of masāq as departure or onward movement.
  75:30/q05/p3 It is the final resting place, return, or destination. — reasons: Al-Māwardī transmits Muqātil's final resting place. Qurṭubī calls it the return; Abū Ḥayyān includes the return and final destination, Paradise or Hell. Ṭabrisī's reported view calls it the place of driving.
  75:30/q05/p4 The word embraces the driving, its destination, and its time. — reasons: Biqāʿī explicitly gives all three aspects, under the rule of the Lord who sent and supported the Prophet.
75:30/q06 What does the definite article in al-masāq do? [ٱلْمَسَاقُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the article generalizes the journey or replaces an understood possessor.)
  75:30/q06/p1 It substitutes for a genitive complement: the human's or particular man's driving. — reasons: Bursevī says the implied complement is the human being. Ibn ʿĀshūr's alternative identifies the man as the one asking when Resurrection will be in 75:6.
  75:30/q06/p2 It is generic and encompasses everyone, including the rejecting human. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr reports the generic reading alongside the implied-genitive alternative.
75:30/q07 How does this clause answer a preceding time expression? [إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ يَوْمَئِذٍ ٱلْمَسَاقُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the following clause supplies the temporal answer overtly or context supplies an omitted answer.)
  75:30/q07/p1 The clause itself occupies the answer-position of idhā. — reasons: Naḥḥās assigns 'to your Lord that day is the journey' the syntactic place of the answer to idhā.
  75:30/q07/p2 The answer to idh is omitted and understood from the context. — reasons: Ālūsī supplies such possibilities as events unfolding, truth being revealed, or the person finding the good or evil deeds he performed.
75:30/q08 Why are 'to your Lord' and 'that day' placed before al-masāq? [إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ يَوْمَئِذٍ ٱلْمَسَاقُ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the order signals exclusive destination and appointed time or merely aligns the verse endings.)
  75:30/q08/p1 Fronting makes God the exclusive destination and specifies the day of departure. — reasons: Ālūsī says the fronting emphasizes exclusivity and prefers God's judgment as the endpoint. Samarrāʾī says comparable return passages allow no other destination; 'to your Lord' comes first because the destination is more important than the specified time. He contrasts earlier open destinations with being driven like a slave to one Lord without choice, and the ordinary undated trip home with the appointed day of final departure. His reference to 75:12 supports the distinct final destination. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the destination phrase is fronted for emphasis because the return to the Lord is precisely what the deniers contest.
  75:30/q08/p2 The order matches the verse endings. — reasons: Samarrāʾī reports this explanation from unnamed interpreters and rejects it in favor of exclusivity.
