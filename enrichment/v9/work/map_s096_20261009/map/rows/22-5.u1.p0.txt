## THE CURRENT MAP
22:5/q01 Who is addressed by 'O people'?  [ٱلنَّاسُ · referent] (turns on: Whether the general wording primarily addresses Meccan deniers or all people.)
  22:5/q01/p1 The unbelievers or polytheists of Mecca, especially resurrection deniers. — reasons: Muqātil, Ibn ʿAbbās via Wāḥidī, and others identify the Meccan audience; Ibn ʿĀshūr says they are the primary target, while the wording remains general.
  22:5/q01/p2 The wording addresses people generally, while deniers are especially in view. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī preserves the general wording; Biqāʿī allows both scopes and associates the broad address with prevalence of denial.
22:5/q02 Why frame the doubt with 'if'? [إِن كُنتُمْ فِى رَيْبٍۢ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the condition depicts entrenched doubt, weak doubt, or accommodates a mixed audience.)
  22:5/q02/p1 It poses actual doubt hypothetically so the proof can dispel or rebuke it. — reasons: Bursevī says the evidence uproots doubt; Ibn ʿĀshūr compares 43:5; Ālūsī says the condition ascribes doubt to deniers without strengthening their case.
22:5/q03 What does rayb mean here? [رَيْبٍۢ · meaning] (turns on: Whether it is plain doubt or doubt colored by disturbance, suspicion, or fear.)
  22:5/q03/p1 Doubt about resurrection. — reasons: Yaḥyā, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, and Ibn ʿAbbās use the ordinary gloss; Qurṭubī also records accusation and need in other contexts.
  22:5/q03/p2 A troubling, ugly doubt, with possible fear or suspicion. — reasons: Ṭabrisī calls it an ugly doubt; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ argues rayb and shakk are not synonyms, citing Qurʾānic distribution, lexicographers, and poetry; Ibn Fāris adds fear.
22:5/q04 What aspect of resurrection is doubted? [مِّنَ ٱلْبَعْثِ · interpretation] (turns on: Possibility or actual occurrence.)
  22:5/q04/p1 Whether reviving the dead is possible and within divine power. — reasons: Bayḍāwī and Ālūsī emphasize possibility because the following creation proofs show capacity; Ālūsī cites 22:7 for occurrence.
  22:5/q04/p2 Whether resurrection will in fact occur. — reasons: Bursevī notes this alternative; Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls it return to God; Islāḥī explains idolaters' reluctance to face accountability.
22:5/q05 How is البعث read? [ٱلْبَعْثِ · readings] (turns on: Whether ʿayn is quiescent or open, and whether vowel alternation is a general guttural rule or only attested usage.)
  22:5/q05/p1 The usual reading has quiescent ʿayn. — reasons: Basrans treat open forms as lexical dialect variants; Kufans permit lightening around gutturals, a generalization contested by Abū Isḥāq and al-Samīn.
  22:5/q05/p2 Al-Ḥasan reads with open ʿayn. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, and later commentators compare other guttural-middle words; Basrans call it dialectal, Kufans a lightening pattern.
22:5/q06 How does 'We created you' answer the condition? [فَإِنَّا خَلَقْنَٰكُم · grammar] (turns on: Whether the clause itself is the answer or evidence for an omitted answer.)
  22:5/q06/p1 Creation is evidence for an implicit command to consider one's origin and recognize resurrection. — reasons: Bursevī and Ibn ʿĀshūr note that creation preceded the hearers' doubt, so the literal act of creation cannot be a consequent event; Ālūsī permits the clause to occupy the answer's place.
  22:5/q06/p2 The creation clause directly presents the proof that resolves the conditional doubt. — reasons: Ṭabarī and others present the staged first creation as the immediate argument; the one who originated life can restore it.
22:5/q07 How were humans created from dust? [تُرَابٍۢ · referent] (turns on: Whether dust refers to Adam's origin or to the food-chain origin of every human.)
  22:5/q07/p1 It refers to Adam, ancestor of humankind, with descendants included through him. — reasons: Zajjāj, Ṭabarī and most commentators name Adam; Rāzī cites 3:59 and 20:55; Ibn ʿĀshūr includes Eve and later reproductive descent; Ālūsī prefers this.
  22:5/q07/p2 Each person derives from earth through nourishment, which becomes reproductive fluid and bodily matter. — reasons: Rāzī traces semen and menstrual blood through food to plants, earth and water; Bayḍāwī and Abū Ḥayyān give the same route; Study Quran includes animals and milk.
  22:5/q07/p3 Dust signals humanity's lowly biological kinship with earthy substances. — reasons: Asad cites 3:59 and 23:12; Muqātil recalls that humans began as nothing.
22:5/q08 What is a nutfah here? [نُّطْفَةٍۢ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the fluid is semen, Adam's own semen, or a fertilized ovum; its base water sense varies.)
  22:5/q08/p1 Semen from which Adam's descendants develop, literally a drop or quantity of water. — reasons: Thaʿlabī, Wāḥidī, Qurṭubī and Ālūsī discuss dripping, flowing, or pouring roots; Ṭabrisī includes male and female fluid; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says water may be little or much.
  22:5/q08/p2 Adam's own semen is the intended drop. — reasons: Al-Naqqāsh is quoted by Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān; Ālūsī calls this remote.
  22:5/q08/p3 It may name the sperm or the fertilized ovum produced from it. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu uses embryonic context; Elmalılı speaks more precisely of the seed within semen.
22:5/q09 What is an ʿalaqah? [عَلَقَةٍۢ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the word describes congealed blood or an attached early embryo.)
  22:5/q09/p1 A piece of fresh, thick, congealed or clotted blood. — reasons: Abū ʿUbayd emphasizes intense redness; Wāḥidī cites al-Qaṭāmī; al-Samīn cites an Arabic saying about battlefield blood; commentators describe the preceding fluid thickening.
  22:5/q09/p2 It denotes a moist, clinging or attached early stage. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī derives it from adhesion; Islāḥī allows a living leech-like referent; Kur'an Yolu prefers a fertilized ovum attached to the uterine wall.
22:5/q10 What is a muḍghah? [مُّضْغَةٍۢ · meaning]
  22:5/q10/p1 A small piece of flesh about the size of a mouthful to chew. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba compares names for scooped or eaten quantities; Ibn ʿĀshūr explains its morphology; a hadith calls the heart a muḍghah.
22:5/q11 What distinguishes formed from unformed? [مُّخَلَّقَةٍۢ غَيْرِ مُخَلَّقَةٍۢ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the contrast concerns form, completeness, ensoulment, miscarriage, term birth, or the primordial covenant.)
  22:5/q11/p1 One embryo is visibly shaped, complete or smooth; the other is not yet or imperfectly shaped. — reasons: Qatāda's complete/incomplete gloss is widespread. Zajjāj, Rāzī, Zamakhsharī, and Nasafī allow deficiencies in limbs and faculties; Rāzī prefers this because the address concerns future humans.
  22:5/q11/p2 The formed pregnancy lives or reaches birth; the unformed one miscarries before completion. — reasons: Mujāhid sometimes describes formed and unformed miscarriages; Ibn Masʿūd's angel report has an unformed drop expelled as blood. Ṭabarī prefers expulsion before completed shaping and ensoulment.
  22:5/q11/p3 The formed receives a spirit; the unformed lacks ensoulment. — reasons: Wāḥidī prefers this explanation, and Ibn ʿAbbās links it to life and the widow's waiting period; Bursevī reports an inward version in which unformed is an image without soul.
  22:5/q11/p4 The formed has the primordial covenant; the unformed does not. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās in ʿAṭāʾ's transmission and a report from Abū Jaʿfar identify formed particles in Adam's loins under the covenant; the latter includes expelled semen and pre-ensoulment miscarriage among unformed.
  22:5/q11/p5 Every developmental stage is created and may count as mukhallaqah. — reasons: Ibn al-ʿArabī reasons from the root; Qurṭubī prefers repeated creation and cites 23:14, against a sense restricted to an imaged fetus.
22:5/q12 How are the adjectives 'formed and unformed' construed or read? [مُّخَلَّقَةٍۢ غَيْرِ مُخَلَّقَةٍۢ · readings] (turns on: Whether they are genitive adjectives or accusative circumstantial expressions.)
  22:5/q12/p1 The common genitive forms modify muḍghah. — reasons: Bursevī and Ālūsī make the attachment explicit; al-Samīn identifies the general reading.
  22:5/q12/p2 Accusative forms are read as circumstantial or detached qualifiers. — reasons: Al-Kisāʾī permits them; Ibn Abī ʿAblah reads both accusative; Sībawayh allows a circumstantial qualifier even after an indefinite noun, though rare.
22:5/q13 What do reports say about fetal stages and angelic decrees? [نُّطْفَةٍۢ عَلَقَةٍۢ مُّضْغَةٍۢ · reports] (turns on: The reports differ on the timing of shaping and angelic writing.)
  22:5/q13/p1 A drop, clot, and morsel each last forty days, followed by ensoulment and decrees. — reasons: Ibn Masʿūd's Prophetic report lists provision, life, deeds, and fortune; Qurṭubī derives four months and links it to the widow's waiting period. Ibn Kathīr transmits Ṣaḥīḥayn versions.
  22:5/q13/p2 An angel shapes or records the embryo after roughly forty to forty-five nights. — reasons: Abū Dharr and Ḥudhayfa versions mention sex and fortune or bodily features; Ibn Kathīr cites Muslim. Bursevī says an early reference to form means its decree, with visible form later.
  22:5/q13/p3 A settled drop is classified as formed or expelled, and the formed child's particulars are copied from the Mother Book. — reasons: Ibn Masʿūd and related reports list sex, provision, lifespan, deeds, fortune, and sometimes burial place; Ṭabāṭabāʾ reconciles changeable and unchangeable tablets.
22:5/q14 What does 'so that We may make clear to you' disclose? [لِّنُبَيِّنَ لَكُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether its object is creation, power, resurrection, guidance, or intentionally left open.)
  22:5/q14/p1 The successive stages and manner of creation in wombs. — reasons: Yaḥyā, Ibn Qutayba, Wāḥidī's Ibn Muslim, and Qatāda focus on explaining the stages of human creation. Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports that incomplete embryos and miscarriages make womb stages clear.
