87:1/q07 Who is commanded to glorify? [سَبِّحِ رَبِّكَ · referent]
  87:1/q07/p1 The Prophet is directly addressed, with a charge that guides believers too. — reasons: Ibn Ashur identifies the addressee; Biqai says the perfected Messenger understands how to glorify fully; Islahi reads the address as strengthening the Prophet amid his preaching fears.
87:1/q08 What does al-aʿla qualify? [ٱلْأَعْلَى · grammar] (turns on: Case and the attachment of the following relative clause 'who created' in 87:2.)
  87:1/q08/p1 It qualifies rabb, 'Lord,' in the genitive. — reasons: Nahhas prefers this. Kashshaf, Alusi and Study Quran call it the more natural or majority parse. Abu Hayyan and Samin note that if 'who created' in 87:2 qualifies rabb, an intervening modifier of ism would separate them. Corpus Coranicum compares the Hebrew epithet following a divine title.
  87:1/q08/p2 It qualifies ism, 'name,' in the accusative. — reasons: Jishumi and Tabrisi connect this to God's exalted names. Abu Hayyan says 'who created' must then be detached as a predicate of an omitted subject or an accusative of praise; Samin also allows it to qualify ism. Alusi says such a name shares its essential meaning with no other; Elmalili says all divine names are beautiful though of ranks.
87:1/q09 What kind of highness does al-aʿla express? [ٱلْأَعْلَى · theology] (turns on: Whether highness denotes spatial direction, absolute excellence and power, ungraspable greatness, or a non-comparative adjective.)
  87:1/q09/p1 Supremacy in power, sovereignty, perfection, and rank, without spatial location. — reasons: Jishumi cites al-Farazdaq's poetry for honor and strength; Tabrisi compares al-akbar. Razi argues a finite or infinite corporeal deity is impossible and creation in 87:2 suits power, not distance. Ibn Ashur reads the superlative as absolute superiority and figurative enduring perfection, citing 79:24 and al-Ghazali's sensory versus intellectual ranks. Qutb evokes limitless horizons.
  87:1/q09/p2 God is elevated in a physical direction. — reasons: Razi attributes this proof from al-a'la to corporealists, then rejects it by arguments about finite and infinite dimensions and by the context of praise and creation.
  87:1/q09/p3 God's grandeur, favors, and rightful claims exceed all human knowledge, praise, thanks, and obedience. — reasons: Razi gives this as one sense; Bursevi says knowers cannot grasp His height and should be humbled.
  87:1/q09/p4 Al-aʿla can simply mean al-ʿali, 'high,' as al-akbar can mean 'great.' — reasons: Razi offers this comparison as another lexical option.
  87:1/q09/p5 The 'Great' and 'Highest' epithets imply two different lords. — reasons: Razi reports a skeptic's objection tied to 56:74 and answers that reason establishes one Creator and neither verse compares Him to another lord.
87:1/q10 Why is the Lord called al-aʿla here? [ٱلْأَعْلَى · rhetoric] (turns on: The explanatory role of the epithet and the extent to which verse-end cadence explains its form.)
  87:1/q10/p1 His superlative rank makes transcendence fitting, and the following acts establish His power and care. — reasons: Biqai says rank is absolute. Elmalili says the title explains why sanctification is owed and verses 2-5 establish it; Ibn Ashur selects creation, guidance and provision as fitting attributes, and links divine highness to the Quran's dignity and the Prophet's reception.
  87:1/q10/p2 The ending contributes to the sura's cadence, but its choice carries meaning beyond rhyme. — reasons: Burhan compares the extra endings in al-A'la and al-'Alaq; Bint al-Shati rejects making cadence the sole reason for al-a'la rather than al-'ula, comparing 92:20. Qutb hears the opening's extended rhythm.
87:1/q11 What variant wording or pronunciation is reported for this opening? [سَبِّحِ ٱسْمَ رَبِّكَ ٱلْأَعْلَى · readings] (turns on: Whether transmitted subhana phrases are variant text or recitation responses, and how the final vowel is pronounced.)
  87:1/q11/p1 The opening is recited as 'Subhana rabbiya al-aʿla,' sometimes followed by 'who created and proportioned.' — reasons: Ibn Umar, Ubayy, Ali, Abu Musa, Ibn al-Zubayr, Malik b. Dinar and others are named across sources. Reports vary between an actual alternate reading and a response after the canonical verse. Qurtubi denies the phrase is Quranic text; Corpus Coranicum retains the common imperative because similar imperatives open early Meccan passages.
  87:1/q11/p2 The opening is also reported as 'Subhana ismi rabbika al-aʿla.' — reasons: Ibn Umar is heard using this form, attributed to Ubayy's reading; the reports vary in whether the possessive of subhana is ismi or rabbi.
  87:1/q11/p3 A shorter wording, 'Sabbih rabbaka al-aʿla,' is reported. — reasons: Durr attributes this to Ibn al-Zubayr and al-Dahhak, who also directed the reciter to add the glorification.
  87:1/q11/p4 The common imperative sabbih isma rabbika al-aʿla is retained. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum cites analogous early Meccan imperative openings, including 69:52 and 56:74; Qurtubi treats the added subhana phrase as devotional response rather than Quran.
  87:1/q11/p5 The rhyme ending al-aʿla admits imala. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari says the imala of endings from al-a'la through Musa was treated in his earlier chapter.
87:1/q12 What should a reciter say after this verse? [سَبِّحِ ٱلْأَعْلَى · reports]
  87:1/q12/p1 Say 'Subhana rabbiya al-aʿla' as a response to the command, including in prayer. — reasons: Reports name the Prophet, Ibn Abbas, Ali, Ibn Umar, Umar, Abu Musa, Ibn al-Zubayr, al-Dahhak, Qatada and al-Baqir. Ibn Abbas also advised responding at 75:40; Ali said he obeyed the command rather than added Quran. Qurtubi recommends the response and denies it is Quranic text. Abu Dawud notes Waki's raised Ibn Abbas report conflicts with mawquf versions from Abu Waki and Shu'ba. Itqan places the response within reflective recitation.
87:1/q13 What glorification belongs in prostration? [سَبِّحِ ٱلْأَعْلَى · law]
  87:1/q13/p1 Use 'Subhana rabbiya al-aʿla' in prostration; the Great Lord's glorification belongs in bowing. — reasons: Reports from Uqba and others assign 87:1 to sujud after assigning 56:74, 56:96, or 69:52 to ruku. Abu Hurayra's report ties revelation to the Companions' question and specifies an odd number of repetitions; Study Quran describes three repetitions in each posture. Ibn Ashur says bodily humility joins spoken praise. Kashshaf and Baydawi also report 'O God, to You I bow/prostrate' supplications.
87:1/q14 What occasion is reported for this verse's revelation? [* · reports] (turns on: The reported triggering event: a human question about sujud or an angel's failed ascent and praise.)
  87:1/q14/p1 The Companions asked what to say in prostration; the verse descended and the Prophet supplied its glorification. — reasons: Abu Hurayra's version adds an odd number of repetitions; Durr and its full account also transmit the question.
  87:1/q14/p2 An angel failed to reach the Throne despite multiplying wings, then glorified the Most High, prompting the verse and prostration instruction. — reasons: Qurtubi transmits the tale from Ja'far b. Muhammad through his forebears and gives no grading; the angel is called Hizqiyal in one account.
87:1/q15 Who first uttered this glorification? [* · reports] (turns on: Which angel the distinct reports identify.)
  87:1/q15/p1 Michael was the first to say 'Subhana rabbiya al-aʿla.' — reasons: Ali's report and several later sources transmit this attribution; no grading is supplied in Qurtubi.
  87:1/q15/p2 An angel named Hizqiyal said it after failing to reach the Throne. — reasons: Qurtubi's ungraded tale gives an extended story of multiplied wings and long flight before the utterance.
87:1/q16 Where and when was this sura revealed? [* · sciences] (turns on: Early transmission and thematic dating versus a later inference from possible references to Eid worship; chronology counts al-Fatiha differently.)
  87:1/q16/p1 The whole sura is Meccan, among the early revelations. — reasons: Ibn Abbas, Ibn al-Zubayr and Aisha are reported; al-Bara says it was taught in Medina before the Prophet arrived. Ibn Atiyya calls the contrary Dahhak report weak; Alusi and Ibn Ashur say Eid and alms references do not prove Medinan origin. Ibn Ashur cites verse 6 and early themes.
  87:1/q16/p2 The whole sura is Medinan. — reasons: Al-Dahhak is named; Ibn al-Faris connects the view to Eid prayer and end-of-Ramadan alms. Ibn Atiyya, Alusi and others dispute the inference on the strength of al-Bara's report and the lack of a decisive ritual reference.
  87:1/q16/p3 Most is Meccan, but verses 14–15 are Medinan. — reasons: Ibn Ashur reports this from Ibn Umar and Ibn Abbas in the full source.
  87:1/q16/p4 It was seventh in the revelation order. — reasons: Ibn Abbas, Ikrima and al-Hasan place it in an early sequence after Iqra, Nun, al-Muzzammil, Tabbat and al-Takwir.
  87:1/q16/p5 It was eighth, after al-Takwir and before al-Layl. — reasons: Jabir b. Zayd's count inserts al-Fatiha after al-Muddaththir; Ibn Ashur says either chronology is early and fits verse 6.
87:1/q17 What are the sura's names and verse count? [* · sciences]
  87:1/q17/p1 It is called al-Aʿla and also Sabbih or Subh, and has nineteen verses. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says al-A'la comes from a word unique to this sura and is favored by commentators and mushaf compilers, while the Prophet's Muadh report and Aisha's witr report use its opening; Baydawi and Ibn Kathir also call it Sabbih. Alusi and Ibn Ashur say the count of nineteen is unanimous.
