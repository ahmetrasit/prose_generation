55:4/q07 Why do Qurʾān teaching, human creation, and teaching bayān appear in this order? [عَلَّمَهُ ٱلْبَيَانَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the order follows rank and purpose, pre-eternal origin, a heaven-and-earth pairing, temporal creation before human instruction, or the sūra's sequence of favors.)
  55:4/q07/p1 The greatest religious favor and its purpose take precedence over creation and the human faculty. — reasons: Zamakhsharī calls revelation and teaching the summit and standard of heavenly books; Nasafī says humanity was created for religion and knowledge of revelation; Ibn ʿĀshūr reports Zamakhsharī and says religion reforms worldly life and secures the next. Sāmarrāʾī cites 51:56: worship is creation's purpose, so the book of worship comes first; Qurʾānic order can follow purpose, merit, or context rather than time. Rāzī says the order of al-ʿAlaq differs only because it lacks explicit Qurʾān teaching; its creation-before-teaching sequence parallels this one.
  55:4/q07/p2 The sequence pairs heavenly instruction with earthly creation and expression. — reasons: Ālūsī says the arrangement is elegant whether the omitted first recipient is angels or Gabriel; Rāzī distinguishes humanity taught the Qurʾān, with the second object omitted to exalt the favor, from angels taught first, requiring the human recipient to be named here. Ālūsī cites 56:77–79 for angels before human creation.
  55:4/q07/p3 The opening lists divine favors while showing mercy and perfected creation. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the sūra highlights God's perfected creation, humanity's dependence on mercy, and worldly and afterlife favors amid punishment of sinners and honor of the pious. Iṣlāḥī connects the opening to God's merciful choice to teach by revelation rather than punish.
  55:4/q07/p4 Qurʾān teaching precedes creation because its origin is pre-eternal; human expression requires creation. — reasons: Bursevī reports that receptive people were readied in pre-eternity; Qurʾān names speech at its unified origin and Furqān its differentiated end, citing 25:1. He therefore places expression after creation, while Qurʾān teaching can precede it.
  55:4/q07/p5 Bayān comes after creation because teaching the human expression follows forming the human. — reasons: Rāzī compares the creation-before-teaching order with 96:1, 3, 4 and 96:4–5; on the speech reading, bodily formation precedes the distinguishing knowledge of expression.
55:4/q08 What does the gift of bayān enable or teach about humanity? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Which consequence of expression the sources foreground: human distinction, religious learning, reasoning and society, eloquence, or the favor's addressees.)
  55:4/q08/p1 It distinguishes humans from animals through speech, thought, and learning. — reasons: Zajjaj, Qushayrī, Wāḥidī, Zamakhsharī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Ṭabrisī, Nasafī, Qāshānī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ālūsī, and Study Quran stress this distinction. Rāzī contrasts bodily form and acquired knowledge. Bīqāʿī adds inference of unseen from signs or calculation and Adam's knowledge before angels. Iṣlāḥī reports Aristotle on intellectual distinction and says refusing to reason despite this gift lowers people to animals; Kuranyolu says comprehension is intellect's practical face.
  55:4/q08/p2 It makes religious instruction, worship, and human accountability possible. — reasons: Qatāda says law and moral discrimination establish proof against people. Iṣlāḥī says speech supports learning from the merciful Qurʾān rather than punishment and should be used for teaching. Kuranyolu says thought and expression allow people to recognize worship and articulate duties and rights. Elmalılı says prophets communicate revelation and communities benefit from scripture.
  55:4/q08/p3 It supports reasoning, self-perfection, shared knowledge, culture, and civilization. — reasons: Bīqāʿī says it grasps particulars and universals, infers absent matters, and lets people perfect themselves and teach others; familiarity hides the extraordinary divine gift. Iṣlāḥī describes movement between particulars and principles, reasoning, inference, and opinions. Kuranyolu links it to culture, civilization, meaningful action, art, and diverse modes from tears to visual arts; Elmalılı calls it essential to Qurʾānic understanding.
  55:4/q08/p4 It is a great blessing that preserves human standing and guides people toward good. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls language one of God's greatest blessings and a condition of human standing; Ibn ʿĀshūr places knowledge and expression among merciful created favors, with the sūra contrasting sinners' punishment and the pious' honor.
  55:4/q08/p5 It honors eloquence and equips understanding and persuasion. — reasons: Burhān says speech with Qurʾān teaching suffices to show eloquence's honor; he cites 3:138 and 16:89 as scriptural praise of bayān, and says rhetorical forms strengthen interpretation, argument, and a hearer's assent.
  55:4/q08/p6 The human is emphasized because he receives and explains revelation to both groups. — reasons: Sāmarrāʾī says the human is singled out as recipient of the book and its interpreter for both groups; expression reveals what is inward.
55:4/q09 What inward or spiritual senses are given to bayān? [ٱلْبَيَانَ · inward] (turns on: Which inner process is identified with bayān: faculties of the person, prior disclosure, guidance toward God, address to God, or purified remembrance.)
  55:4/q09/p1 Speech arises from spirit and unfolds through reason, heart, thought, and nature. — reasons: Tustarī describes Adam's speech from spirit, rational understanding, heart discernment, human thought, and natural disposition, subsequently clarified by God. Sulamī transmits Sahl's parallel account: speech is creatures' mind and spirit's essence, with intellect understanding, heart discernment, and self nature.
  55:4/q09/p2 Bayān teaches the way to God after humanity was created ignorant of Him. — reasons: Sulamī attributes this to Junayd; Māwardī transmits the same from unnamed companions of thoughts.
  55:4/q09/p3 Bayān discloses a teaching already present in pre-eternity. — reasons: Sulamī transmits al-Wāsiṭī: the spirit had been disclosed before time, and bayān reveals an earlier teaching.
  55:4/q09/p4 For believers and knowers it is addressing the Lord through speech, breath, tears, longing, and sighs. — reasons: Qushayrī contrasts ordinary address by likeness and form with the knowers' modes of addressing God.
  55:4/q09/p5 Remembrance and reflection become clear spiritual perception when directed through God. — reasons: Al-Wāsiṭī, via Sulamī, says self-directed remembrance and reflection cut one off; for and through God they connect and purify the senses, letting the clear heart behold divine dominion. Increasing both brings nearness and knowledge.
55:4/q10 Can unqualified bayān name ugly speech? [ٱلْبَيَانَ · meaning]
  55:4/q10/p1 No; God's praise reserves the unqualified term for worthy speech, though an ugly utterance can be called clarification with a qualifier. — reasons: Rummānī distinguishes the praised absolute name from the qualified sense of merely making one's intended meaning understood.
55:4/q11 How does divine or Qurʾānic bayān compare with other speech? [ٱلْبَيَانَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the point is the Qurʾān's superior expressive rank or recognition of genuinely divine speech.)
  55:4/q11/p1 Qurʾānic expression is the highest and most guiding form. — reasons: Bāqillānī calls it noblest, most complete, elevated, eloquent, radiant, and rightly guiding. Burhān cites 3:138 and 16:89 as praise of divine clarification. Zamakhsharī and Nasafī call Qurʾānic teaching the chief religious favor and standard for scriptures.
  55:4/q11/p2 Speech from divine sovereignty can be recognized as different from human pretension. — reasons: Bāqillānī reports Abū Bakr judging Musaylima's utterance not to be from God; the episode illustrates distinguishing divine speech from other speech.
55:4/q12 What doctrinal inference is drawn from the pairing of Qurʾān and human creation? [* · theology]
  55:4/q12/p1 The Qurʾān is uncreated. — reasons: Bursevī reports Abū al-Suʿūd's count: the Qurʾān is mentioned in 54 places without being called created, while humanity is called created in 18; this passage pairs their different descriptions.
