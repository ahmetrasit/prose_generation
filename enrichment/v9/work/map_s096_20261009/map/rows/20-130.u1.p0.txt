## THE CURRENT MAP
20:130/q01 What speech must the Prophet endure, and why? [فَٱصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the command is read primarily against the opponents' speech and its delayed punishment or as inward formation under divine decree.)
  20:130/q01/p1 He must endure hostile denials, insults, and demands for punishment until God's appointed judgment, while continuing his mission. — reasons: Muqatil specifies denial of threatened punishment; Yahya, Tabari, Mawardi, Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Razi, Bursevi and Ibn Ashur list accusations including sorcery, madness, poetry, lying and soothsaying. Tabari, Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Bursevi, Alusi and Ibn Ashur relate patience to the fixed time of punishment; Razi adds continued preaching, and Islahi relates it to pressure from those demanding punishment.
  20:130/q01/p2 He should patiently accept God's decree, recognizing that opponents act under divine power and that their objections train him spiritually. — reasons: Qashani presents patience through God and divine decree; Bursevi transmits the Najmi inward interpretation. Tabatabai joins acceptance of delayed judgment to rejecting shirk and praising God.
20:130/q02 What does patience mean here as a virtue? [فَٱصْبِرْ · interpretation]
  20:130/q02/p1 It is self-restraint appropriate to reason and divine law, expressed differently in battle, calamity, and silence; enduring abuse can be good or obligatory. — reasons: Bursevi quotes Raghib's definition and his examples of courage, broadness of chest and keeping silent, with 2:177, 22:35 and 33:35; Jishumi says endurance of abuse is praiseworthy and sometimes obligatory.
20:130/q03 Was the command to endure their speech abrogated by fighting? [فَٱصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ · sciences] (turns on: Whether patient endurance of speech is incompatible with the later command to fight.)
  20:130/q03/p1 Yes; the later fighting command ended the earlier permission or duty simply to endure abuse. — reasons: Wahidi says God subsequently decreed their killing; Ibn Jawzi and the reported Kalbi-Muqatil, Baghawi and Bursevi accounts cite the fighting or sword verse.
  20:130/q03/p2 No; combat and patience under verbal abuse can coexist, so the command remains applicable. — reasons: Jishumi argues that the passages do not conflict; Qurtubi reports that fighting did not eradicate all unbelievers; al-Kabir says fighting need not end endurance of speech; Alusi reads patience as composure, not cessation of fighting.
20:130/q04 What does the command to glorify God require here? [وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether tasbih names ritual prayer, direct remembrance and transcendence, or an inward spiritual process; the time phrases and context are used to decide.)
  20:130/q04/p1 It means prayer, including praise and glorification, at the specified times. — reasons: Tabari explains prayer while praising God; Bursevi calls tasbih a part naming the whole; Ibn Ashur says prayer includes glorification. Most commentators in Razi and the early authorities map the phrases to prayers. Razi prefers the complete five-prayer construal among prayer interpretations; Tabatabai notes that this is the majority's identification, though the word alone does not compel it. Muqatil links the command to hoped-for afterlife reward.
  20:130/q04/p2 It means declaring God's transcendence and majesty through remembrance, in speech, thought or action, rather than specifying ritual prayer. — reasons: Abu Muslim and Razi relate this reading to countering the opponents' denial and shirk; Razi considers it closer to context. Ibn Atiyya allows the formula subhan Allah wa bihamdihi after dawn and before sunset and cites seventy glorifications; Tabarsi allows continuous praise; Abu Hayyan distinguishes a spoken formula from semantic exaltation; al-Izz calls the ba instrumental and says negating deficiency affirms perfection. Alusi notes both an objection from specified times and a reply taking them as signs of continuity; Tabatabai says the night part complicates that reply. Kur'an Yolu cites the prayer chronology.
  20:130/q04/p3 It signifies inward stripping of selfhood and manifestation of the Lord's qualities, with spiritual illumination across the named times. — reasons: Qashani reads the rising and setting as disclosure and concealment of spirit and night as the veiled ego; the Study Quran transmits Aj's path of patience and remembrance until selfhood vanishes and the Beloved is witnessed.
20:130/q05 How does prayer or remembrance help the Prophet endure? [فَٱصْبِرْ وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ · interpretation]
  20:130/q05/p1 It brings solace, perseverance and divine aid amid abuse and hardship. — reasons: Razi compares 2:45 and says remembrance gives rest; Biqai says worship raises the soul; Bursevi cites 13:28 and the Prophet's recourse to prayer in distress; Alusi compares 2:45 and 2:153. Islahi extends the lesson to community hardship and says prayer teaches that events follow God's will for good, while the Prophet's role is to teach as well as recite. Ibn Ashur directs attention from worldly comforts to spiritual purification and the God-fearing outcome.
20:130/q06 How is bi-ḥamdi rabbika construed? [بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ · grammar] (turns on: The force of ba and whether praise is annexed to its agent or object.)
  20:130/q06/p1 It is circumstantial accompaniment: glorify or pray while praising God, especially for His help, guidance and blessings. — reasons: Tabari makes the praise God's object by comparison with an Arabic verbal noun construction; Kashshaf, Razi and Nasafi specify praise for enabling prayer; Baydawi adds guidance and blessings. Alusi takes the ba as accompaniment and praise as an object-directed verbal noun; Ibn Ashur calls it a circumstantial phrase with an agent relation; Samin likewise calls it a hal.
  20:130/q06/p2 The ba is instrumental, and divine praise may be understood as God's own praise of Himself. — reasons: Al-Izz ibn Abd al-Salam permits agent, object and specifying annexation; Alusi prefers agent annexation because perfectly true praise is God's praise of Himself.
20:130/q07 Which worship is indicated before sunrise? [قَبْلَ طُلُوعِ ٱلشَّمْسِ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the temporal instruction specifies obligatory Fajr or includes direct and optional morning remembrance.)
  20:130/q07/p1 The dawn or Fajr prayer. — reasons: This is the repeated mapping from Muqatil onward. Ibn Ashur says the prayer times are clarified by mass-transmitted Sunna; the Jarir and two-asr reports identify the morning prayer; Biqai includes it at the first endpoint.
  20:130/q07/p2 The time also accommodates direct glorification and remembrance after dawn, extending toward the forenoon prayer. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya cites the formula through the two Duha rakas; Abu Hayyan reports it. Bursevi transmits a report valuing remembrance until sunrise above freeing eighty descendants of Ishmael. Islahi includes ishraq.
20:130/q08 Which worship is indicated before sunset? [وَقَبْلَ غُرُوبِهَا · interpretation] (turns on: How broadly before sunset is taken, and whether tasbih is prayer or direct remembrance.)
  20:130/q08/p1 The afternoon or Asr prayer. — reasons: A widespread mapping supported by the Jarir report, the two-asr report transmitted by Durr and Alusi, and ordinary pre-sunset usage as Alusi observes. Alusi's note calling the sunset prayer here differs from his later alternative.
  20:130/q08/p2 Both noon or Dhuhr and afternoon or Asr prayers. — reasons: They both fall after solar noon and before sunset, according to Bursevi and Alusi; Nasafi calls both part of the latter half of day. Razi reports Ibn Abbas's complete five-prayer mapping; Ibn Ashur allows this alongside Asr alone.
  20:130/q08/p3 The sunset prayer. — reasons: Alusi calls the pre-sunset phrase the sunset prayer in one note, while elsewhere he reports the Asr and Dhuhr-Asr readings.
  20:130/q08/p4 Direct glorification before sunset, including a repeated formula. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya reports seventy glorifications before sunset bring forgiveness; Abu Hayyan and Alusi transmit the sunset report.
20:130/q09 What do ānāʾ al-layl mean lexically? [ءَانَآئِ ٱلَّيْلِ · meaning]
  20:130/q09/p1 The hours, periods or watches of night; several singular forms are transmitted. — reasons: Majaz cites the Hudhali poet al-Mutanakhkhal or Abu Athila; Tabari cites a line attributed to al-Munkhil al-Sadi; Wahidi cites al-Asha; Jishumi and Ibn Atiyya cite a Hudhali line. Qurtubi, Alusi and Ibn Ashur list variant singulars including ini, inu, ina and ana; Tabatabai derives the plural from ina or inw; Alusi cites Raghib's parallel at 33:53 and al-Hutay'a on delaying supper.
20:130/q10 Which night worship do the night hours designate? [وَمِنْ ءَانَآئِ ٱلَّيْلِ فَسَبِّحْ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the phrase specifies one or two fixed evening prayers, spans all or part of night, or adds voluntary worship.)
  20:130/q10/p1 The sunset and nightfall prayers, Maghrib and Isha. — reasons: Qatada and Ibn Abbas transmissions place both in early night; Baydawi and Bursevi add that quiet and inclination to rest make this worship demanding. Alusi sees the renewed command and fronted hours as emphasis.
  20:130/q10/p2 The Isha prayer specifically. — reasons: Tabari first glosses the evening prayer after a portion of night and reports Ibn Zayd; Baghawi, Ibn Jawzi, Qurtubi, Nasafi and Study Quran give Isha alone; Kashshaf and Abu Hayyan use nightfall prayer.
  20:130/q10/p3 Prayer or remembrance throughout the whole night. — reasons: Ibn Abbas is reported by Tabari, Mawardi and Tabrisi; al-Hasan spells out beginning, middle and end in Tabari and Ibn Jawzi.
  20:130/q10/p4 The middle of the night. — reasons: Ibn Abbas is reported with this more restricted time by Tabari and Ibn Jawzi.
