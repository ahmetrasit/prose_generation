[IBNASHUR:1:7/r2] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The prayer first asks for a clear, easy way to salvation; identifying it as the way of the favored adds a further honor.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r20] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Whether the favored means prophets, their best followers, or earlier Muslims, the request is for a complete path suited to guidance available in each age.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r21] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Under each account of the favored, the request for guidance has the same possible force explained for the preceding verse: attainment, increase, or continuance. | mentions 1:6
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r22] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The prayer discounts benefits that are not ultimately pure: unbelievers enjoy real worldly gifts, but these bring anxiety about their end and punishment in the afterlife; the scholarly dispute over calling them favored is fruitless.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r23] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · القراء العشرة | reports | All ten readers pronounce ghayr in the genitive; syntactically it may modify the favored or stand in apposition, with the author preferring the adjectival reading.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r24] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | prefers | He prefers treating ghayr as an adjective: its descriptive sense outweighs apposition, which conventionally names the same entity as its antecedent.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r25] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Although ghayr is usually indefinite, it can describe this relative phrase because “those You favored” denotes no one specified group and is generic in sense.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r26] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · ابن هشام عن ابن السراج والسيرافي | reports | Ibn Hishām attributes to Ibn al-Sarrāj and al-Sīrāfī the account that ghayr becomes definite by negating a unique opposite; Ibn al-Ḥājib selects it for 4:95, and the author sees its result as close to the other account. | mentions 4:95
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r27] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · سيبويه | reports | Sībawayh treats ghayr like an active participle meaning “different,” and similarly classifies mithl, siwā, and ḥasb; it becomes definite when permanence is intended.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r28] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The qualification seeks refuge from communities once guided who later corrupted understanding, disobeyed, privileged worldly desires, or failed to preserve their guidance.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r29] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The groups are broader than Jews and Christians: the first exemplifies deliberate religious rebellion, the second losing the way through misunderstanding; this reading makes the prayer meaningful after Islam superseded their religions.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r3] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The appositive restates the path generally and then specifies it, strengthening its meaning through a repetition comparable to emphasis.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r30] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | He says their earlier error was not yet deliberate and had not incurred divine anger; Jews exemplify the first group and Christians the second, but neither label is exclusive.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r31] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Adding the second negation advances from refuge against the graver fate to the lesser: escaping anger-inducing error does not cover lesser misguidance; the order also suits the verse endings.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r32] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Human anger is a psychic response to what displeases, producing aversion and harsh treatment; revenge may follow but is not essential, contrary to those who make it definitional.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r33] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Because literal anger entails change, which cannot apply to God, the attribution means its consequence—punishment, humiliation, or worldly exclusion—or a representational metaphor.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r34] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Early generations often left ambiguous attributes uninterpreted to focus on practice; later scholars used sound Arabic-based interpretation to answer false claims and clarify doctrine.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r35] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Divine anger is God’s treatment of those who oppose guidance, ranging up to the lasting punishment of polytheists and hypocrites; dislike is lesser, and both differ from creative will. | mentions 39:7, 6:112, 10:99
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r36] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · النبي | reports | A hadith says God dislikes gossip, excessive questioning, and idle talk; the author uses it to distinguish dislike from anger.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r37] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Ethicists treat anger as the root of courage within the primary virtues; balanced force yields courage, while excess produces rashness and rancor and deficiency produces cowardice.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r38] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · النبي | reports | When a man asked for advice, the Prophet repeatedly answered, “Do not become angry”; the author applies this to anger for oneself, which leads to injustice.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r39] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · بعض ملوك الفرس | reports | An unnamed Persian king said his reign endured because he punished according to the offense rather than according to anger.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r4] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | He finds no meaningful superiority between parsing the phrase as a matching appositive and as explanatory apposition; grammarians offer no decisive semantic distinction.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r40] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · النبي | reports | The Prophet did not become angry for himself, but became angry for God when a divine sanctity was violated; the author approves anger used to protect public and religious interests.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r41] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | prefers | Lā coordinates the straying with the objects of ghayr; contrary to the reported view of Makki ibn Abī Ṭālib, it is not needed to prevent attachment to the favored.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r42] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | He takes the extra lā as verbal reinforcement of negation after ghayr, following Arabic coordination under negation, as in 5:19. | mentions 5:19
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r43] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · السيد | reports | Al-Sayyid says lā prevents the mistaken inference that only the combined group is excluded, allowing one group to remain affirmed; the author distinguishes this from the semantic extra lā in 7:12. | mentions 7:12
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r44] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Ḍalāl is missing the intended road through error, knowingly or unknowingly, like a lost camel; its use for religious or intellectual error is metaphorical.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r45] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · قالوا | reports | In religious usage, misguidance opposes full faith and ranges from neglecting recommended practice to unbelief; the author defines it as absence of guidance and notes sealing as its extreme form.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r46] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The angry group knowingly rejects religion or relies on wildly strained interpretations driven by desire; the straying group includes those who distort true religions through misunderstanding. Both are accountable for failing to seek truth.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r47] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | An ellipsis-like matching (iḥtibāk) implies both groups share both qualities, while assigning each its leading description shows that the strayers’ error is less severe.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r48] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · النبي | reports | A report in al-Tirmidhī, which the author grades ḥasan, names Jews as an example of the angered and Christians as an example of the straying, not as exclusive referents.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r49] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Arab listeners knew Jews in Khaybar, al-Naḍīr, Medina, and Yemen, and Arab Christians such as Taghlib, Kalb, and parts of Quḍāʿa; both had altered their faith. | mentions 5:77
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r5] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · الزمخشري | reports | The author reads al-Zamakhsharī’s account as finding emphasis in the apposition’s doubling and repetition of wording and syntactic government.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r50] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Islam’s straight path is clear in proof and sound in method, as the Qurʾān says; the law also distinguishes valid disagreement within Islam from disagreement that leaves it. | mentions 6:161, 6:153, 27:79
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r51] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · النبي | reports | The Prophet says a qualified jurist who reaches the right answer earns two rewards, and one who errs earns one, acknowledging differing degrees of sound judgment.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r52] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · الجمهور | reports | Most readers kasrate the hāʾ in plural and dual pronouns after a silent yāʾ to avoid heaviness; this matches the dialects of Qays, Tamīm, and Saʿd ibn Bakr.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r53] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · حمزة ويعقوب | reports | Ḥamza dammas the hāʾ in ʿalayhim, ilayhim, and ladayhim only, while Yaʿqūb dammas it in every pronoun of this class; the former follows a Quraysh and Ḥijāzī dialect.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r54] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · يعقوب | reports | Yaʿqūb reads every pronoun of this type with a preceding silent yāʾ and a dammated hāʾ.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r55] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · الجمهور وابن كثير وأبو جعفر وقالون | reports | At pause-free connection before a vowel, most readers leave the plural mīm silent; Ibn Kathīr, Abū Jaʿfar, and Qālūn in one transmission lengthen its damma, a dialect also illustrated by Labīd.
[IBNASHUR:1:7/r56] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · ورش | reports | Warsh lengthens a damma on the plural mīm when a hamza follows, but not before ghayr here; all readers leave the mīm silent at pause.
