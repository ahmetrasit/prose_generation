  39:23/q13/p3 The Quran is recited repeatedly without becoming wearisome. — reasons: Māwardī, Zamakhsharī, Ṭabrisī, Qurṭubī, Nasafī, Biqāʿī and Bursevī cite enduring pleasure; Bursevī adduces the Burda and a report about ʿUthmān's worn codices. Al-Khujandī contrasts receptive hearts hearing the Quran continually with false people who tire of it.
  39:23/q13/p4 Its parts turn back to and interpret one another. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr speak of reciprocal explanation; Ṭabāṭabāʾī derives the plural from a thing turned back, citing 4:82.
  39:23/q13/p5 Its suras occur in complementary pairs. — reasons: Islahi values this reading for teaching, pointing to 15:87 and the differing but connected themes of suras; he rejects mere repetition.
  39:23/q13/p6 It derives from praise, since the Quran praises God or is itself praised. — reasons: Bursevī appeals to 56:77 and 85:21 and the admired eloquence; Ālūsī reports the derivation but finds it less fitting beside mutashābih unless it praises its Speaker. Al-Kashf permits a verbal noun functioning adjectivally.
  39:23/q13/p7 It denotes Quranic verse endings or subdivisions. — reasons: Māwardī reports Ibn Baḥr: the Quran is whole, sura section, verse division, and mathānī verse endings.
  39:23/q13/p8 It means the whole Quran as a category. — reasons: Ṭabarī and al-Durr report this designation, as does Ibn Kathīr of Mujāhid; Ibn Qutayba relates it to recurring accounts.
  39:23/q13/p9 It denotes al-Fātiḥa, the seven oft-repeated verses. — reasons: Ṭabarī cites repetition in prayer and rajaz poetry; Māwardī cites al-Ḥasan and a poem; Burhān cites 15:87 and Fātiḥa's many names.
  39:23/q13/p10 It is a class of suras following the miʾūn. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba and Ṭabarī explain that the hundred-verse group comes first and mathānī second; poetry lists the categories. Māwardī's report gives suras of roughly two hundred verses as another delimitation.
  39:23/q13/p11 It means the succession of verses and suras. — reasons: Māwardī reports al-Kalbī's sequential account of mathānī.
  39:23/q13/p12 Its repeated descent marks stages before and after inward annihilation. — reasons: Qāshānī reads the true Book as descending to the heart while it alternates between the Real and creation.
39:23/q14 Why does the Quran repeat material? [مَّثَانِىَ · interpretation]
  39:23/q14/p1 Repetition makes admonition take root and reaches audiences who receive different passages. — reasons: Zamakhsharī and Biqāʿī say resistant souls need repeated counsel; the Prophet repeated admonition three or seven times. Ibn al-Jawzī says scattered Arab delegations heard the stories in different suras; KURANYOLU says repeated forms enlighten readers.
  39:23/q14/p2 Repetition renews delight and benefit without exhausting the Quran. — reasons: Māwardī and Bursevī say recurrent recitation stays pleasing; Bursevī quotes Burda, a report of ʿUthmān's worn codices, and inexhaustible wonders.
39:23/q15 What is the grammatical role of mathānī? [مَّثَانِىَ · grammar] (turns on: How a plural form qualifies a singular Book and whether it is adjective, specification, or circumstantial.)
  39:23/q15/p1 It qualifies kitāban despite its plural form, because the Book contains many parts. — reasons: Zajjāj and Naḥḥās call it an adjective and a diptote irregular plural; Ibn ʿAṭiyya notes lack of tanwīn. Zamakhsharī, Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn, Bursevī and Ālūsī compare a human made of bones and veins, or supply 'sections'.
  39:23/q15/p2 It is an accusative specification of mutashābihan: its parts or qualities resemble one another. — reasons: Zamakhsharī compares 'a man beautiful in qualities'; Bayḍāwī, Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, Biqāʿī and Ālūsī report the transformed underlying clause.
  39:23/q15/p3 It is a descriptive plural like collective expressions for a pot or cloth. — reasons: Zamakhsharī compares برمة أعشار and ثوب أخلاق; Abū Ḥayyān reports this.
  39:23/q15/p4 It can be a circumstantial qualifier of the Book. — reasons: Ālūsī and al-Samīn permit the analysis.
39:23/q16 How is mathānī read? [مَّثَانِىَ · readings] (turns on: Whether the yāʾ bears fatḥa or sukūn, and how the silent-yāʾ form is parsed.)
  39:23/q16/p1 The majority pronounce the final yāʾ with fatḥa. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn report the common reading; al-Samīn allows adjectival, circumstantial, or specification syntax.
  39:23/q16/p2 Hishām from Ibn ʿĀmir and Abū Bishr pronounce a still yāʾ. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn and Ālūsī attribute the reading; heaviness may prompt sukūn, with either an accusative or omitted-subject predicate analysis. Al-Samīn adduces a comparable reading and poem, but objects that a nominative predicate should lose yāʾ and take tanwīn.
39:23/q17 What is the form and root of mathānī? [مَّثَانِىَ · grammar] (turns on: The proposed derivation from doubling or return versus praise.)
  39:23/q17/p1 It is a plural connected with mathnā or muthannā, from doubling or turning back. — reasons: Jishumī derives repetition by adding a like; Zamakhsharī compares karratayn at 67:4 and paired forms; Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān, Suyūṭī, Bursevī and KURANYOLU give related forms; Ṭabāṭabāʾī gives mathniya, a turned-back thing.
  39:23/q17/p2 It may derive from praise. — reasons: Bursevī relates it to muthnā ʿalayh; Ālūsī and al-Kashf examine the adjectival form.
39:23/q18 What are the linked traditional Quranic divisions called mathānī? [مَّثَانِىَ · sciences] (turns on: Different units and starting points are reported for the traditional sura divisions.)
  39:23/q18/p1 They are a sura group after the seven long suras and miʾūn, before the mufassal. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, Ṭabarī and Burhān define miʾūn as near one hundred verses and mathānī as succeeding them. A poem lists ṭawāsīn, ḥawāmīm and mufassal; basmala divisions explain mufassal. Wāthila's report compares the four groups with Torah, Gospel, Psalms and a special gift; Burhān calls it gharīb with weak Saʿīd b. Bashīr.
  39:23/q18/p2 The seven long suras end at Yūnus. — reasons: Saʿīd b. Jubayr and Ibn ʿAbbās list Baqara through Aʿrāf then Yūnus; Māwardī calls it correct; Ṭabarī says ʿUthmān's Anfāl/Barāʾa report does not establish those two in the seven.
  39:23/q18/p3 The seven long suras treat al-Anfāl and Barāʾa as one ending unit. — reasons: A poem and Burhān give this count; ʿUthmān said similar stories and absence of clarification led him to pair them without basmala. Al-Ālūsī discusses whether this reflects independent judgment or a prescribed order.
  39:23/q18/p4 The mufassal begins at Sūrat Muḥammad. — reasons: Māwardī attributes it to most scholars; Burhān lists it among twelve reported starting points.
  39:23/q18/p5 The mufassal begins at Qāf. — reasons: ʿĪsā b. ʿUmar reports this from many Companions; a report from the Thaqīf delegation says a Companion hizb began there; Burhān also lists it.
  39:23/q18/p6 The mufassal begins at al-Ḍuḥā. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās connects this beginning with Meccan takbīr between suras.
  39:23/q18/p7 Other reported mufassal starts include al-Jāthiya and al-Ḥujurāt. — reasons: Burhān counts twelve views and lists these alongside Muḥammad and Qāf.
  39:23/q18/p8 Al-Fātiḥa bears the name seven mathānī. — reasons: Poetic evidence and its repeated prayer recitation support this title; Burhān lists it among more than twenty names.
39:23/q19 How does taqshaʿir describe the skin? [تَقْشَعِرُّ جُلُودُ · meaning]
  39:23/q19/p1 It means intense contraction, trembling, and raised hair from fear. — reasons: Baghawī, Rāzī, Abū Ḥayyān, Qurṭubī, Bursevī and others give bodily definitions; Zamakhsharī derives qashʿ from dry hide with extra rāʾ intensifying the form, followed by Bayḍāwī and Ālūsī. Biqāʿī instead mentions dry, aged fat; Imruʾ al-Qays's poem uses the form for a fearful heart. Qurṭubī lists verbal noun and participial forms.
39:23/q20 What do the 'skins' denote? [جُلُودُ · referent] (turns on: Whether the noun is literal bodily skin or a transferred name for the inner organ.)
  39:23/q20/p1 The body's actual outer covering and sometimes its hair. — reasons: Biqāʿī names the body's outer surfaces; Islahi notes Arabic can use part for whole and whole for part, including hair.
  39:23/q20/p2 The hearts of the God-fearing. — reasons: Baghawī reports this alternative transferred sense.
39:23/q21 How is the clause 'their skins shiver' attached syntactically? [تَقْشَعِرُّ مِنْهُ جُلُودُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the listeners' response remains a modifier of the Book or begins a new statement.)
  39:23/q21/p1 It qualifies the Book as an adjectival clause. — reasons: Naḥḥās, Ṭabāṭabāʾī and al-Samīn permit this; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says no pause at mathānī on this parse.
  39:23/q21/p2 It is a circumstantial clause for the Book. — reasons: Al-Samīn and Ālūsī report this analysis.
  39:23/q21/p3 It begins an independent sentence about the Book's effects. — reasons: Ālūsī prefers the transition after the Book's attributes; Ibn ʿAṭiyya says one may pause at mathānī on this parse.
39:23/q22 Is skin shivering literal or a figure for fear? [تَقْشَعِرُّ مِنْهُ جُلُودُ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the bodily sign is an observed reaction or figurative depiction; several sources allow both.)
  39:23/q22/p1 It is an actual bodily response to revelation and warning. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān, Bursevī and Ālūsī prefer a palpable effect; Ṭabarī and others describe skin reacting to heard recitation. The Companions' weeping and shivering corroborate its actuality.
  39:23/q22/p2 It is a vivid portrayal of overwhelming fear. — reasons: Zamakhsharī, Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān and Ālūsī describe fear through its visible sign; Ālūsī prefers figurative exemplification to a state-to-state parable but still allows actual bodily change.
