[QURTUBI:1:2/r12] QURTUBI, d. 671 · طائفة | reports | One group considers praise of God better than the declaration of unity because praise includes both tawhid and praise, while the declaration includes tawhid alone.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r13] QURTUBI, d. 671 · طائفة | reports | Another group considers la ilaha illa Allah better because it repels unbelief and associating partners, and people fight on its basis; they cite the Prophet’s command to fight until people say it.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r14] QURTUBI, d. 671 · ابن عطية | reports | Ibn ʿAtiyya chooses la ilaha illa Allah as superior, citing the report that it is the best thing the Prophet and earlier prophets said.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r15] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | holds | Muslims agree that God is praised for all blessings, including faith; since faith belongs to the created worlds, Qurtubi infers that faith is God’s act and creation, contrary to the Qadari claim that humans create it.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r16] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | holds | In Arabic, al-ḥamd is complete praise; the definite article encompasses every kind of praise, all of which God deserves because He has the most beautiful names and highest attributes.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r17] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | holds | The cited verse uses the word aḥmadī as a plural of ḥamd, supporting the linguistic discussion of the word’s forms.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r18] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | holds | Ḥamd is the opposite of blame; ḥamīd and maḥmūd describe one praised, and taḥmīd is more emphatic than ḥamd.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r19] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | holds | The poet’s use of muḥammad for a person rich in praiseworthy qualities explains the name Muhammad; another verse says the Throne’s Lord is Maḥmūd and the Prophet is Muhammad.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r2] QURTUBI, d. 671 · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Anas reports that God is pleased when a servant eats or drinks and praises Him for it; Qurtubi cites this from Muslim.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r20] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | holds | Maḥmada means the opposite of blame; aḥmada can mean one’s affairs turned praiseworthy or that one found a place pleasing, while a ḥamada is someone who overpraises things and ḥamadat al-nār is the sound of its flames.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r21] QURTUBI, d. 671 · الطبري والمبرد | reports | Al-Ṭabarī and al-Mubarrad hold that ḥamd and shukr have the same meaning; Qurtubi says this view is not sound.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r22] QURTUBI, d. 671 · ابن عطاء | reports | Al-Sulami transmits from Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq and Ibn ʿAṭāʾ the synonymy view; Ibn ʿAṭāʾ explains praise as gratitude for God enabling people to learn and praise Him.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r23] QURTUBI, d. 671 · الطبري | reports | Al-Ṭabarī argues the terms are synonymous because Arabic permits the phrase al-ḥamdu lillāhi shukran, praise to God in gratitude.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r24] QURTUBI, d. 671 · ابن عطية | reports | Ibn ʿAṭiyya counters that adding shukran narrows the praise to gratitude for a particular blessing, which shows the words are not identical.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r25] QURTUBI, d. 671 · بعض العلماء | reports | Some scholars say gratitude is broader because it can be expressed by tongue, limbs, and heart, while praise is verbal alone.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r26] QURTUBI, d. 671 · بعض العلماء | reports | Another view makes praise broader because it includes both gratitude and commendation, can stand in place of gratitude, and is not itself replaced by gratitude.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r27] QURTUBI, d. 671 · ابن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbās calls praise the word of every grateful person and illustrates its use by Adam, Noah, Abraham, David and Solomon, Muhammad, and the people of Paradise. | mentions 23:28, 14:39, 27:15, 17:111, 35:34, 10:10
[QURTUBI:1:2/r28] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | prefers | Qurtubi’s preferred distinction is that praise commends attributes without requiring prior kindness, whereas gratitude praises a benefactor for a favor; praise is broader because it includes commendation as well as thanks.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r29] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | holds | Ḥamd can also mean satisfaction: one says I tested him and praised him to mean I was pleased with him; Qurtubi links this usage to maqāman maḥmūdan and a prophetic phrase about approving washing the urethra. | mentions 17:79
[QURTUBI:1:2/r3] QURTUBI, d. 671 · الحسن | reports | Al-Hasan says that no blessing exists for which praise of God is not better than the blessing itself.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r30] QURTUBI, d. 671 · جعفر الصادق | reports | Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq gives a symbolic reading of the letters: ḥāʾ signifies unity, mīm dominion, and dāl permanence; knowing these is knowing God.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r31] QURTUBI, d. 671 · شقيق بن إبراهيم | reports | Shafīq ibn Ibrāhīm says gratitude has three conditions: recognizing the giver, being content with what was given, and not disobeying God while one retains strength.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r32] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | holds | God praises Himself and opens His Book with praise, while people are forbidden to claim purity for themselves; Qurtubi cites the command not to praise yourselves and the report to throw dust in flatterers’ faces. | mentions 53:32
[QURTUBI:1:2/r33] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | holds | God’s self-praise precedes any creature’s praise and is without cause, while created praise is mixed with ulterior causes.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r34] QURTUBI, d. 671 · علماؤنا | reports | Qurtubi’s scholars say an imperfect creature should not praise himself to attract benefits or repel harm.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r35] QURTUBI, d. 671 · قيل | reports | One explanation says God praised Himself eternally because His servants cannot fulfill the duty of praising Him; even the Messenger declared that he could not enumerate praise to God.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r36] QURTUBI, d. 671 · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | The Prophet’s confession that he cannot enumerate praise to God illustrates the limits of human praise.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r37] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | reports | A poet says that when people praise God rightly, He is as they praise Him and greater than their praise can express.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r38] QURTUBI, d. 671 · قيل | reports | Another explanation says God praised Himself eternally because He knew His blessings would overwhelm servants’ ability to repay them, thereby easing the burden of indebtedness and making the blessing more enjoyable.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r39] QURTUBI, d. 671 · سفيان بن عيينة ورؤبة بن العجاج | reports | The seven readers and most people read the dāl in al-ḥamdu with nominative ḍamma; Sufyān ibn ʿUyayna and Ruʾba ibn al-ʿAjjāj are reported to read it accusative, with an implied verb.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r4] QURTUBI, d. 671 · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | Anas reports from the Prophet that when God gives a servant a blessing and the servant praises Him, the praise is better than what was received.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r40] QURTUBI, d. 671 · سيبويه | reports | Sībawayh says nominative al-ḥamdu lillāh conveys that praise from the speaker and all creatures belongs to God, while the accusative reports only the speaker’s own praise.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r41] QURTUBI, d. 671 · غير سيبويه | reports | Another explanation takes the nominative as an appeal for pardon and as exaltation and glorification, so it functions as supplication rather than a plain report.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r42] QURTUBI, d. 671 · الطبري | reports | Al-Ṭabarī says God’s praise of Himself implicitly commands His servants to praise Him, as though the wording said, Say praise belongs to God.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r43] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | holds | Qurtubi treats the implied command to say as a common Arabic ellipsis whose context supplies the omitted words, and says the same construction applies to the later address, as though it said, Say, You alone we worship. | mentions 1:5
[QURTUBI:1:2/r44] QURTUBI, d. 671 · ابن أبي عبلة | reports | Ibn Abī ʿAbla reads the dāl and lām with ḍamma by vowel assimilation for phonetic harmony, a common pattern illustrated by other words and a poetic line.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r45] QURTUBI, d. 671 · الحسن بن أبي الحسن وزيد بن علي | reports | Al-Ḥasan ibn Abī al-Ḥasan and Zayd ibn ʿAlī read the dāl of al-ḥamd with kasra by assimilation of the first vowel to the second.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r46] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | holds | Rabb means owner; al-Ṣiḥāḥ says it is a divine name used of others only in an annexed construction, though pre-Islamic Arabs also used it for a king.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r47] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | holds | Rabb also means master: Qurtubi cites the command to remember Joseph with his master and the report that a slave woman gives birth to her mistress. | mentions 12:42
[QURTUBI:1:2/r48] QURTUBI, d. 671 · الهروي وغيره | reports | Al-Harawī and others say rabb can mean one who rectifies, manages, mends, or tends something to completion; they connect rabbāniyyūn with tending the books.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r49] QURTUBI, d. 671 · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | A prophetic question about a blessing one tends means taking responsibility for it and putting it right.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r5] QURTUBI, d. 671 · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | A report in Nawādir al-uṣūl from Anas says that if someone possessed the whole world and then praised God, the praise would be better than it.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r50] QURTUBI, d. 671 · author | holds | Rabb can also mean the deity, as shown by a poet’s line about a lord upon whose head a fox urinates.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r51] QURTUBI, d. 671 · النحاس | reports | Al-Naḥḥās records the intensive forms rabbāhu, rabbabahu, and rabbatahu; al-Ṣiḥāḥ says rabbahu, rabbabahu, and tarabbabahu all mean he raised him, and marbūb means the one raised.
[QURTUBI:1:2/r52] QURTUBI, d. 671 · بعض العلماء | reports | Some scholars call Rabb the greatest name of God because callers frequently invoke Him by it and it conveys the bond of care, mercy, and need between Lord and servant.
