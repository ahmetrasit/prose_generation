## THE CURRENT MAP
15:73/q01 What does it mean that the cry seized them? [فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ · meaning]
  15:73/q01/p1 It overpowered them and took control of them. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī explains seizure as overpowering and control, and notes that akhīdh can mean a captive.
15:73/q02 Who were seized? [فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ · referent]
  15:73/q02/p1 The people of Lot. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj, al-Wāḥidī's commentators, al-Baghawī, al-Biqāʿī, al-Bursawī, Iṣlāḥī and Kur'an Yolu identify the afflicted people as Lot's people.
15:73/q03 What was the ṣayḥa that struck them? [ٱلصَّيْحَةُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the sound is identified with Gabriel, divine production, a thunderbolt, or a more general destructive blast; al-Rāzī and the Imam demand strong evidence for Gabriel's identification.)
  15:73/q03/p1 A tremendous, deadly cry or blast of punishment. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj and al-Qurṭubī call it punishment; al-Jishumī defines ṣayḥa as a loud forceful sound. Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it a sudden blast of impact unlike Thamud's, while Abū Ḥayyān reports his description as a cry of terror. Al-Bayḍāwī describes a terrifying deadly blast. Al-Biqāʿī says its force made other sounds negligible. Al-Ālūsī takes the definite article generically. Asad calls it a blast of God's punishment. Iṣlāḥī says it is a shout denoting punishment, aptly named for the storm wind's thunder and lightning. Kur'an Yolu calls it a terrible cry; al-Rāzī says the verse itself establishes only a tremendous deadly cry, absent strong external proof of Gabriel.
  15:73/q03/p2 It was Gabriel's cry or shout. — reasons: Muqātil, al-Wāḥidī's commentators, al-Wāḥidī's Wajīz, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Nasafī and al-Bursawī identify Gabriel; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Bayḍāwī report that identification. Al-Ālūsī reports that on this view the definite article points to a known event. Al-Rāzī's semanticists and al-Ālūsī's Imam object that the verse alone does not identify Gabriel and strong external evidence would be needed.
  15:73/q03/p3 It resembled a thunderbolt in its destructive force. — reasons: Ibn Jurayj says it was like a thunderbolt and that anything destroying a people can be called either ṣāʿiqa or ṣayḥa; al-Ālūsī transmits this explanation.
  15:73/q03/p4 It was a punitive thunderbolt. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī describes the cry as a thunderbolt of punishment and glosses their being cried against as their destruction.
  15:73/q03/p5 It may have been a great sound produced by God. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī reports this alternative from the semanticists.
  15:73/q03/p6 It was a blast in the air involving thunderbolts, earthquakes and baked-clay stones. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr describes those components and points to his discussion of the event in Hūd.
15:73/q04 How is mushriqīn construed grammatically? [مُشْرِقِينَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the circumstantial participle describes sunrise already being entered or sunrise still impending.)
  15:73/q04/p1 It is an accusative circumstantial participle describing them as they entered sunrise. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī pairs it with muṣbiḥīn as a circumstantial accusative; al-Thaʿlabī, al-Jishumī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Samīn and Ibn ʿĀshūr also parse it as circumstantial, with Ibn ʿĀshūr deriving it from the plural verb. Al-Bursawī says active and passive participles describe an event in progress; calling someone maqṭūʿ after completion would figuratively mean about to be cut off.
  15:73/q04/p2 It can be a prospective circumstantial state. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports this grammatical gloss, which takes the state as impending.
15:73/q05 When did the punishment strike, and how does that relate to daybreak? [مُشْرِقِينَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether mushriqīn fixes actual sunrise, can denote daybreak, or marks the completion of a punishment that began at daybreak; the promise of morning destruction in 15:66 prompts the reconciliation.)
  15:73/q05/p1 They were struck at sunrise, as the sun rose or its light brightened. — reasons: Ibn Jurayj, al-Ṭabarī and most cited commentators place it when the sun had risen. Al-Zajjāj explains ashraqnā as encountering sunrise like aṣbaḥnā for morning; he allows sharaqat and ashraqat for the sun's rising. Al-Wāḥidī's linguists say the root can describe anything rising in the east, including dawn, stars, sun and moon, and that the people entered sunrise as one enters morning or evening. Al-Jishumī distinguishes sharaqat, rising, from ashraqat, shining and clearing; al-Qurṭubī and Ibn al-Jawzī report both that distinction and the view that the verbs are synonymous. Al-Thaʿlabī says the risen sun brightened. Al-Biqāʿī calls this the brightness of sunrise and says ṣubḥ and ṣabāḥ name the day's beginning, perhaps extending toward midday. Al-Rāzī notes the root applies to anything appearing from the east.
  15:73/q05/p2 Punishment began at dawn and reached completion at sunrise. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī reports this as one reconciliation with the promised daybreak. Al-Baghawī, al-Wāḥidī's Wajīz, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Biqāʿī and al-Bursawī adopt the sequence; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Qurṭubī report it, and al-Ālūsī attributes it to al-Mudaqqiq. Al-Biqāʿī says uprooting began at dawn and the cry may have marked the end at sunrise. Al-Bursawī relates the morning cutting off in 15:66 to the beginning, and the lifting, overturning and Gabriel's cry to completion at sunrise. Al-Ālūsī also reports a gloss of maqṭūʿ as soon to be cut off.
  15:73/q05/p3 Sunrise here may mean daybreak itself, so the punishment struck at dawn. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī reports semanticists who equate sunrise with dawn to reconcile the promised daybreak. Al-Qurṭubī reports a daybreak identification. Al-Biqāʿī and Iṣlāḥī describe the destructive cry as arriving at daybreak; Kur'an Yolu also gives dawn as a time.
  15:73/q05/p4 The cry may have struck at an unexpected hour. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu gives this alternative alongside dawn.
15:73/q06 What had Lot done before this punishment? [* · reports]
  15:73/q06/p1 He offered his daughters to divert the people from their vile intention, but they ignored him. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī reports this from an unnamed group as Lot's unsuccessful counsel.
15:73/q07 What warning does their fate convey? [* · interpretation]
  15:73/q07/p1 People who defy God, ignore a prophet's warnings and moral values, and persist openly in immorality face ruin. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu draws this lesson and cites parallels at 7:80, 7:84, 11:69, 11:83, 26:160, 26:173, 27:54 and 27:58.
15:73/q08 How does this account connect with the account in Hūd? [* · links]
  15:73/q08/p1 The account in Hūd supplies the related decree and details of the towns' overthrow and the raining of stones. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the seizure follows the prior decree and points to 11:82 for thunderbolts, earthquakes and baked-clay stones. Al-Biqāʿī describes turning the towns upside down and raining stones, and notes a difference in pronoun reference from Hūd. Asad refers readers to his note 98 on sūra 11; The Study Quran directs them to its commentary on 11:82–83.
15:73/q09 How is this account compared with the account in al-Naml? [* · links]
  15:73/q09/p1 Al-Ḥijr describes a harsher punishment than al-Naml. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī points to the sunrise cry, overturning of the city and baked-clay stones here, compared with the rain punishment mentioned in al-Naml.
