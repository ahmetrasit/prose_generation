[TABATABAI:2:228-242#20/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | In pre-Islamic societies governed mainly by inherited custom, including China, India, ancient Egypt, and Iran, women's status followed local practice rather than scripture or codified law.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#20/r2] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Women lacked independent will and action, had no public role in government or adjudication, and were expected both to work alongside men and to manage the home and obey them.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#20/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Compared with women in less settled societies, they were generally spared killing and eating, but men could take unlimited wives, divorce them, and remarry after a wife's death.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#20/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | A woman could sometimes acquire property by inheritance, marriage, or other means, yet could not dispose of it independently.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#20/r5] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Customs differed by region and class: some elite Iranian women could participate in property, rule, or sovereignty, and marriages among close relatives occurred.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#20/r6] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | In China, marriage resembled purchasing the woman into subjection; women lacked inheritance and even shared meals with men, while multiple men could share one wife and children were assigned chiefly to the strongest husband.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#20/r7] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | In India, widows could not remarry and were either burned with their husbands or left humiliated; menstruating women and what touched them were treated as impure.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#20/r8] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Al-Tabataba'i summarizes these societies as treating women like an inferior being between animal and human, useful as a weak dependent who must remain under guardianship.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#21/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Some societies, including the Chaldeans, Romans, and Greeks, lived under written law or scripture, unlike the customs-based societies discussed earlier.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#21/r2] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Under Hammurabi's law, a wife was subordinate; disobedience could lead to expulsion or treatment as a concubine, and proven wasteful household management could lead to drowning.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#21/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Rome began codifying civil law around 400 BCE and gradually expanded it; household heads exercised authority over domestic affairs.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#21/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The Roman paterfamilias was treated as a household deity and had unrestricted command over wife and children, even authority to kill them without challenge.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#21/r5] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Roman women had less standing than dependent male children: they could not complain, transact, or participate in public life, while men, including adopted sons, could be authorized to act independently.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#21/r6] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | In Greek and Roman-style households, formal kinship and inheritance linked men; women lacked official kinship even with mothers, daughters, sisters, husbands, and sons, although natural kinship still existed and could shape marriage and household authority.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#22/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | A woman's life was controlled by her father or husband: he could sell, give, lend, punish, control her property, arrange her marriage, and divorce her.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#22/r2] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Greek household organization and the subordination of women were broadly similar to the Roman pattern.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#22/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | These legal systems contradicted themselves: women were punished independently for wrongdoing but recognized or rewarded only through male guardianship, reflecting a view of them as dangerous yet necessary for reproduction and useful to men.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#22/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Because society was understood to depend on men, male descendants alone counted as true continuers of a household; adoption and having a male relative impregnate a barren man's wife were used to prevent extinction.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#22/r5] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Greek men could have multiple wives, but only one was an official wife; the others lacked that formal status.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#23/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | The Arabs lived on a hot, arid peninsula, mostly as nomadic raiding tribes connected to Persia, Rome, Abyssinia, and Sudan; their customs were mainly uncivilized but borrowed regional practices.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#23/r2] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Arab women generally lacked independent standing, inheritance, and social honor beyond family reputation; men could have unlimited wives and divorce freely.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#23/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Female infanticide began among Banū Tamīm after the capture of their daughters in a conflict with al-Nuʿmān ibn al-Mundhir, then spread to other Arabs.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#23/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Arabs regarded a daughter as a disgrace and hid her birth, while celebrating sons even when obtained by adoption or affiliation; rival chiefs sometimes claimed the same child.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#23/r5] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Some elite households allowed women, especially daughters, a say in choosing marriage, resembling Iranian aristocratic custom.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#23/r6] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Arab treatment combined urban restrictions on women's rights and public roles with the coercion of barbarian societies; it arose from power over the weak, not household-head worship.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#23/r7] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Arab men and women worshipped idols tied to tribal preferences, including stars, angels imagined as God's daughters, and objects such as stones and wood.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#24/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | reports | A satirical verse mocked Banū Ḥanīfa for worshipping an idol made from date paste and then eating it during famine.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#24/r2] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Arabs might replace a stone idol with a better stone, or make an idol by pouring milk over a handful of soil and worship it.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#24/r3] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Al-Tabataba'i says deprivation and suffering weakened women's thought and made them susceptible to strange superstitions about events, recorded in historical works.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#24/r4] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | His first conclusion is that societies viewed women as animal-like or as deficient, unsafe human beings if freed from dependence; he associates the harsher view with uncivilized peoples.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#24/r5] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Second, women were excluded from society's structure, viewed instead as a necessary condition of it or as captive dependents exploited by dominant society.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#24/r6] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Third, women were denied nearly all rights except to the extent that men responsible for them could benefit.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#24/r7] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Fourth, relations with women rested on the strong exploiting the weak; settled societies additionally claimed that women's nature made them incapable of independence and dangerous if trusted.
[TABATABAI:2:228-242#25/r1] TABATABAI, d. 1402 · author | holds | Across languages, idioms and figures of speech used womanhood to taunt cowards and condemn the weak or humiliated; al-Tabataba'i treats this vocabulary as evidence of women's social standing.
