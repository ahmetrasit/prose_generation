## THE CURRENT MAP
27:28/q01 To whom does Solomon give these commands? [ٱذْهَب · referent]
  27:28/q01/p1 He addresses the hoopoe, making it his letter bearer and observer of the response. — reasons: Muqātil says it is sent to Sheba; Elmalılı distinguishes its observation duty from simple pigeon delivery; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī names the queen and council as its destination.
27:28/q02 What is the letter called 'my book, this'? [بِّكِتَٰبِى هَٰذَا · referent] (turns on: Whether the letter is identified as the physical document in the story or read symbolically.)
  27:28/q02/p1 It is Solomon's present, prepared letter, written and sealed before being entrusted to the hoopoe. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says hādhā points to the letter just written, and the narrative leaves its preparation implicit; al-Ālūsī likewise takes it as present. Muqātil, al-Wāḥidī, al-Ṭabrisī and Ibn al-Jawzī report writing and sealing; al-Baghawī adds perfume and Solomon's ring; Abū Ṣāliḥ specifically reports the seal. Ibn ʿĀshūr infers it was loaded on the bird.
  27:28/q02/p2 In an inward reading, Solomon's book signifies practical wisdom and divine law. — reasons: Al-Qāshānī gives this symbolic identification.
27:28/q03 What did the letter say and what kind of message was it? [بِّكِتَٰبِى · interpretation] (turns on: The notes emphasize either the stated contents or the force those contents acquire in the continuation.)
  27:28/q03/p1 It was a concise summons to submission, opening with Solomon's name and the basmala and forbidding the recipients to exalt themselves over him. — reasons: Muqātil says these were its only contents; Mujāhid also mentions the basmala and summons to submit, linking the account to 27:30. Al-Biqāʿī stresses its brevity and the urgency of correcting sun worship.
  27:28/q03/p2 It was an ultimatum, as the following verses show. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī cites the later verses for its force.
27:28/q04 Why did Solomon choose the hoopoe to carry the letter? [ٱذْهَب بِّكِتَٰبِى · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the choice is explained by the bird's moral and intellectual fitness or its capacity to carry a message.)
  27:28/q04/p1 Its demonstrated knowledge, wisdom, truthfulness and counsel qualified it despite stronger available servants. — reasons: Bursevī and al-Ālūsī say Solomon could have used powerful jinn but saw signs of intelligence in the bird, leaving the queen without excuse. Al-Taʾwīlāt al-Najmiyya, quoted by Bursevī, calls the commission a reward for its truthful report and sincere counsel despite its weak outward form.
  27:28/q04/p2 The bird was usable as a domesticated letter carrier. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr classifies the hoopoe with pigeons and infers Solomon fastened the prepared letter to it; Elmalılı adds that this messenger also had to watch the response.
27:28/q05 Why did Solomon send this letter to Sheba? [ٱذْهَب بِّكِتَٰبِى · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the immediate purpose was primarily verification of the bird's claim or a contact Solomon had already contemplated.)
  27:28/q05/p1 The queen's answer would test the hoopoe's report; silence would expose it as false. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr connects the letter with the preceding promised verification in 27:27; Study Quran cites the hoopoe's categorical claim in 27:22; Kur'an Yolu says Solomon sent the letter to verify the report and obtain her answer.
  27:28/q05/p2 Solomon already intended contact with Sheba, and the letter sought submission and religious reform. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī finds a mere truth test improbable and suggests the lapwing flew there because Solomon already had Sheba in mind. Ibn ʿĀshūr says divine inspiration directed contact, extending influence over the kingdom and its resources and trade; he adds that nearby kings feared and favored Solomon despite his smaller realm and locates that realm around Jordan, Egypt's frontier and the Mediterranean. Al-Biqāʿī stresses prompt removal of sun worship if the report proved true.
27:28/q06 Who is meant by 'to them'? [إِلَيْهِمْ · referent] (turns on: Whether the plural names the whole people or specifically the queen's council.)
  27:28/q06/p1 Bilqis and her people, the sun-worshipping group mentioned in the hoopoe's report. — reasons: Al-Rāzī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān and Bursevī appeal to 27:24 and the earlier mention of queen and people; al-Biqāʿī says their shared worship makes religion the reason for the plural; al-Irshād, quoted by Bursevī, says the noble letter calls everyone to Islam; al-Ālūsī says it is meant for the whole people. Al-Ṭabrisī and Ibn al-Jawzī explicitly name the people of Sheba.
  27:28/q06/p2 The queen and her ruling council or the country's decision-making notables. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī names the Queen of Sheba and her council; Elmalılı interprets the collective address as the country's notables.
27:28/q07 What does the fā before 'cast it' signal? [فَأَلْقِهْ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the particle chiefly marks haste or an understood narrative transition.)
  27:28/q07/p1 It conveys urgency in sending the bird to correct sun worship. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī imagines the bird flying like lightning with God's added help, in honor of His prophet.
  27:28/q07/p2 It is an explicative fā, making omitted intervening acts understood. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī compares unspoken steps after commands to go and strike the stone, and to the hoopoe's carrying and delivery of the letter.
27:28/q08 Why is the verb 'cast it' used, and how literally may it be taken? [فَأَلْقِهْ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the verb describes the bird's physical dropping of the document or extends to handover from its leg.)
  27:28/q08/p1 It means physically dropping the letter. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says delivery required casting; Ibn ʿĀshūr says ilqāʾ literally means casting to the ground and allows dropping it from the beak, comparing 12:10.
  27:28/q08/p2 It can figuratively describe people taking the letter from the bird's leg. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr compares people casting words to each other in 16:86.
27:28/q09 How did the hoopoe carry the letter? [بِّكِتَٰبِى فَأَلْقِهْ · reports] (turns on: The reports place the document in the beak, at the foot, on the wing, or around the neck.)
  27:28/q09/p1 It carried the letter in its beak. — reasons: Muqātil, Mujāhid and a report in Ibn Kathīr say so; Mujāhid adds that it circled the queen's chamber, and al-Māwardī says she remarked on the bird; al-Qurṭubī has it flutter above her troops; Abū Ḥayyān also records this report.
  27:28/q09/p2 It carried the letter in or on its foot. — reasons: Yazīd ibn Rūmān reports the foot; Ibn ʿĀshūr suggests a letter fastened to the leg could be taken off by people.
  27:28/q09/p3 It bore the letter on its wing. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr records this as one report.
  27:28/q09/p4 It hung the letter around its neck. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān records this account, followed by its landing over Bilqis among her troops.
27:28/q10 Where and in what circumstances did the hoopoe deliver the letter? [فَأَلْقِهْ إِلَيْهِمْ · reports] (turns on: The reports differ on her location and state, and on whether the letter fell on throne, lap or chest.)
  27:28/q10/p1 It dropped the letter to the queen on or at her throne. — reasons: Al-Ḍaḥḥāk says it flew level with her throne; Ibn ʿAbbās says it reached her throne, while al-Suyūṭī's Ibn ʿAbbās report specifies its middle and says she read the message.
  27:28/q10/p2 It dropped the letter into her lap while she sat among her troops. — reasons: Muqātil, al-Qurṭubī's Muqātil report, al-Nasafī's alternative and Abū Ḥayyān's neck-carrying account place the queen with soldiers; some say it fluttered overhead. Yazīd ibn Rūmān instead has it drop through her window onto her lap after blocking the sunlight.
  27:28/q10/p3 It entered the locked sleeping queen's chamber through an opening and dropped the letter on her chest or belly. — reasons: Qatāda says keys lay under her head and gives the landing as belly or between breasts; Ibn Abī Ḥātim and al-Māwardī say chest; Wahb says she awoke startled and summoned her court. Al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ibn Kathīr and Bursevī report variants; Bursevī names Maʾrib. Some say the bird then hid in the opening.
  27:28/q10/p4 It approached through or at the sun-facing window, blocked the sun and then delivered the letter. — reasons: Ibn Zayd says it blocked her window before casting the letter and flying away; Yazīd ibn Rūmān says Bilqis worshipped the sun there; Wahb calls it the opening for sunrise; Abū Ḥayyān says blocking the light made her rise to receive the letter.
27:28/q11 What do the reports say about the queen's identity and background? [إِلَيْهِمْ · reports] (turns on: The reports supply different ancestry, personal capacities and descriptions of her people rather than a single agreed biography.)
  27:28/q11/p1 Bilqis was a Yemeni queen of a royal family, named as the daughter of Sharāḥbīl, who ruled after her people died. — reasons: Qatāda is reported by Yaḥyā ibn Sallām and Ibn Abī Ḥātim; al-Māwardī's Yazīd ibn Rūmān instead describes earlier rulers in her family and her training in government.
  27:28/q11/p2 She was a literate Arab descendant of Tubbaʿ al-Ḥimyarī. — reasons: Muqātil calls her and her people Arabs of Tubbaʿ ibn Abī Sharāḥīl; Abū Ḥayyān and Bursevī add her literacy, and Bursevī says she read and wrote Arabic. Names and lineage wording vary.
  27:28/q11/p3 She was an astute, educated ruler from a royal house whose people practiced Zoroastrianism. — reasons: Yazīd ibn Rūmān gives this description in Ibn Abī Ḥātim.
27:28/q12 What effect did the bird and seal have on the queen? [بِّكِتَٰبِى فَأَلْقِهْ · reports]
  27:28/q12/p1 The seal frightened her and her troops, and a bird serving as envoy convinced her that Solomon's kingship surpassed hers. — reasons: Muqātil says Solomon's authority resided in his seal; Bursevī says its imposing appearance made her tremble and submit, while the bird's obedience showed the greater king.
