## THE CURRENT MAP
87:1/q01 What does sabbih command? [سَبِّحِ · meaning] (turns on: Whether tasbih names declaration, a particular utterance, prayer, vocal praise, or a comprehensive devotional act.)
  87:1/q01/p1 Declare God free of all defects, false claims, rivals, and unworthy attributes and acts. — reasons: Muqatil makes this denial of shirk and affirmation of God's oneness. Zajjaj, Ibn Atiyya, Jishumi, Biqai, Ibn Ashur and others include evil, change, deficiency, idolatry, anthropomorphism, coercion, and claims of polytheists; Jishumi adds wisdom and goodness of divine acts. Elmalili includes spoken, inward, and practical worship; Qutb insists on inward awareness, not rote repetition. Ibn Ashur says the religious sense may be borrowed from Hebrew; Corpus Coranicum instead adduces Syriac shabbeh and the Hebrew psalmic imperative.
  87:1/q01/p2 Say 'Glory be to my Lord, the Most High' as the command's verbal fulfillment. — reasons: Ibn Abbas, Qatada and groups of Companions and Successors explain the command by the formula; transmitted recitation practice supports it. Alusi says an utterance of subhan requires treating ism as inserted. Tabari and Nahhas cite the Prophet's and Companions' practice in support of their interpretations.
  87:1/q01/p3 Magnify and exalt the Lord. — reasons: Tabari reports that none is greater or higher. Ibn Abbas and al-Suddi say ism is added to magnify the referent; Labid's verse supports that idiom. Jarir's verse is cited for vocal devotional praise.
  87:1/q01/p4 Pray to the Lord or pray using His name or command. — reasons: Ibn Abbas variously says by the Lord's command or in His name, likening it to beginning in God's name with an omitted preposition. Mawardi adds mentioning Him at prayer's opening or holding Him in mind at intention; Razi cites prostration reports and 30:17. Elmalili notes prayer as a conventional tasbih sense but objects that the omitted preposition is against the surface construction; Alusi doubts the Ibn Abbas report.
  87:1/q01/p5 Raise the voice in remembrance and praise of God. — reasons: Mawardi and Qurtubi cite Jarir's line about pilgrims chanting and magnifying God; Bursevi says this makes ism meaningful.
  87:1/q01/p6 Practice sustained inward remembrance, obedience, and moral discipline. — reasons: Tustari joins outward denial of rivals to inward witnessing in prayer. Sulami forbids idle talk and lies afterward. Qushayri describes knowledge of the names and inward swimming in exaltedness; Islahi links remembrance and tahajjud to comfort, perseverance, protection from Satan, and a sound grasp of God. Qutb calls praise an experiential gift.
87:1/q02 What does ism mean here? [ٱسْمَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the direct object is the divine word, an honorific stand-in for God, the act of naming, an attribute, or a broader sign.)
  87:1/q02/p1 God's name itself is the object of sanctification. — reasons: Tabari, Abu Hayyan, Alusi and Elmalili emphasize the direct object; the name must not be given to idols, misinterpreted, or used irreverently. Ibn Ashur says spoken reports and sacred attributes are applied to names designating God; purely inward contemplation concerns God directly. Elmalili says the clearest sense is revealed linguistic names and attributes, though signs can indicate Him more broadly.
  87:1/q02/p2 Ism is an honorific added expression, or stands for God, the one named. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and al-Suddi use Labid's 'name of Peace' to illustrate the idiom; Razi says Arabic mentions an exalted person's name instead of the person. Abu Ali adduces 'the name of peace be upon you.' Baghowi and Qurtubi argue that worshippers say 'Glory to my Lord,' not to His name. Alusi conditionally favors insertion if sabbih means utter subhan; Elmalili says sanctification of the referent still proceeds through its name.
  87:1/q02/p3 Ism means naming, mention, or the act of speaking of God. — reasons: Tabari and others require fearful humility. Ibn Atiyya cites Arabic usage and the hadith on God's ninety-nine names for naming as an act. Qurtubi reports this but favors the named referent.
  87:1/q02/p4 Ism means God's attribute or attributes. — reasons: Abu Muslim cites 7:180; Jishumi says to cleanse divine attributes of what is unfitting. Elmalili describes a Sufi account in which names are attributes and effects follow them.
  87:1/q02/p5 Ism means God's oneness. — reasons: Damghani compares 73:8, taking glorification and remembrance of the name as affirmation of divine unity.
  87:1/q02/p6 Ism means God's effects or created signs. — reasons: Isam al-Din says effects point to God as a name does and cites 67:3. Alusi finds it remote. Elmalili says one must not blame creatures insofar as they are God's work, but finds the sense strained for lack of a contextual cue; effects remain distinct from names in the Sufi account.
  87:1/q02/p7 Ism comprises whatever makes God known, including names, attributes, reason, and natural signs. — reasons: Abduh relates this to God's face in 55:27-78 and Adam's taught names in 2:31; this rules out likeness, incarnation, partners and offspring, while verses 2-3 identify Creator and Guide. Elmalili allows it as metaphor but finds leaving the ordinary linguistic sense remote.
87:1/q03 Do sabbih isma and sabbih bi-ismi mean the same thing? [سَبِّحِ ٱسْمَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether bi- is freely alternating, semantically distinct, or understood as omitted in an interpretation of prayer.)
  87:1/q03/p1 Both Arabic constructions convey the same glorification. — reasons: Al-Farra affirms both forms; Thalabi restricts the equivalence to contexts of remembrance and glorification. Razi reports the comparison with 56:74. Samarrai lists Quranic transitive and prepositional patterns.
  87:1/q03/p2 The direct object sanctifies the name; bi- means glorifying God by means of His name. — reasons: Nahhas rejects a simple deletion of the preposition and says transitivity changes sense. Wahidi distinguishes the object from instrument; Elmalili says the chosen direct object deliberately sanctifies the name itself. Ibn Ashur says bi pairs speech with speech in other praise constructions.
  87:1/q03/p3 An omitted bi- permits reading the command as praying in the Lord's name. — reasons: Ibn Abbas compares 'begin in God's name'; Alusi doubts the transmission and Elmalili says the analysis opposes the apparent wording.
87:1/q04 Is God's name identical with the one named? [ٱسْمَ · theology] (turns on: Whether ism denotes a spoken designation or the divine referent, and whether the customary formula proves identity.)
  87:1/q04/p1 The name is identical with the named God in this context. — reasons: The author of al-Nazm says only God, not a distinct name, can be glorified. Baghowi and Qurtubi cite 'Glory to God/my Lord,' never 'Glory to God's name'; Sa'di al-Mufti uses the hadith formula likewise.
  87:1/q04/p2 The wording and the entity differ; calling them identical equivocates or is tautological. — reasons: Razi says a word differs from its referent, while identity follows trivially if both terms denote the entity; perhaps confusion arose because 'name' is itself a noun for a timeless meaning. He rejects the formula argument since the verse admits several analyses. Alusi rejects identity.
87:1/q05 How must God's names be guarded? [ٱسْمَ · interpretation] (turns on: The scope of sanctifying a divine designation: assignment, interpretation, occasion and manner of speech, authorization, and figurative poetic address.)
  87:1/q05/p1 Do not transfer exclusive divine names to idols, false claimants, or creatures as God's equals. — reasons: Tabari cites al-Lat and al-Uzza; Nahhas derives the idol names from al-Aziz and Allah. Razi adds Musaylima's 'Merciful of Yamama.' Alusi distinguishes exclusive names from shared names used as equal providers. Tabatabai expands the rule to attributing divine acts to others and associating intercessors.
  87:1/q05/p2 Interpret divine names without unworthy implications such as embodiment, spatial settlement, compulsion, or false likeness. — reasons: Kashshaf specifically opposes coercion and anthropomorphism. Baydawi and Alusi reject unjustified figurative interpretation as well as impossible literal senses; Ibn Ashur makes divine perfection the governing rule for ambiguous descriptions. Elmalili cites 42:11.
  87:1/q05/p3 Utter God's names only with humility, understanding, and in fitting contexts. — reasons: Tabari and Thalabi stress reverent remembrance; Sulami warns against lies and idle talk afterward. Bursevi names yawning, relieving oneself, and excessive oaths. Alusi cites Malik declining to invoke God before a beggar who disliked hearing it. Ibn Ashur joins speech to understanding.
  87:1/q05/p4 Avoid provoking unbelievers to insult God. — reasons: Tabari and Razi connect this to the prohibition on reviling pagan deities in 6:108.
  87:1/q05/p5 Use only names established by revelation when naming God. — reasons: Razi cites 17:110 and the distinction from pagan whistling and clapping; Alusi's critics cite 7:180 against addressing God as Layla or Na'im. Bursevi reports the fixed-name position. Ibn Ashur says al-A'la is not separately counted among beautiful names because al-Ali already names the quality and names require name-like established usage.
  87:1/q05/p6 Any designation free of deficiency may name God even if revelation did not establish it. — reasons: Razi attributes this to the Mu'tazilites, contrasting it with his revealed-name position.
  87:1/q05/p7 Poetic Layla and Na'im are defensible as extended metaphors, without literally naming God by them. — reasons: Alusi reports a defense by allusion and analogy to allegorical readings of 2:67, says critics remain unsatisfied, and prefers a whole-expression metaphor; his full account compares metaphorical basmala interpretation.
