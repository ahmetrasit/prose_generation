2:31/q16 What pronoun readings are reported for ‘displayed them’? [عَرَضَهُمْ · readings] (turns on: Masculine plural of the received text versus feminine plural and singular variants.)
  2:31/q16/p1 Ibn Masʿūd or ʿAbd Allāh reads feminine plural ʿaraḍahunna. — reasons: This can point to names or named entities as a nonrational plural; sources differ in their explanation.
  2:31/q16/p2 Ubayy reads feminine singular ʿaraḍahā. — reasons: The pronoun can refer to names, named entities, or, in one Ibn Kathīr explanation, heavens.
  2:31/q16/p3 The received reading is masculine plural ʿaraḍahum. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ takes embodied persons as natural; al-Ṭabarī and other exegetes allow mixed kinds by masculine predominance.
2:31/q17 Which angels were shown the beings? [ٱلْمَلَٰٓئِكَةِ · referent] (turns on: All angels or the group associated with Iblis.)
  2:31/q17/p1 The addressed angels were those with Iblis, including his troops in the war against jinn. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās reports this restriction; al-Rāzī's narration connects their prior question to anger and later repentance.
  2:31/q17/p2 The whole company of angels was addressed. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān records this as a possible scope of the definite plural.
2:31/q18 What does ‘inform Me’ mean here? [أَنۢبِـُٔونِى · meaning] (turns on: Simple informing versus a weighty report that produces knowledge.)
  2:31/q18/p1 It means tell or inform Me of the names. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī cites al-Nābigha's use of anبأ for informing; Ibn ʿAbbās gives the direct gloss. Al-Māwardī relates nabāʾ to report and prophetic title; the verb can take two objects or three through aʿlama.
  2:31/q18/p2 It asks for an important report that conveys knowledge rather than mere utterance. — reasons: Al-Jishumī says some limit nabaʾ to consequential news; Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it significant eagerly sought information, and al-Ālūsī says this suits the names' elevated rank.
  2:31/q18/p3 Adam informs the angels but does not teach them his comprehensive knowledge. — reasons: Al-Ḥaralī says Adam possessed what the angels could receive only as information and lacked capacity to master; Elmalılı distinguishes direct grasp of reality from mediated verbal signs.
2:31/q19 How is ‘inform Me’ read or pronounced? [أَنۢبِـُٔونِى · readings] (turns on: Whether the hamza is maintained, softened or elided.)
  2:31/q19/p1 Al-Aʿmash and another transmitted form elide the hamza: anbūnī. — reasons: Qirāʾāt Er explains a dialectal or phonological omission without a change in meaning.
  2:31/q19/p2 The hamza is pronounced in anbiʾūnī, from anبأ yunbiʾu. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās calls it hamzat qaṭʿ and allows softening or replacement.
2:31/q20 What does ‘these’ point to, and how is it parsed? [هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ · grammar] (turns on: Present referents versus names representing absent referents.)
  2:31/q20/p1 It points to named beings made present in the display. — reasons: Al-Mujāhid says these are the taught things; Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān argue demonstratives normally point to visible present entities. Al-Samīn calls it genitive after asmāʾ and built on kasra.
  2:31/q20/p2 Hāʾulāʾ is an indeclinable near demonstrative in genitive position. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās says the preposition makes asmāʾ genitive and annexation makes hāʾulāʾ genitive; Abū Ḥayyān analyzes hāʾ as attention and ulāʾ as built on kasra.
  2:31/q20/p3 It can point to absent referents through their names. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Qurṭubī allow names present by relation; Abū Ḥayyān finds the reading strained.
2:31/q21 What pronunciations are reported for ‘these’? [هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ · readings] (turns on: Length of its parts, shortening and deletion, and the origin of its final hamza.)
  2:31/q21/p1 Tamīm and some Asad and Qays speakers shorten or reduce hāʾulāʾ, sometimes deleting alif and hamza. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās, Ibn ʿAṭiyya and al-Qurṭubī cite poetic forms hāʾulā or hūlāʾ; Abū Ḥayyān reports Quṭrub's rounded variant and al-Shalawbīn's deletion.
  2:31/q21/p2 Some readers shorten hāʾ and lengthen ūlāʾ; others lengthen both. — reasons: Al-Jishumī and al-Ṭabrisī list the readers; al-Farrāʾ assigns long ūlāʾ to the Ḥijāz, short to Tamīm. Al-Aʿshā's poetry is cited.
  2:31/q21/p3 The final hamza is original, or replaces an earlier yāʾ. — reasons: Abū ʿAlī supports an original final radical; Abū al-ʿAbbās and al-Mubarrad derive it from yāʾ changed after alif.
2:31/q22 How are the two adjacent hamzas in ‘these, if’ recited? [هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ إِن · readings] (turns on: Different readers pronounce both, soften either one, omit one, or replace one with a glide.)
  2:31/q22/p1 Soften or replace the first hamza and pronounce the second. — reasons: Nāfiʿ is reported with a glide varying by vowel; al-Jishumī and al-Ṭabrisī give Ismāʿīl from Nāfiʿ and Ibn Fulayḥ from Ibn Kathīr. Ibn Mujāhid prefers an intermediate first hamza.
  2:31/q22/p2 Pronounce only one of the two hamzas. — reasons: Abū ʿAmr and al-Bazzī or related Ibn Kathīr transmissions are cited; al-Jishumī says deletion eases articulation. Reports differ on which hamza is lost.
  2:31/q22/p3 Pronounce both hamzas clearly. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀmir, Ḥamza, ʿĀṣim, al-Kisāʾī and other Kufans are named; keeping both preserves the underlying form.
  2:31/q22/p4 Pronounce the first and soften the second. — reasons: Attributed to Abū Jaʿfar, Warsh from Nāfiʿ, Qawwās from Ibn Kathīr, and Yaʿqūb. Al-Khalīl and Sībawayh prefer this according to al-Naḥḥās; softening eases pronunciation.
  2:31/q22/p5 Replace the second kasra hamza with a long yāʾ or pronounce the resulting yāʾ with kasra. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān attributes the long yāʾ to Warsh and Qunbul; Ibn Mujāhid reports kasra on the yāʾ from some Nāfiʿ routes. Comparable contexts include 24:33.
  2:31/q22/p6 Soften both hamzas. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās lists this as another pronunciation.
2:31/q23 Why did God ask angels to give the names? [فَقَالَ أَنۢبِـُٔونِى بِأَسْمَآءِ · interpretation] (turns on: Exposing ignorance, displaying Adam's merit, demonstrating fitness for rule, or an impossible command.)
  2:31/q23/p1 The challenge exposes the angels' ignorance of what is before them and of the unseen future. — reasons: Al-Akhfash says no prior claim is needed; al-Wāḥidī compares 2:23. The inability rebukes conjecture about corruption; many sources stress God already knew they could not answer.
  2:31/q23/p2 It is a teaching prompt contingent on whether the angels know; no obligation to do the impossible was imposed. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī reports al-Akhfash, al-Jubbāʾī and ʿAlī ibn ʿĪsā; Ibn ʿAṭiyya's discerning exegetes and many later authors reject an impossible-duty inference.
  2:31/q23/p3 The question demonstrates Adam's knowledge and superior fitness for vicegerency. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī says governance and justice require knowledge of capacities and rights; al-Ṭabrisī names crafts, farming and finding resources; Ibn ʿĀshūr stresses analysis and guidance, whereas angels execute fixed tasks.
  2:31/q23/p4 It is a permissible incapacitating command even though God knew the angels could not comply. — reasons: Some infer that God may command the impossible; Bursevī likens this to commanding image makers to animate their images at Judgment Day.
  2:31/q23/p5 It invites the angels to learn by direct test, observation and reflection. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu contrasts a reported explanation with demonstrated certainty; the test gives the angels a higher certainty and recognition of Adam's capacities.
2:31/q24 What is the syntax of ‘if you are truthful’? [إِن كُنتُمْ صَٰدِقِينَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the preceding imperative itself is the apodosis or only indicates an omitted one.)
  2:31/q24/p1 The apodosis is omitted and understood as 'then inform Me.' — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās and Ibn ʿAṭiyya attribute this to al-Mubarrad, but Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn correct the attribution to Sībawayh and most Basrans; al-Samīn says a genuine preceding apodosis would need fāʾ.
  2:31/q24/p2 The preceding imperative is the conditional's answer. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās and Ibn ʿAṭiyya attribute it to Sībawayh; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn assign it instead to the Kufans, Abū Zayd and al-Mubarrad. The divergence partly concerns terminology for a preceding clause.
2:31/q25 Does ‘if’ here mean a condition or ‘when’? [إِن · meaning] (turns on: A temporal paraphrase is reported but rejected by several grammarians.)
  2:31/q25/p1 It is a genuine condition. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās, al-Ṭabarī, Abū ʿUbayd, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī reject substitution with idh or idhā; they say it removes the clause's conditional force.
  2:31/q25/p2 It means 'when you were' rather than setting a condition. — reasons: Reported from some exegetes, but the named grammarians reject it.
2:31/q26 What claim is ‘if you are truthful’ testing? [إِن كُنتُمْ صَٰدِقِينَ · interpretation] (turns on: Sources connect it to several distinct prior or implied angelic claims, or simply to accuracy in naming.)
  2:31/q26/p1 Their assumption that God would create none more knowledgeable, honored or excellent than themselves. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan and Qatāda's accounts say they privately boasted of prior worship and precedence; Adam's lesson and their admission correct the assumption.
  2:31/q26/p2 Their forecast that the earthly successor would corrupt and shed blood. — reasons: Reports from Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Masʿūd and Compani
