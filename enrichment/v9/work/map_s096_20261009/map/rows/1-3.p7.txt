[ELMALILI:1:3#3/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | The truth is neither pure determinism nor pure autonomy, but a middle position between the two.
[ELMALILI:1:3#3/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Human life combines body and spirit, reason and heart, capacity and action, necessity and choice; humans are neither absolutely compelled nor absolutely self-acting.
[ELMALILI:1:3/r1] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Human mercy begins as an affective tenderness toward the afflicted and aims to remove harm and establish good; sometimes a word or gesture suffices, while its complete end is voluntary beneficence.
[ELMALILI:1:3/r10] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Divine knowledge and will are eternal creator-attributes and the source of human counterparts; human acquisition, desire, and temporal change are incidental conditions, not their essence.
[ELMALILI:1:3/r11] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | He distinguishes divine rahmet from human merhamet in Turkish, so translating Rahmân Rahîm simply as “very compassionate” is inadequate.
[ELMALILI:1:3/r12] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | A fuller explanation is boundless mercy and beneficent outpouring, combining the essential will of good with rescue from harm and delivery to blessings.
[ELMALILI:1:3/r2] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | In ordinary speech, calling someone merciful usually means tender-hearted capacity, while calling them very merciful points to visible beneficial action.
[ELMALILI:1:3/r3] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | Because God is beyond change and affect, divine mercy cannot be explained as the same psychological state as human compassion; both rational and revealed indications support this.
[ELMALILI:1:3/r4] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · müfessirîn | reports | Exegetes allow some lexical metaphor in divine mercy: either its necessary result, deliverance and beneficence, or the prior will of good that causes beneficence.
[ELMALILI:1:3/r5] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | On the first interpretation mercy is an attribute of action; on the second, the will of good is an essential attribute, since willing is choice rather than affect.
[ELMALILI:1:3/r6] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | prefers | Since divine mercy is perfect and both adjectives occur, he prefers including both the essential will of good and its acts of beneficence.
[ELMALILI:1:3/r7] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | holds | He explains Rahmân as a special, stable adjective for God's essential will of good, and Rahîm as an intensive active participle for its manifested beneficence; some reverse this mapping.
[ELMALILI:1:3/r8] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · Bunu aks edenler | reports | Some interpreters reverse the allocation of essential and active meanings between Rahmân and Rahîm.
[ELMALILI:1:3/r9] ELMALILI, d. 1361 · author | prefers | His preferred account treats divine names and attributes as literally true, not metaphorical, while recognizing that their divine meanings are not identical to human ones.
[QUTB-ZILAL:1:1/r1] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | Al-Fatiha is Meccan and has seven verses; Muslims recite it at least seventeen times daily, more with voluntary prayers, and may repeat it without limit.
[QUTB-ZILAL:1:1/r10] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | Beginning in God's name expresses the first major Islamic worldview principle, God's oneness and proper reverence; comprehensive mercy expresses the second and defines the bond between God and servants.
[QUTB-ZILAL:1:1/r2] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · رسول الله [ ص ] | reports | The Prophet says prayer is invalid without reciting the Opening of the Book; the report is in both Sahih collections.
[QUTB-ZILAL:1:1/r3] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | The short surah gathers core Islamic creed, worldview, feelings, and guidance, explaining its repetition in every rakʿa and why prayer without it is invalid.
[QUTB-ZILAL:1:1/r8] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | Rahman and Rahim together encompass every meaning and condition of mercy; God alone bears both, and alone may be called Rahman, though a servant may be called Rahim.
[QUTB-ZILAL:1:1/r9] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | Quṭb leaves aside which mercy-name has broader scope and stresses that together they cover mercy in all its meanings, conditions, and domains.
[QUTB-ZILAL:1:3/r1] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | The repeated mercy-names form a separate verse to underscore mercy as the defining feature of comprehensive lordship and the enduring relationship between Creator and creatures.
[QUTB-ZILAL:1:3/r2] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · author | holds | God's bond with servants is mercy and care, which elicit praise, reassurance, and affection, unlike vindictive or capricious gods in Greek myths and the Babel story in Genesis 11.
[QUTB-ZILAL:1:7/r2] QUTB-ZILAL, d. 1386 · رسول الله | reports | Abu Hurayra reports in Sahih Muslim that God calls al-Fatiha a prayer divided between Himself and His servant: praise, mercy, and glorification are the Lord's; worship and aid are shared; guidance and the servant's request belong to the servant, who receives what he asks.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r1] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm are both derived from the root r-ḥ-m.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r10] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Mercy’s essence has no discrete external instances; what has instances are its effects.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r11] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Languages describe God with mercy according to what their communities believe can or cannot be attributed to God; most earlier nations were anthropomorphists.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r12] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Scriptural language expresses elevated meanings as far as ordinary language allows, while believers affirm God’s transcendence from created accidents, as in 42:11. | mentions 42:11
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r13] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Believers do not understand divine mercy as the human emotional affect; for God it means the intended goal of mercy, the issuance of gentleness, kindness, beneficence, and aid.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r14] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The human limitations normally included in mercy’s meaning matter only insofar as they enable mercy’s effects in people.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r15] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | A person may pity someone yet be unable to benefit them because of incapacity, showing that an inner feeling and its effective outcome differ.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r16] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · أبو حامد الغزالي | reports | Al-Ghazālī says a capable person who wants to meet a need but does not do so is not merciful; an incapable person may be called merciful for the pity felt, but that mercy is incomplete.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r17] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | These divine attributes are not ambiguous in the relevant sense: frequent use makes the intended meaning readily apparent, and reason establishes that God lacks unbefitting implications, as with knowledge, life, and power. | mentions 3:7

[IBNASHUR:1:3/r21] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Although ṣifa mushabbaha is normally formed from an intransitive verb, a transitive verb can become an innate disposition and be treated like an instinctive verb, with its middle vowel changed to ḍamma.
[IBNASHUR:1:3/r22] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | This conversion is recognized either by hearing the converted verb, as in faquha, or by its resulting adjective when the verb is rarely heard, as with balīgh; al-Raḥmān is analogous though raḥuma is unattested.
