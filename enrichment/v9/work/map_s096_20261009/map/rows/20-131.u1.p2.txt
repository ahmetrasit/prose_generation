20:131/q18 What ethical counsel is drawn from others' worldly display? [* · interpretation]
  20:131/q18/p1 Believers should avert admiring attention from ostentatious wealth and practice contentment. — reasons: Pious scholars in Kashshāf and others say spectators reward tyrants' displays; al-Ḥasan urges seeing sin's humiliation beneath garments and mounts. Jishumī urges little and detachment; Wāsiṭī consoles the poor. A reported hadith counsels comparison with more devout and less wealthy people; Jesus warns that enthroning the world enslaves. Qushayrī's poem disciplines the eye for admiring other than the beloved.
  20:131/q18/p2 Worldly ease is no proof of divine favor; lawful goods may be enjoyed and shared responsibly. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu warns against envy while affirming lawful enjoyment, aid to others, endurance of deprivation, and seeking God's favor. Rāzī reports that God looks at hearts and deeds rather than forms and wealth; Abū Ḥayyān cites the world as cursed except what is sought for God. Ibn Kathīr portrays the Prophet sharing all he received and not storing it. The report of ʿUmar contrasts his sparse home with Caesar and Khosrow, whose goods came early.
  20:131/q18/p3 The world's sweetness can enslave and deceive the attached. — reasons: Bursevī cites Khujandī's honey with greater bitter trouble, Mathnawī's carrion, and Ḥāfiẓ's seductive bride; Qushayrī cautions that distraction breeds arrogance and excess.
20:131/q19 How is the verse read inwardly? [* · inward]
  20:131/q19/p1 The outer and inner gaze must turn from worldly and even afterlife enjoyments toward God alone. — reasons: Tustarī and Sahl name satanic whispers, disobedience, egoistic hopes, and habitual ease as barriers to remembrance. Bursevī follows Najmī in distinguishing bodily and inward vision, and says enjoyment of either abode may block beholding divine beauty. Qushayrī depicts extreme ascetic exertion; Shiblī questions being occupied even by Paradise's delights.
  20:131/q19/p2 Extending the gaze represents the soul's shifting inclinations, while true provision is spiritual light and knowledge. — reasons: Qāshānī sees worldly adornments as tests for worldly people and names afterlife realities and knowledge as the lasting provision; Bursevī speaks of nourishment for the holy spirit.
20:131/q20 Is 20:131 Meccan or Medinan? [* · sciences] (turns on: Whether the collateral narrative establishes a later descent or the contextual style and Meccan classification govern.)
  20:131/q20/p1 It is Meccan with the sūra. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya uses the Meccan setting to object to Abū Rāfiʿ's Medinan incident; Kur'an Yolu doubts Medinan reports because verses 130–131 have Meccan style; Study Quran says context favors Mecca.
  20:131/q20/p2 It may be a Medinan exception. — reasons: Suyūṭī uses Abū Rāfiʿ's loan report; Ibn ʿĀshūr reports an exception at 20:130 and a likely one at 20:131; Study Quran notes some classify verses 130–131 Medinan.
20:131/q21 What recitational and counting details attach to the verse ending? [زَهْرَةَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا · sciences]
  20:131/q21/p1 Some regional verse counts include 'زهرة الحياة الدنيا' as an ending. — reasons: Nashr says the Medinan, Meccan and Basran counters include it among five disputed endings in Ṭā Hā; its imāla treatment across eleven sūras depends on six counting systems, with Medinan counting guiding Nāfiʿ and Basran Abū ʿAmr. The reliably transmitted choice for al-Azraq from Warsh and the other cited reader in the preceding imāla discussion is fatḥ.
20:131/q22 What morphological rule is illustrated by زهرة? [زَهْرَةَ · grammar]
  20:131/q22/p1 Words bearing a noun's sense need not take an open tāʾ. — reasons: Zarkashī lists zahrah alongside ṣibghah, zalzalah, taḥillah, riḥlah and ḥammālah, citing 30:30, 101:4, 66:2, 106:2 and 111:4.
20:131/q23 What reports connect knowledge-seeking with provision? [وَرِزْقُ رَبِّكَ · reports]
  20:131/q23/p1 God guarantees or preserves provision for those who seek knowledge. — reasons: Durr reports Ziyād al-Ṣudāʿī's Prophetic saying that God guarantees provision, and Abū Saʿīd's report that angels shade and bless a seeker without diminishing livelihood.
20:131/q24 What other Qur'anic topics appear in the excerpted source material? [* · links]
  20:131/q24/p1 Several Qur'anic passages establish the jinn and devils' existence and action. — reasons: Rāzī cites jinn hearing the Qur'an (46:29), devils in Solomon's era (2:102), their building and making vessels (34:13), their diving and chains (38:37–38), wind and jinn serving Solomon (34:12), the challenge to jinn and humans (55:33), and stars guarding heaven from devils (37:6). An ungraded Abū Saʿīd report about a snake and a dying newlywed cuts off before the Prophet's response.
  20:131/q24/p2 The Qur'an has concise, varied, ordered eloquence. — reasons: Zarkashī praises its inimitable concision, wisdom, literal and figurative speech, and changes in rhetorical form and tone.
20:131/q25 What does the cited discussion say about the Fātiḥa? [* · sciences] (turns on: The reported place and recurrence of its revelation, plus the meaning of its name.)
  20:131/q25/p1 It was revealed in Mecca. — reasons: Ālūsī reports ʿAlī, Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatāda and most Companions. He prefers eyewitness Companion testimony; 15:87's seven oft-repeated verses may mean the Fātiḥa or seven long sūras. Future-oriented past tense (48:1) is possible, though 20:131's link suggests earlier delivery.
  20:131/q25/p2 It was revealed in Medina. — reasons: Ālūsī reports Mujāhid's isolated claim, counted a mistake.
  20:131/q25/p3 It descended both in Mecca and Medina. — reasons: Ālūsī reports a view tied to prayer's obligation and the qibla change, then rejects repetition because descent cannot recur and reports of benefits, readings and basmala do not prove a second descent.
  20:131/q25/p4 Some verses are Meccan and some Medinan. — reasons: Ālūsī reports and calls this weak.
  20:131/q25/p5 The source also explains surah and Fātiḥa as names and grammatical forms. — reasons: Ālūsī derives surah from remnant, enclosure or rank, defines it as an independent unit with transmitted name, rejects a purported ban on saying 'Sura X' because Anas's report is weak or fabricated and Ibn ʿUmar's only his statement, and analyzes Fātiḥa as the opening of the compiled Book in divine knowledge, the Preserved Tablet or Bayt al-ʿIzza.
20:131/q26 What inward disturbance can worldly testing produce? [لِنَفْتِنَهُمْ فِيهِ · meaning]
  20:131/q26/p1 Fitnah is fear, anticipation, and confused mental turmoil. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says shirk gives disbelievers gloom and apprehension, with a further trial in the afterlife.
20:131/q27 What determines a person's worldly allotment? [مَا مَتَّعْنَا بِهِۦٓ · theology]
  20:131/q27/p1 God's wisdom makes each allotment appropriate to its recipient's decreed destiny. — reasons: Asad gives this as the significance of what God grants a person.
20:131/q28 What conduct can God's provision reward? [وَرِزْقُ رَبِّكَ · interpretation]
  20:131/q28/p1 Patience and indifference to worldly abundance receive God's reward. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān transmits this as one explanation of the Lord's provision.
