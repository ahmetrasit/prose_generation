# 12:43: 35 questions

## 12:43/q01 Who is the king, and what position does he hold?  [ٱلْمَلِكُ · referent]
turns on: The sources give differing personal names or a proposed historical dynasty; none supplies a decisive test.
- 12:43/q01/p1 He is al-Rayyan ibn al-Walid, the Egyptian king. — Ibn Ishaq says he recognized a true vision without understanding it; al-Kashshaf, al-Nasafi, al-Qurtubi, and Abu Hayyan name him. Al-Alusi calls him an unbeliever; al-Biqa'i describes his political and administrative power. [8 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن إسحاق); IBNABIHATIM(مجاهد); KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 12:43/q01/p2 He is al-Walid ibn al-Rayyan, Egypt's ruler, with al-Aziz as minister. — Al-Mawardi gives the name; al-Tabrisi adds the minister. [2 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI]
- 12:43/q01/p3 He is probably one of the Hyksos rulers of Egypt. — Asad proposes Arab origin and kinship with Hebrews to explain his confidence in Joseph and Israelite settlement. Kuranyolu places six Hyksos rulers around 1700–1580 BCE, entering by Sinai; the 'shepherd kings' label suggests Syrian Arabs. It suggests shared nomadic origins with Hebrews and contrasts Moses' later Coptic Pharaoh, citing 2:49. [4 notes: ASAD-NOTES; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 12:43/q01/p4 He is the chief king of Egypt. — Ibn al-Jawzi gives the political designation without a personal name. [1 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD]

## 12:43/q02 May an unbelieving ruler be called a king?  [ٱلْمَلِكُ · law]
turns on: Whether a royal title implies rightful authority.
- 12:43/q02/p1 Yes, the verse itself calls the unbelieving Rayyan king. — Al-Alusi cites this title as evidence of permissibility. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 12:43/q02/p2 Some prohibit king or amir when the title implies rightful authority. — Al-Alusi reports their concern and Muhammad's letter calling Heraclius the 'great Roman.' [0 notes: ALUSI-FULL-]

## 12:43/q03 What circumstances led to the king's dream and Joseph's release?  [ٱلْمَلِكُ أَرَىٰ · reports]
turns on: The reports differ on whether Gabriel's prior announcement is included and what preceding events are named.
- 12:43/q03/p1 The king's dream arose as Joseph's release approached and became its means. — Mujahid says the released prisoner's forgotten promise delayed Joseph's release because Satan caused him to forget remembrance of his Lord. Al-Kalbi places seven prison years after five earlier years. Al-Mawardi says the dream also warned of drought for preparation. Al-Biqa'i says God provided another cause for Joseph's elevation after the forgotten request. Islahi links the earlier inmates' dreams and Joseph's interpretations to the king's consultation; God's plan brings unlikely means into being. [9 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); THALABI(الكلبي); MAWARDI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL]
- 12:43/q03/p2 Gabriel announced Joseph's coming release, power, and reunion before the king dreamed that night. — Wahb reports the appointed prison term ending. Al-Qurtubi reports Gabriel saying the dream would bring freedom and power. [2 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD(وهب بن منبه); QURTUBI-FULL]

## 12:43/q04 Does 'I see' describe a dream or a waking sight?  [أَرَىٰ رُءْيَٰىَ · interpretation]
- 12:43/q04/p1 It describes a dream seen in sleep. — Al-Farra says Arabic normally uses this wording for a dream and cites Abraham's explicit sleep vision in 37:102; al-Tabari notes the verse does not itself say 'asleep.' Al-Tabrisi reports this as the majority view. The king later calls it his ru'ya, as Abu Hayyan observes; Study Quran describes it as veridical. [5 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL; TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; STUDYQURAN]

## 12:43/q05 Why does the king use the present tense 'I see'?  [أَرَىٰ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether present tense merely enlivens a past report or hints that the vision recurred.
- 12:43/q05/p1 He vividly recounts a past dream as though seeing it now. — Ibn Atiyya says he contemplates the vision now; Abu Hayyan calls the scene present to the hearer; al-Biqa'i ties vividness to his alarm. Ibn al-Jawzi and al-Alusi explicitly read it as 'I saw.' [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 12:43/q05/p2 The wording may indicate a repeated dream. — Al-Tabataba'i compares the earlier prisoners' dreams. [1 notes: TABATABAI]

## 12:43/q06 What did the king see, and how did the images unfold?  [سَبْعَ بَقَرَٰتٍۢ سِمَانٍۢ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌۭ وَسَبْعَ سُنۢبُلَٰتٍ خُضْرٍۢ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَٰتٍۢ · reports]
- 12:43/q06/p1 Seven lean cows consumed seven fat cows, and dry ears overcame green ears. — Al-Suddi says God showed a frightening vision; several accounts say fat cows emerged from a dry river before the lean cows swallowed them, sometimes without visible change, and report the green ears being overcome or consumed. Ibn Atiyya reports the lean cows emerged after the fat and disappeared inside their bellies. Al-Wahidi says none of the fat remained visible; ripe green ears were overcome. The reports vary in these details. ## 12:43/q07 What do the cows and ears in the dream signify?  [سَبْعَ بَقَرَٰتٍۢ سِمَانٍۢ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌۭ وَسَبْعَ سُنۢبُلَٰتٍ خُضْرٍۢ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَٰتٍۢ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the images principally forecast years, prescribe famine management, or take meaning from contextual dream details.
- 12:43/q07/p1 They forecast fertile years followed by barren years and abundant harvests followed by drought. — Qatadah assigns fat and lean cows to fertile and barren years and green and dry ears to abundant harvests and drought. Ali says this is the meaning of fat and lean cattle seen in a town. Al-Mawardi calls the dream a warning enabling preparation; Joseph explains it in 12:47–49, as Study Quran notes. [4 notes: DURR-FULL(قتادة); QURTUBI-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب); MAWARDI-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 12:43/q07/p2 The dream chiefly depicts the practical measures needed to survive famine. — Al-Tabataba'i makes fat cows provisions from repeated planting, lean cows shortage, their eating consumption of reserves, green ears stored grain, and dry ears preservation unthreshed. He says unnumbered dry ears fit duties rather than counted famine years. [4 notes: TABATABAI; TABATABAI+]
- 12:43/q07/p3 Cattle symbols vary with the dream's setting and details. — Ali says coastal sailing season can make cows arriving ships; otherwise they may be successive turmoil. Uniform yellow can suggest disease; varied colors, ugly horns, or fiery mouths can signal army, raid, or enemy. Al-Qurtubi adds wife, servant, crop yield, or year because cattle bear offspring and produce yield. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(علي بن أبي طالب)]

## 12:43/q08 What does 'fat' mean, and what forms does the word have?  [سِمَانٍۢ · meaning]
- 12:43/q08/p1 The cows are full of flesh and fat; siman is the plural of samin or samina. — Al-Nasafi calls it a regular plural; al-Samin notes samina and shared-gender samin like kiram. Abu Hayyan and al-Samin describe saman as noun or verbal noun from samina and samin as active adjective, with irregular vowel pairing; al-Alusi derives forms from samana and samn. [7 notes: NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 12:43/q09 What does 'fat' grammatically describe, and why?  [سَبْعَ بَقَرَٰتٍۢ سِمَانٍۢ · grammar]
turns on: The scope of the adjective: the counted cows or the numeral, with a difference in emphasis.
- 12:43/q09/p1 It qualifies the cows, selecting seven of the fat kind. — Al-Kashshaf contrasts describing the numeral, which first identifies the count by cow-kind. Abu Hayyan cites the difference between 'three noble men' constructions; al-Samin says an adjective of the genitive classifier makes the counted set possess the quality. Al-Alusi prefers this to describing number and says a derived adjective cannot formally discriminate the count; al-Qurtubi observes another construction is possible outside Qur'anic wording. [10 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); TABATABAI(الكشاف); ALUSI-FULL+]
- 12:43/q09/p2 Outside this wording, 'fat' can qualify the numeral itself. — Al-Qurtubi cites a comparable construction at 67:3; al-Tibi says describing the number before its discriminator stresses both count and quality, whereas naming the discriminator first focuses the description. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الطيبي)]

## 12:43/q10 Why is the lean cows' number followed directly by an adjective?  [سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌۭ · grammar]
turns on: Whether an adjective may itself supply the numeral's counted kind or an understood 'cows' is needed.
- 12:43/q10/p1 The noun 'cows' is understood from the earlier phrase, and lean qualifies the seven. — Abu Hayyan says the earlier seven cows supply the omitted noun; numerals do not normally take mere adjectives as their genitive discriminator except poetic necessity or substantivized adjectives such as knights. Al-Kashshaf and al-Samin likewise distinguish substantivized nouns; al-Qutb says the prior cows identify the kind. Al-Baydawi ties the feminine form to the understood cows. [9 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL(الطيبي,القطب)]
- 12:43/q10/p2 The adjective may stand for cows as the numeral's discriminator, avoiding an omitted noun. — Al-Fara'id objects that numerical discriminators normally take genitive construction and offers this alternative; al-Qutb replies that when lean is so used it ceases to be an adjective in the relevant analysis. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(صاحب الفرائد); ALUSI-FULL(القطب)-]

## 12:43/q11 Why is the cows' eating expressed in the imperfect?  [يَأْكُلُهُنَّ · rhetoric]
- 12:43/q11/p1 It makes a past, startling scene present to the listener. — Al-Alusi says 'they eat' means 'they ate'; he parses the clause as circumstantial or adjectival. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 12:43/q12 What does 'lean' mean?  [عِجَافٌۭ · meaning]

## 12:43/q13 How is the form 'ijaf' derived and pluralized?  [عِجَافٌۭ · grammar]
- 12:43/q13/p1 It is an unusual plural of feminine ajfa', patterned after its opposite siman. — Al-Wahidi, al-Layth, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, al-Alusi, and al-Tabataba'i give analogy with siman; expected alternatives are 'ujuf or 'ujf. ## 12:43/q18 Did the dry ears also number seven?  [وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَٰتٍۢ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the vision's dry ears represent a counted period or unnumbered duties.
- 12:43/q18/p1 Yes, seven is omitted but supplied by the repeated pattern and 'others'. — Al-Kashshaf and al-Nasafi point to the recurring sevens; Abu Hayyan invokes the matching fat/lean and green/dry divisions; al-Biqa'i says the lean cattle have already suggested the count. Al-Tabari glosses 'others' as seven other ears. Al-Alusi says the earlier cows supply it. [9 notes: TAB-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI(الكشاف)]
- 12:43/q18/p2 The dry ears are deliberately unnumbered because the vision describes famine duties, not counted famine years. — Al-Tabataba'i says a forecast reading would require their number. [1 notes: TABATABAI]

## 12:43/q19 How does 'other dry ears' attach grammatically?  [وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَٰتٍۢ · grammar]
turns on: The coordinate's scope and whether an omitted numeral or noun is supplied without making the seven green ears also a different dry set.
- 12:43/q19/p1 It coordinates with 'seven', with an omitted seven and understood ears. — Al-Samin says the matching cow and ear groups supply the numeral; al-Kashshaf, Abu Hayyan, and al-Alusi reject treating 'others' as a modifier of the seven green ears, which would make the same seven both green and other. Al-Samin contrasts 'seven green and dry ears' and al-Kashshaf's standing/sitting men analogy. Al-Alusi prefers ellipsis or Ibn al-Hajib's withdrawal analysis, and argues 'others' is useful emphasis. [8 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 12:43/q19/p2 It coordinates with 'green', leaving the noun 'ears' expressed for both colors. — Al-Fara'id argues coordination normally repeats the numeral, creating fourteen ears, while an ellipsis analysis might wrongly total seven; attaching at 'green' avoids supplying a new ears noun. Al-Alusi replies that neither repetition nor omission cures the contradiction once a seven-ear discriminator is fixed. [2 notes: ALUSI-FULL(صاحب الفرائد); ALUSI-FULL(صاحب الفرائد)+; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 12:43/q20 Is the dry ears' overcoming of the green ears omitted in the wording?  [وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَٰتٍۢ · rhetoric]
- 12:43/q20/p1 Yes; the cow imagery supplies the action, later clarified by verse 48. — Al-Baydawi says the ears' state is omitted because cows already explain it; Islahi supplies 'the dry ears consume the green ears' from 12:48. [2 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 12:43/q22 Why did the king seek an interpretation from his council?  [يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلْمَلَأُ أَفْتُونِى فِى رُءْيَٰىَ · interpretation]

## 12:43/q26 How is 'my vision' or 'the vision' read?  [رُءْيَٰىَ لِلرُّءْيَا · readings]
turns on: The variants concern hamza, imala, and final ya pronunciation; the notes assign no semantic difference.
- 12:43/q26/p1 The word has hamza elision and assimilation variants without change of meaning. — Qiraat-ER gives ruyaaya by hamza elision, pause with silent final ya, and ruyyaya by waw-to-ya assimilation; al-ru'ya likewise has hamzless and assimilated ruyya. Abu Ja'far's assimilated reading is reported by Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, and al-Alusi; grammarians call its phonological sequence irregular because the waw substitution is optional. [8 notes: QIRAAT-ER; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو جعفر); SAMIN-DURR(أبو جعفر); ALUSI-FULL(أبو جعفر)]
- 12:43/q26/p2 Ru'ya admits full opening or imala. — Ibn Khalawayh says imala signals the feminine final alif corresponding to ta'; al-Kisa'i reportedly uses imala here but opening at ru'yaka in 12:5. He says this is mistaken as a case distinction, valid as dialect variation. [2 notes: IBNKHALAWAYH-HUJJA(الكسائي)]
- 12:43/q26/p3 The first-person ya of 'my vision' is vocalized in one canonical form. — Qiraat-ER contrasts it with its silence in the standard reading. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER]

## 12:43/q27 What kinds of dreams can be true or interpretable?  [رُءْيَٰىَ · theology]
turns on: Whether ordinary bodily and mental causes exhaust dreams, or some dreams truly disclose hidden or future realities.
- 12:43/q27/p1 Some dreams disclose real events and can be interpreted, while others arise from the sleeper or deception. — Al-Tabrisi distinguishes dreams from God or angels, Satanic dreams without interpretation, and dreams from beliefs and prior thoughts. Study Quran calls this vision veridical. Al-Tabataba'i says bodily state, temperament, emotion, chance, and preoccupation shape ordinary dreams; sound dreams disclose future or hidden facts. He argues common experience and noncoincidental fulfillment establish these, while naturalist accounts explain only some dreams. [9 notes: TABRISI; STUDYQURAN; TABATABAI]
- 12:43/q27/p2 All dreams lack real or scientific connection to external events. — Al-Tabataba'i attributes this to European natural scientists and rejects the universal claim: bodily and emotional causes do not account for sound dreams. He notes some psychologists study dreams. [0 notes: TABATABAI-]

## 12:43/q28 How can a true dream disclose hidden realities, and when does it need interpretation?  [رُءْيَٰىَ · theology]
- 12:43/q28/p1 The sleeping soul connects with higher causes; images may directly show truth or symbolize it. — Al-Tabataba'i distinguishes physical changing matter, an imaginal realm of immaterial forms causing physical events, and an intellectual realm of universal causes. A strong soul can see universal causes directly; otherwise it clothes them in familiar images. Clear dreams need no interpretation, symbolic images or opposites such as crown/honor and darkness/ignorance do, and tangled transformations resist interpretation. [6 notes: TABATABAI]

## 12:43/q29 What does it mean to 'cross' or interpret a dream?  [تَعْبُرُونَ · meaning]
- 12:43/q29/p1 It is to pass from dream images to their eventual meaning or outcome. — Al-Zajjaj, al-Azhari, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Kashshaf, Abu Hayyan, al-Nasafi, al-Alusi, al-Tabataba'i, and Study Quran derive the sense from crossing a river to the far bank; al-Azhari says the interpreter examines both sides and traces beginning to end. Al-Baydawi calls it passage to corresponding psychic meaning, al-Alusi external or inward reality; ta'wil names the destination. Al-Samin cites al-Kashshaf's gloss. The author of al-Nazm compares adjectival usage in 4:90 and 3:7, and replacement of verb by adjective in 7:193. [16 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(الأزهري,صاحب النظم); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(الأزهري); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]

## 12:43/q30 Which verb form for interpreting dreams is established?  [تَعْبُرُونَ · meaning]
turns on: Established linguistic usage versus the Bedouin poetic attestation of the doubled form.
- 12:43/q30/p1 The light, ungeminated 'abara is the established and stronger usage. — Al-Kashshaf and al-Nasafi say authorities reject the doubled verb, ta'bir, and mu'abbir; al-Samin transmits al-Kashshaf, and Ibn Ashur cites his preference. Al-Alusi agrees on the lighter form's strength but acknowledges evidence for the doubled form. [5 notes: ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL+; NASAFI-FULL+; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري)+]
- 12:43/q30/p2 The doubled form is attested, although some reject it. — Al-Mubarrad cites a Bedouin line using dream-interpreter wording; Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, al-Alusi, and Ibn Ashur transmit it. Al-Alusi says the light form remains more established. [5 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL(المبرد); IBNASHUR-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL-; NASAFI-FULL-; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري)-]

## 12:43/q31 What work does the lam in 'li-l-ru'ya' do?  [لِلرُّءْيَا · grammar]
turns on: Whether lam directly marks an object, strengthens fronting, gives direction, or belongs to a different underlying predication or verb.
- 12:43/q31/p1 It clarifies or specifies the object of interpretation. — Al-Zajjaj and transmitters gloss 'if you interpret, namely the dream'; al-Samin compares 12:20 and proposes 'I mean the dream.' Al-Kashshaf, al-Nasafi, and al-Baydawi also permit this analysis. [9 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL(الزجاج); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان)]
- 12:43/q31/p2 It emphasizes or strengthens a fronted object and weakened delayed verb. — Ibn al-Anbari calls it emphatic; Ibn Atiyya links it to fronting. Abu Hayyan prefers strengthening when the object precedes, not when it follows. Al-Samin says such additions normally need fronting or a derived governor, noting exceptions or containment with 11:107 and 27:72. Ibn Ashur adds foregrounding the dream and cadence. [11 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن الأنباري); IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن الأنباري); KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; IBNASHUR-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان)+]
- 12:43/q31/p3 It means 'toward,' directing interpretation to the dream. — Ibn al-Anbari offers this as an alternative. [2 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن الأنباري); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن الأنباري)]
- 12:43/q31/p4 It is a lam of attachment linking interpreters to the vision. — Ahmad ibn Yahya gives the sense 'interpreters of the dream.' [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أحمد بن يحيى)]
- 12:43/q31/p5 It belongs to an understood or included verb meaning undertake the dream's interpretation. — Al-Kashshaf suggests volunteering for the task; al-Samin says ta'burun contains a verb governing lam; Abu Hayyan reports al-Zamakhshari's understood verb; al-Baydawi allows a lam-taking verb. Al-Alusi reports an eagerness reading. [5 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان)]
- 12:43/q31/p6 The phrase with lam is the predicate of kana, denoting competence in visions. — Al-Kashshaf, al-Razi, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, and al-Samin report this; ta'burun is another predicate or a circumstantial clause. Al-Alusi finds these alternatives strained. [5 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان)-]

## 12:43/q32 Why is 'for the dream' placed before 'interpret'?  [لِلرُّءْيَا تَعْبُرُونَ · rhetoric]
- 12:43/q32/p1 It foregrounds the dream and maintains the verse's cadence. — Ibn Ashur says the definite article is generic; grammarians also link the position to the strengthening lam. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 12:43/q33 What is the predicate of 'if you are' in this phrase?  [إِن كُنتُمْ لِلرُّءْيَا تَعْبُرُونَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether ta'burun alone completes kana or the prepositional phrase predicates interpretive competence.
- 12:43/q33/p1 Ta'burun is the straightforward predicate of kuntum. — Abu Hayyan prefers the apparent syntax over al-Zamakhshari's more complex parses. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL+]
- 12:43/q33/p2 Li-l-ru'ya is the predicate, with ta'burun a second predicate or circumstance. — Al-Zamakhshari's analysis is reported by al-Razi, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, and al-Samin; it conveys competence in dreams. ## 12:43/q34 Why did the court fail to interpret the king's dream?  [أَفْتُونِى فِى رُءْيَٰىَ إِن كُنتُمْ لِلرُّءْيَا تَعْبُرُونَ · links]
- 12:43/q34/p1 They called it confused dreams and said it exceeded their interpretive skill. — Al-Suddi and Ibn Abi Hatim describe consulted specialists. Al-Razi says they did not deny dream interpretation itself and implied a more skilled interpreter might succeed. Al-Qurtubi and al-Durr report the dismissal. Al-Razi likens a confused dream to a bundle of unrelated plants; al-Harawi derives adghath from bundled grass. Ata and Ibn Abbas call such dreams false, Mujahid a jumble or frightening terrors, Abu Ubayda uninterpretable. Al-Tabataba'i distinguishes tangled transformations from clearer symbolic dreams. [15 notes: TAB-FULL(السدي); IBNABIHATIM(السدي); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(أبو عبيدة,الضحاك عن ابن عباس,الهروي,عطاء,مجاهد); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,مجاهد); TABATABAI]

## 12:43/q35 How does the king's dream connect to Joseph's story and other dream reports?  [* · links]
turns on: These are distinct links drawn from the narrative rather than competing explanations.
- 12:43/q35/p1 A dream begins Joseph's ordeal and another brings release and eventual vindication. — Al-Nasafi draws the symmetry; al-Tabataba'i says the passage leads to release, rule, and renewed investigation proving innocence. He lists Jacob's explanation of Joseph's dream, Joseph's own and the prisoners' and king's dreams, and waking predictions taught by God, citing 12:4, 12:36, and 12:43. Islahi traces the prisoners' dreams to the opportunity to interpret the king's. [5 notes: NASAFI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 12:43/q35/p2 The story illustrates God's unexpected direction of human affairs. — Asad contrasts Genesis' romantic telling with the Qur'anic lesson that people can dislike what benefits them and love what harms them, citing 2:216; Islahi says God's plans generate their own means. [3 notes: ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BIQAI-FULL]
- 12:43/q35/p3 Royal dream consultation was a recognized Egyptian and ancient court practice. — Ibn Ashur says priestly rules made dream interpretation an Egyptian science, cites the prisoners' request in 12:36 and the Torah's Pharaoh summoning magicians and sages; he reports Kisra summoning the soothsayer Satih after an alarming dream near the Prophet's birth, through Abd al-Masih. [3 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
