# 2:266: 36 questions

## 2:266/q01 What does the opening question convey?  [أَيَوَدُّ أَحَدُكُمْ · rhetoric]
- 2:266/q01/p1 It repudiates the prospect: no one would wish such a loss. — Al-Razi says the wish to avoid calamity is especially intense; Abu Hayyan says ahadukum distributes the question to each person; Bursavi distinguishes denying a wished prospect from denying an existing act, with two Arabic examples; Ibn Ashur compares the repudiating question at 49:12. Al-Biqai stresses the initially attractive orchard. [10 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 2:266/q02 What does the verb ayawaddu mean here?  [أَيَوَدُّ · meaning]
- 2:266/q02/p1 It means would love or wish. — Al-Razi defines wudd as complete love; al-Tabatabai adds an element of wishing. Jishumi relates wudud, love and will, distinguishing future-directed love from mawadda, which can concern past or future. [5 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL; JISHUMI]

## 2:266/q03 How is the opening verb read?  [أَيَوَدُّ · readings]
turns on: Which verb expresses the wish.
- 2:266/q03/p1 The transmitted wording is ayawaddu, would wish. — Al-Alusi contrasts a report of Umar's alternate wording. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 2:266/q03/p2 Umar is reported to have read yuhibbu, would love. — Al-Alusi reports the reading. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:266/q04 What is the janna in the scene?  [جَنَّةٌ · meaning]
turns on: Whether janna itself names the vegetation or the land containing it.
- 2:266/q04/p1 A densely planted orchard or its covering trees. — Al-Tabrisi and al-Tabatabai explain that dense growth shades and covers the earth; al-Mawardi calls it an orchard. Bursavi and al-Alusi say taking it as trees lets rivers run beneath them directly; al-Alusi prefers this. Al-Tabatabai compares 23:50 and other gardens with water below. [7 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI; BURSEVI; TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 2:266/q04/p2 The land containing the trees. — Bursavi and al-Alusi say an omitted reference to trees is then needed to explain rivers beneath it; al-Alusi finds the tree sense more fitting. [2 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:266/q05 What variant is reported for janna?  [جَنَّةٌ · readings]
turns on: Singular or plural noun.
- 2:266/q05/p1 A singular garden. — The verse wording contrasted with al-Hasan's plural reading. [1 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL]
- 2:266/q05/p2 Al-Hasan reads jannat, gardens, in the plural. — Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan transmit it; al-Kashshaf also notes a gardens reading. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحسن); KASHSHAF-FULL]

## 2:266/q06 What do nakhil and anab denote and how are they formed?  [نَّخِيلٍ أَعْنَابٍ · meaning]
- 2:266/q06/p1 Nakhil denotes date palms, as a collective noun or plural; anab denotes grapes, and anab is plural of inaba. — Abu Hayyan and al-Samin give both analyses of nakhil and record inba as the singular of grapes, with inba as a further extended form; al-Alusi reports the same morphology. Al-Tabrisi says nakhla is a palm and can be masculine or feminine, citing 69:7 and 54:20; al-Raghib derives nakhil from selected or sifted trees and notes its pollination and uses. [7 notes: TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الراغب); ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:266/q07 Why are palms and vines named before the other fruits?  [نَّخِيلٍ أَعْنَابٍ كُلِّ ٱلثَّمَرَٰتِ · rhetoric]
- 2:266/q07/p1 They are the finest, most useful or prized core trees; the garden also contains other fruit. — Al-Mawardi notes flowing water as the orchard's best feature; al-Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Bursavi, Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi cite the trees' excellence. Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi say the whole palm is useful whereas the vine's chief benefit is its grapes, explaining the switch from tree to fruit. Al-Biqai says naming them specifies an earlier generic garden, 2:265; al-Harali likens the useful palm to a believer and the spreading vine to a pious believer. [17 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL(الحرالي); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 2:266/q08 What does min in min nakhil do?  [مِّن نَّخِيلٍۢ وَأَعْنَابٍۢ · grammar]
- 2:266/q08/p1 It explains the orchard's predominant type or contents without excluding other plants. — Al-Tabrisi calls min explanatory. Al-Tabatabai cites the subsequent all fruits as proof that palms and vines are not exhaustive. Al-Samin parses min nakhil as a nominative adjective of janna. [3 notes: TABRISI; TABATABAI; SAMIN-DURR]

## 2:266/q09 What does the phrase about rivers beneath the garden describe?  [تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ · grammar]
turns on: The antecedent of the pronoun and the syntactic role of the river clause.
- 2:266/q09/p1 It describes the garden, with beneath referring to its trees. — Ibn Atiyya explicitly identifies the trees. Al-Samin permits a nominative adjectival clause or an accusative circumstantial clause from the described garden. Al-Tabrisi permits the garden as antecedent; al-Tabatabai explains water runs beneath dense tree growth. Al-Tabrisi defines anhar as broad channels of flowing water and adds a lexical aside that tahut means people low under others' feet and unnoticed. Jishumi reports the trees as one possible referent. Al-Samarrai stresses that the rivers certainly flow, while the idea of gardens themselves flowing is not established by ordinary experience, even if possible in the afterlife. [8 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; SAMIN-DURR; TABATABAI; BURSEVI; JISHUMI; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 2:266/q09/p2 It may describe the date palms, with the pronoun returning to them. — Al-Tabrisi supplies this alternative antecedent. [1 notes: TABRISI]
- 2:266/q09/p3 It may be the predicate of takuna. — Al-Samin reports this parse from Makki. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR]
- 2:266/q09/p4 The streams may flow beneath the garden's buildings. — Jishumi reports buildings as another possible referent for beneath. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 2:266/q10 What do the running rivers add to the image?  [تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the detail is treated as orchard abundance or as a Paradise echo.
- 2:266/q10/p1 They make the orchard fertile, valuable and secure, making its loss more devastating. — Al-Mawardi calls water the best orchard feature; al-Razi says rivers and diverse produce complete its beauty and utility. Al-Biqai contrasts this irrigated orchard with the rain-fed ridge of 2:265, noting that irrigation failure is costlier. Jishumi rehearses the flowing streams and every kind of fruit as part of the attractive scene. Al-Samarrai compares 47:15, where flow needs no separate assurance that river water is not stale. [5 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; JISHUMI; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 2:266/q10/p2 Running rivers also recall the recurring Paradise image. — Study Quran cites 2:25; al-Tabatabai compares 23:50 and gardens with water below. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN; TABATABAI]

## 2:266/q11 What does every fruit mean here?  [مِن كُلِّ ٱلثَّمَرَٰتِ · meaning]
turns on: Whether thamarat names produce or the garden's benefits, and whether all is exhaustive.
- 2:266/q11/p1 Many different kinds of actual fruit, adding to palms and grapes. — Al-Qurtubi, al-Biqai and al-Baydawi take the clause as adding other varieties. Bursavi compares the broad expression in 47:15; al-Samin and al-Akhfash say exhaustive ownership is impossible. Al-Alusi rejects taking it literally as every individual fruit and rejects treating it merely as a generalizing restatement after the two named kinds. Jishumi explicitly describes every kind of fruit alongside the palms and vines. [8 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الأخفش); STUDYQURAN; JISHUMI]
- 2:266/q11/p2 Fruits may stand for all the orchard's benefits. — Al-Kashshaf compares the two gardens in al-Kahf, 18:32 and 18:34; al-Alusi records this interpretation while favoring many actual varieties. [2 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 2:266/q12 How is min kull al-thamarat construed?  [لَهُۥ فِيهَا مِن كُلِّ ٱلثَّمَرَٰتِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether min is licensed as an extra particle and whether all implies literal totality.
- 2:266/q12/p1 An omitted noun such as provision, fruit or food is understood before the prepositional phrase. — Abu Hayyan rejects extra min under Kufan and standard Basran conditions and supplies rizq or thamarat; al-Samin supplies rizq or fakiha, citing al-Nabigha and 37:164; Bursavi compares 37:164. Al-Alusi also gives this parse, with lahu as fronted predicate. Al-Samin notes that a prepositional phrase cannot literally be the subject. In a separate grammatical illustration at 6:100, Jurjani parses a fronted object and says fronting changes the scope of denial; his comparison supplies background for the significance of constituent placement, not an alternative parse of this verse. [8 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; JURJANI-DALAIL]
- 2:266/q12/p2 Min is extra, leaving the sense he has all kinds of fruit. — Al-Akhfash imposes no condition on extra min; al-Samin says all means abundance, since literal totality is impossible. Abu al-Baqa objects under both Sibawayh's and al-Akhfash's analyses if literal totality is implied, but permits it on the abundance sense. Abu Hayyan objects that neither Kufan nor normal Basran conditions are met. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الأخفش); SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء,الأخفش); ALUSI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]

## 2:266/q13 How does the past wording for old age fit the wish construction?  [وَأَصَابَهُ ٱلْكِبَرُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether waw is circumstantial, or the past clause follows an implied law or the sense of the wish.
- 2:266/q13/p1 The waw introduces a circumstantial clause, with qad understood, describing the owner when he has the garden. — Al-Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, al-Razi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, al-Qurtubi and al-Alusi give or permit this analysis. Al-Samin attaches it to ahadukum under yawaddu and compares 2:28 and 3:168; Abu Hayyan calls it the apparent reading. [10 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL]
- 2:266/q13/p2 The past form has future force because wishes take an or law, whose constructions can interchange. — Al-Farra licenses the implied law after wudd and cites 30:51, 4:102, 68:9 and parallel mixing of forms at 52:40; Abu's reading of 4:102 has subjunctive fa-yamilu matching an, while indicative fa-yamiluna follows law. Al-Thalabi cites 3:30 and a poetry line; al-Wahidi cites 2:221, 30:51 and 68:9; al-Samin compares 11:98. Al-Tabari and Ibn al-Jawzi adopt the same account. Al-Alusi reports Abu Hayyan's objection that it would make old age wished, which he finds unconvincing. The Farra heading identifies Arabic and exegetical analysis of this passage. Jishumi transmits al-Farra's explanation that an and law interchange after yawaddu; he also reports Abu al-Hasan that an marks futurity while the sentence bears the sense of law, since wishing may concern past or future. [16 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الفراء وثعلب); RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الفراء); SAMIN-DURR(الفراء); ALUSI-FULL(الفراء، أبو حيان); JISHUMI(الشيخ أبو الحسن,الفراء)]
- 2:266/q13/p3 The clause is coordinated by the sense: would one wish to have a garden and then have old age strike him? — Al-Kashshaf supplies the implicit hypothetical; al-Samin describes conversion from present sense to past. Abu al-Baqa calls this unnecessarily weak, while Abu Hayyan and al-Shaykh object that no one wishes old age itself. Al-Samin replies that the rejected wish is the combined state, not old age alone. [8 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري)+; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء,الشيخ)-]

## 2:266/q14 Why say old age struck him, and what does old age imply?  [وَأَصَابَهُ ٱلْكِبَرُ · rhetoric]
- 2:266/q14/p1 It stresses the owner's reduced earning power and increased need, making the loss irreparable; asabahu makes the impact vivid. — Al-Mawardi adds that old age can erase memory of youthful labor, weaken hope and increase regret. Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Razi, al-Bursavi, al-Tabatabai and Ibn Ashur stress inability to earn and the dependents' needs; al-Tabatabai contrasts young owners and elders with other support. Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi note the force of repeated asaba; Abu Hayyan compares the neighboring garden and stone examples, 2:264-265. Ibn Ashur cites al-Asha's poem for anticipation of harvest; al-Biqai emphasizes their lost wealth. Ibn Qutayba reports Ibn Abbas and others that age and weak children make the orchard indispensable exactly when it is lost; Jishumi adds that the family is left poor, helpless, needy and bewildered. Al-Farra notes the Arabic allowance for repeating a form to emphasize, among other purposes. [25 notes: MUQATIL; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; KASHSHAF-FULL(الحسن); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ELMALILI; FARRA; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL(ابن عباس وغيره); JISHUMI]

## 2:266/q15 What does al-kibar mean?  [ٱلْكِبَرُ · meaning]
- 2:266/q15/p1 Old age, a kind of greatness in age rather than multitude. — Abu Hayyan glosses it as old age. Al-Tabrisi distinguishes kabir, size or age even of one house, from kathir, which necessarily implies number. Damghani confirms old age here, comparing 28:23 and 12:78; Jishumi specifies gray hair. Ibn al-Jawzi's lexical survey says kabir is relational and elsewhere signifies might, intensity, burden, number or seniority in judgment, whereas this passage uses advanced age. [12 notes: TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; DAMGHANI; JISHUMI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أهل التفسير)]

## 2:266/q16 Who are the weak offspring, and why mention them?  [وَلَهُۥ ذُرِّيَّةٌۭ ضُعَفَآءُ · interpretation]
- 2:266/q16/p1 Young, helpless dependents cannot work, support him or restore the orchard, intensifying his care and the loss. — Ibn Abbas calls them part of the parable. Al-Mawardi and Ibn al-Jawzi note a parent's special tenderness; al-Nasafi calls the garden their livelihood too. Al-Tabatabai says an elder with strong children could rely on them, unlike this owner. Ibn Ashur says daif can mean young, limited or poor, citing 2:282 and Abu Khalid al-Attabi's verse; Abu Hayyan also allows needy dependents. Bursavi and al-Alusi stress inability to earn. Damghani glosses daif as unable to manage or devise an escape, while distinguishing that sense from believers at Uhud who were not helpless fighters. Jishumi says the children cannot support themselves and that the loss leaves them poor and bewildered. [23 notes: MUQATIL; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; DAMGHANI; JISHUMI]

## 2:266/q17 What forms are reported for the plural weak?  [ضُعَفَآءُ · readings]
turns on: Equivalent plural forms and their readings.
- 2:266/q17/p1 Duafa is the verse form, a regular plural of daif. — Al-Akhfash compares fail plurals such as zurafa and zuraf. Al-Tabari compares 4:9; Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi recognize both plural patterns. Jishumi identifies duafa as a plural of daif and compares karim/kurama; al-Burhan points to the different plural forms for vulnerable offspring here and in the warning about children. [8 notes: AKHFASH; TAB-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; JISHUMI; BURHAN]
- 2:266/q17/p2 Di'af or du'af is another regular plural form or variant reading. — Al-Kashshaf notes a weak offspring reading; al-Tabari cites 4:9 for diafa, while al-Akhfash, Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi give the alternate plural patterns. Jishumi distinguishes duaf as a plural of daf from duafa as a plural of daif; al-Burhan contrasts the plural wording with the other warning about vulnerable children. [9 notes: AKHFASH; TAB-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; JISHUMI; BURHAN]

## 2:266/q18 How is the clause about offspring attached?  [وَلَهُۥ ذُرِّيَّةٌۭ ضُعَفَآءُ · grammar]
- 2:266/q18/p1 It is circumstantial, describing the owner when old age strikes. — Ibn Atiyya and al-Nasafi take the waw as circumstantial; al-Samin derives the circumstance from the pronoun in asabahu. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]

## 2:266/q19 What is an i'sar?  [إِعْصَارٌۭ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the word itself is a general rotating wind, a hot samum, or a cold blast.
- 2:266/q19/p1 A fierce, rotating wind or whirlwind rising like a column, often lifting dust. — Al-Tabari gives its plural a'asir and cites Yazid ibn Mufarrigh; al-Zajjaj calls it zawba'a and cites an unnamed poet. Al-Thalabi cites al-Kumayt and Yazid ibn al-Muqri; Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Razi, al-Tabrisi, al-Samin and Ibn Abbas in al-Durr cite poetry or the wind proverb. Al-Jawhari adds umm zawba'a and says the name also belongs to a jinn chief. Al-Alusi describes colliding air currents, possible upward or downward motion and force enough to uproot trees or snatch boats. Al-Tabatabai describes a twisting dust column between earth and sky. Damghani says the fierce wind can be cold or hot. Jishumi describes its column rising from ground to sky, or a wind that raises clouds. Al-Itqan cites an anonymous poetic line and gives the singular-plural pair isar/aasir. Bint al-Shati cites Ibn Abbas's fierce-wind gloss and poem, al-Jawhari's dust-pillar or thundercloud, al-Tabari's comparable gloss and al-Raghib's dust-stirring wind; she finds the severe-wind reading suited to the root. [41 notes: TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ(الشاعر); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); THALABI(الكميت,يزيد بن المقرع الحميري); MAWARDI-FULL(السدي); WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(الزجاج); TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الجوهري,الزجاج); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزجاج); SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(الأصفهاني); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI(الصادق عليه السلام); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); JISHUMI; ITQAN; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن عباس,الراغب); BINTSHATI-IJAZ+; DAMGHANI+]
- 2:266/q19/p2 It is specifically the deadly hot wind called samum. — Al-Suddi gives this gloss through al-Mawardi; Ibn Atiyya calls the whirlwind violent and scorching. Ibn Zayd and al-Suddi in al-Qurtubi describe the hot wind. Ibn Ashur identifies a violent hot wind likened to fire or samum. Bint al-Shati reports al-Tabari's transmission from Ibn Abbas that the fierce wind carries scorching heat, including samum. [5 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(السدي); IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن زيد); IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن عباس)]
- 2:266/q19/p3 It is a cold blast or frost. — Al-Hasan gives this second gloss in Ibn Abi Hatim; al-Hasan and al-Dahhak elsewhere describe cold within the whirlwind. [3 notes: IBNABIHATIM(الحسن); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن بن أبي الحسن,الضحاك)]

## 2:266/q20 Why is the whirlwind called i'sar?  [إِعْصَارٌۭ · meaning]
turns on: Which observed squeezing or twisting motivates the word; Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi disagree about the cloth derivation.
- 2:266/q20/p1 Its coiling motion resembles cloth being wrung. — Al-Mawardi cites a poem; al-Mahdawi gives the derivation in Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi. Ibn Atiyya calls it weak, while al-Qurtubi endorses it as observable; al-Samin, al-Alusi and al-Tabatabai also mention twisting cloth. [7 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(المهدوي); QURTUBI-FULL(المهدوي); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; QURTUBI-FULL+; IBNATIYYA-FULL-]
- 2:266/q20/p2 The wind squeezes or compresses clouds or bodies. — Ibn Atiyya cites the wind proverb and relates mu'sirat either to pregnant rain clouds or clouds compressed by winds; he reports al-Anbari and Ibn Sida's alternatives, the latter taking mu'sirat as winds. Al-Qurtubi repeats the derivations. Abu Hayyan describes a hot cloud-compressing wind; al-Harali links the name to squeezing hidden contents. Bint al-Shati derives the asr forms from pressure and extraction, exemplified by grape pressing, rain-producing clouds and the wind driving them. She notes the whirlwind form occurs once in the Quran and compares related forms in Yusuf, al-Naba and al-Asr; she takes the strong-wind sense to fit that root. [13 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن سيده,عبيد الله بن الحسن العنبري); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزجاج); BIQAI-FULL(الحرالي); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; BINTSHATI-IJAZ; BINTSHATI-IJAZ+]

## 2:266/q21 What is the fire in the whirlwind?  [فِيهِ نَارٌۭ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the destructive fire is heat, cold, actual flame, lightning, or Hell's breath.
- 2:266/q21/p1 Scorching samum or exceptionally hot wind. — Ibn Abbas repeatedly calls it samum; Qatada calls the wind a carrier of it and al-Suddi distinguishes wind from its fire. Al-Tabari also transmits Ibn Abbas that jinn-creating samum is one seventieth of fire; Ibn Atiyya attributes this proportion to Ibn Masud and identifies the Fire as the afterlife's, while Abu Hayyan repeats it. Ibn Ashur says the wind is emphatically likened to fire. Al-Alusi says the indefinite fire magnifies it. Jishumi reports a scorching wind that burns the fruit; Bint al-Shati reports Ibn Abbas's fierce wind carrying samum through al-Tabari. [18 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,السدي,قتادة); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس,ابن مسعود,السدي); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن زيد); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL; JISHUMI; BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن عباس)]
- 2:266/q21/p2 Biting cold, frost or hail that burns and ruins the garden. — Al-Hasan gives bitter cold in Abd al-Razzaq and severe cold and hail in al-Tabari; al-Dahhak agrees. Al-Harali calls a cold edge a kind of fire. [9 notes: ABDURRAZZAQ(الحسن); TAB-FULL(الحسن,الضحاك); IBNABIHATIM(الحسن); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الحسن بن أبي الحسن,الضحاك); QURTUBI-FULL(الحسن); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحسن والضحاك); BIQAI-FULL(الحرالي)]
- 2:266/q21/p3 It may be literal fire carried by the wind. — Ibn Atiyya allows literal fire, and al-Tabrisi states that the wind's fire burns the garden. Jishumi also reports the explanation of a fierce wind containing fire. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; BURSEVI; JISHUMI]
- 2:266/q21/p4 It may be lightning. — Abu Hayyan records this alternate explanation. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 2:266/q22 How is fihi nar parsed?  [فِيهِ نَارٌۭ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the locative itself modifies the whirlwind or belongs to a modifying clause.
- 2:266/q22/p1 Fihi is a modifying locative phrase and nar is its raised subject. — Al-Nasafi says the locative functions as an adjective of i'sar. Abu Hayyan says the masculine pronoun agrees with masculine i'sar. Al-Samin prefers this direct adjectival parse for simplicity. [4 notes: NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 2:266/q22/p2 Fihi is the predicate of nar, and the entire clause modifies i'sar. — Abu Hayyan and al-Samin allow this analysis; al-Samin prefers the simpler direct locative adjective. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]

## 2:266/q23 What do the successive fa and burning verb convey?  [فَأَصَابَهَآ فَٱحْتَرَقَتْ · grammar]
- 2:266/q23/p1 The storm strikes after the garden has become useful, then the garden burns up as the intransitive result of burning it. — Abu Hayyan reads the successive fa as sequence after ripening; he and al-Samin distinguish intransitive ihtaraqa from transitive ahraqa. Al-Samin gives the burning then burned-up sequence; both cite the expression harqa nab and a poetic line with nominative or accusative nab. Ibn Abi Hatim reports Ibn Abbas's direct gloss that the garden itself burned. Ibn Kathir says fruit and trees were consumed. Abu al-Baqa instead coordinates the storm clause with the earlier adjectival garden description. Jishumi explicitly distinguishes causative ahraqa from resultant intransitive ihtaraqa and says the whole garden and its contents burn. [9 notes: IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء); IBNKATHIR-FULL; JISHUMI]

## 2:266/q33 How is the wider spending passage organized?  [* · links]
- 2:266/q33/p1 It forms a connected teaching on the purpose, manner, quality, recipient and reward of giving. — Al-Tabatabai says the connected themes suggest revelation as one unit: seven-hundredfold or greater growth, sincere fruitfulness, loss through ostentation or harm, giving good wealth, aid to the needy detained in God's path, and great reward. ## 2:266/q34 How does the extended garden image give the warning force?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the source emphasizes the scene's accumulated particulars or general principles of figurative assertion.
- 2:266/q34/p1 Its accumulated details complete the illustrative comparison and make the loss feel inescapably severe. — Al-Itqan calls the sequence from crops and rivers through aged owner, weak offspring, fiery storm and burning exhaustive elaboration, eliminating imagined escapes; it reports Ibn Abi al-Isba's distinction between completion, supplementation and exhaustive elaboration. Ibn Ashur calls the scene an extended illustrative simile that magnifies the loss. [3 notes: ITQAN(ابن أبي الإصبع); IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 2:266/q34/p2 A figure gains persuasive force through a shared quality and through an asserted meaning supported by an observable sign. — In general discussions of figuration, Jurjani compares sewing with chainmail making, faces with suns and brave men with lions to distinguish shared qualities from surface labels; his courage example distinguishes a quality from every outward act. He says figures depend on interpreted meaning, whereas plain glosses lose their force; representational merit lies in how a quality is asserted, and kinaya argues from a sign. His lion example contrasts certain, inherent courage with mere possibility. [8 notes: JURJANI-ASRAR; JURJANI-DALAIL]

## 2:266/q36 What background does al-Alusi give about composing the commentary cited for this verse?  [* · reports]
