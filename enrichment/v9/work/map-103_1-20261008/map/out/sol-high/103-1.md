# 103:1: 23 questions

## 103:1/q01 What does al-ʿaṣr in the oath designate?  [ٱلْعَصْرِ · referent]
turns on: Whether the unqualified word is inclusive or specifies a time of day, a prayer, a paired period, or a marked era; arguments use Arabic usage, poetry, report, context and analogous oaths.
- 103:1/q01/p1 Time or an age broadly, encompassing its changing periods and events. — Al-Tabari declines restriction without textual proof; al-Nahhas and Elmalılı likewise allow several Arabic senses. Ibn Abbas and others identify an age; Imru al-Qays and Ibn al-Sikkit's poetry attest the usage. Farāhī and Asad distinguish fleeting ʿaṣr from dahr; Cuypers calls it a general span without further grounds. Ibn Kathir calls this the well-known view. Bint al-Shati follows al-Tabari, Ibn Qayyim and most exegetes; Kur'an Yolu finds this best suited to the sūra. Al-Razi questions treating a reported expanded wording as Qur'an because that would invalidate prayer. Elmalılı cites a poet likening unseen time's motion toward death to a ship. Al-Damghani glosses al-ʿaṣr as time or its passage; al-Suyuti includes time as a whole among the oath's possible referents. [74 notes: FARRA; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; NAHHAS; THALABI(ابن عباس); MAWARDI-FULL(ابن عباس، زيد بن أسلم); QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن السكيت,ابن عباس,الفراء); WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(ابن عباس والكلبي وأبو علي); BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن عباس); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن عباس); TABRISI(ابن عباس، الكلبي، الجبائي); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس وغيره,الشاعر); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR-FULL(مالك عن زيد بن أسلم); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); ELMALILI(Râzî); TABATABAI(unnamed view); ASAD-NOTES; BINTSHATI(ابن القيم,الرازي); CORPUSCORANICUM(Cuypers); ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Im ām Far āhī,Ḥam īd al-Dīn Far āhī); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(müfessirler); STUDYQURAN; DAMGHANI; ITQAN; BINTSHATI(ابن قيم الجوزية,الطبري)+; ELMALILI(İbn Cerîr)+; JISHUMI(علي)+; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR+; TAB-FULL+; ALUSI-FULL-; CORPUSCORANICUM-]
- 103:1/q01/p2 Late afternoon, the day's declining period from after noon toward sunset. — Al-Hasan and Qatada give the afternoon interval; al-Mawardi cites poetry, while Ibn Ashur defines it by shadow length and al-Harith ibn Hilliza's verse. The declining sun and approaching night signify divine power (Tabrisi, Nasafi, Qatada), and the closing market images judgment (Razi from Hasan). Robinson connects merchants' reckoning of earnings with verse 2's loss. Corpus Coranicum favors a specific daytime by analogy with other opening oaths, though observes late afternoon is unusual among early Meccan oaths. Ubayy's Prophetic report says God swears by day's end. Durr also reports Ibn Abbas and Qatada saying an hour of daytime; Tustari records 'afternoon or time itself'. Al-Suyuti also gives the day's latter part as the oath's referent. - 103:1/q01/p3 The afternoon prayer, the middle prayer distinguished by special merit. — Muqatil calls it the middle and best prayer. Hafsa's 2:238 recension and a reported reading of 5:106 are cited by Zamakhshari, Razi and others; Qurtubi cites an authentic identifying report. A Prophetic report likens missing it to losing family and property. Its hour conflicts with trade and livelihood, and Razi says it closes daily worship as repentance closes deeds. Razi also cites a report about a false oath after Asr and explains that God swears by His prescribed noble command. Alusi rejects Razi's sinful-woman story as unreliable. Neuwirth links prayer-time oaths to liturgical experience, though Corpus Coranicum disputes the proposed hymn tone. - 103:1/q01/p4 The Prophet's era, honored by renewed prophethood and his community. — Mawardi and Qurtubi cite renewed prophethood. Razi and Elmalılı invoke the workers' parable: the final workers labor from Asr to sunset and receive double pay; compare oaths by the Prophet's place and life (90:2; 15:72). Elmalılı describes the mission, Hijra, victory, and extension of Muhammad's community to the end of time; the afternoon of human history follows earlier ages. Tabataba'i favors this because the next verses treat truth and perseverance. Bursevi cites a report of a day or half-day for the community and Najmiyya reports of the Prophet's primordial status. [35 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; RAZI-FULL(النبي); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية,رسول الله); ALUSI-FULL(قيل); ELMALILI; BINTSHATI(الرازي); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(müfessirler); STUDYQURAN(some); ELMALILI+; TABATABAI+]
- 103:1/q01/p5 Night and day together, called the two ʿaṣrs. — Ibn Kaysan and others give the pair; Humayd ibn Thawr's poem uses the dual for day and night, as cited by Tha'labi, Wahidi, Qurtubi and others. [16 notes: THALABI(ابن كيسان); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن السكيت,ابن كيسان,حميد بن ثور); BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن كيسان); IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبو علي); TABRISI(ابن كيسان); QURTUBI-FULL(حميد بن ثور); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(الزجاج); TABATABAI(unnamed view); CORPUSCORANICUM; JISHUMI(ابن كيسان)]
- 103:1/q01/p6 The final hour of daytime, near sunset. — Qatada specifies the last hour, while Abu Muslim calls it one end of the day; this narrower reading is distinguished from the whole interval after noon. [8 notes: THALABI(قتادة); WAHIDI-BASIT(قتادة); JISHUMI(أبو مسلم); BAGHAWI-FULL(قتادة); QURTUBI-FULL(قتادة); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); ALUSI-FULL(قتادة); STUDYQURAN]
- 103:1/q01/p7 Morning and evening together, called the two ʿaṣrs. — Ibn al-Sikkit's and other poetic witnesses use the dual for morning and evening; Ibn Atiyya calls them the two cooler parts of day. [8 notes: THALABI(ابن كيسان); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن السكيت); IBNATIYYA-FULL(بعض العلماء); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(قيل); CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 103:1/q01/p8 The Lord of Time, implied as the object of the oath. — Baghawi cites comparable oaths and Qurtubi supplies the omitted 'Lord of the age'. [3 notes: TUSTARI(قيل); BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); QURTUBI-FULL]
- 103:1/q01/p9 The time of Adam's creation on Friday afternoon. — Biqai invokes a sound report in Muslim; Alusi reports this from Qatada. Elmalılı ties late creation to the world's closing day. [3 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(قتادة); ELMALILI]
- 103:1/q01/p10 Each person's lifetime, the finite capital in which one acts. — Biqai includes each individual life. Elmalılı's poems picture each passing day or breath consuming it; Islahi and Farāhī call it an investment whose melting-ice speed makes present duties urgent. [15 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Im ām Far āhī)]
- 103:1/q01/p11 The era of the Mahdi, when truth prevails fully. — Tabataba'i reports this from unnamed narrations. [1 notes: TABATABAI(unnamed reports)]
- 103:1/q01/p12 Time's vicissitudes or calamities. — Ali's interpretive recitation adds the vicissitudes of time and loss until its end; Jishumi reports this gloss but prefers time itself. [5 notes: TAB-FULL(علي); THALABI(علي); JISHUMI(علي); ELMALILI]

## 103:1/q02 What meanings and derivations does the word ʿaṣr have in Arabic?  [ٱلْعَصْرِ · meaning]
turns on: The sources distinguish its concrete pressing root, wide Arabic uses, bounded temporal force and the definite article's scope.
- 103:1/q02/p1 Its root denotes squeezing or pressing, extended to time, confinement, and the extraction of an essence. — Jishumi describes wringing cloth, grape extract, deferred prayer, rain-bearing clouds and a rising dust column. Tabarsi applies the pressed-events image to time. Bint al-Shati adds press equipment, lineage and the hardship that presses out a person's essence; she cites 12:36, 12:49, 78:14 and 2:266. Biqai connects the sūra name to extracting the saved essence. Bint al-Shati reads human life as pressed and refined by trials, exposing its essence; she cites the root's occurrences in Yusuf's dream and harvest year and the whirlwind in al-Baqara, calls this Qur'anic form unique, and rejects al-Raghib's derivation of temporal ʿaṣr from what remains after squeezing. Her criticism of proposed links in other oaths cautions against assuming a simple analogy for this image. [20 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; ELMALILI; BINTSHATI; STUDYQURAN; BIQAI-FULL; BINTSHATI-IJAZ; BINTSHATI-IJAZ+]
- 103:1/q02/p2 The noun also spans an age, day or night, a part of day, prayer, group and rain. — Elmalılı adds Turkish senses of the current era and eighty or a hundred years and notes vocalizations ʿaṣr, ʿiṣr and ʿuṣr. Wahidi says these temporal senses are valid Arabic usage. [8 notes: NAHHAS; WAHIDI-BASIT; ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; CORPUSCORANICUM]
- 103:1/q02/p3 Unlike dahr's vast or unlimited duration, ʿaṣr marks a bounded, passing period. — Asad describes dahr as unbounded; Elmalılı cites Raghib's range from the universe's beginning to end, and Farāhī stresses swift passage, supported by Imru al-Qays and ʿUbayd ibn al-Abras. Bint al-Shati reports Razi's suggestion that 'asr avoids unbelievers' exalted dahr. [8 notes: ASAD-NOTES; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Im ām Far āhī,Ḥam īd al-Dīn Far āhī); BINTSHATI(الرازي)]
- 103:1/q02/p4 The word's definite article can be generic or point to a particular known time. — Elmalılı says the generic article can gather all senses while the covenant article directs the first hearers to their own era; Ibn Ashur allows contextual indication, addition, or shared reference. [3 notes: ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 103:1/q03 How does the opening oath work, and what does it emphasize?  [وَٱلْعَصْرِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether wāw is explained as a conventional emphatic oath or as time's witness to the proposition.
- 103:1/q03/p1 The wāw introduces an oath whose answer affirms that humanity is in loss. — Jishumi identifies the oath particle and its answer; Elmalılı compares 93:1. Ibn Ashur says an oath confirms the assertion and its object displays God's creation, power and knowledge. Bint al-Shati says wāw magnifies what is sworn by and moves from perceptible reality to moral truth. Corpus Coranicum notes a possible pre-Islamic seer formula, a connection not yet studied adequately. Al-Zarkashi and al-Suyuti count oath openings as the fifth of the sūra-opening modes and list fifteen such sūras; al-Suyuti further classifies al-ʿAṣr as an oath followed by a proposition about human conduct or condition, namely humanity's loss. [10 notes: JISHUMI; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM; BURHAN; ITQAN]
- 103:1/q03/p2 Time itself bears witness to humanity's loss. — Islahi gives wāw this witness force: history shows recompense, while the rapidly spent lifespan makes a person's deeds profitable or ruinous. [4 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Im ām Far āhī)]
- 103:1/q03/p3 A thematic link between an oath's object and its answer must be argued in each case, not assumed from other oaths. — Bint al-Shati questions proposed links between night and day and differing human striving, and between the falling star and the Prophet's freedom from error; these analogies cannot by themselves establish the link in al-ʿAṣr. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ]

## 103:1/q04 Why is time or afternoon singled out for the oath?  [وَٱلْعَصْرِ · interpretation]
turns on: The explanations appeal to divine signs, the day's closure, the prayer's status, the Prophet's era, historical recompense, or the value and moral use of time.
- 103:1/q04/p1 Time displays divine power and wondrous changes, so its oath invites reflection. — Alternating nights and days, shifting light and temperature, health and illness, wealth and poverty, and other events disclose God's power (Razi, Ibn Qayyim, Tabarsi). Razi cites 25:62 and 6:13. Bursevi relates the oath to dawn, sun, moon, night, day and forenoon oaths. Ibn Ashur adds the solar-earth cycle and daily social life. Bint al-Shati cautions against loading the verse with speculative philosophy and cites 28:71–72. [33 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); WAHIDI-BASIT(أهل المعاني); JISHUMI(ابن عباس والكلبي وأبو علي,الحسن وقتادة); KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(ابن عباس، الكلبي، الجبائي,الحسن، قتادة); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس وغيره); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس,قتادة); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(unnamed view); BINTSHATI(ابن القيم,الرازي); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 103:1/q04/p2 Time is precious life capital; wasting it causes loss, while a final good act can gain lasting reward. — Mawardi cites the day's closing acts. Razi says a last moment of repentance can redeem a wasted life (25:62); al-Hasan compares closing markets to Judgment (21:1). Elmalılı cites elegies and poems about breath and passing days; Sufis urge afterlife-oriented use of the moment, while those denying afterlife seek worldly pleasure. Islahi and Farāhī compare time to melting ice and insist present obligations cannot be deferred. [36 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; RAZI-FULL(الحسن); BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Im ām Far āhī); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; BINTSHATI(الرازي,النيسابوري)]
- 103:1/q04/p3 The oath honors time and corrects blaming it for human misfortune. — Razi says Arabs blamed time; Elmalılı cites a poem saying people, not time, are corrupt, and 45:24 on materialists' dahr. Qashani and Bursevi cite 'do not revile time, for God is time'; Qashani says God, not time, acts through it. Muhammad Abdu posits blameworthy afternoon talk, but Bint al-Shati rejects a special gathering hour because talk can occur at any time. [20 notes: RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; QASHANI(النبي عليه السلام); BURSEVI(رسول الله); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; BINTSHATI(الرازي,النيسابوري,محمد عبده); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 103:1/q04/p4 Afternoon closes work and gathers gains, picturing final reckoning and death. — Biqai sees the end of work and beginning of rest; Razi's Abu Muslim pairs morning's resurrection signs with evening's death signs, and his al-Hasan likens it to closing markets. Al-Nisaburi urges a trade without loss before sunset; Robinson links merchants' daily reckoning to verse 2. [10 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; RAZI-FULL(أبو مسلم,الحسن); BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI(النيسابوري); CORPUSCORANICUM(Robinson)]
- 103:1/q04/p5 The afternoon prayer's difficulty and merit make its mention an exhortation to observe it. — The cited middle-prayer readings and missing-prayer report support its rank. Razi says God magnifies a prescribed command, and Alusi objects to the story of the sinful woman. [19 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(رسول الله); RAZI-FULL(النبي,مقاتل); NASAFI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(مقاتل); ELMALILI(Mukātil); BINTSHATI(الزمخشري); STUDYQURAN(many)]
- 103:1/q04/p6 The Prophetic era is the decisive setting in which truth appeared and rejection became especially grave. — Razi uses the last workers' double wage and compares oaths by the Prophet's place and life; Elmalılı traces his mission, revelation, victory and continuing community. Tabataba'i invokes the following verses on truth and patience. [21 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; RAZI-FULL(النبي); BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية,رسول الله); ELMALILI; TABATABAI]
- 103:1/q04/p7 Past peoples' histories bear witness to God's law of recompense. — Farāhī says virtue gained ascendancy and oppression led to destruction after respite; their past eras urge belief and action before time passes. [8 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Im ām Far āhī,Ḥam īd al-Dīn Far āhī)]
- 103:1/q04/p8 Time presses and refines people through hardship, exposing their worth as the following verses distinguish loss from salvation. — Bint al-Shati connects the pressing sense of ʿaṣr to humanity's trials and to the next verses' account of loss and rescue. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ]

## 103:1/q05 How is al-ʿaṣr read?  [ٱلْعَصْرِ · readings]
turns on: Whether the transferred kasra is confined to pause or transmitted in connected recitation.
- 103:1/q05/p1 The received reading keeps ṣād vowelless in pause; Salām's reading gives ṣād kasra by vowel transfer. — Ibn Atiyya limits Salām's al-ʿaṣri and al-ṣabri to pause; Razi attributes the sound to breath or another impediment. Abu al-Fadl says transfer marks inflection and avoids two vowelless letters; Samīn gives poetic examples and also ḍamma transfer. A rajaz line in fī al-ʿaṣr supports the form. [9 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(سلام أبو المنذر); RAZI-FULL(سلام بن المنذر); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية,الراجز,الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR(أبو الفضل,الشاعر)]
- 103:1/q05/p2 Reports also allow the kasra in connected recitation. — Al-Hudhali transmits Abu ʿAmr routes for al-ʿaṣr, al-ṣabr, al-fajr and al-watr in pause and liaison; Ibn Khalawayh does not restrict al-ṣabri to pause. Samīn records an imāla-like variant on bāʾ. [4 notes: SAMIN-DURR(ابن خالويه,الهذلي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية)]

## 103:1/q06 What expanded recitations of the verse are reported?  [وَٱلْعَصْرِ · readings]
turns on: Whether the additions are transmitted interpretations or canonical text, and which expanded wording is attributed to whom.
- 103:1/q06/p1 Ali's recitation names the vicissitudes of time and extends human loss until time's end. — Tabari, Tha'labi and Elmalılı transmit the interpretive expansion with time's calamities; Razi refuses to treat an expanded report as Qur'anic wording because it would invalidate prayer. [4 notes: TAB-FULL(علي); THALABI(علي); ELMALILI]
- 103:1/q06/p2 A recitation attributed to Ali and Ibn Masʿud adds loss until time's end without naming vicissitudes. — Tha'labi gives both attributions; the wording differs from his other Ali report. [2 notes: THALABI(ابن مسعود,علي)]
- 103:1/q06/p3 The standard codices and general reciters have the sound reading. — Tha'labi prefers it; Razi's prayer argument weighs against elevating the expanded wording to Qur'an. [2 notes: THALABI; RAZI-FULL]

## 103:1/q07 How long is an oath not to speak for an ʿaṣr?  [ٱلْعَصْرِ · law]
turns on: The word's range and the role of the swearer's intention.
- 103:1/q07/p1 A year. — Malik adopts the most expansive duration, consistent with his stricter handling of oaths. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(مالك)]
- 103:1/q07/p2 An hour, unless the speaker intended a different duration. — Al-Shafi'i gives an hour. Ibn al-Arabi prefers this, allowing a plausible stated intention for an Arab swearer except the shortest interpretation. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الشافعي); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن العربي)+]

## 103:1/q08 Does the word al-ʿaṣr have a theological implication about who causes events?  [ٱلْعَصْرِ · theology]
turns on: Whether the oath is read as correcting causal blame or as prompting inquiry into time's nature.
- 103:1/q08/p1 God acts through time; time itself does not cause calamity or loss. — Qashani invokes 45:24 and the ungraded 'God is time' report; Bursevi also quotes the prohibition on reviling time. Razi, Alusi and Elmalılı put blame on human conduct rather than the age. [11 notes: RAZI-FULL; QASHANI(النبي عليه السلام); BURSEVI(رسول الله); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; BINTSHATI(الرازي)]
- 103:1/q08/p2 Time's existence is philosophically difficult to characterize. — Razi notes that past and future are absent and the indivisible present resists simple existence claims; Elmalılı recounts philosophers' and theologians' debate. Bint al-Shati records a report likening time to something both existent and like nothing. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL; ELMALILI(Râzî); BINTSHATI(الرازي)]

## 103:1/q09 How does this opening connect with other Qur'anic passages?  [وَٱلْعَصْرِ · links]
turns on: The links draw on the preceding sūra, analogous oaths, parallels to the exception, and the sūra's own later clauses.
- 103:1/q09/p1 It follows al-Takāthur by exposing the loss caused by worldly distraction. — Jishumi says the prior warning against abundance leads to the demand for deeds; Abu Hayyan distinguishes believer and unbeliever. Biqai and Alusi continue the warning of 102; Study Quran cites 102:1 and 102:8 about wasted time and accountable blessings. [7 notes: JISHUMI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 103:1/q09/p2 It complements oaths by the forenoon and other day and night phases. — Zamakhshari, Nasafi and others pair afternoon with 93:1; Razi contrasts prior midmorning rising fortunes with afternoon decline. Bursevi cites oaths in 89, 91, 92 and 93; Corpus Coranicum observes this afternoon oath is unusual among early Meccan oaths. [11 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(الحسن، قتادة); RAZI-FULL(أبو مسلم); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قتادة); BURSEVI; ELMALILI(Katâde); CORPUSCORANICUM; BINTSHATI]
- 103:1/q09/p3 The exception here adds mutual counsel to faith and works mentioned elsewhere. — Samarrai says counsel in truth and patience prevents further loss of reward. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR]
- 103:1/q09/p4 A verse about protected life uses this sūra as a parallel for a generic statement with an exception. — Qurtubi compares the generic definite al-nafs and its legally qualified exception to generic human statements in 103:1–3, citing 70:19 and 70:22. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]
- 103:1/q09/p5 Its message distinguishes those saved from those ruined, framing the whole sūra. — Biqai maps the clauses to knowledge of God and afterlife, worship, doubt and patience, and says preventing harm precedes benefit. Qutb calls faith, work and mutual counsel the one path of success; Study Quran extends the decline image to each generation. Bint al-Shati links the opening's pressing image to the later contrast between human loss and salvation. [13 notes: BIQAI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; STUDYQURAN; BINTSHATI-IJAZ]

## 103:1/q10 Was the sūra revealed in Mecca or Medina?  [* · sciences]
turns on: The authorities' differing attributions; the notes supply no decisive historical test.
- 103:1/q10/p1 It was revealed in Mecca. — Ibn Abbas, Ibn al-Zubayr and most authorities support Mecca (Abu Hayyan, Alusi); Tabataba'i says it seems more likely Meccan, while Study Quran also reports this. Al-Suyuti cites al-Bayhaqi's transmission from Ikrima and al-Hasan naming al-ʿAṣr among the Meccan sūras. [9 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس وابن الزبير والجمهور); IBNKATHIR-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس وابن الزبير والجمهور); QUTB-ZILAL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN; ITQAN(عكرمة والحسن بن أبي الحسن)]
- 103:1/q10/p2 It was revealed in Medina. — Mawardi cites one narration from Ibn Abbas and Qatada; Abu Hayyan and Alusi add Mujahid and Muqatil. Tabataba'i says Medina remains possible. [4 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس وابن الزبير والجمهور); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس وابن الزبير والجمهور); TABATABAI]

## 103:1/q11 How many verses does the sūra have, and where is its first break?  [* · sciences]
turns on: The position of the first verse boundary; the reported total count is three.
- 103:1/q11/p1 It has three verses, with the oath as its own first verse. — Alusi calls the three-verse count undisputed; Neuwirth favors an independent oath in line with other openings. Al-Zarkashi counts al-ʿAṣr as a one-word opening verse by analogy with al-Fajr and al-Ḍuḥā and their rhyme despite different lengths, and notes that a single utterance can form a verse. Al-Suyuti reports scholars adding wa-l-ʿaṣr to standalone verses; the counters other than the later Medinans count this opening separately. [8 notes: ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس وابن الزبير والجمهور); QUTB-ZILAL; CORPUSCORANICUM(Neuwirth); BURHAN; ITQAN(المدني الأخير والباقون,غيره)]
- 103:1/q11/p2 Medina II joins the oath to verse 2 in its division. — Spitaler reports this verse division. Al-Suyuti reports that the later Medinan count instead makes wa-tawāṣaw bi-l-ḥaqq a separate verse while all still total three. Al-Mawsili's scheme distinguishes disagreements in internal breaks from disagreements in totals. [3 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Spitaler); ITQAN(المدني الأخير والباقون,الموصلي)]
- 103:1/q11/p3 Under Abu ʿAmr al-Dani's one-word rule, wa-l-ʿaṣr would not form a standalone verse. — Al-Suyuti reports al-Dani's assertion that mudhāmmatān is the only word that constitutes an āya on its own; the report does not itself supply an alternative three-verse division for al-ʿAṣr. [1 notes: ITQAN(أبو عمرو الداني)]

## 103:1/q12 When in the revelation sequence did this sūra come?  [* · sciences]
- 103:1/q12/p1 It followed shortly after Sūra 94. — Asad states this; Study Quran calls the sequence likely. [2 notes: ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN]
- 103:1/q12/p2 In Jābir ibn Zayd's reported Meccan sequence it is thirteenth, with al-Fajr tenth. — Al-Suyuti transmits Jābir ibn Zayd's order with these numbered placements. [1 notes: ITQAN(جابر بن زيد)]

## 103:1/q13 What does the sūra as a whole teach?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes differ in emphasis between the sūra's moral summary, its extraction imagery, decline, and inward lessons.
- 103:1/q13/p1 It compresses the Qur'an's path of salvation: faith, good works, truth and patience avert universal loss. — Muhammad ibn Kaʿb says belief alone does not suffice; the four qualities follow in order. Sulami lists neglect of God's rights, desires and lack of fear as causes of loss. Qutb calls the sūra a concise Muslim constitution, while Biqai relays Ghazali's six Qur'anic themes and maps the clauses to them. Al-Shafi'i says reflection on this sūra would suffice; Akif's poem repeats its sequence. [26 notes: IBNABIHATIM(محمد بن كعب القرظي); SULAMI(بعضهم); JISHUMI; BIQAI-FULL(الشافعي,الغزالي); QUTB-ZILAL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Mehmet Âkif Ersoy,İmam Şâfiî); STUDYQURAN(al-Shāfiʿī); IBNKATHIR-FULL(الشافعي); ALUSI-FULL(الشافعي)]
- 103:1/q13/p2 Its imagery extracts the enduring saved essence from transient worldly life. — Biqai explains the name through extraction, describes the doomed and saved, and reads the basmala as contrasting perishing things with God's enduring mercy. [8 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 103:1/q13/p3 Each generation falls below its predecessor unless it follows the saving path. — Study Quran applies the decline reading both to individuals and to human history. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN; QUTB-ZILAL]
- 103:1/q13/p4 Affliction drives God's servants toward Him and falls on the spiritually best first. — Qashani describes affliction as God's whip and ranks prophets, saints and others by the order of its incidence. [2 notes: QASHANI]
- 103:1/q13/p5 Hearing, knowing and accompanying God's name call for wealth, self and spirit to be given to it. — Qushayri says hearing yields generosity, knowing ends preference for self, and companionship gives eternal life and the spirit's surrender. [3 notes: QUSHAYRI]

## 103:1/q14 What reports concern reciting or first hearing this sūra?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports describe different occasions and effects.
- 103:1/q14/p1 Two Companions recited it to one another on meeting and exchanged greetings before parting. — Tabarani's report via Abu Madina is cited by Ibn Kathir; Alusi, Elmalılı, Qutb, Kur'an Yolu and Study Quran repeat it. Qutb says it renewed commitment to the guidance. [7 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(عبد الله بن حصن أبو مدينة); ALUSI-FULL(أبو حذيفة); ELMALILI(Ebû Medîne); QUTB-ZILAL(الرجلان من أصحاب رسول الله); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Mehmet Âkif Ersoy); STUDYQURAN]
- 103:1/q14/p2 Before Islam, ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ recited the brief sūra to Musaylima, whose parody failed. — Ibn Kathir describes al-wabr as a small catlike animal with notable ears, says the parody impressed no one, and notes al-Khara'iti transmitted a close version. [3 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(عمرو بن العاص)]
- 103:1/q14/p3 Ubayy recited the sūra to the Prophet and asked him to explain it. — Tha'labi reports the exchange. [1 notes: THALABI(أبي بن كعب)]
- 103:1/q14/p4 At Umar's stabbing, ʿAbd al-Rahman recited al-ʿAṣr and al-Naṣr in Fajr. — Durr transmits the report from Maymun. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(ميمون)]
- 103:1/q14/p5 Its recitation brings patience and companionship with the people of truth at Resurrection. — Jishumi relays this Prophetic promise from Ubayy. [1 notes: JISHUMI(أبي)]

## 103:1/q15 Who do a reported interpretation and pulpit recitation identify with the sūra's clauses?  [* · reports]
- 103:1/q15/p1 Abu Jahl is the person in loss; Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali correspond successively to faith, good works, truth and patience. — Tha'labi attributes the identification to the Prophet and reports Ali ibn Abdullah ibn Abbas repeating it from the Prophet's pulpit. [2 notes: THALABI(النبي,علي بن عبد الله بن عباس)]

## 103:1/q16 What does the sūra's opening suggest about its literary form?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the oath's prayer-time association produces a hymn-like effect.
- 103:1/q16/p1 It is a single-member oath followed by a rebuke, with three rhymes. — Corpus Coranicum describes the structure; Neuwirth supports keeping the oath as an independent verse. Al-Zarkashi groups it with fifteen oath openings; al-Suyuti describes the specific oath-plus-human-condition pattern. Al-Zarkashi also notes the short opening's rhyme analogy with al-Fajr and al-Ḍuḥā. [6 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Neuwirth); BURHAN; ITQAN]
- 103:1/q16/p2 Oaths by prayer times preserve liturgical experience and give a hymn-like tone. — Neuwirth proposes this reading. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Neuwirth); CORPUSCORANICUM-]
- 103:1/q16/p3 The sūra has no hymn-like resonance. — The Corpus Coranicum commentator objects to Neuwirth and refers to critical discussion at 93:1–2. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM+]
- 103:1/q16/p4 Across its three verses, the sūra uniquely has ten wāws among three-verse sūras. — Al-Suyuti cites Ibn Khalawayh's letter-count observation through the end of al-ʿAṣr. [1 notes: ITQAN(ابن خالويه)]

## 103:1/q17 What does the sūra's basmala imply?  [* · interpretation]
- 103:1/q17/p1 Everything perishes except God, whose mercy reaches all and whose complete gifts mark His chosen allies. — Biqai gives this reading in two parallel entries. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 103:1/q18 Why is the loss described as grave?  [* · interpretation]
- 103:1/q18/p1 The servant sins against the Lord despite immense benefaction; the indefinite form can mark a distinctive loss. — Bursevi offers both the moral and rhetorical explanations. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 103:1/q19 Does the sūra contain both abrogating and abrogated material?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether the four-part classification of sūras by abrogation is sound.
- 103:1/q19/p1 It is listed among sūras containing both abrogating and abrogated material. — Al-Zarkashi reports al-ʿAṣr in a list of thirty-one such sūras. [1 notes: BURHAN; ITQAN-]
- 103:1/q19/p2 The four-part classification behind this list is open to question. — Al-Suyuti questions that classification and points to later discussion, without specifying an abrogating or abrogated passage here. [1 notes: ITQAN+]

## 103:1/q20 How is the transition from al-ʿAṣr to the next sūra recited?  [* · readings]
turns on: The transmitted practice of a reader who ordinarily joins or pauses between sūras.
- 103:1/q20/p1 Some readers who generally join sūras make a brief pause at this transition. — Ibn al-Jazari reports a special sakt among several Hamza, Abu ʿAmr, Ibn ʿAmir, Yaʿqub and Warsh readers. He explains that separating the ending wa-tawāṣaw bi-l-ṣabr from the next opening wayl avoids a jarring joined phrase; special sakt presupposes joining elsewhere. [3 notes: NASHR]
- 103:1/q20/p2 Some readers who generally pause insert the basmala at this transition. — Ibn al-Jazari reports this practice from Ibn Ghalbun, the Hidaya author, Makki and others; it separates the same jarring phrase, and the special basmala presupposes pausing elsewhere. [3 notes: NASHR]
- 103:1/q20/p3 Most authorities give this transition no special treatment, and joiners do not insert an untransmitted basmala. — Ibn al-Jazari names Faris ibn Ahmad, Ibn Sufyan, Abu al-Tahir, Iraqi authorities and al-Dani among those making no distinction. He says an optional basmala cannot override explicit transmission for readers who join and that special treatments follow the reader's general joining or pausing practice. [3 notes: NASHR; NASHR+]

## 103:1/q21 Where does al-ʿAṣr stand in the reported codex order of Ibn Masʿūd?  [* · sciences]
- 103:1/q21/p1 It appears among the mufassal sūras after al-Fajr. — Al-Suyuti reports this order for Ibn Masʿūd's codex. [1 notes: ITQAN(عبد الله بن مسعود)]

## 103:1/q22 What principle determines whether the opening of al-ʿAṣr is a separate āya?  [وَٱلْعَصْرِ · sciences]
turns on: Whether verse boundaries can be inferred by analogy and form or require transmitted specification.
- 103:1/q22/p1 An āya is a marked, separate unit of Qur'anic speech, understood through its boundaries or function as a sign. — Al-Suyuti relays al-Jaʿbari's sentence-based definition, another scholar's separated-portion definition, and explanations that the name āya marks a break or signifies proof of the messenger's truth. [4 notes: ITQAN(الجعبري,غيره,قيل)]
- 103:1/q22/p2 The actual verse boundaries are fixed by revelation and prophetic transmission, not inferred from definitions or analogy. — Al-Wahidi says a general definition could include less than a full verse but revelation fixed the divisions; others and al-Zamakhshari insist on transmitted specification. Al-Suyuti also cites a report about thirty verses in al-Ahqaf as evidence used in verse-counting discussion. [4 notes: ITQAN(ابن مسعود,الزمخشري,الواحدي,بعضهم)]
- 103:1/q22/p3 Rhyme and analogy with other one-word openings support counting wa-l-ʿaṣr separately. — Al-Zarkashi compares it with al-Fajr and al-Ḍuḥā despite their differing lengths; he also notes that a word can be a complete single utterance even when short. [2 notes: BURHAN]

## 103:1/q23 What does the general day-night pattern of revelation say about this sūra's timing?  [* · sciences]
- 103:1/q23/p1 Most Qur'anic revelation occurred by day; this report gives no specific time of day for al-ʿAṣr. — Al-Suyuti relays Ibn Habib's general statement and notes that examples of night revelation were collected separately. [1 notes: ITQAN(ابن حبيب)]
