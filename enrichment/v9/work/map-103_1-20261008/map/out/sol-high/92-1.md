# 92:1: 29 questions

## 92:1/q01 What does the night signify here?  [ٱلَّيْلِ · referent]
turns on: Whether the night is read as the physical phenomenon, the natural self, or an inward cosmic experience.
- 92:1/q01/p1 The actual recurring night, marked by darkness, obscurity, and concealment. — Al-Biqāʿī says it gradually obscures what daylight showed and suits secrecy; Elmalılı allows the whole class of nights, and says its darkness follows existing light rather than primordial nonexistence. He also allows a particular terrifying night, literally or figuratively. Al-Biqāʿī adds that night brings confusion and ambiguity to people's circumstances, and that its gradual concealment displays its Creator's majesty and power to efface and bring forth. Ibn ʿĀshūr says night envelops the earth and its creatures in darkness. [6 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; BIQAI; IBNASHUR]
- 92:1/q01/p2 The natural self. — This is Sahl al-Tustarī's inward reading. [1 notes: TUSTARI]
- 92:1/q01/p3 Cosmic and inward darkness that overwhelms perception and may bring the soul from multiplicity into annihilating unity. — Elmalılı describes movement from differentiation to unity, multiplicity to solitude, and witnessing to annihilation; he relates passing away to 55:26–27 and 28:88 and an ensuing abiding witness. [3 notes: ELMALILI]

## 92:1/q02 What does the definite article in ٱلَّيْلِ specify?  [ٱلَّيْلِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the article denotes the class or a specific night.
- 92:1/q02/p1 The whole kind of night. — Elmalılı offers generic reference. [1 notes: ELMALILI]
- 92:1/q02/p2 A particular terrifying night, literally or figuratively. — Elmalılı offers particular reference. [1 notes: ELMALILI]

## 92:1/q03 What does يَغْشَىٰ mean?  [يَغْشَىٰ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the phrase is glossed by covering and concealment or by its resulting darkness.
- 92:1/q03/p1 It covers, veils, or enshrouds, so darkness conceals what was visible. — Ibn Jubayr and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr explain covering and concealment; Qatādah describes creatures encompassed and filled. Al-Naḥḥās compares darkness to a garment and cites al-Nābigha's poem of night pursuing and catching a fugitive. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ traces gh-sh-y through veils, sleep, fainting, dulled sight and insight, rejection in 71:7 and 11:5, the lote tree in 53:16, coverings of senses in 3:154, 8:11, 33:19, 47:20, 2:7 and 45:23, and judgment or punishment in 88:1, 44:10–11, 29:55, 14:50 and 7:41. She notes terror and darkness in 24:40, 10:27, 31:32, 20:78, 13:3, 7:54 and 91:4, and understands this night as deep darkness. Al-Jishumī contrasts ghishāʾ, covering, with tajallī, clarity and illumination. Al-Durr reports Saʿīd ibn Jubayr's gloss that night approaches and covers everything. Al-Biqāʿī emphasizes that the covering gradually hides what daylight showed. [15 notes: IBNABIHATIM(سعيد بن جبير); NAHHAS(النابغة الذبياني); MAWARDI(ابن جبير,قتادة); JISHUMI; QURTUBI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; BINTSHATI; QURTUBI; DURR(سعيد بن جبير); BIQAI; BINTSHATI+]
- 92:1/q03/p2 It means that night darkens. — Mujāhid gives this concise gloss; the physical effect is the night removing daylight's light. Al-Dāmghānī compares the sense 'become dark' with 91:4; al-Durr reports the same gloss from Ibn ʿAbbās. [4 notes: MAWARDI(مجاهد); TAB-FULL; DAMGHANI; DURR(ابن عباس)]

## 92:1/q04 What does the night cover?  [ٱلَّيْلِ يَغْشَىٰ · referent]
turns on: Which implicit object is supplied for the verb, or how broadly its covering is understood.
- 92:1/q04/p1 The day or daylight. — Al-Ṭabarī says night removes daylight's light until day clears and reveals what was hidden; al-Kashshāf and al-Rāzī cite 13:3; al-Nasafī and Abū Ḥayyān cite 7:54; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites 13:3; al-Qummī also calls this an oath. Elmalılı includes daylight among the possible objects. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Baghawī say night removes the day's light; al-Ṭabarī adds that the next day reveals what night concealed. Al-Rāzī also cites 7:54 for the day as object. [24 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI(القمي); STUDYQURAN(some); KASHSHAF; RAZI; QURTUBI(قيل); BAYDAWI; NASAFI; ABUHAYYAN; ALUSI; BAGHAWI+; TAB+]
- 92:1/q04/p2 The sun. — Al-Kashshāf and al-Rāzī invoke 91:4; al-Nasafī points to the sun in the preceding oath; Abū Ḥayyān and Elmalılı likewise connect the possibility with 91:4. Al-Ālūsī says the sworn object is the entire night whichever object is supplied. Al-Kashshāf, al-Rāzī and al-Nasafī support the sun as object by 91:4. [14 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; KASHSHAF; RAZI; BAYDAWI; NASAFI; ABUHAYYAN; ALUSI]
- 92:1/q04/p3 Everything hidden by darkness, including all creatures or all creation. — Qatādah says night encompasses and fills all creatures. Al-Kashshāf and al-Rāzī cite 113:3; al-Nasafī cites the nightfall verse 113:3. Al-Ālūsī says the object may be whatever night veils in general, while the oath is by the whole night. Ibn ʿĀshūr makes universal coverage the lesson of the intransitive use. Al-Qurṭubī separately reports creatures and everything covered by darkness. Ibn Kathīr describes creation as covered in darkness; al-Durr reports Saʿīd ibn Jubayr's 'everything' gloss. [24 notes: IBNABIHATIM(سعيد بن جبير); NAHHAS; MAWARDI(قتادة); KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN(some); KASHSHAF; RAZI; QURTUBI(قيل); BAYDAWI; NASAFI; ABUHAYYAN; IBNKATHIR; ALUSI; IBNASHUR; DURR(سعيد بن جبير)]
- 92:1/q04/p4 The earth and what is on it. — Al-Qurṭubī gives earth as one possible object; Abū Ḥayyān says earth and all it contains; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī and Study Quran also offer earth. Ibn ʿAṭiyya explicitly names the earth and everything in it; Ibn ʿĀshūr says night envelops the earth and its creatures. [8 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(some); QURTUBI(قيل); ABUHAYYAN; IBNASHUR; IBNATIYYA+]
- 92:1/q04/p5 The horizons, or the horizon and everything between heaven and earth. — Al-Wāḥidī specifies the horizon under darkness; Ibn al-Jawzī reports al-Zajjāj's wider horizon-to-earth account; Elmalılı includes all horizons. [3 notes: WAHIDI-QT; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); ELMALILI]
- 92:1/q04/p6 The world. — Study Quran reports this as a possible unexpressed object; Quṭb depicts the world as enveloped and hidden. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN(some); QUTB-ZILAL]
- 92:1/q04/p7 Grief or people's eyes, hearts, and faculties through sleep and heedlessness. — Elmalılı extends the physical image to affect and perception, citing 6:60. [1 notes: ELMALILI]

## 92:1/q05 Why is the object of يَغْشَىٰ left unexpressed?  [يَغْشَىٰ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the missing object is recoverable, intentionally left wide, or absent because the verb is used intransitively.
- 92:1/q05/p1 The listener can infer the object from context. — Al-Naḥḥās and al-Qurṭubī say it is understood; al-Nasafī takes the preceding mention of the sun as contextual evidence, citing 91:4. [4 notes: NAHHAS; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; QURTUBI]
- 92:1/q05/p2 Leaving it open broadens the image to every possible object. — Al-Naḥḥās says the omission may deliberately leave the object vague. Elmalılı says it broadens the meaning and heightens terror, while pointing back to 91:4; al-Sāmarrāʾī says it keeps all possible objects in view. [3 notes: NAHHAS; ELMALILI; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 92:1/q05/p3 The verb functions intransitively here to teach that night covers everything. — Ibn ʿĀshūr explicitly explains the omission as intransitive use and draws the universal lesson. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNASHUR+]

## 92:1/q06 How does إِذَا describe the time of the covering?  [إِذَا · grammar]
- 92:1/q06/p1 It presents the covering as a realized event that may occur now or later. — Elmalılı says conditional idhā asserts realization and the imperfect verb can indicate present or future time. [1 notes: ELMALILI]
- 92:1/q06/p2 إِذَا is purely temporal in the oath and does not make the oath conditional. — Al-Zarkashī argues that a conditional reading would make the oath take place only when night fades, whereas the oath is made unconditionally now. [1 notes: BURHAN+]

## 92:1/q07 How is the case of إِذَا governed in the coordinated oaths?  [إِذَا · grammar]
turns on: Whether the coordinating wāws themselves must govern two cases, or coordination joins paired constituents under the first oath governor.
- 92:1/q07/p1 The construction poses a governance difficulty: coordination appears to make the wāws govern both genitive and accusative, or the oath wāws create a disputed construction. — Abū Ḥayyān reports al-Zamakhsharī's account and objects that coordinating wāws are not themselves governors; he says al-Zamakhsharī merely calls the disputed construction disliked where al-Khalīl's wording prohibits it. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]
- 92:1/q07/p2 The later wāws coordinate genitive-and-accusative pairs; the first oath particle supplies governance rather than each coordinating wāw governing two cases. — Abū Ḥayyān gives مررت بزيد أمس وعمرو اليوم as a parallel and cites Sībawayh's poetic coordination of a genitive and nominative in the same pattern. He reports al-Khalīl that the first wāw acts like oath bāʾ or tāʾ, while the later wāws join nouns by ordinary coordination; he reads al-Khalīl as prohibiting the competing account. [5 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الخليل,سيبويه); ABUHAYYAN-FULL+]

## 92:1/q08 Why is يَغْشَىٰ imperfect while the following daylight verb is perfect?  [يَغْشَىٰ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the tense contrast chiefly depicts the different pace of night and day or creates immediate presence through a past form.
- 92:1/q08/p1 Night's covering unfolds gradually, whereas daylight appears at once at sunrise. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī compares the paired verbs in al-Shams, 91:3–4; he explains the imperfect for the continuing covering and the perfect for sudden manifestation. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 92:1/q08/p2 A past form can make a present event vivid and immediate by placing the listener at the event. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī explains that Arabic past verbs have more temporal uses than simple past reference; he illustrates the shift with the Prophet turning toward heaven in 2:144 and the reproach for killing prophets in 3:21. [2 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 92:1/q09 Why is yaghshā apt here while 93:2 uses sajā?  [يَغْشَىٰ · rhetoric]
- 92:1/q09/p1 Yaghshā suggests movement and covering; sajā gives both stillness and darkness suited to the pause in revelation. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī says sajā fits revelation's suspension and loss of light, whereas yaghshā and yasrī imply movement; he compares 92:1 and 89:4. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 92:1/q10 How do night and day function in creation and human life?  [ٱلَّيْلِ يَغْشَىٰ · interpretation]
turns on: Which aspect of the paired phenomena carries the argument: daily benefit, divine governance, human divergence, the Hereafter, or secrecy.
- 92:1/q10/p1 They alternate as divine signs and provide complementary times for shelter, sleep, activity, and livelihood. — Qatādah calls them two great signs God rotates over creation. Al-Rāzī describes animals sheltered and creatures stilled for restorative sleep, then people, birds and other creatures moving for livelihood, citing 25:62 and 14:33. Abū Ḥayyān contrasts creatures' night shelter with daytime dispersal. Quṭb stresses their opposed appearance, qualities, effects, and recurring cycle. Al-Rāzī calls sleep rest for bodies and nourishment for spirits, and says perpetual night would prevent livelihood while perpetual day would remove rest; he cites 25:62 and 14:33. He also mentions birds leaving nests and vermin leaving hiding places in daylight. [9 notes: TAB-FULL(قتادة); RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; TAB(قتادة); RAZI; ABUHAYYAN]
- 92:1/q10/p2 Their precise alternation points to a power governing the cosmos and purposeful human life. — Quṭb says the broad change touches the soul, evokes human inability to control the mysterious universe, and shows another hand ordering the heavens and directing lives; instinct and reflection recognize this director even amid denial. Küran Yolu sees the worth of creatures and greatness of their Creator in the oaths. Al-Biqāʿī reads the gradual covering as a sign of God's power to efface and bring forth and of His voluntary direction of souls. Ibn ʿĀshūr finds signs of divine power and fine workmanship in the ordered night and day, referring to 91:3–4. [8 notes: QUTB-ZILAL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; BIQAI; IBNASHUR]
- 92:1/q10/p3 Their contrast provides a cosmic frame or matrix for divergent human deeds and recompense. — Ibn Kathīr pairs night darkness and day brightness with the created male and female, citing 78:8 and 51:49. Quṭb relates the two opposed outcomes to night/day and male/female. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ traces the contrast to fire for the denier and satisfaction for the pious giver. Corpus Coranicum says the two cosmic oppositions frame varied human aims and their recompense, with God later speaking in 92:7–10; Neuwirth calls the elemental pairs a structural matrix. Study Quran likewise links the polarity with opposed ends of deeds. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the contrast fits believers' and unbelievers' different conditions in this life and the next, while al-Durr reports Ibn ʿAbbās that the surah concerns generosity and miserliness. [13 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM(Neuwirth); STUDYQURAN; IBNKATHIR; BIQAI; ALUSI; IBNASHUR; DURR(ابن عباس)]
- 92:1/q10/p4 Their complementary pairing argues that creation has a completing counterpart, ultimately the Hereafter. — Iṣlāḥī says night/day and male/female fill one another's void, making their capacities and purpose intelligible; their harmony argues for monotheism and judgment, with 51:49 as a reminder. He says outer and inner evidence in 92:1–4 establishes the need for the Hereafter as this world's counterpart. [3 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 92:1/q10/p5 Night suits concealment and day publicity, whatever the moral quality of an act. — Al-Biqāʿī says the righteous and wicked alike choose night for secrecy and day for publicity. Al-Biqāʿī says night conceals secrets, joins the good and separates the wicked. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; BIQAI]

## 92:1/q11 What is the oath by the night doing?  [وَٱلَّيْلِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the note explains the oath's argumentative function or its proposed literary antecedent.
- 92:1/q11/p1 It invokes a natural sign to establish the ensuing premise and direct attention to the surah's subject. — Al-Māwardī identifies the oath; Iṣlāḥī says Qurʾānic oaths by natural phenomena establish a premise. Küran Yolu says they show the worth of created things and their Creator's greatness while introducing the subject. Corpus Coranicum points to 93:1–2 for this oath type's structure and function. Al-Rāzī says Qurʾānic oaths stress resurrection because revelation establishes its occurrence decisively, whereas oneness and prophethood have rational or widely transmitted signs; he compares 37:4, 91:1 and 85:1. Al-Suyūṭī lists this among oaths by created things. Abū Ḥayyān cites an unnamed poet's night-travel line with a bright, well-fattened mount in connection with the oath. Ibn ʿĀshūr says it confirms the ensuing assertion, whose broad statement of differing striving creates suspense before 92:5. [10 notes: MAWARDI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; CORPUSCORANICUM; RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN(الشاعر); IBNKATHIR; ITQAN; IBNASHUR]
- 92:1/q11/p2 Its form probably draws on characteristic pre-Islamic soothsayers' expression. — Corpus Coranicum proposes the connection, notes that it is not sufficiently studied, and compares the oath sequence in 100:1–5. [1 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM]

## 92:1/q12 Who may swear by created things?  [وَٱلَّيْلِ · law]
- 92:1/q12/p1 God may swear by any creature; creatures may swear only by God. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports Imam al-Bāqir's answer to Muḥammad ibn Muslim and a parallel transmission in al-Faqīh through ʿAlī ibn Mahziyār from Imam al-Bāqir II. [2 notes: TABATABAI(علي بن مهزيار,محمد بن مسلم)]

## 92:1/q13 What origin of night and day do the sources describe?  [ٱلَّيْلِ · reports]
turns on: Whether light and darkness are described as jointly created before differentiation, or night darkness as preceding solar daylight.
- 92:1/q13/p1 God first created light and darkness, then distinguished them as dark night and bright seeing day. — Al-Qurṭubī reports this from Qatādah through Saʿīd. Al-Qurṭubī transmits Qatādah's account that night became black and dark and day bright and perceptive after God first created and separated light and darkness. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قتادة عن سعيد); QURTUBI(قتادة)]
- 92:1/q13/p2 Night darkness was the solar system's original state; daylight followed the sun's creation, so reckoning began with nights. — Ibn ʿĀshūr presents this as an allusion in the passage. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 92:1/q14 What further inward or ominous meaning can the night's covering bear?  [ٱلَّيْلِ يَغْشَىٰ · inward]
turns on: Which extended spiritual or eschatological effect is drawn from the covering image.
- 92:1/q14/p1 Night prayer and good deeds can turn spiritual light into sensory light after death. — Al-Biqāʿī reports this from some interpreters. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL(بعضهم)]
- 92:1/q14/p2 Its engulfing darkness can warn of punishment, ignorance, death agonies, or resurrection events. — Elmalılı offers a town or people engulfed by punishment, the age of ignorance around the Prophet's mission, death agonies, and resurrection scenes as possible images. [1 notes: ELMALILI]
- 92:1/q14/p3 It can signify passing away of existent things before abiding witness to God. — Elmalılı distinguishes this accidental darkness from primordial nonexistence, cites 55:26–27 and 28:88 on all perishing except God's face, and describes movement from annihilation to abiding witness. [2 notes: ELMALILI]

## 92:1/q15 What was the surah's place and period of revelation?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether the whole surah belongs to Mecca, Medina, or both; the notes also differ in confidence about the Meccan attribution.
- 92:1/q15/p1 It is Meccan, with Study Quran calling it early Meccan and often ninth in revelation order. — Al-Māwardī calls the Meccan attribution a consensus; Abū Ḥayyān reports it as his own view. Al-Ālūsī says most hold it. Study Quran adds the common ninth-place ordering. Al-Durr reports Meccan classification from Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn al-Zubayr. Ibn ʿĀshūr places it ninth in revelation, after al-Aʿlā and before al-Fajr. [18 notes: IBNATIYYA(الجمهور); QURTUBI; DURR(ابن الزبير,ابن عباس); ITQAN; ALUSI; IBNASHUR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; ABUHAYYAN+; ALUSI-FULL+; BAGHAWI+; BAYDAWI+; IBNKATHIR+; KASHSHAF+; MAWARDI+; NASAFI+; STUDYQURAN+]
- 92:1/q15/p2 It is Medinan. — Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī attribute this to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥa; Study Quran reports the view without naming its holder. Al-Suyūṭī connects the reported Medinan classification with the palm-tree occasion; Ibn ʿĀshūr reports al-Mahdawī's Medinan or partly Medinan attribution on the same basis. [9 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; STUDYQURAN; IBNATIYYA(الجمهور); QURTUBI; ABUHAYYAN; ITQAN; ALUSI; IBNASHUR]
- 92:1/q15/p3 Part of it is Medinan and part Meccan. — Abū Ḥayyān, al-Ālūsī and Study Quran report a divided setting. Ibn ʿĀshūr reports that the partly Medinan attribution has been tied to the palm-tree occasion for 92:5. [8 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; STUDYQURAN; IBNATIYYA(الجمهور); ABUHAYYAN; ITQAN; ALUSI; IBNASHUR]

## 92:1/q16 How many verses does the surah have?  [* · sciences]
- 92:1/q16/p1 Twenty-one verses. — Al-Ālūsī says the count is unanimous. Al-Qurṭubī likewise calls the twenty-one-verse count a consensus. [7 notes: ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI+; BAGHAWI+; BAYDAWI+; KASHSHAF+; NASAFI+; QURTUBI+]
- 92:1/q16/p2 Twenty verses. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls this count consensual, and Ibn ʿĀshūr also gives twenty verses. [2 notes: IBNASHUR+; IBNATIYYA+]

## 92:1/q17 Why is the surah named al-Layl?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether the explanation identifies the naming source or the significance al-Biqāʿī finds in it.
- 92:1/q17/p1 It is named after its opening oath by night. — Study Quran states the naming basis. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]
- 92:1/q17/p2 The name signals night as frustration of ordinary desire through darkness and sleep, the sibling of death. — Al-Biqāʿī explains the name's thematic force. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 92:1/q18 What event occasioned the surah's revelation?  [* · reports]
turns on: Which charitable act is connected with revelation and how the palm narrative's chain is assessed.
- 92:1/q18/p1 A believer acquired a palm from a stingy owner and gave it to a deprived household. — Ibn Abī Ḥātim's Ibn ʿAbbās report says fallen dates were withheld, the buyer traded forty palms for the tree, and a palm in Paradise was promised. Ibn Kathīr's Ibn ʿAbbās version adds that the owner snatched fruit from poor children, refused the Prophet's offer of a palm in Paradise, and another man donated the purchased tree; Ibn Kathīr says the story explains the opening contrast of giving and withholding. Al-Ālūsī identifies the buyer in al-Suddī's version as Abū al-Daḥdāḥ and the beneficiaries as orphans, but reports al-Suyūṭī's judgment that the Ibn ʿAbbās report is weak; Ibn Kathīr calls Ibn Abī Ḥātim's report very unusual. Al-Baghawī reports ʿAṭāʾ's version: Abū al-Daḥdāḥ exchanged his garden for the owner's palm and gave it to the neighbor; the giver and miser exemplify 92:5–10. Al-Durr calls the Ibn ʿAbbās version weak in chain and says the buyer traded forty palms. Ibn ʿĀshūr says this occasion for 92:5 underlies some Medinan attributions. [9 notes: IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس); ALUSI-FULL; BAGHAWI(عطاء); DURR(ابن عباس); ALUSI(السدي); IBNASHUR; IBNKATHIR-FULL-]
- 92:1/q18/p2 It concerned Abū Bakr's charitable action, including his purchase and freeing of Bilāl. — Al-Ālūsī says most reports concern Abū Bakr, citing sound chains from Ibn Masʿūd, Ibn ʿAbbās and others. Ibn Masʿūd reports that Abū Bakr bought Bilāl from Umayya ibn Khalaf for a cloak and ten uqiyyas and freed him, after which the opening was revealed. Al-Rāzī reports al-Qaffāl that the surah concerns Abū Bakr's spending on Muslims against Umayya ibn Khalaf's miserliness and disbelief. Al-Ālūsī says most reports support an Abū Bakr occasion with sound chains and that some commentators identify him with the most God-fearing in 92:17, some claiming consensus. [5 notes: ELMALILI(İbn Mes‘ûd); RAZI(القفال); ALUSI(الجمهور,بعض المفسرين); ALUSI-FULL+]
- 92:1/q18/p3 Some Shiʿite interpreters assign the passage about the most God-fearing in 92:17 to ʿAlī. — Al-Ālūsī reports this attribution alongside the Abū Bakr attribution for that passage. [1 notes: ALUSI(بعض المفسرين)]

## 92:1/q19 What reward is reported for reciting this surah?  [* · sciences]
- 92:1/q19/p1 The reciter receives until contentment, is protected from hardship, and has ease made easy. — Al-Jishumī transmits this as a prophetic report from Ubayy. [1 notes: JISHUMI(أبي)]

## 92:1/q20 What recitation of al-Layl did the Prophet direct in prayer?  [* · reports]
turns on: The reports describe different instructions and prayer settings.
- 92:1/q20/p1 Muʿādh was told to use shorter chapters, among them al-Layl, instead of prolonging prayer. — Al-Durr reports the Prophet's rebuke and his examples of chapters beginning with glorification, sun, dawn and night, mentioning 87:1, 91:1 and 92:1. Ibn Kathīr recalls the Prophet asking Muʿādh why he had not used al-Aʿlā, al-Shams and al-Layl (87:1, 91:1). [2 notes: DURR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNKATHIR(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 92:1/q20/p2 The Night and Sun surahs were to be recited in the dawn prayer. — Al-Durr reports the Prophet's instruction to Ibn ʿAbbās. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 92:1/q20/p3 The Prophet recited al-Layl and similar surahs in the noon and afternoon prayers. — Al-Durr transmits this from Jābir ibn Samura. [1 notes: DURR(جابر بن سمرة)]

## 92:1/q21 How were the endings such as يَغْشَىٰ read?  [يَغْشَىٰ · readings]
turns on: The transmitted degree and distribution of imālah across the sūrah endings.
- 92:1/q21/p1 With open vowels, without imālah. — Al-Fārisī reports this for Ibn Kathīr, ʿĀṣim and Ibn ʿĀmir across the relevant endings of al-Layl and al-Ḍuḥā; some Nāfiʿ transmissions through Warsh and Qālūn also open them all. [2 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(ابن كثير وعاصم وابن عامر,نافع)]
- 92:1/q21/p2 With full imālah of the endings. — Al-Fārisī reports this throughout for al-Kisāʾī; ʿAbbās transmits fully inclined forms from Abū ʿAmr for selected endings. [2 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(أبو عمرو,الكسائي)]
- 92:1/q21/p3 With intermediate imālah. — Some Nāfiʿ transmissions give an intermediate pronunciation, and Abū ʿAmr is also reported to use intermediate imālah. [2 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(أبو عمرو,نافع)]
- 92:1/q21/p4 With selective opening or inclination depending on the ending. — Another Nāfiʿ report opens only talāhā and inclines the rest; al-Fārisī records differing Abū ʿAmr transmissions across verse endings, including 79:30. [2 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(أبو عمرو,نافع)]

## 92:1/q22 How was the oath's male-and-female clause read?  [* · readings]
turns on: Whether the early reported wording without mā remains the operative reading or was superseded by the agreed muṣḥaf wording.
- 92:1/q22/p1 It was recited as 'the male and the female' without mā. — Ibn Jinnī reports this from the Prophet, ʿAlī, Ibn Masʿūd, Abū al-Dardāʾ and Ibn ʿAbbās; he treats it as evidence for a variant making the pair genitive in apposition to mā. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites the two Ṣaḥīḥs' report from ʿAlqama: Abū al-Dardāʾ heard the Prophet recite Ibn Masʿūd's wording. He says al-Zamakhsharī called it the Prophet's reading, but explains it as an early permitted variant later replaced. Al-Durr reports Abū Isḥāq's account of ʿAbdallāh's connected recitation of the night and day oaths followed by 'the male and female'; Ibn ʿĀshūr reports ʿAlqama's account that Abū al-Dardāʾ testified to hearing it from the Prophet. [5 notes: IBNJINNI-MUHTASAB(النبي وعلي بن أبي طالب وابن مسعود وأبو الدرداء وابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL(علقمة); DURR(أبو إسحاق); IBNASHUR(علقمة)]
- 92:1/q22/p2 The final agreed reading retains mā in the clause. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says this is the reciters' agreed final reading and the form written in Abū Bakr's muṣḥaf. Ibn ʿĀshūr explains the earlier variant as an early license superseded by the Prophet's final-life reading and says the canonical readers and Abū Bakr's codex retain mā. [2 notes: IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL+]

## 92:1/q23 How does this opening connect with the preceding surah?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the link lies in repeated oath wording, development of the moral polarity, or reversal of the prior narrative movement.
- 92:1/q23/p1 It echoes the preceding surah's night-and-day oath wording and temporal restrictions. — Ibn ʿĀshūr links the time restrictions to explanations of the oaths in 91:3–4. Corpus Coranicum calls 92:1–2 especially close to 91:3–4 and compares early Meccan oath parallels in 93:1–2, 89:1–4, 84:17, 81:17–18 and 74:33–34; it notes that brightening day corresponds to ḍuḥā in 91:1 and 93:1. Elmalılı also points back to 91:4. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the opening oath continues the style of the preceding two surahs and points to the explanation of night and day in 91:3–4. [5 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; CORPUSCORANICUM; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR]
- 92:1/q23/p2 It develops the prior surah's purified-versus-corrupted souls by showing divergent deeds and their outcomes. — Al-Jishumī says the believer/disbeliever contrast recurs. Abū Ḥayyān connects success and failure in 91:9–10 to the traits that cause them and to the warning of Fire. Al-Biqāʿī links purified and corrupted souls and Thamūd's fate to good and evil efforts under God's freely chosen governance. Iṣlāḥī says al-Layl identifies what corrupts the soul and how to protect and purify it; Study Quran says the first three verses echo the prior polarity before the opposing human ends. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the night/day contrast suits differing good and evil striving and believers' and unbelievers' outcomes in this life and the next. [12 notes: JISHUMI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN; ABUHAYYAN; BIQAI; ALUSI; IBNASHUR]
- 92:1/q23/p3 It reverses the preceding movement from light toward ruin into a passage from warning and annihilation toward renewal and glad tidings. — Elmalılı says al-Shams moved from light to darkness, self-knowledge and Thamūd's ruin, while al-Layl moves toward witnessed light, purification, growth and abiding in God. The prior ending warns, while this surah proceeds from warning to glad tidings; the Creator can renew creation through day, male and female despite a people's destruction, citing 91:15. [2 notes: ELMALILI]
- 92:1/q23/p4 Opening with night reverses the later al-Shams sequence because this surah was revealed earlier, when disbelief predominated and Islam was just emerging like day after night. — Ibn ʿĀshūr ties the order of the oaths to the surahs' revelation order and the condition of early Islam. [1 notes: IBNASHUR+]

## 92:1/q24 What does the paired day's tajallī mean against this night's covering?  [* · meaning]
- 92:1/q24/p1 It is appearing, becoming uncovered, and making things clear. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ illustrates the physical senses with a receding hairline, polish that clears sight, the display of a bride, and polishing a blade or mirror; this supplies the lexical opposite of the night’s covering. Ibn ʿĀshūr says attributing tajallī to day praises a brightness that everyone perceives, including the blind. [2 notes: BINTSHATI; IBNASHUR]

## 92:1/q25 What other titles are attested for this surah?  [* · sciences]
- 92:1/q25/p1 Sūrat al-Layl, Sūrat wa-l-Layl, and Sūrat wa-l-Layl idhā yaghshā. — Ibn ʿĀshūr finds al-Layl in most Qurʾān copies and some tafsīrs, wa-l-Layl in most tafsīrs, and wa-l-Layl idhā yaghshā in al-Bukhārī and al-Tirmidhī. [1 notes: IBNASHUR]

## 92:1/q26 Does the reported occasion limit the surah's meaning?  [* · interpretation]
- 92:1/q26/p1 No; its statements about differing human effort and the warning of fire apply generally. — Al-Rāzī says the surah's general wording at 92:4 and 92:14 extends beyond the reported Abū Bakr and Umayya occasion. [1 notes: RAZI]

## 92:1/q27 What formal feature do the paired night and day oaths illustrate?  [وَٱلَّيْلِ · rhetoric]
- 92:1/q27/p1 They illustrate patterned Qurʾānic verse endings. — Al-Zarkashī uses the pair as an example of patterned endings. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 92:1/q28 What overall message does the surah develop after its opening oath?  [* · interpretation]
- 92:1/q28/p1 It contrasts believers' virtues and reward with unbelievers' faults and recompense, and presents God's guidance and the Messenger's warning. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says God guides and rewards people in this life and the next, while the Messenger reminds them of God and His reward; the fearful heed him and the wretched turn away toward the great fire. [2 notes: IBNASHUR]

## 92:1/q29 How is the expression 'the Prophet's reading' used for the reported variant?  [* · readings]
- 92:1/q29/p1 Ibn ʿĀshūr directs readers to his sixth introduction for its technical meaning. — Ibn ʿĀshūr refers to his sixth introduction when discussing the reported reading without mā. [1 notes: IBNASHUR]
