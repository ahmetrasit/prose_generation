# 18:11: 22 questions

## 18:11/q01 What does the opening fa connect the sleep to?  [فَضَرَبْنَا · links]
turns on: Whether the conjunction resumes the youths' prayer or their entry into the cave.
- 18:11/q01/p1 Their preceding prayer for mercy and guidance was answered by the long sleep. — Alusi compares the response to Zachariah's prayer at 21:90; sleep, turning, and awakening answer 18:10. Ibn Ashur says this favor saved them from torture, confirmed their truth, and became a sign. Bursevi includes their turning as a hidden grace. Qurtubi reports the harm of their people was diverted. [5 notes: ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BURSEVI; QURTUBI-FULL; TABRISI]
- 18:11/q01/p2 The fa connects to their taking refuge in the cave: God promptly gave more than they expected. — Ibn Ashur offers this alternate connection to 18:10. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 18:11/q02 What does God striking over their ears mean?  [فَضَرَبْنَا عَلَىٰٓ ءَاذَانِهِمْ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the wording directly denotes sleep, a barrier to hearing that preserves sleep, or a broader cutoff from outside sounds.
- 18:11/q02/p1 God imposed sleep upon the youths in the cave. — Muqatil says their hearing was sealed for 309 years; Yahya, al-Farra, Ibn Qutayba, Tabari, al-Tha'labi, al-Wahidi, Jishumi, al-Tabrisi, Ibn Kathir, and others read the action as making them sleep. Ibn Qutayba cites Abu Dharr's same expression. Al-Tha'labi calls the sleep overpowering. Ibn Atiyya says it attached firmly, as do Arabic expressions for imposed humiliation, tax, or levies. Al-Majma's explanation is preferred by Tabataba'i over Zamakhshari's paraphrase. Study Quran reports the deep-slumber reading. Asad also reports that classical commentators read the veiled ears as putting the youths to sleep. Ibn Qutayba says rendering the expression as putting them to sleep conveys its intended meaning, since a word-for-word rendering of striking ears would be unintelligible. [24 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; FARRA; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB(أبو ذر); TAB-FULL; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(المفسرون); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNKATHIR-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن جريج); ELMALILI; TABATABAI(المجمع); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN(IK, Th, Ṭs); QURTUBI-FULL; ASAD-NOTES(classical commentators); IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL+; TABATABAI+]
- 18:11/q02/p2 God barred sound from their ears, making their sleep deep and undisturbed. — Zajjaj explains that sound wakes sleepers. Mawardi stresses that blocking hearing preserves sleep rather than causing it. Wahidi cites Ibn Abbas's 'struck with sleep' wording. Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi, Alusi, Ibn Ashur, and others understand a veil or barrier; ordinary sounds cannot rouse them. Ibn Ashur contrasts covering the ears with ordinary closing of eyes. Tabataba'i reports Zamakhshari's account; Study Quran reports that incoming sounds were prevented. [25 notes: ZAJJAJ; SULAMI(ابن عطاء); MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس,الزجاج); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(الزمخشري); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(R, Z); TABATABAI-]
- 18:11/q02/p3 The expression means preventing the ears from functioning, as an idiom for stopping someone's action. — Qutrub compares a ruler stopping subjects or a master stopping a servant's trade by 'striking' their hands. Alusi objects that bare auditory prevention does not establish sleep or fit the resurrection setting, though its proponents may intend sleep. Al-Rummani, al-Baqillani, and al-Burhan explain that sensation through the ears was suspended without the youths becoming deaf; al-Burhan calls the expression metaphorical. [9 notes: THALABI(قطرب); JISHUMI(قطرب); TABRISI(قطرب); QURTUBI-FULL(قطرب); TABATABAI(قطرب); BAQILLANI+; BURHAN+; RUMMANI+; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 18:11/q02/p4 They were cut off, physically or figuratively, from the sounds and bustle outside. — Asad leaves both physical and metaphorical separation open while also reporting the classical sleep interpretation. [1 notes: ASAD-NOTES]

## 18:11/q03 What rhetorical picture does the ear expression convey?  [فَضَرَبْنَا عَلَىٰٓ ءَاذَانِهِمْ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether its force is an imposed covering, a representation of deep sleep, or tender lulling.
- 18:11/q03/p1 It pictures sleep as something firmly imposed or a cover laid over the ears. — Tabari compares striking someone with paralysis. Tha'labi and Wahidi cite al-Aswad ibn Ya'fur's barriers over the earth; Jishumi notes the poet was blind. Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, and Samin cite al-Farazdaq's line; Abu Hayyan also cites al-Aswad and an unnamed poet, and compares humiliation, poll tax, conscription, and fixed traits. Alusi mentions a struck lock and pitched tent; Ibn Ashur cites imposed humiliation at 2:61 and also mentions 2:26. Alusi answers al-Qutb's objection to metonymy and offers an implicit metaphor; other investigators prefer a representational metaphor. Qurtubi and Tabrisi independently transmit al-Aswad's verse; Tabrisi uses it for the image of confinement. Al-Rummani compares the silencing of their ears to striking a book shut. Al-Burhan calls the absence of ear sensation a metaphor and explains allusive metaphor through an unnamed borrowed image hinted at by an attribute, such as predation implying a lion for a brave man; it also reports al-Sakkaki's broader account of figurative predication. Al-Itqan defines linguistic metaphor as using a word outside its original assignment and gives naming a whole for a part as one type, illustrated by 'fingers' for fingertips and 'bodies' for faces. [25 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI(الأسود بن يعفر); WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI(الأسود بن يعفر); IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الأسود,الفرزدق); ALUSI-FULL(القطب,بعض المحققين); IBNASHUR-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الأسود بن يعفر); TABRISI(الأسود بن يعفر); BURHAN; ITQAN; BURHAN+; RUMMANI+]
- 18:11/q03/p2 It is a notably eloquent Qur'anic idiom whose full verbal force cannot be conveyed in translation. — Tha'labi calls it unmatched; Qurtubi says its eloquence challenged the Arabs. Tabrisi and Tabataba'i say translation cannot preserve its wording. Ibn Ashur calls its metonymy unknown before the Qur'an and an aspect of inimitability. Ibn Qutayba says a literal translation of striking the ears is unintelligible, whereas 'putting them to sleep' preserves the sense. Al-Burhan cites Ibn Faris's example of this compact phrase, which requires explanatory expansion in another language; it says translation loses Arabic expressive precision and even an Arabic paraphrase cannot replace the challenged wording. Al-Qaffal, as reported by al-Burhan, says no complete Persian translation is possible, though tafsir can convey some intended meaning. [9 notes: THALABI; TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL; TABATABAI; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; BURHAN(القفال)]
- 18:11/q03/p3 It evokes a mother gently tapping a child near the ear to lull it to sleep. — Tabataba'i suggests tender maternal care. Islahi traces an affectionate lulling sense of the idiom and calls the verse an eloquent image of loving care. [3 notes: TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 18:11/q04 What is understood as the omitted object of 'struck'?  [فَضَرَبْنَا عَلَىٰٓ ءَاذَانِهِمْ · grammar]
- 18:11/q04/p1 A veil, covering, or barrier over the ears is understood. — Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Samin, and Ibn Ashur supply the omitted object; Abu Hayyan compares pitching a tent over one's wife. Alusi analyses the covering as an implicit metaphor. Al-Burhan says earlier wording can identify an omission; it cites alternative accounts of ma manaʿaka and reads balagh in 'they remained but an hour of a day—balagh' as 'this is a balagh,' stated in Ibrahim (14:52). [9 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(القطب); IBNASHUR-FULL; BURHAN]

## 18:11/q05 Why are the ears singled out?  [ءَاذَانِهِمْ · rhetoric]
- 18:11/q05/p1 Hearing is what commonly wakes a sleeper; suspending it makes the sleep especially deep. — Zajjaj supplies the causal point; Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi, and Alusi repeat it. Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi cite the prophetic report of Satan urinating in the ear of one who sleeps through the night; Abu Hayyan says this signifies heavy sleep without rising. Alusi says blocked eyes would suggest only sleep's onset, since sights do not rouse a sleeper. Samin says striking ears brings sleep. Al-Rummani contrasts ears with eyes: closed eyes may reopen, whereas stopping hearing prevents awakening. [10 notes: ZAJJAJ; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; RUMMANI]

## 18:11/q06 What inward meaning is drawn from the blocked ears and sleep?  [ءَاذَانِهِمْ · inward]
turns on: The notes offer different spiritual applications: exclusive perception of God, transformation of the self, heedlessness, and retreat from human impressions.
- 18:11/q06/p1 Their hearing and sight were withdrawn from all but God. — Sulami reports 'some' saying they heard and saw only Him, so no creature or harm had a share in them. Another Sulami report says the covered ears also gave rest from creatures. [2 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء,بعضهم)]
- 18:11/q06/p2 Their human qualities gave way to sacred qualities until God returned them to bodily life. — Ibn Ata, as reported by Sulami, says their outward and inward were sanctified and held in God's grasp, then returned to bodily form and attributes at raising. [1 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء)]
- 18:11/q06/p3 The sleep signifies spiritual heedlessness during bodily existence until maturity or death. — Qashani cites the prophetic saying that people are asleep and awaken when they die; he relates this to immersion in bodily management until voluntary or natural death. [2 notes: QASHANI(عليه السلام)]
- 18:11/q06/p4 For retreat seekers, shutting the senses protects the heart and opens it to divine knowledge. — Bursevi's Ta'wilat Najmiyya blocks human speech from impressing the heart, erases earlier impressions through repeated 'There is no god but God,' and describes divine manifestation, annihilation of self, and preservation through God. Alusi also reports allegorizing the sleep as absorption in God and annihilation in all besides Him. [4 notes: BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية); ALUSI-FULL]

## 18:11/q07 What do reports say happened as the youths entered the cave?  [فِى ٱلْكَهْفِ · reports]
turns on: Accounts vary in when sleep began and in surrounding details.
- 18:11/q07/p1 God put them to sleep in the cave, with the dog and their provisions beside them. — Tabari places sleep after their meal and conversation, with the dog at the entrance. Tha'labi puts it at sunset, provisions by their heads. A report from Abu Abd Allah says they entered with the dog, slept until the king and city disappeared, then awoke thinking a day or part of one had passed. Tabataba'i summarizes the protected cave sleep. [4 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI; TABATABAI(أبو عبد الله عليه السلام)]
- 18:11/q07/p2 They slept as soon as they entered the cave, hiding them from the kingdom. — Ibn Atiyya reports this chronology. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 18:11/q07/p3 The youths fled an idol worshipping tyrant with a shepherd and his dog. — Abu Abd Allah's report has the tyrant kill dissenters; the youths feigned a hunting trip and the shepherd joined their faith. Tha'labi adds that Tamlikha warned them of Daqyanus, after which they prostrated and sought protection; the king later could not find them and leaders accused them of rebellion. [3 notes: TABATABAI(أبو عبد الله عليه السلام); THALABI]

## 18:11/q08 How was the cave sealed and later opened?  [ٱلْكَهْفِ · reports]
turns on: Reports differ on the king's reason for sealing and on who later opened the cave.
- 18:11/q08/p1 Daqyanus sealed it so the youths would die of hunger and thirst. — Tha'labi says he believed them awake; Ibn Atiyya reports the searchers' advice. Wahb's report in Tha'labi and Ibn Jawzi says the king's men feared entering, so they blocked the entrance. [4 notes: THALABI(وهب بن منبه); IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(وهب بن منبه)]
- 18:11/q08/p2 God inspired the king to seal it so the youths' bodies would be preserved as a future resurrection sign. — Tabari and Tha'labi give this divine purpose. Tabari says two hidden believers recorded names and story on lead tablets in a sealed copper chest; Tha'labi names Biedros and Rutas, adding lineage, and reports another record preserved in the royal treasury. [4 notes: TAB-FULL; THALABI(عبيد بن عمير)]
- 18:11/q08/p3 A shepherd reopened it to shelter his flock from rain; their souls returned the following morning. — Wahb's account appears in Tha'labi and Ibn Jawzi. [2 notes: THALABI(وهب بن منبه); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(وهب بن منبه)]
- 18:11/q08/p4 A man removed its covering to build a sheep pen from the stones. — Tha'labi names him Olyas; Ibn al-Sa'ib's account in Ibn Jawzi calls him the landowner. [2 notes: THALABI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن السائب)]

## 18:11/q09 Where was the cave thought to be?  [ٱلْكَهْفِ · reports]
turns on: Whether archaeology and geography can identify a proposed cave.
- 18:11/q09/p1 Rujayb in Jordan is the strongest proposed fit, though no cave is conclusively identified. — Tabataba'i favors the excavated Rujayb site for its signs and some Muslim reports, while also saying none is proved. He notes Amman occupied ancient Philadelphia under Roman control until the Muslim conquest. [3 notes: TABATABAI; TABATABAI+]
- 18:11/q09/p2 Other proposals include caves near Damascus, Petra, Scandinavia, and Nakhchivan. — Tabataba'i lists Mount Qasiyun, Petra, a Scandinavian cave with seven uncorrupted Roman-looking bodies, and Nakhchivan. He says Scandinavian and Caucasus proposals conflict with a Roman setting and the limits of Roman power. [1 notes: TABATABAI; TABATABAI-]

## 18:11/q10 Were the youths alive during their long sleep?  [فَضَرَبْنَا سِنِينَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the condition is ordinary sleep, a sleep-like death, or an intermediate state.
- 18:11/q10/p1 They remained alive, asleep, and protected; they had not died. — Mawardi says the stopped hearing indicates sleep rather than death. Tabrisi says they slept safely after their prayer. Razi says they survived 309 years unharmed and shielded from sun, citing 18:17–18. Tabataba'i argues that a small counted span fits the passage because the sleepers were not dead. Study Quran attributes the same sleep-not-death conclusion to Tabarsi. [6 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN(Ṭs)]
- 18:11/q10/p2 God had taken their souls in a death resembling sleep. — Tha'labi says this while recounting the king's plan to seal them in alive. [1 notes: THALABI]
- 18:11/q10/p3 Their centuries-long sleep represents an intermediate realm between life and death. — Study Quran compares it with the state between individual death and universal resurrection. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 18:11/q11 How long did they sleep?  [سِنِينَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the notes state only a counted span or identify it with 309 years in 18:25.
- 18:11/q11/p1 The stay lasted 309 years. — Muqatil and Mujahid give 309; Razi calls it 309 years of protected sleep. Qurtubi reports reading 18:25's 300 plus nine as specifying this verse's duration. Bursevi and Study Quran also connect the span with 309; Study Quran notes 18:19 says they felt it as a day or part of one. [6 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(مجاهد); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI; STUDYQURAN]
- 18:11/q11/p2 The verse states a counted span of years without specifying its number here. — Yahya and Ibn Kathir say counted years; Wahidi and others describe the duration here as counted. Wahidi says the cave identifies place and the years identify duration. Baydawi calls both adverbial complements. Ibn Qutayba glosses al-amad in the following context as the limit or endpoint. [7 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; WAHIDI-BASIT; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; TABATABAI; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB]

## 18:11/q12 How does 'years' function syntactically?  [سِنِينَ · grammar]
- 18:11/q12/p1 It is an accusative time adverb governed by the action of striking or sleeping. — Nahhas notes its nūn may also take declension and says the ear noun is feminine with a heavy damma omitted. Wahidi says the cave phrase gives the place and years the duration. Baydawi, Abu Hayyan, Samin, Bursevi, Alusi, and Tabataba'i agree on the time complement; Alusi says a temporal and a spatial adverb may combine without difficulty. [8 notes: NAHHAS; WAHIDI-BASIT; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 18:11/q13 How is 'ʿadadan' parsed?  [عَدَدًۭا · grammar]
turns on: Whether it modifies 'years' as an adjective or verbal noun, or acts as an independent cognate accusative.
- 18:11/q13/p1 It modifies 'years' as counted or possessing a number. — Farra, Zajjaj, Tha'labi, Wahidi, Razi, Ibn Jawzi, Qurtubi, and Samin give the adjectival analysis. Nahhas distinguishes Farra's 'counted' and the Basrans' 'possessing a number.' Wahidi and Razi report omitted noun or passive verbal noun analyses. Abu Hayyan allows a verbal noun used adjectivally or an omitted preposition; Samin allows ellipsis of dhawat. Tabataba'i allows an elided genitive complement. [17 notes: AKHFASH; ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء والزجاج); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(الفراء); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(الراغب وغيره); TABATABAI]
- 18:11/q13/p2 It is an accusative verbal noun or cognate expression: the years were counted with counting. — Zajjaj, Nahhas, Abu Ubayda, Wahidi, Ibn Jawzi, Razi, Qurtubi, Tabrisi, Samin, Alusi, and Tabataba'i allow this. Samin says it may intensify the count or be governed by an implied taʿudd; Tabrisi also treats 'years' as a time expression. [14 notes: TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; NAHHAS; THALABI(أبو عبيدة); WAHIDI-BASIT(الزجاج); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL(أبو عبيدة); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(الراغب وغيره); TABATABAI]

## 18:11/q14 What does calling the years 'counted' imply?  [سِنِينَ عَدَدًۭا · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether explicit counting evokes abundance, brevity, either scale, complete years, or an unspecified length.
- 18:11/q14/p1 It suggests many years, since a small quantity needs no counting. — Zajjaj states the principle; Wahidi, Ibn Atiyya, Ibn Jawzi, Qurtubi, Nasafi, Biqa'i, and Tabataba'i's report of Zajjaj repeat it. Aisha's report about the Prophet's many nights at Hira illustrates the usage for Ibn Ashur. Alusi says abundance displays divine power; al-Kashf connects it to plural addressees. [13 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT; BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL(الزجاج); BIQAI-FULL(الزجاج); ALUSI-FULL(الكشف); IBNASHUR-FULL(عائشة); TABATABAI(الزجاج)]
- 18:11/q14/p2 It can suggest a small or readily numbered span. — Zamakhshari argues even long human time is small beside God; Tabataba'i prefers scarcity as fitting 18:9's denial that this story is uniquely astonishing, comparing 12:20. Alusi says it suits the same point, and al-Kashf links it to a singular addressee. Abu Hayyan rejects comparison with 23:113, which concerns the sight of promised punishment. Al-Izz ibn Abd al-Salam considered scarcity impossible here, though Alusi disputes him. [5 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الكشف); TABATABAI(الزمخشري); TABATABAI+; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري)-; ALUSI-FULL(العز بن عبد السلام)-]
- 18:11/q14/p3 The number can suggest either abundance or scarcity according to the point of comparison. — Kashshaf gives both; Baydawi makes it depend on reference point. Alusi reports al-Raghib and others and notes Ibn Hajar's discussion, which he finds unpersuasive. Study Quran reports this two-way sense from Zamakhshari. [5 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(ابن حجر,الراغب وغيره); STUDYQURAN(Z)]
- 18:11/q14/p4 It denotes complete years without fractions of months or days. — Mawardi gives this as an alternative to enumerated years. [1 notes: MAWARDI-FULL]
- 18:11/q14/p5 The indefinite count leaves the duration unspecified in the wording. — Biqa'i links that indefiniteness to the indeterminate age of polytheism. Bagawi reports that counting may simply emphasize number or a long period. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL]

## 18:11/q15 What does the prolonged sleep show about extraordinary divine acts?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether its delayed verification makes it a saintly gift or a prophetic miracle.
- 18:11/q15/p1 It is a gift to the youths as saints, not a prophet's miracle. — Razi says the sleep itself was ordinary; the extraordinary fact could be confirmed only after the same youths were recognized 309 years later. He also notes their unharmed survival and shielding from sun in 18:17–18. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL; RAZI-FULL+]
- 18:11/q15/p2 An extraordinary sleep would require a prophet in that era to count as a prophetic miracle. — Al-Qadi's objection, reported by Razi, rests on a prophet's presence for a prophetic miracle; Razi answers that this event could not serve that purpose. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL(القاضي); RAZI-FULL-]

## 18:11/q16 How are these verses related to the surrounding story?  [* · links]
- 18:11/q16/p1 They summarize the flight and prayer, years of sleep, and awakening before the detailed account. — Tabataba'i assigns one element to each opening verse and says later verses add the public discovery at 18:21. Islahi says advance summary keeps the purpose visible and prevents secondary details from obscuring it, a Qur'anic narrative pattern. [3 notes: TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 18:11/q17 Why is hearing the beloved's story from God especially valued?  [* · interpretation]
- 18:11/q17/p1 The beloved's own account is the highest source and deepens longing. — Qushayri cites a poem asking Sa'd to repeat the beloved's story because hearing it increases longing. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]

## 18:11/q18 What can be learned from the varying historical accounts of the cave story?  [* · reports]
- 18:11/q18/p1 Reports diverge greatly; the Qur'an selects what serves guidance and omits many historical particulars. — Tabataba'i lists disagreement over dates, kings, lineage, looks, names, and the title People of the Inscription. He traces divergence to Christian and other People of the Book traditions passed to Quraysh, monuments and images, and Muslims' broad collection of outside material through converts such as Wahb and Ka'b; later transmitters treated some as prophetic. Names, occupations, places, rulers, dates, and the dog are omitted because guidance does not depend on them. He notes historians often place the event between Jesus and Muhammad; the two Testaments lack it, Christian sources preserve it, and Christian versions differ over sleeper count, dog, and sleep length. [8 notes: TABATABAI(أهل الرواية والتاريخ)]

## 18:11/q19 How does Ibn Qutayba approach the figurative wording of this verse amid criticism of Qur'anic style?  [فَضَرَبْنَا ءَاذَانِهِمْ · rhetoric]
- 18:11/q19/p1 He explains its metaphor through tafsir, Arab language, and possible readings while resisting unsupported personal interpretation and later charges against Qur'anic style. — Ibn Qutayba says literal translation of the ear expression obscures its sense. He describes later accusations of contradiction, impossibility, faulty grammar, and disorder, unlike early opponents' charges of magic, soothsaying, or old tales; he says he gathered tafsir, expanded it, and used Arab usage to explain metaphor and possible readings without imposing a personal interpretation. He mentions 3:7. [3 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL]

## 18:11/q20 What verbal features does al-Baqillani identify in Qur'anic eloquence?  [* · rhetoric]
- 18:11/q20/p1 Its expression has harmonious letters, meaning-led verse endings, and related word forms; its sound, clear meaning, and proof affect discerning listeners. — Al-Baqillani defines tala'um as fitting letter arrangement and contrasts it with the harsh 'grave of Harb' tongue-twister; he places poetry's harmony below the Qur'an's highest grade, while noting variation among listeners. He says pleasant sound, easy articulation, impact on the heart, clarity, and sound proof reveal inimitability. He reports al-Khalil's account of dissonance from articulation points too near or too far apart. He distinguishes Qur'anic fawasil, whose identical or neighboring end letters follow meaning, from saj' and the tighter constraints of poetic rhyme and meter. He also introduces tajanis for forms sharing a root. [7 notes: BAQILLANI(الخليل)]

## 18:11/q21 May the Arabic wording of this verse be recited in translation?  [* · law]
turns on: Whether recitation requires the Arabic form tied to the Qur'an's inimitability, and whether inability to read Arabic permits Persian.
- 18:11/q21/p1 No; recitation must retain its Arabic form, in and out of prayer, even for someone unable to read Arabic. — Al-Burhan holds that the Qur'an's inimitability is bound to its Arabic form and reports consensus on retaining that form: translation loses expressive precision, and even Arabic paraphrase cannot replace the challenged wording. It reports al-Qaffal's view that a complete Persian translation is impossible, although tafsir can convey some of the meaning. [3 notes: BURHAN(القفال); BURHAN+]
- 18:11/q21/p2 Persian recitation is allowed without qualification, according to a view attributed to Abu Hanifa. — Al-Burhan reports this attribution to Abu Hanifa but says his retraction is authentic. [1 notes: BURHAN(أبو حنيفة وأبو يوسف); BURHAN-]

## 18:11/q22 What does the divine plural in فَضَرَبْنَا convey?  [فَضَرَبْنَا · rhetoric]
- 18:11/q22/p1 It conveys divine majesty and abundance; a following singular indication prevents reading the plural as multiple gods. — Al-Samarrai says the divine plural appears in contexts of greatness and abundance, while singular forms occur elsewhere, citing 94:1 and 74:11. He says a singular indicator always follows a divine plural to rule out plurality for an audience steeped in shirk, citing 108:1, 97:1, and 48:1. [2 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
