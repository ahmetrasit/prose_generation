# 74:34: 11 questions

## 74:34/q01 By what is the oath in the morning clause sworn?  [وَٱلصُّبْحِ · referent]
turns on: Whether the named created phenomena are themselves the objects of a divine oath or stand for their Lord, given the rule that an oath may only be made by God.
- 74:34/q01/p1 By the morning itself, as the third object of the oath after the moon and night. — Al-Mawardi calls it the third oath object; al-Tabrisi reports Qatada's wording that morning is sworn by when it shines. Ibn Atiyya says the three oaths honor God's signs. Al-Nasafi says the oath's answer follows. Bursevi says the night and dawn clauses are coordinated with the moon. Kur'an Yolu names all three objects. [6 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI(قتادة); IBNATIYYA-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; TABRISI-]
- 74:34/q01/p2 By the Lord of the morning and the other phenomena. — Al-Tabrisi reports this alternative because an oath may only be made by God; he also describes morning as clearing darkness and revealing visible objects. [1 notes: TABRISI]

## 74:34/q02 What does 'when' convey in the dawn clause?  [إِذَآ · grammar]
- 74:34/q02/p1 It looks ahead to the coming dawn after night. — Bursevi says idha is future-looking here because dawn follows night, and the night and dawn clauses are coordinated with the moon. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 74:34/q03 What does 'asfara' mean here?  [أَسْفَرَ · meaning]
- 74:34/q03/p1 Dawn becomes bright, clear, and visible as darkness gives way. — Ibn Qutayba, al-Tabari, Qatada, al-Qushayri, al-Wahidi, al-Wahidi's Wajiz, al-Baghawi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Razi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Bursevi and al-Alusi gloss it as brightening or becoming clear; al-Tabari and Qatada add that morning comes on. Ibn Atiyya says its light spreads before sunrise. Al-Tabrisi says it clears darkness and reveals visible things. Jishumi calls isfar appearance and visibility, citing sufur for a woman appearing without her veil; his account of safara says both forms can mean brightening, perhaps like a woman uncovering her face. Al-Wahidi and al-Razi cite the prophetic dawn-prayer wording, and both compare the radiant faces of 80:38; al-Wahidi says prayer waits until light is evident and doubt is removed. Al-Qurtubi says the light brightens and adds the images of a shining face, an unveiled woman's face, and a broom sweeping darkness from a house. Al-Raghib, reported by Bursevi, distinguishes safara for removing a physical covering from asfara for a color becoming clear and bright; Bursevi says isfar can mean entering dawn time. Al-Samin calls the removal of darkness from dawn's face a metaphor. Ibn al-Sumayfi and Isa ibn al-Fadl's safara is explained by Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi as stripping darkness from dawn's face. Qut al-qulub, reported by Bursevi, describes the second dawn as the sun's white glow beneath red twilight before the solar disk appears: sunset goes red, white, dark, and sunrise reverses the sequence. Al-Tabatabai calls isfar dawn's clearing and unveiling. [26 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(قتادة); QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(ابن السميقع); BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(قتادة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن السميفع وعيسى بن الفضل); SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI(الراغب,قوت القلوب); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI]

## 74:34/q04 How is the dawn verb read?  [أَسْفَرَ · readings]
turns on: The transmitted verb form, quadriliteral asfara or triliteral safara; Abu Hatim is reported to call the latter weak, while Jishumi calls both valid words.
- 74:34/q04/p1 Asfara, with the alif in the quadriliteral form, is the common or majority reading. — Al-Qurtubi calls it the common reading, Abu Hayyan attributes it to the majority, al-Samin contrasts it with the triliteral variant, and al-Alusi calls it the majority's form and glosses it as becoming bright and clear. [4 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR(عيسى بن الفضل وابن السميفع); ALUSI-FULL]
- 74:34/q04/p2 Safara, without the alif in the triliteral form, is a reported reading. — Jishumi attributes it to Ibn al-Sumayqa, says both safara and asfara are valid for brightening, and suggests uncovering a woman's face as an analogy. Ibn Atiyya attributes it to Isa ibn al-Fadil and Ibn al-Sumayfi, likens it to dawn casting darkness from its face, and reports Abu Hatim's judgment that this reading is weak. Al-Qurtubi calls it Ibn al-Sumayqa's other dialectal form. Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, and al-Alusi attribute it to Ibn al-Sumayfi and Isa ibn al-Fadl; Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi explain it as removing darkness from dawn's face. The names Isa ibn al-Fadil/al-Fadl and Ibn al-Sumayqa/al-Sumayfi vary across the notes. [6 notes: JISHUMI(ابن السميقع); IBNATIYYA-FULL(عيسى بن الفضيل وابن السميفع); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن السميفع وعيسى بن الفضل); SAMIN-DURR(عيسى بن الفضل وابن السميفع); ALUSI-FULL]

## 74:34/q05 How are the preceding night's readings related to 'when dawn brightens'?  [إِذَآ أَسْفَرَ · readings]
turns on: Whether the point is the attested idha-adbara parallel or the acceptability of both night-clause readings; the notes do not set these assessments against one another.
- 74:34/q05/p1 A reading with idha and hamzated adbara in the night clause parallels idha asfara. — Abu Hayyan attributes this reading to al-Hasan, Abu Razin, Abu Raja, Ibn Yamar, al-Sulami, Talha, al-Amash, Yunus ibn Ubayd and Matar, and says the parallel is reflected in the codices of Abd Allah and Ubayy. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحسن وأبو رزين وأبو رجاء وابن يعمر والسلمي وطلحة والأعمش ويونس بن عبيد ومطر)]
- 74:34/q05/p2 Both readings of the preceding adbara are sound. — Ibn Atiyya reports this assessment from Abu Ali al-Farisi, without restricting it to one night-clause form. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبو علي الفارسي)]

## 74:34/q06 How is the prophetic instruction to pray fajr at isfar understood?  [أَسْفَرَ · law]
turns on: Whether isfar locates the start after visible light, describes prolonging prayer toward sunrise, or names entry into dawn time.
- 74:34/q06/p1 Pray after dawn light has become visible and uncertainty has passed. — Al-Wahidi gives this explanation of the hadith instruction. Al-Qurtubi reports the explanation as praying after the light appears. [2 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 74:34/q06/p2 Lengthen the dawn prayer until sunrise approaches. — Al-Qurtubi reports this as another explanation of the Prophet's instruction to delay until brightening. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 74:34/q06/p3 Isfar can mean entering the dawn time. — Bursevi gives this possible sense of the phrase 'pray at isfar' while glossing asfara as dawn brightening and clearing. [1 notes: BURSEVI]

## 74:34/q07 What does the passage from night to bright morning convey?  [وَٱلصُّبْحِ أَسْفَرَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the sequence is read as the observable daily contrast, an inward change, the arrival of guidance, or an image of the coming Judgment.
- 74:34/q07/p1 A natural sequence of opposites: retreating night gives way to unveiled morning. — Al-Tabatabai says night retreat is opposite morning unveiling, idbar reverses night's arrival, and isfar is dawn's clearing. Bursevi says dawn follows night and makes idha future-looking. [2 notes: TABATABAI; BURSEVI]
- 74:34/q07/p2 The heart passes from ego-darkness to the full light of spirit or spiritual illumination. — Al-Qasim, reported by al-Sulami, says the dawn's brightening is the appearance of spiritual lights in hearts. Al-Qashani interprets moon as receptive, clear heart, night as departing ego-darkness, and dawn as spirit-light fully illuminating it. Kashifi, reported by Bursevi, likewise makes the moon a clear receptive heart, night the lower self, and dawn spirit-light dispelling the heart's darkness. [3 notes: SULAMI(القاسم); QASHANI; BURSEVI(الكاشفي)]
- 74:34/q07/p3 Dawn pictures knowledge and guidance arriving and dispelling confusion. — Al-Biqai uses sunrise as an analogy for knowledge and guidance illuminating and removing confusion. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 74:34/q07/p4 Daily dawn prefigures Judgment arriving at its appointed time after the darkness of this world. — Islahi says night yielding to morning makes the arrival of Judgment as sure as daily dawn; the alternation continually supplies evidence of that Day to sincere truth-seekers. He cites 81:17-18 for the night and morning sequence. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 74:34/q08 What do the moon, night and dawn oaths establish?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the cited purpose is reflection on the divine signs themselves or their proof of the timed Judgment and its warning; Kur'an Yolu connects both.
- 74:34/q08/p1 They draw attention to God's signs, their ordered marvels, and the need to reflect on their Sustainer. — Ibn Atiyya says the oaths honor these signs and direct reflection to God, who sustains all existence. Fath al-Rahman, reported by Bursevi, says specifying dawn invites contemplation of its marvels and its many regularly ordered motions. Kur'an Yolu says the three oaths draw attention to divine signs. [3 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; BURSEVI(فتح الرحمن); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 74:34/q08/p2 They substantiate the warning that Judgment will come at God's appointed time, despite demands for haste. — Islahi says Quranic oaths support a following claim and here answer why Judgment has not yet arrived. The moon's slow phases, compared with humanity's stages, show a divinely appointed course; he cites 36:39. Nature's signs argue for the Hereafter and expose heedlessness; God alone knows the benefit of the prescribed plan, and justice arrives at its time. Night giving way to dawn makes the coming Day certain and its daily repetition supplies evidence; he cites 81:17-18. Kur'an Yolu also says the oaths impress the importance of the warning to humanity. [7 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 74:34/q09 What does the preceding 'Nay' reject, and why does it lead the oath?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Which denial or defiance the surrounding kalla addresses, and in Islahi's account why it is stated before the oath's proof.
- 74:34/q09/p1 It rebukes disbelievers and rebels before drawing attention to God's signs. — Ibn Atiyya says kalla rebukes disbelievers and rebels, then the oaths by moon, night and morning honor signs of the God who sustains all existence. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 74:34/q09/p2 It rejects claims that people can count God's hosts or overpower them. — The Study Quran reports this explanation from Al and Tabatabai, alongside its comparison with other moon and night-day oaths. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN(Āl, Ṭ)]
- 74:34/q09/p3 It rejects the audience's denial or challenge over the delayed Judgment before giving proof. — Islahi says the denial is so objectionable that emphatic negation comes before the evidentiary oath; the oaths then answer disbelievers asking why the promised Judgment has not yet arrived. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 74:34/q10 Which other Qur'anic oaths or passages echo this sequence?  [* · links]
- 74:34/q10/p1 Other passages swear by the moon and by the succession of night and day. — The Study Quran cites 84:18 and 91:2 for moon oaths and 81:17-18, 84:16-17, 89:1-4, 91:1-4 and 92:1-2 for related oaths; its note also lists 91:1-4 as a group. Islahi cites 81:17-18 for night yielding to morning as an image of Judgment and for the daily alternation as evidence of that Day. [3 notes: STUDYQURAN(Āl, Ṭ); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 74:34/q11 Which moon is meant in the preceding oath alongside night and dawn?  [* · referent]
- 74:34/q11/p1 The crescent after its third night. — Bursevi reports this specification from Abu al-Layth for the moon in the preceding oath. [1 notes: BURSEVI(أبو الليث)]
