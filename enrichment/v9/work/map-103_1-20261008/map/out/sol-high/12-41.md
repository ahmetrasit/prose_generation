# 12:41: 56 questions

## 12:41/q01 Why does Joseph again call them his prison companions?  [يَٰصَىٰحِبَىِ ٱلسِّجْنِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Which rhetorical effect of the renewed address is emphasized.
- 12:41/q01/p1 The address renews their attention and marks the importance of his answer. — Abu Hayyan says Joseph returns to their interpretation after the more important teaching about religion; al-Alusi says interpretation is a new topic after his proof of knowledge; Ibn Ashur says the vocative stresses the interpretation. [3 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 12:41/q01/p2 The address invokes shared hardship and friendship. — Al-Biqa'i says shared imprisonment removes worldly pretension and softens the heart. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 12:41/q02 Which prisoner receives each predicted fate?  [أَحَدُكُمَا ٱلْءَاخَرُ · referent]
- 12:41/q02/p1 The cupbearer returns to royal wine service, and the baker is crucified and eaten by birds. — Muqatil, al-Thalabi, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, al-Tabrisi, and others specify three more prison days; some say release and crucifixion occur on the fourth. The cupbearer dreamed of grapes or a vine, the baker of bread baskets and birds. Ibn Ishaq, Mujahid and later reports name them variously Nabu, Nabwa or Banu, and Mujlith, Majlatha, Majlath, Malhab or Mujlab; the name forms vary. Al-Mawardi calls the condemned man the king's baker; the Imami report calls both king's servants. Study Quran says the baker's head is eaten after death and for his crime. [40 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(ابن إسحاق); ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(مجاهد); THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL(ابن جرير); WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن السائب); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن زيد,عكرمة,قتادة); ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI(أبو جعفر عليه السلام); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN(My,N and Kl)]

## 12:41/q03 Why are the two prisoners not named in the prediction?  [أَحَدُكُمَا ٱلْءَاخَرُ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the verse reproduces Joseph's veiled speech or condenses a more explicit speech.
- 12:41/q03/p1 Joseph deliberately veiled the identities to spare the condemned man. — Ibn Kathir says this spared the other prisoner; al-Biqa'i says each could hope for restoration unless he asked directly; al-Alusi invokes trust in the interpretation and good companionship; Ibn Ashur says the condemned man could grasp his fate gradually. [4 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 12:41/q03/p2 The verse may abbreviate Joseph's direct assignment of each fate. — Ibn Ashur says the verse may condense the original speech in which Joseph named each man's outcome. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 12:41/q04 How does the cupbearer's dream yield the timing of his restoration?  [فَيَسْقِى رَبَّهُۥ خَمْرًۭا · interpretation]
- 12:41/q04/p1 Three grape clusters or vine branches mean three more prison days before his return to service. — Muqatil gives three days. Al-Thalabi speaks of three clusters; al-Kalbi in al-Wahidi of a vine with three branches and pressed grapes; al-Tabrisi specifies release on the fourth day. Al-Zamakhshari and al-Alusi take the vine as the king and favorable standing. Ikrima's report in al-Durr describes planting a seed, its growth into clusters, pressing, and serving the king. [14 notes: MUQATIL; THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن السائب); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; DURR-FULL(عكرمة); ALUSI-FULL]

## 12:41/q05 How do the baker's baskets yield the timing of his crucifixion?  [فَيُصْلَبُ فَتَأْكُلُ ٱلطَّيْرُ مِن رَّأْسِهِۦ · interpretation]
- 12:41/q05/p1 Three baskets mean three more days before execution and the birds eating his head. — Al-Thalabi says crucifixion is on the fourth day; al-Kalbi, Ibn al-Sa'ib, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, and al-Alusi link the three baskets to three days. Al-Baghawi says release after three days is followed by crucifixion. Al-Mawardi notes that birds had eaten bread from the dream baskets. [13 notes: THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن السائب); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 12:41/q06 How is فيسقي read?  [فَيَسْقِى · readings]
turns on: The verb's active or passive morphology and, for the passive, who receives the drink.
- 12:41/q06/p1 The common active reading derives from saqā: he gives his master wine. — Abu Hayyan calls it the majority reading; al-Samin describes its open yāʾ and derivation from saqā. Al-Durr records Ikrima reciting the active wording. [5 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR(العامة); DURR-FULL(عكرمة)]
- 12:41/q06/p2 An active reading derives from asqā, with the same drink-serving sense. — Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan report the variant; al-Samin attributes a damma to Ikrima and compares 56:69; al-Alusi also reports it. [4 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR(العامة); ALUSI-FULL]
- 12:41/q06/p3 The passive reading yusqā rabbahu has the master or king given wine. — Ikrima and al-Jahdari are named by Ibn Jinni, Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and al-Samin; Abu Hayyan explicitly identifies the king as recipient. Al-Samin says rabbahu is nominative and notes al-Zamakhshari attributes the reading only to Ikrima. Ibn Jinni sees parallel favorable and punitive passive outcomes. Al-Alusi also reports a passive with riyyihi as a second object. [5 notes: IBNJINNI-MUHTASAB(عكرمة والجحدري); IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(عكرمة والجحدري); SAMIN-DURR(عكرمة والجحدري); ALUSI-FULL]
- 12:41/q06/p4 The passive reading means the cupbearer himself is given drink. — Al-Zamakhshari glosses Ikrima's passive as his being given drink to satisfy him; Ibn Atiyya also says he is given wine to drink. [2 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(عكرمة); IBNATIYYA-FULL]

## 12:41/q07 Do saqā and asqā have the same meaning?  [فَيَسْقِى · meaning]
turns on: Whether Arab usage treats the two forms as synonyms or distinguishes direct drinking from provision of water.
- 12:41/q07/p1 They can both mean giving a person a drink. — Ibn Atiyya calls them synonymous; al-Qurtubi reports some linguists' synonymy; Abu Hayyan cites the Lawami author; al-Samin compares the reading at 56:69; al-Alusi allows this shared sense. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(صاحب اللوامح); SAMIN-DURR(العامة); ALUSI-FULL]
- 12:41/q07/p2 Saqā gives a drink directly, whereas asqā provides water or irrigation. — Al-Zajjaj states the distinction; al-Nahhas says most linguists make it, cites Qur'anic form against a poem claiming synonymy, and prefers it. Al-Qurtubi cites al-Nahhas with 77:27; Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi call it the usual distinction. [5 notes: ZAJJAJ; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(صاحب اللوامح); ALUSI-FULL; NAHHAS+]

## 12:41/q08 Who is the cupbearer's rabb?  [رَبَّهُۥ · referent]
- 12:41/q08/p1 His master, the king of Egypt. — Al-Tabari and Ibn al-Sa'ib gloss rabb as master or king. Al-Tabrisi compares 'lord of a house' or estate. Al-Razi infers the prisoners served the king from this expression and the request to be remembered in 12:42. Abu Hayyan attributes the master gloss to Ibn Atiyya; Ibn Zayd uses lord or master. ## 12:41/q09 What does the passive form of crucifixion convey?  [فَيُصْلَبُ · rhetoric]
- 12:41/q09/p1 It points to the king's sole authority to crucify. — Al-Biqa'i says only the king can order or carry out this punishment. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 12:41/q10 How do the paired fate clauses work rhetorically?  [فَيَسْقِى رَبَّهُۥ خَمْرًۭا فَيُصْلَبُ فَتَأْكُلُ ٱلطَّيْرُ مِن رَّأْسِهِۦ · rhetoric]
- 12:41/q10/p1 Each stated outcome implies the opposite counterpart left unspoken for the other prisoner. — Al-Biqa'i describes ellipsis across the paired clauses. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 12:41/q11 What does quḍiya mean here?  [قُضِىَ · meaning]
- 12:41/q11/p1 It means completed, settled, or fully executed. — Al-Jishumi derives the sense from completing and executing, compares lammā quḍiya and the naming of a qāḍī. Al-Tabari says God's decree has taken effect; al-Alusi glosses it as completed or settled. Ibn Qutayba traces qaḍā to determination and completion, cites a poem about completing affairs, and explains qāḍī as one who settles disputes; he compares the decreed death in 39:42. Al-Damghani says the prison interpretation is settled or due. Ibn al-Jawzi distinguishes completion in 6:60, 20:114 and 28:28–29, settlement in 6:58, 10:47, 10:54 and 39:69, and irrevocable decree here and in 19:21, 34:14 and 39:42. [11 notes: TAB-FULL; JISHUMI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; DAMGHANI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA]

## 12:41/q12 What is the 'matter' that has been decided?  [ٱلْأَمْرُ · referent]
turns on: Whether the inquiry's object is the dreams and their interpretation, the prisoners' eventual fates, or the accusation underlying both cases.
- 12:41/q12/p1 The men's eventual fates: one will be saved and one killed. — Al-Zamakhshari, Abu Hayyan, and al-Samin connect the singular matter to their shared imprisonment or accusation; al-Baydawi says they sought their shared outcome. Al-Nasafi and Ibn Ashur take it as what would happen to them. Al-Alusi prefers this as less strained: they asked about dreams in order to learn their fate, so fī is causal; he says inquiry can treat dream and outcome together. [8 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(الكثير)+]
- 12:41/q12/p2 The requested interpretation or the dreams as the object of inquiry has been settled. — Al-Mawardi gives the question and answer as finished; Ibn al-Jawzi says the interpretation is settled whether the dreams were true or false. Ibn Abbas, Ibn Masud and Qatada in al-Wahidi say Joseph only finished the interpretation. Al-Alusi's investigator says a question concerns the dream as an incident, compares the king's request in 12:43, and says 'matter' magnifies obscure dreams; singular matches the prisoners' own wording. Al-Alusi objects that dream and outcome need not be separated this way. [10 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس,ابن مسعود,قتادة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; ALUSI-FULL(بعض المحققين); ALUSI-FULL-]
- 12:41/q12/p3 The matter is the accusation or crime for which both men were imprisoned. — Al-Alusi reports this view with an omitted term meaning the consequence of that accusation. Al-Zamakhshari and Abu Hayyan likewise invoke their shared poisoning accusation, while reading the decided matter as its outcome. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعضهم)]

## 12:41/q13 Why is 'matter' singular for two dreams?  [ٱلْأَمْرُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the singular gathers a shared case and fate or echoes the prisoners' phrasing.
- 12:41/q13/p1 The prisoners share one case or sought one combined outcome. — Al-Zamakhshari identifies the poisoning accusation and the opposed results; al-Baydawi says their inquiry was about the shared case; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin similarly identify the joint accusation and final outcome. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري)]
- 12:41/q13/p2 The singular follows the prisoners' own singular wording for their dream inquiry. — Al-Alusi's investigator says they asked about the image of an outcome, not their crime or its cause. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(بعض المحققين)]

## 12:41/q14 What made Joseph's declaration of a settled outcome certain?  [قُضِىَ ٱلْأَمْرُ · theology]
turns on: Whether the declaration reports revealed certainty or merely completion of a probable dream interpretation.
- 12:41/q14/p1 God revealed or granted Joseph certain knowledge of the men's fates. — Al-Mawardi and Ibn al-Jawzi offer revelation; al-Wahidi prefers it as fitting a prophet, citing fulfillment despite fabricated dreams; al-Tabrisi sees the categorical forecast as revelation of unseen things; Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan describe God-given unseen knowledge. Al-Razi allows God to disclose outcomes while Joseph presents dream interpretation. Al-Tabatabai rejects conjecture by the declaration and God's teaching of interpretation. Al-Qurtubi says God fulfilled it to confirm prophethood. [10 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; TABATABAI; WAHIDI-BASIT+]
- 12:41/q14/p2 As a prophet, Joseph could trust what God caused him to say. — Al-Mawardi distinguishes a prophet's certainty from a nonprophet's. [1 notes: MAWARDI-FULL]
- 12:41/q14/p3 He completed the interpretation without guaranteeing the predicted events. — Ibn Abbas, Ibn Masud and Qatada as reported by al-Wahidi take the declaration as the end of the answer. Ibn al-Jawzi offers the same alternative. Al-Razi invokes 12:42's ظن against revealed certainty and allows interpretive judgment without inevitability. Al-Qushayri says even true dream interpretation works by preponderant probability. [7 notes: QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس,ابن مسعود,قتادة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); WAHIDI-BASIT-; TABATABAI-]

## 12:41/q15 Does interpretation itself make a dream's predicted event binding?  [قُضِىَ ٱلْأَمْرُ · theology]
turns on: Whether interpretation normally fixes a dream's fulfillment or Joseph's fulfilled words were a special prophetic confirmation.
- 12:41/q15/p1 A dream is suspended until interpreted and then follows its first interpretation, even if invented. — Abu Ruzayn's Prophetic report adds that a dream is one forty-sixth of prophecy and should be shared only with a discerning person; Anas reports it follows the first interpreter. Ibn Kathir cites Ahmad through Muawiya ibn Hayda and Abu Ya'la through Anas and extends the rule to invented dreams. Al-Alusi repeats the bird's-wing maxim: once told and interpreted, it falls. Al-Durr's Ibn Masud report says the test dreams' interpretation fixed the outcome. [8 notes: THALABI(أبو رزين العقيلي,أنس); IBNKATHIR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ALUSI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن مسعود); QURTUBI-FULL-]
- 12:41/q15/p2 A false interpretation does not bind someone; Joseph's case was exceptional divine confirmation. — Al-Qurtubi says God made Joseph's interpretation occur to confirm his prophethood. His Qatada report has Umar predict alternating faith and apostasy, ending in unbelief, from a claimed dream of alternating growth and drought; the man then denied dreaming. Al-Qurtubi treats Umar as an exceptional divinely inspired person and cites two further accurate intuitions. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قتادة)]

## 12:41/q16 What did the prisoners say after hearing Joseph's interpretation?  [قُضِىَ ٱلْأَمْرُ ٱلَّذِى فِيهِ تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ · reports]
turns on: Whether both men invented the dreams, both truly dreamed and later denied it, or only one dreamer lied or recanted.
- 12:41/q16/p1 Both admitted they had fabricated dreams to test Joseph or play with him. — Ibn Masud reports this through al-Tabari, Ibn Abi Hatim, al-Thalabi, al-Baghawi, Ibn Kathir, al-Durr and al-Alusi; some say they had seen nothing, and Joseph replied the decision stood. Al-Zamakhshari reports the test; Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan include it among alternatives. Al-Alusi suggests the cupbearer could have lied to protect his companion. [12 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(عبد الله); IBNABIHATIM(عبد الله بن مسعود); THALABI(ابن مسعود); BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن مسعود); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(عبد الله); DURR-FULL(ابن مسعود); ALUSI-FULL(ابن مسعود)]
- 12:41/q16/p2 Both had real dreams but denied them after the unwelcome interpretation. — Mujahid says the true predictions had already taken effect when the youths claimed play. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan report real dreams followed by denial; al-Razi gives displeasure with the answer as a motive. Al-Suddi reports panic; Ibn Kathir's Ibn Masud report includes their later denial. Al-Baydawi says they accused Joseph of lying. [8 notes: TAB-FULL(مجاهد); IBNABIHATIM(السدي); IBNATIYYA-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL(عبد الله); DURR-FULL(مجاهد)]
- 12:41/q16/p3 Only the condemned baker denied or invented his dream. — Al-Zajjaj says the condemned man denied dreaming; al-Mawardi reports his protest that they were playing. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan preserve the only-condemned-man variant; al-Nasafi says the baker denied. An Imami report says the second prisoner admitted inventing his dream; the report of Abu Jafar identifies him as the baker. Al-Alusi also reports this variant. [8 notes: ZAJJAJ; MAWARDI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI(أئمة أهل البيت عليهم السلام,أبو جعفر عليه السلام)]
- 12:41/q16/p4 One prisoner lied about a dream, without identifying which. — Abu Majliz gives the unspecified one-prisoner report. [1 notes: DURR-FULL(أبو مجلز)]
- 12:41/q16/p5 The men denied having seen dreams, without a settled account of whether they had fabricated them. — Al-Farra reports denial after the crucifixion forecast; al-Tabari's Ibn Masud report, Ibn Abi Hatim's al-Suddi and Ibn Masud reports, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, al-Biqa'i and al-Durr's Qatada also describe denial. Al-Biqa'i says Joseph rebutted the implication of ignorance or error; al-Alusi allows his answer to rebut denial or confirm interpretation. [9 notes: FARRA; TAB-FULL(عبد الله); IBNABIHATIM(السدي,عبد الله بن مسعود); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(قتادة); ALUSI-FULL]

## 12:41/q17 What does the inquiry verb تستفتيان mean?  [تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ · meaning]
- 12:41/q17/p1 It means asking for a fatwa or an answer resolving the matter. — Al-Jishumi calls istifta asking for a legal opinion and says a fatwa answers the question itself, not its reasoning. Al-Rummani in al-Biqa'i distinguishes an answer about meaning from one giving the underlying cause. Ibn Ashur describes a fatwa as an explanation resolving doubt and says some connect aftā, whose hamza remains, to fatā, youth, because clarity strengthens the listener. Al-Wahidi points to his earlier gloss in al-Nisa. [4 notes: JISHUMI; WAHIDI-BASIT; BIQAI-FULL(الرماني); IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 12:41/q31 What constructions can Arabic fa introduce after a preceding clause?  [فَيَسْقِى فَيُصْلَبُ فَتَأْكُلُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the following verb is governed by implicit an, coordinates with the preceding clause, or starts a new predicate.
- 12:41/q31/p1 A following verb may be subjunctive through an implicit an, often expressing a consequence of a negative clause. — Sibawayh says the an cannot be uttered; he explains a negative verb as a noun-like construction, gives alternative conditional and negative readings, cites 35:36, and adduces lines by al-La'in and al-Farazdaq. He prefers the subjunctive in one example to keep both clauses unified. [8 notes: SIBAWAYH; SIBAWAYH+]
- 12:41/q31/p2 A following verb may be indicative by coordination with the syntactic position of the previous clause. — Sibawayh illustrates raising with 77:35–36 and permits it alongside the subjunctive in his negative-clause examples. [4 notes: SIBAWAYH]
- 12:41/q31/p3 A following indicative may begin a fresh predicate. — Sibawayh paraphrases the raised verb as an independent clause and cites poetry. [1 notes: SIBAWAYH]

## 12:41/q32 How may Arabic temporal nouns and related expressions attach to clauses?  [* · grammar]
- 12:41/q32/p1 Temporal nouns commonly govern verbal clauses; past expressions also take nominal clauses, whereas future expressions take verbs. — Sibawayh gives a day-clause and Quranic examples at 77:35 and 5:119, extends verbal attachment to mundhu, mudh and a dhu expression, and explains past and future patterns through idh and idha. Yunus reports that Arabs reject a future day expression followed by a nominal clause. [6 notes: SIBAWAYH(يونس)]

## 12:41/q33 Can the verb taqulu govern two accusative complements?  [* · grammar]
- 12:41/q33/p1 Yes; it can take two accusatives by analogy with taẓunnu. — Sibawayh gives the example of asking whether one says Zayd is departing. [1 notes: SIBAWAYH]

## 12:41/q34 How do prophetic signs suit the arts of their audiences?  [* · theology]
- 12:41/q34/p1 Moses' signs suit an age of sorcery, Jesus' an age of medicine, and Muhammad's inimitable Quran an age of eloquence. — Ibn Qutayba names Moses' sea, hand, staff and water signs; Jesus' raising, healing and clay birds; and the Quran's challenge to humans and jinn. [3 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL]

## 12:41/q35 What features of Arabic speech make expression fit its occasion?  [* · rhetoric]
- 12:41/q35/p1 Orators vary length, repetition, concealment, clarity and polish to suit listeners and setting. — Ibn Qutayba relates the manner to situation, audience and dignity, says rougher passages can heighten finer ones, and illustrates varied brilliance through lights and a mixed necklace. [4 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL]

## 12:41/q36 What properties of Arabic letters and case endings are noted?  [* · grammar]
- 12:41/q36/p1 Arabic has twenty-eight letters, and its case endings can distinguish otherwise ambiguous subjects and objects. — Ibn Qutayba treats apparent additional sounds in other languages as intermediate articulations and says case endings both adorn and disambiguate speech. [2 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL]

## 12:41/q37 What other senses of qaḍā are illustrated outside this verse?  [قُضِىَ · meaning]
- 12:41/q37/p1 Elsewhere qaḍā can mean commanding, informing, fashioning, carrying out, making a covenant, judging, or giving a testament. — Ibn Qutayba cites 17:23, 17:4, 41:12, 20:72, 10:71 and Abu Dhu'ayb's poem. Ibn al-Jawzi adds the formation of heavens, the slaughter of death, Hell's closed gates, Muqatil's covenant gloss, judgment and Moses' testament. [11 notes: IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(مقاتل)]

## 12:41/q38 How many recitation modes were granted according to the cited reports?  [* · readings]
turns on: Whether the three-mode report describes a final limit or a limited or earlier case.
- 12:41/q38/p1 Seven modes were granted after requests for additional ease. — Ibn Abbas, Umm Ayyub, Sulayman ibn Surad and Ubayy transmit versions of successive increase; the Hisham and Umar report approves both recitations, and Abu Hurayra gives a limit on reversing mercy and punishment. [7 notes: TAB-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); BURHAN(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 12:41/q38/p2 One report gives three modes, possibly an early stage or words with three attested forms. — Al-Zarkashi relays Samura's report and Abu Ubayd's judgment that seven-mode reports are mass transmitted; Abu Shama offers the two reconciliations. [2 notes: BURHAN(أبو شامة,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 12:41/q39 What are the seven aḥruf said to comprise?  [* · readings]
turns on: Whether aḥruf name alternate expressions, subject categories, or types of textual variation.
- 12:41/q39/p1 They are alternative words, dialect forms or readings for one meaning, without changing lawful and forbidden rulings. — Ibn Shihab states the legal limit in al-Tabari, Ibn Atiyya and al-Zarkashi. Ibn Atiyya calls the synonym account weak and Ibn Shihab's statement possible but ambiguous; al-Zarkashi accepts varied languages and near synonyms. [5 notes: TAB-FULL(ابن شهاب); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن شهاب,فريق من العلماء); BURHAN(ابن شهاب); BURHAN+]
- 12:41/q39/p2 They are subject categories such as commands, prohibitions, promises, stories and parables. — Ibn Atiyya reports the category account; Qadi Abu Bakr transmits a prophetic report of seven doors and categories. [2 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم,فريق من العلماء); IBNATIYYA-FULL-]
- 12:41/q39/p3 A category account is unsuitable because these topics are not called aḥruf and legal meanings cannot be changed. — Ibn Atiyya rejects the subject-category proposal on these grounds. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL+]
- 12:41/q39/p4 They may be classes of variation in vowel, syntax, letters, form, order, addition or omission. — Ibn Atiyya reports the author of al-Dala'il's scheme, illustrated from 101:5, 56:29, 50:19 and 38:23. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(صاحب الدلائل)]

## 12:41/q40 What wider senses does al-amr bear in the cited Quranic examples?  [ٱلْأَمْرُ · meaning]
- 12:41/q40/p1 The word can denote a matter or condition, divine rule, revelation, command, resurrection, death, victory, caution, drowning, fertility, abundance, consultation, or sin and its consequence. — Ibn al-Jawzi catalogs these uses with examples including 2:109, 16:1, 7:54, 32:5, 3:154, 5:95, 11:97, 57:14, 7:110, 9:50, 11:43, 5:52, 4:58 and 17:16; some dispute whether the last is abundance or a command to obey. [14 notes: IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(بعضهم)]

## 12:41/q41 What can mathal mean, and how does a parable convey meaning?  [* · meaning]
- 12:41/q41/p1 Mathal can mean likeness, lesson or description; a concise comparison clarifies an obscure point without needing essential resemblance. — Ibn Qutayba supplies three senses; specialists distinguish replaceable equals from illustrative likeness, Tha'lab calls proverbs transmitted concise wisdom, and Ibn al-Jawzi cites comparisons in 14:24, 22:73 and 62:5. [6 notes: IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أهل التفسير,ابن قتيبة,بعض أهل المعاني,ثعلب)]

## 12:41/q42 What senses of muḥṣanāt and related forms are distinguished?  [* · meaning]
- 12:41/q42/p1 The forms can describe chaste, protected or married women; Qur'anic muḥṣanāt can mean chaste or free women. — Ibn al-Jawzi distinguishes the horse term and cites Tha'lab's vowel distinction for chaste and married women; exegetes cite 4:24, 4:25, 5:5, 21:91, 24:4, 24:23 and 66:12. [4 notes: IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أهل التفسير,ثعلب)]

## 12:41/q43 Should recitation keep the transmitted order within a surah?  [* · law]
turns on: Which version of the report about Bilal is more reliable and what transmitted order requires.
- 12:41/q43/p1 Readers should preserve the revealed sequence and avoid mixing passages from different surahs. — Al-Hulaymi calls it etiquette, al-Bayhaqi attributes arrangement through the Prophet to Gabriel, Ibn Sirin favors God's ordering, and Qadi Abu Bakr reports consensus against alternating single verses. Abu Ubayd's Bilal report says to read a surah in order; al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi says mixed themes serve listeners and describes Bilal's preference for mercy passages. [7 notes: BURHAN(أبو عبيد,ابن سيرين,البيهقي,الحكيم الترمذي,الحليمي,القاضي أبو بكر)]
- 12:41/q43/p2 Mixing passages is allowed according to the stronger report that all three reciters did well. — The Prophet is reported to approve Abu Bakr, Umar and Bilal; Abu Ubayd judges this wording more reliable and consonant with scholarly transmission. [2 notes: BURHAN(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); BURHAN(أبو عبيد)+]
- 12:41/q43/p3 Mixing passages is disliked. — Khalid ibn al-Walid apologized for doing so under the pressures of jihad; Abu Ubayd initially cites apparent disapproval of Bilal, Khalid's apology and Ibn Sirin's dislike, while acknowledging a stronger favorable report. [2 notes: BURHAN(أبو عبيد,خالد بن الوليد); BURHAN(أبو عبيد)-]

## 12:41/q44 Why is the agent omitted from the passive quḍiya?  [قُضِىَ · rhetoric]
- 12:41/q44/p1 Omitting the agent magnifies the one who decreed the matter. — Al-Zarkashi attributes this rhetorical effect to the passive wording and compares 11:44. [1 notes: BURHAN]

## 12:41/q45 Does aṣbaḥa add meaning when a state already existed?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the morning reference has a temporal rhetorical purpose.
- 12:41/q45/p1 It is substantive if the state also held the prior evening; otherwise it may be extra. — Hazim gives this distinction and uses honey becoming sweet as an example of an extra use. [1 notes: BURHAN(حازم)]
- 12:41/q45/p2 Morning can mark when sufferers realize the extent of loss after a troubled night. — Al-Rummani rejects treating aṣbaḥa as extra in the cited context because sufferers await morning relief. [1 notes: BURHAN(الرماني)+]

## 12:41/q46 Is a final hāʾ al-sakt reliably transmitted for tastaf tiyān?  [تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ · readings]
- 12:41/q46/p1 The isolated report of hāʾ in this verb is weak against the other transmitters. — Ibn al-Jazari rejects Abu al-Hasan ibn Abi Bakr's lone report. His surrounding discussion limits acceptable hāʾ readings to attested authority, records disputed Ruways and Ya'qub forms, accepts both Ruways forms where sound, and notes Arabic permits hāʾ by usage and analogy. [6 notes: NASHR; NASHR+]

## 12:41/q47 What does al-sijn mean in the prison address?  [ٱلسِّجْنِ · meaning]
- 12:41/q47/p1 With kasra, it denotes the place of confinement. — Ibn al-Jazari says all readers agree on the kasra here, at 12:36 and 12:42, distinguishing the place noun from the verbal noun; he contrasts Ya'qub's fatḥa at 12:33. [1 notes: NASHR]

## 12:41/q48 What does aḥad mean when annexed in aḥadukumā?  [أَحَدُكُمَا · meaning]
- 12:41/q48/p1 It may designate the first or one of the two. — Al-Suyuti cites the prisoner's dream interpretation as the example of the annexed form. [1 notes: ITQAN]

## 12:41/q49 Is this clause cited as an expression used proverbially?  [قُضِىَ ٱلْأَمْرُ ٱلَّذِى فِيهِ تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ · rhetoric]
- 12:41/q49/p1 Yes; it appears in a list of Qur'anic clauses used as proverbs. — Al-Suyuti reports Ja'far ibn Shams al-Khilafa's classification as irsal al-mathal. [1 notes: ITQAN(جعفر بن شمس الخلافة)]

## 12:41/q50 How is taʾwīl related to tafsīr in the cited usage?  [* · meaning]
turns on: Whether the terms reflect shared linguistic usage or narrower scholarly conventions.
- 12:41/q50/p1 Taʾwīl can mean explanation of the intended meaning, much like tafsīr. — Ibn Ashur reports Tha'lab, Ibn al-Arabi and Abu Ubayda's equation, prefers it from language and transmitted usage, cites al-A'sha's poem and 7:53, and explains the Prophet's prayer for Ibn Abbas and Aisha's report of acting on 110:3. Umar and Ibn Abbas found in that surah a sign of the Prophet's approaching departure. [9 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(الأعشى,ثعلب وابن الأعرابي وأبو عبيدة,رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم,عائشة,عمر وابن عباس); IBNASHUR-FULL+]
- 12:41/q50/p2 Taʾwīl may be reserved for ambiguous passages or for an evidence-based move away from a word's apparent sense. — Ibn Ashur reports both technical conventions, contrasting literal extraction of a bird from an egg with figurative extraction of a believer from an unbeliever. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(بعض الأصوليين,بعض العلماء)]

## 12:41/q51 What verbal noun patterns do transitive Arabic verbs take?  [* · grammar]
- 12:41/q51/p1 Their usual verbal noun is faʿl, but fuʿūl, fiʿl, fiʿāl, faʿlān and lexical exceptions also occur; the active participle is generally fāʿil. — Sibawayh describes three transitive conjugational patterns with faʿl as their usual verbal noun and fāʿil as the active participle. He cites ḥalb and ṭard for faʿl across conjugational classes; luzūm, nuhūk, wurūd and juḥūd for fuʿūl by analogy with intransitives; and both faʿl and fiʿl as variable, often lexical, forms. He also records fiʿāl, kitb beside kitāba, and faʿlān in ḥirmān, wijdān, ityān and ʿirfān, with alternate forms. Rare exceptions must be learned while common patterns support analogy. [7 notes: SIBAWAYH]

## 12:41/q52 What verbal noun patterns do intransitive Arabic verbs take?  [* · grammar]
- 12:41/q52/p1 Fuʿūl is common, while faʿāl, faʿl and multiple lexical alternatives also occur; their active participle is generally fāʿil. — Sibawayh gives fuʿūl and fāʿil as common, with occasional faʿāl. He cites faʿl in verbs for silence, calm, incapacity and withdrawal, and contrasts labitha/labthan with makatha/mukūthan; some speakers analogize makatha to ẓarufa. Faʿāl also occurs in sneezing and drowsiness, while al-sukāt names a disease. He records both wathban and wuthūban, raqṣan, and sound nouns such as khabīb, alongside al-dhamīl and al-ṣahīl. [5 notes: SIBAWAYH]

## 12:41/q53 How can an apparently transitive Arabic verb imply an omitted preposition?  [* · grammar]
- 12:41/q53/p1 A preposition can be omitted for brevity, leaving its complement directly after the verb. — Sibawayh explains dakhal-tuhu and walaj-tuhu as dakhal-tu fīhi and walaj-tu fīhi, and compares nubbiʾtu Zaydan with nubbiʾtu ʿan Zayd. [1 notes: SIBAWAYH]

## 12:41/q54 How do Arabic noun forms express a person's quality, role, undertaking or craft?  [* · grammar]
- 12:41/q54/p1 Faʿīl can designate a quality or role; feminine faʿāla forms can name an undertaking, responsibility or craft. — Sibawayh says faʿīl may characterize a person rather than name an action, giving ḍarīb qidāḥ and ṣarīm by analogy with ẓarīf. He quotes Ṭarīf ibn Tamīm al-ʿAnbarī using ʿarīf for ʿārif. He gives feminine ʿimāra for undertaking a building's cultivation, wakāla and wiṣāya by their resemblance to wilāya in taking charge, and tijāra, khiyāṭa and qaṣṣāba as names of crafts. [5 notes: SIBAWAYH(طريف بن تميم العنبري)]

## 12:41/q55 How do shared meanings and action results shape Arabic verbal noun patterns?  [* · grammar]
- 12:41/q55/p1 Related meanings can share forms, while a form may distinguish an action from its result, endpoint or resulting state. — Sibawayh gives faʿlān in rujḥān, shukrān and riḍwān; fiʿāl for related rutting and agitation; faʿāl for harvest, shearing or cutting as an endpoint versus faʿl for the act; and related patterns for flight, avoidance and distancing such as firār and shurād. Fuʿāl names fragments or remnants such as rufāt, judhādh, ḥuṭām and futāt. In marking, faʿāl can name the resulting mark while faʿl names the act, with other forms for the image left. He groups renouncing terms such as yaʾs and zuhd and states or conditions such as qanāʿa, baṭan, taban, thamal and ṭaban. [8 notes: SIBAWAYH]

## 12:41/q56 What verbal noun forms describe movement and sound in Arabic?  [* · grammar]
- 12:41/q56/p1 Faʿalān frequently names movement or agitation; faʿīl and faʿla can name sounds, with analogical extensions and forms that cannot be fully predicted. — Sibawayh gives faʿalān for bodily shaking, bounding, boiling, surging, flickering and heat, with variants; faʿīl for heartbeat and sounds such as hadīr and ṣahīl, though faʿalān is more common in that group. He cites ṭawafān, dawarān and jawalān for related circling actions but says, following al-Khalīl, that the class cannot be fully predicted by analogy. He records faʿla in sound nouns such as razma and jalaba. He also explains nafayān al-maṭar by analogy with ṭayarān: clouds scatter rain as wings disperse things and wind scatters dust. [7 notes: SIBAWAYH]
