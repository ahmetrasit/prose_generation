2:264/q09 How is riyāʾ read with a lightened hamza? [رِئَآءَ · readings]
  2:264/q09/p1 The first hamza is replaced by yāʾ after kasra, yielding رياء without a change of meaning. — reasons: Ṭalḥa ibn Muṣarrif and, by report, ʿĀṣim read it so; al-Ālūsī adds al-Khuzāʿī and al-Shamūnī. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn explain easing a heavy hamza after kasra.
2:264/q10 Who is the showy spender who lacks faith? [كَٱلَّذِى يُنفِقُ مَالَهُۥ رِئَآءَ ٱلنَّاسِ وَلَا يُؤْمِنُ بِٱللَّهِ وَٱلْيَوْمِ ٱلْءَاخِرِ · referent] (turns on: Whether public display with professed faith requires hidden unbelief, or can describe open unbelief or even a believer.)
  2:264/q10/p1 He is a hypocrite, outwardly religious but inwardly seeking human praise without faith. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī argues a declared unbeliever is known to act for Satan, while ostentation requires outward piety and hidden desire for praise; al-Thaʿlabī, Makkī as reported by Abū Ḥayyān, and al-Ālūsī's majority follow this rationale.
  2:264/q10/p2 He may be an openly unbelieving giver who seeks public praise. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports this as an option; Abū Ḥayyān reports unnamed exegetes; Ibn ʿĀshūr contrasts Muslims seeking reward but later harming recipients with unbelievers who seek only praise.
  2:264/q10/p3 The class may include a believer or an unbeliever acting ostentatiously. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports this minority view before stating most call the figure a hypocrite.
2:264/q11 What does the show-off's lack of belief mean? [وَلَا يُؤْمِنُ بِٱللَّهِ وَٱلْيَوْمِ ٱلْءَاخِرِ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the denial is general creed or unbelief expressed specifically in the act of spending.)
  2:264/q11/p1 He denies divine oneness or lordship, resurrection, and recompense. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says this marks a hypocrite; Saʿīd ibn Jubayr glosses unbelief in God as not affirming His oneness.
  2:264/q11/p2 He fails to believe in God's call to spend and the promised reward in this deed, without necessarily disbelieving altogether. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī grounds this in the coordinated present-tense clauses and says ostentation entails unbelief in the relevant promised recompense.
2:264/q12 Whose likeness is introduced by 'his likeness'? [فَمَثَلُهُۥ · referent] (turns on: Which antecedent the singular pronoun selects.)
  2:264/q12/p1 The pronoun returns to the nearest antecedent, the ostentatious spender. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn prefer proximity; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the analogy depicts the spender even when its pronoun can also describe the analogous figure.
  2:264/q12/p2 It returns to the donor who reproaches and harms, making two successive comparisons. — reasons: This is reported as an alternative; al-Samīn notes that it requires a shift in person and number.
2:264/q13 What does ṣafwān denote? [صَفْوَانٍ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the term is explained by smoothness and size or a particular softness/clarity.)
  2:264/q13/p1 It is a smooth stone or large smooth rock. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī transmits ṣafā/ṣafāh and cites a line with ṣafī; al-Thaʿlabī cites gravel planted on a smooth expanse. Al-Wāḥidī and al-Rāzī report al-Aṣmaʿī's equivalent forms ṣafwā, ṣafwān, and ṣafā. Ibn ʿAṭiyya cites Imruʾ al-Qays's slipping horse image and Abū Dhuʾayb's blows on stone at al-Mushaqqar. Ibn ʿAbbās cites Aws ibn Ḥajar's poetry. Al-Qummī's report calls it a large rock.
  2:264/q13/p2 It may denote a softer stone, or a smooth stone named for its clarity. — reasons: Al-Māwardī reports the alternative from Abān ibn Taghlib.
2:264/q14 Is ṣafwān singular, collective, or plural? [صَفْوَانٍ · grammar] (turns on: Whether its number is lexical singular, collective with ṣafwāna, or plural of ṣafā; the singular pronoun عليه and disputed plural patterns serve as evidence.)
  2:264/q14/p1 It is a singular noun, with reported plurals ṣafwān, ṣafī, or ṣafiyy. — reasons: Al-Kisāʾī supplies plural variants; al-Naḥḥās favors singular because عليه uses a singular pronoun and rejects al-Kisāʾī's ṣafī plural analysis. Al-Mubarrad says ṣafī is instead a plural of ṣafā; al-Samīn rejects al-Kisāʾī's singular claim.
  2:264/q14/p2 It is a collective noun whose unit is ṣafwāna. — reasons: Al-Akhfash and al-Thaʿlabī compare ṣafwāna/ṣafwān with tamra/tamr and nakhla/nakhl; Abū al-Baqāʾ favors the collective to explain عليه.
  2:264/q14/p3 It is a plural of ṣafā; ṣafī is likewise a plural of ṣafā. — reasons: Al-Mubarrad rejects al-Kisāʾī's derivations; al-Qurṭubī cites the weak crow's-day analogy for karawān and compares ṣafī/ṣafā to ʿaṣā. Abū al-Baqāʾ calls the fiʿlān plural pattern rare; al-Samīn prefers ṣafwān as plural of ṣafā.
  2:264/q14/p4 Either singular or collective/plural analysis can describe the rock here. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī and al-Ālūsī allow multiple analyses; al-Naḥḥās allows singular or plural while preferring singular.
2:264/q15 What variant vocalization of ṣafwān is reported? [صَفْوَانٍ · readings]
  2:264/q15/p1 Ibn al-Musayyib and al-Zuhrī read it with fatḥa on the fāʾ rather than the common still fāʾ. — reasons: Ibn Jinnī calls the faʿlān pattern common in descriptions and verbal nouns but possible for other nouns. Al-Thaʿlabī cites al-Zaʿrī and compares karawān and warshān. Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it dialectal; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn call it irregular/anomalous in ordinary noun usage; al-Qurṭubī also cites Quṭrub.
2:264/q16 What does turāb mean and what related forms are noted? [تُرَابٌۭ · meaning]
  2:264/q16/p1 It is soil or dust, with related forms and associations of poverty and wealth. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī connects ذا متربة (90:16) to need and notes ترب can mean a peer; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn give توراب, ترب 'become poor,' and أترب 'become rich,' explained by removal of dust or abundance like earth.
  2:264/q16/p2 The related word tarāʾib names chest bones. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī adds this related lexical form.
2:264/q17 How is 'upon it was dust' constructed? [عَلَيْهِ تُرَابٌۭ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the whole dust clause or only the prepositional phrase qualifies the stone.)
  2:264/q17/p1 The phrase is an adjectival clause describing the stone. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī says the dust clause qualifies the smooth stone; al-Samīn lists the full clause as an option.
  2:264/q17/p2 عليه alone modifies the stone, with dust as the subject of an understood 'settled upon it'. — reasons: Al-Samīn prefers this parsing and compares 2:261; Abū Ḥayyān supplies the underlying verb and coordinates فأصابه with it.
2:264/q18 What is wābil? [وَابِلٌۭ · meaning]
  2:264/q18/p1 It is heavy, abundant, forceful rain, often with large drops. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī cites Imruʾ al-Qays for driving rain and gives conjugations; al-Māwardī and al-Kashshāf specify large drops. Al-Naḍr lists stages from رش/طش through drizzle and drops to وابل and جودة; al-Samīn gives بلت/أوبل and says the adjective can stand alone. ʿIkrima glosses rain while al-Rabīʿ and Qatāda specify heavy rain. Al-Qummī also distinguishes nighttime dew ṭall.
  2:264/q18/p2 Its root also conveys severity or a bad consequence. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī adduces severe taking at 73:16 and blows or punishment; al-Samīn adds وبيل for harshness, وبيلة for a firewood bundle and thick objects.
2:264/q19 What is the antecedent of 'it' in 'it struck it,' and how is the verb formed? [فَأَصَابَهُۥ · grammar]
  2:264/q19/p1 The rain strikes either the stone or its dust; 'then left it' refers to the stone. — reasons: Both grammarians coordinate فأصابه with the understood settling verb; both restrict the later pronoun to the stone.
  2:264/q19/p2 The verb aṣāba derives from ṣāba/yaṣūbu, its alif from wāw. — reasons: Al-Samīn gives the morphological derivation.
2:264/q20 What does ṣald mean? [صَلْدًۭا · meaning] (turns on: Whether the relevant quality is exposed hard barrenness or the reported softness of the stone.)
  2:264/q20/p1 It means bare, clean, hard, smooth, barren, with nothing remaining or growing on the stone. — reasons: Qatāda, Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Suddī, al-Ḍaḥḥāk and others say no soil remains; Ibn Abī Ḥātim adds dry, fruitless and disgraced. Al-Ṭabarī cites Ruʾba's bare forehead and Taʾabbaṭa Sharran's hard isolated rock; al-Thaʿlabī and Ibn ʿAṭiyya repeat the bald-head use. Al-Naqqāsh calls it Hudhayl usage. Al-Ṭabrisī extends it to sterile ground, miser, unproductive flint and resistance to flow; al-Wāḥidī gives a fire-starter that fails to spark; al-Ṭabarī and ʿAlī ibn ʿĪsā mention a slow-boiling pot. Ibn ʿAbbās cites Abū Ṭālib's poetry.
  2:264/q20/p2 It can specifically describe soft stone. — reasons: Al-Samīn reports Abān ibn Taghlib's gloss.
2:264/q21 What grammatical role does ṣaldan have? [فَتَرَكَهُۥ صَلْدًۭا · grammar]
  2:264/q21/p1 It is an accusative circumstantial state of the stone after the downpour. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī gives this parsing.
2:264/q22 What does the rock, soil, and rain parable portray? [فَمَثَلُهُۥ كَمَثَلِ صَفْوَانٍ عَلَيْهِ تُرَابٌۭ فَأَصَابَهُۥ وَابِلٌۭ فَتَرَكَهُۥ صَلْدًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the act was empty from the outset and exposed at judgment, or earned reward later erased by reproach; sources also differ over one composite likeness and separate mapped elements.)
