2:266/q14 Why say old age struck him, and what does old age imply? [وَأَصَابَهُ ٱلْكِبَرُ · rhetoric]
  2:266/q14/p1 It stresses the owner's reduced earning power and increased need, making the loss irreparable; asabahu makes the impact vivid. — reasons: Al-Mawardi adds that old age can erase memory of youthful labor, weaken hope and increase regret. Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Razi, al-Bursavi, al-Tabatabai and Ibn Ashur stress inability to earn and the dependents' needs; al-Tabatabai contrasts young owners and elders with other support. Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi note the force of repeated asaba; Abu Hayyan compares the neighboring garden and stone examples, 2:264-265. Ibn Ashur cites al-Asha's poem for anticipation of harvest; al-Biqai emphasizes their lost wealth. Ibn Qutayba reports Ibn Abbas and others that age and weak children make the orchard indispensable exactly when it is lost; Jishumi adds that the family is left poor, helpless, needy and bewildered. Al-Farra notes the Arabic allowance for repeating a form to emphasize, among other purposes.
2:266/q15 What does al-kibar mean? [ٱلْكِبَرُ · meaning]
  2:266/q15/p1 Old age, a kind of greatness in age rather than multitude. — reasons: Abu Hayyan glosses it as old age. Al-Tabrisi distinguishes kabir, size or age even of one house, from kathir, which necessarily implies number. Damghani confirms old age here, comparing 28:23 and 12:78; Jishumi specifies gray hair. Ibn al-Jawzi's lexical survey says kabir is relational and elsewhere signifies might, intensity, burden, number or seniority in judgment, whereas this passage uses advanced age.
2:266/q16 Who are the weak offspring, and why mention them? [وَلَهُۥ ذُرِّيَّةٌۭ ضُعَفَآءُ · interpretation]
  2:266/q16/p1 Young, helpless dependents cannot work, support him or restore the orchard, intensifying his care and the loss. — reasons: Ibn Abbas calls them part of the parable. Al-Mawardi and Ibn al-Jawzi note a parent's special tenderness; al-Nasafi calls the garden their livelihood too. Al-Tabatabai says an elder with strong children could rely on them, unlike this owner. Ibn Ashur says daif can mean young, limited or poor, citing 2:282 and Abu Khalid al-Attabi's verse; Abu Hayyan also allows needy dependents. Bursavi and al-Alusi stress inability to earn. Damghani glosses daif as unable to manage or devise an escape, while distinguishing that sense from believers at Uhud who were not helpless fighters. Jishumi says the children cannot support themselves and that the loss leaves them poor and bewildered.
2:266/q17 What forms are reported for the plural weak? [ضُعَفَآءُ · readings] (turns on: Equivalent plural forms and their readings.)
  2:266/q17/p1 Duafa is the verse form, a regular plural of daif. — reasons: Al-Akhfash compares fail plurals such as zurafa and zuraf. Al-Tabari compares 4:9; Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi recognize both plural patterns. Jishumi identifies duafa as a plural of daif and compares karim/kurama; al-Burhan points to the different plural forms for vulnerable offspring here and in the warning about children.
  2:266/q17/p2 Di'af or du'af is another regular plural form or variant reading. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf notes a weak offspring reading; al-Tabari cites 4:9 for diafa, while al-Akhfash, Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi give the alternate plural patterns. Jishumi distinguishes duaf as a plural of daf from duafa as a plural of daif; al-Burhan contrasts the plural wording with the other warning about vulnerable children.
2:266/q18 How is the clause about offspring attached? [وَلَهُۥ ذُرِّيَّةٌۭ ضُعَفَآءُ · grammar]
  2:266/q18/p1 It is circumstantial, describing the owner when old age strikes. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and al-Nasafi take the waw as circumstantial; al-Samin derives the circumstance from the pronoun in asabahu.
2:266/q19 What is an i'sar? [إِعْصَارٌۭ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the word itself is a general rotating wind, a hot samum, or a cold blast.)
  2:266/q19/p1 A fierce, rotating wind or whirlwind rising like a column, often lifting dust. — reasons: Al-Tabari gives its plural a'asir and cites Yazid ibn Mufarrigh; al-Zajjaj calls it zawba'a and cites an unnamed poet. Al-Thalabi cites al-Kumayt and Yazid ibn al-Muqri; Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Razi, al-Tabrisi, al-Samin and Ibn Abbas in al-Durr cite poetry or the wind proverb. Al-Jawhari adds umm zawba'a and says the name also belongs to a jinn chief. Al-Alusi describes colliding air currents, possible upward or downward motion and force enough to uproot trees or snatch boats. Al-Tabatabai describes a twisting dust column between earth and sky. Damghani says the fierce wind can be cold or hot. Jishumi describes its column rising from ground to sky, or a wind that raises clouds. Al-Itqan cites an anonymous poetic line and gives the singular-plural pair isar/aasir. Bint al-Shati cites Ibn Abbas's fierce-wind gloss and poem, al-Jawhari's dust-pillar or thundercloud, al-Tabari's comparable gloss and al-Raghib's dust-stirring wind; she finds the severe-wind reading suited to the root.
  2:266/q19/p2 It is specifically the deadly hot wind called samum. — reasons: Al-Suddi gives this gloss through al-Mawardi; Ibn Atiyya calls the whirlwind violent and scorching. Ibn Zayd and al-Suddi in al-Qurtubi describe the hot wind. Ibn Ashur identifies a violent hot wind likened to fire or samum. Bint al-Shati reports al-Tabari's transmission from Ibn Abbas that the fierce wind carries scorching heat, including samum.
  2:266/q19/p3 It is a cold blast or frost. — reasons: Al-Hasan gives this second gloss in Ibn Abi Hatim; al-Hasan and al-Dahhak elsewhere describe cold within the whirlwind.
2:266/q20 Why is the whirlwind called i'sar? [إِعْصَارٌۭ · meaning] (turns on: Which observed squeezing or twisting motivates the word; Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi disagree about the cloth derivation.)
  2:266/q20/p1 Its coiling motion resembles cloth being wrung. — reasons: Al-Mawardi cites a poem; al-Mahdawi gives the derivation in Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi. Ibn Atiyya calls it weak, while al-Qurtubi endorses it as observable; al-Samin, al-Alusi and al-Tabatabai also mention twisting cloth.
  2:266/q20/p2 The wind squeezes or compresses clouds or bodies. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya cites the wind proverb and relates mu'sirat either to pregnant rain clouds or clouds compressed by winds; he reports al-Anbari and Ibn Sida's alternatives, the latter taking mu'sirat as winds. Al-Qurtubi repeats the derivations. Abu Hayyan describes a hot cloud-compressing wind; al-Harali links the name to squeezing hidden contents. Bint al-Shati derives the asr forms from pressure and extraction, exemplified by grape pressing, rain-producing clouds and the wind driving them. She notes the whirlwind form occurs once in the Quran and compares related forms in Yusuf, al-Naba and al-Asr; she takes the strong-wind sense to fit that root.
2:266/q21 What is the fire in the whirlwind? [فِيهِ نَارٌۭ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the destructive fire is heat, cold, actual flame, lightning, or Hell's breath.)
  2:266/q21/p1 Scorching samum or exceptionally hot wind. — reasons: Ibn Abbas repeatedly calls it samum; Qatada calls the wind a carrier of it and al-Suddi distinguishes wind from its fire. Al-Tabari also transmits Ibn Abbas that jinn-creating samum is one seventieth of fire; Ibn Atiyya attributes this proportion to Ibn Masud and identifies the Fire as the afterlife's, while Abu Hayyan repeats it. Ibn Ashur says the wind is emphatically likened to fire. Al-Alusi says the indefinite fire magnifies it. Jishumi reports a scorching wind that burns the fruit; Bint al-Shati reports Ibn Abbas's fierce wind carrying samum through al-Tabari.
  2:266/q21/p2 Biting cold, frost or hail that burns and ruins the garden. — reasons: Al-Hasan gives bitter cold in Abd al-Razzaq and severe cold and hail in al-Tabari; al-Dahhak agrees. Al-Harali calls a cold edge a kind of fire.
  2:266/q21/p3 It may be literal fire carried by the wind. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya allows literal fire, and al-Tabrisi states that the wind's fire burns the garden. Jishumi also reports the explanation of a fierce wind containing fire.
  2:266/q21/p4 It may be lightning. — reasons: Abu Hayyan records this alternate explanation.
  2:266/q21/p5 Its heat or cold may be a breath of Hell. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya cites a Prophetic report about intense heat and the Fire's complaint; al-Qurtubi reports his view and the prayer-delaying report.
2:266/q22 How is fihi nar parsed? [فِيهِ نَارٌۭ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the locative itself modifies the whirlwind or belongs to a modifying clause.)
  2:266/q22/p1 Fihi is a modifying locative phrase and nar is its raised subject. — reasons: Al-Nasafi says the locative functions as an adjective of i'sar. Abu Hayyan says the masculine pronoun agrees with masculine i'sar. Al-Samin prefers this direct adjectival parse for simplicity.
  2:266/q22/p2 Fihi is the predicate of nar, and the entire clause modifies i'sar. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and al-Samin allow this analysis; al-Samin prefers the simpler direct locative adjective.
2:266/q23 What do the successive fa and burning verb convey? [فَأَصَابَهَآ فَٱحْتَرَقَتْ · grammar]
  2:266/q23/p1 The storm strikes after the garden has become useful, then the garden burns up as the intransitive result of burning it. — reasons: Abu Hayyan reads the successive fa as sequence after ripening; he and al-Samin distinguish intransitive ihtaraqa from transitive ahraqa. Al-Samin gives the burning then burned-up sequence; both cite the expression harqa
