## THE CURRENT MAP
79:46/q01 Who are the people described as seeing it? [كَأَنَّهُمْ · referent] (turns on: Whether the verse's general plural is read as humanity at resurrection or as the deniers addressed by the closing warning.)
  79:46/q01/p1 People generally, when confronted with the Resurrection or Hereafter. — reasons: Muqatil, Qatada as transmitted by Abd al-Razzaq and al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir and others describe people without restricting the group. Ibn Kathir places the perception after they rise from their graves.
  79:46/q01/p2 The disbelievers, specifically the Meccan or Quraysh unbelievers in some accounts. — reasons: Al-Tabari calls them deniers; al-Wahidi al-Basit and Ibn al-Jawzi identify Quraysh, as does al-Baghawi; Bursevi particularly names Meccan unbelievers. Al-Biqai says disbelievers give differing estimates of their stay.
79:46/q02 What do they see? [يَوْمَ يَرَوْنَهَا · referent]
  79:46/q02/p1 The Hour, Resurrection, or the reality of the Hereafter that had been promised. — reasons: Muqatil and al-Tabari say the Hour; al-Wahidi al-Basit says what they denied; al-Razi says what was promised. Ibn Kathir places the sight after rising from the graves; al-Tusi's reported Qatada wording speaks of the affair of the Hereafter.
79:46/q03 Which stay will seem brief? [لَمْ يَلْبَثُوٓا۟ · interpretation] (turns on: The antecedent of the implied stay: earthly life, the grave period, or the time since the question and warning; al-Tabatabai tests this against what is witnessed at resurrection and what immediately precedes it.)
  79:46/q03/p1 Their earthly life. — reasons: Qatada, reported by Abd al-Razzaq, al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir and al-Suyuti, says the worldly life shrinks when the Hereafter is seen. Al-Mawardi cites 10:45; al-Wahidi al-Basit and al-Razi invoke 46:35. Al-Tabatabai cites 23:114 while rejecting this as the intended sojourn. Alusi allows this for some people.
  79:46/q03/p2 Their time in the graves, or the interval from death to resurrection. — reasons: Al-Wahidi al-Wajiz attributes their underestimation to the terror of resurrection. Al-Qurtubi reports this from some exegetes. Study Quran calls it the view of most commentators and cites 10:45, 17:52, 20:103–104, 23:112–114, 30:55 and 46:35. Al-Tabatabai prefers it because all earthly life is counted for judgment; Alusi allows it for others. Some sources present world and grave as alternatives, while al-Biqai mentions both together.
  79:46/q03/p3 The interval from asking about, or being warned of, the Hour until its arrival. — reasons: Al-Tabatabai reports and rejects the interval from asking: people witness the span only at resurrection, and death rather than their question precedes that sight. Alusi prefers understanding a short stay after the warning or threat; the context of the impatient challenge supports it.
79:46/q04 How long does their stay seem to have lasted? [إِلَّا عَشِيَّةً أَوْ ضُحَىٰهَا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the stated evening or morning remains a fraction of one day or is expressed in related reports as an hour or a day.)
  79:46/q04/p1 Only one end or part of a day, an evening or morning, less than a complete day. — reasons: Muqatil describes afternoon to sunset or morning until the sun rises higher; al-Tabari calls them the first and last parts of a day. Al-Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, al-Nasafi, al-Biqai and others stress less than a whole day. Al-Qummi, reported by al-Tabatabai, also says part of a day; al-Qurtubi relates the short stay to 10:45.
  79:46/q04/p2 As little as an hour or moment. — reasons: Al-Wahidi al-Basit and al-Razi compare 46:35. Al-Biqai recalls the judgment scene in 10:45 where people say an hour, alongside reports of a day or part of one; he says differing estimates express the same brevity beside the endless future.
  79:46/q04/p3 One whole day. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi transmits this from al-Dahhak on Ibn Abbas. Al-Biqai links an answer of a day or part of one to the question about years in 23:112–113.
79:46/q05 What time does ʿashiyyah denote here? [عَشِيَّةً · meaning]
  79:46/q05/p1 The later part of the day, from noon or afternoon until sunset. — reasons: Muqatil gives afternoon to sunset; Ibn Abbas as transmitted by Ibn Kathir defines it from noon to sunset. Al-Tabari and al-Farra treat it as the day's final end; al-Qurtubi calls it afternoon. Sībawayh reports al-Khalil's plural ʿashiyāt/ʿashiyānāt for successive portions of an evening, each reckoned an ʿashiyyah.
79:46/q06 What does ḍuḥā mean in this phrase? [ضُحَىٰهَا · meaning] (turns on: Whether ḍuḥā names a bounded morning period, extends to the whole day, or symbolizes worldly life; Bint al-Shati appeals to other Quranic uses.)
  79:46/q06/p1 The day's bright morning or forenoon, from sunrise until noon in explicit definitions. — reasons: Muqatil says morning until the sun rises higher; Ibn Abbas as transmitted by Ibn Kathir and Bursevi define sunrise to noon. Bint al-Shati cites Quranic uses opposite evening, the bringing forth of sunlight at 79:29, and appointed event time at 7:98 and 20:59; these oppose taking it as the whole day. Al-Damghani defines ḍuḥā as the sunlit beginning of the day, opposed here to evening. Ibn al-Jawzi derives it from visibility and the spread of sunlight, adducing ordinary uses for open exposure, sacrifice at forenoon, bright nights, and prominent outskirts; he and reported exegetes identify 79:46 as a forenoon use, also citing 20:59 and 93:1.
  79:46/q06/p2 The early or dawn side of the day. — reasons: Al-Wahidi al-Wajiz and al-Baghawi describe the early morning or dawn; Ibn al-Jawzi calls it the first part. Al-Farra's Arabic pairing makes it the beginning opposed to evening's end.
  79:46/q06/p3 It can stand metaphorically for the whole day. — reasons: Al-Tibi, reported by Alusi, allows evening or forenoon to stand for the day; the added pronoun reinforces it, like saying one saw with one's own eyes. Alusi allows the pronoun to reinforce this reading too. Bint al-Shati weighs against it from Quranic usage. Al-Zarkashi says the short forenoon can stand for the whole day.
  79:46/q06/p4 The morning represents worldly life when people see the Hereafter. — reasons: Study Quran reports this from some commentators and mentions 93:1.
79:46/q07 How does 'or its forenoon' attach the morning to the evening? [عَشِيَّةً أَوْ ضُحَىٰهَا · grammar] (turns on: Whether the suffix has a same-day antecedent, licenses an adjacent morning, is merely additive, or reinforces a whole-day metaphor; the Quranic phrasing and Arabic idiom are invoked.)
  79:46/q07/p1 The suffix refers to the evening by an expansive attachment: its forenoon is the morning of the same day. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj and Ibn al-Jawzi identify the final alif and ha as referring to ʿashiyyah; al-Tabrisi, al-Razi's grammarians, al-Qurtubi, al-Nasafi and others license the attachment because both periods belong to one day, though morning ordinarily precedes evening. Al-Samin calls it figurative and notes the cadence. Al-Farra's example from a Banu Uqayl poet uses ʿashiyyat al-hilal for the new moon's setting or hidden phase; al-Thaʿlabi, Ibn al-Jawzi and al-Qurtubi repeat it. Ibn Atiyya cites brevity; Abu Hayyan calls the idafa expansive. Ibn ʿAbd al-Salam, as reported by al-Zarkashi, says relating the forenoon to the evening's day makes the spans comparable: a long day's forenoon could otherwise equal a short day's evening.
  79:46/q07/p2 The expression can pair an evening with its following morning, as Arabic pairs the ends of adjacent periods. — reasons: Al-Farra allows an evening with the next morning or a morning with its previous evening. Al-Zajjaj distinguishes 'morning and its evening' of the same day from 'evening and its morning' of the following day. Al-Wahidi and al-Razi report al-Farra and al-Zajjaj's day association while also citing the next-morning idiom; Ibn al-Jawzi transmits al-Farra's paired-end explanation. Al-Kashshaf as reported by Alusi says the possessive wording rules out a stretch from one day's evening to another day's forenoon.
  79:46/q07/p3 The final alif and ha are grammatical additions, so the sense is simply 'an evening or a morning'. — reasons: Ata from Ibn Abbas, reported by al-Wahidi al-Basit and al-Razi, treats the ending as additive rather than a time relation.
  79:46/q07/p4 The suffix reinforces a metaphor in which either end stands for the entire day. — reasons: Al-Tibi, reported by Alusi, compares the emphasis to 'I saw with my own eyes.' Alusi allows this reinforcement, though he prefers the less forced ordinary attachment.
  79:46/q07/p5 The suffix refers to the day implied by the evening, since an evening itself has no morning. — reasons: Al-Suyuti explicitly assigns the pronoun to the implied day. Al-Zarkashi also identifies it as that same day's forenoon.
79:46/q08 Why is their past stay perceived as so brief? [كَأَنَّهُمْ لَمْ يَلْبَثُوٓا۟ · interpretation] (turns on: The notes variously ground the perceived compression in encounter with the Hereafter, terror, ordinary perception of the past, or the nature of earthly time.)
  79:46/q08/p1 Seeing the Hereafter or Resurrection makes worldly duration shrink in retrospect. — reasons: Qatada's formulation appears through Abd al-Razzaq, al-Tabari, al-Tabrisi, Ibn Kathir and al-Suyuti. Al-Mawardi compares 10:45; al-Wahidi al-Basit 46:35. Al-Biqai contrasts the moment with endless future and cites the Prophet's image of a finger dipped in the sea.
  79:46/q08/p2 The terror of what they see makes the grave or worldly stay seem short. — reasons: Al-Wahidi al-Wajiz expressly attributes underestimation to terror; al-Qurtubi's reported grave view does likewise. Al-Nasafi and Jishumi stress the Resurrection's overwhelming condition and terrors.
  79:46/q08/p3 Past time feels short because it is finished, while an uncertain future draws attention and anxiety. — reasons: Kur'an Yolu gives this psychological explanation.
