it.
  18:19/q37/p4 The youths' fear of discovery was an excuse for delegation. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi says all scholars agree an excused person may appoint an agent, even where they dispute healthy present persons.
18:19/q38 Does the shared silver support pooling money and food? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ هَٰذِهِۦٓ · law] (turns on: Whether the plural possessive establishes shared ownership or merely a pooled errand.)
  18:19/q38/p1 Yes: companions may pool costs, buy jointly and eat together despite unequal appetites. — reasons: Al-Mawardi calls this munahada; Ibn Khuwayz Mindad cites this verse and 2:220; al-Qurtubi cites school permission to mix donated meals and the orphan and shared eating verses (2:220, cited as 24:16). Al-Kiya permits an eater to pay extra for a portion.
  18:19/q38/p2 Their communal purse expresses mutual care and rebukes habitual begging. — reasons: Alusi says one youth may have had no money, perhaps a shepherd, yet the group calls it theirs; Tabatabai sees equal delegation and mutual concern.
  18:19/q38/p3 The verse alone does not prove a partnership, since each might have handed over money separately. — reasons: Ibn al-Arabi relies instead on hadiths. Ibn Umar's two-dates prohibition concerns companions' permission; al-Qurtubi says the army-of-khabt report may reflect equal distribution, so its joint-eating proof is weaker.
18:19/q39 What does their carrying provisions teach about trust in God? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ · theology] (turns on: The dispute concerns ordinary means versus exceptional abandonment by those held to have special certainty.)
  18:19/q39/p1 Prudent provisioning and work are compatible with reliance on God. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf cites Aisha on a pilgrim's money belt and an ascetic scholar's refusal of patrons; al-Razi and Ibn Kathir agree. Bursevi cites the Mathnawi: cultivate before relying; Alusi invokes tying the camel. Abu Hayyan and al-Nasafi repeat the money belt maxim.
  18:19/q39/p2 Those especially firm in certainty may relinquish ordinary means. — reasons: Alusi records eminent scholars who cite Khalid drinking poison and other extraordinary reports; Ahmad, Ishaq and others permit desert travel without supplies or foregoing earning and treatment. Abraham's refusal of Gabriel's help illustrates direct reliance. Alusi rejects attributing this only to Sufis.
18:19/q40 What does the brief time estimate teach about judgment? [لَبِثْنَا يَوْمًا أَوْ بَعْضَ يَوْمٍۢ · law] (turns on: The first position concerns permissibility of probable judgment; the second explains its limit.)
  18:19/q40/p1 Reasoned conjecture can be acted upon and is not a lie if later shown mistaken. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf derives permission for ijtihad; al-Nasafi, al-Biqai, Abu Hayyan and Bursevi say their mistaken estimate remained sincere. Tabatabai distinguishes judgment about duration from blameworthy dissension in action.
  18:19/q40/p2 An exact factual judgment is unavailable from signs alone, so knowledge returns to God. — reasons: Tabatabai says sun, light and shadow cannot determine a full day; humans infer through signs while God knows each event directly.
18:19/q41 How is this scene linked to resurrection and the perception of time? [لَبِثْنَا يَوْمًا أَوْ بَعْضَ يَوْمٍۢ · links] (turns on: The parallel may serve bodily resurrection, Barzakh perception, or a wider lesson on the nature of time.)
  18:19/q41/p1 The youths' brief estimate parallels the revived passerby and people at resurrection or in Barzakh. — reasons: Elmalili cites 2:259; Study Quran lists 2:259, 10:45, 17:52, 20:104, 23:112–13, 30:55, 46:35 and 79:46. Asad connects 2:259 and creation-life-resurrection passages (3:27, 6:95, 10:31, 30:19). Islahi takes it as a preview of perceived Barzakh time; Tabatabai likens worldly life to a brief dream and quotes Ali that people awaken on dying.
  18:19/q41/p2 The youths' answer discloses the deception of earthly time perception. — reasons: Asad says an earthbound conception of time is deceptive; Qushayri's inward reading says days of union feel short, citing poems.
  18:19/q41/p3 The story shows truth survives long spans despite apparent triumph of coercive falsehood. — reasons: Tabatabai compares the century-old revival at 2:259 and says time neither destroys truth nor revives falsehood.
18:19/q42 What inward reading is given to their duration and awakening? [بَعَثْنَٰهُمْ لَبِثْنَا يَوْمًا أَوْ بَعْضَ يَوْمٍۢ · inward] (turns on: The notes read the temporal disorientation as a spiritual state.)
  18:19/q42/p1 Spiritual awakening follows absorption; apparent time vanishes in love or union. — reasons: Qashani reads the youths as faculties awakening to discuss latent truths; al-Qushayri says self-absorption removes detailed awareness and cites poetry on short-feeling union; Ibn Ata says longing makes any union seem short. Alusi describes sobriety after intoxication and cites a poem comparing a life of union to uncounted nights.
  18:19/q42/p2 Return to embodied sobriety makes food an immediate concern. — reasons: Al-Qushayri says bodily appetite returns after absorption; Alusi says spiritual principles must then be enacted in ordinary life.
18:19/q43 What do the silver, city and food signify inwardly? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ ٱلْمَدِينَةِ أَزْكَىٰ طَعَامًۭا · inward] (turns on: The allegorical mappings differ: knowledge, intellect, or scrupulous livelihood.)
  18:19/q43/p1 Silver is innate first principles, the city a gathering place of knowledge, and food sound knowledge that nourishes the heart. — reasons: Qashani cites the saying that Muhammad is the city of knowledge and Ali its gate; he rejects empty dialectic and superficial appearances, with 88:7 as a contrast. One learner may then teach the rest, as 9:122 suggests.
  18:19/q43/p2 The envoy is thought among spiritual faculties, seeking intellect instead of illusion, imagination or sensation. — reasons: Qashani gives this alternate symbolic mapping of youths and city.
  18:19/q43/p3 Pure food signifies scrupulous lawful sustenance that preserves insight. — reasons: Alusi quotes Dhu al-Nun on not extinguishing gnosis with scruple; he criticizes a shaykh accepting money known to come from an oppressor.
18:19/q44 What inward instruction is drawn from acting gently and concealing the youths? [وَلْيَتَلَطَّفْ وَلَا يُشْعِرَنَّ بِكُمْ أَحَدًا · inward] (turns on: The inward application concerns spiritual training and the audience for hidden knowledge.)
  18:19/q44/p1 Choose an upright, verified guide and deal gently with spiritually mature people. — reasons: Qashani warns against pretenders; Sulami transmits Abu Uthman that gnostics receive gentleness and novices firmness; Yusuf ibn al-Husayn gives gnostics delicate food and ascetics ordinary food. Qushayri reports the same food distinction. Alusi criticizes a mandatory delicate-food rule because major exemplars lived simply.
  18:19/q44/p2 Conceal spiritual realities from people unable to receive them. — reasons: Qashani says ignorant people should not be told the youths' state or faith; Alusi says veiled people cannot perceive such light.
18:19/q45 Why must discovery be avoided, and what threat follows? [وَلَا يُشْعِرَنَّ بِكُمْ أَحَدًا · interpretation] (turns on: Sources parse the danger as discovery followed by bodily or verbal attack and coercion.)
  18:19/q45/p1 The youths feared being found by persecutors, stoned, or forced back to idolatry. — reasons: Yahya and the narrative sources say discovery would destroy their religious success; Tabatabai says stoning signals collective hostility. Abu Hayyan prefers literal stoning over verbal abuse and says 'return' can mean entering an old creed even without earlier membership. Tabatabai says forced participation, not merely verbal assent, was feared.
  18:19/q45/p2 The next verse's 'appearance over you' can mean discovering their location or gaining ascendancy. — reasons: Abu Hayyan gives both; Tabatabai cites al-Raghib's semantic path from visibility to finding and prevailing, preferring location discovery because it leads to capture.
  18:19/q45/p3 The threatened rajm can mean verbal abuse. — reasons: Hajjaj and Ibn Jubayr are reported with this gloss; Abu Hayyan favors stoning because the king meant to kill and earlier people used it against dissent.
  18:19/q45/p4 Someone trapped under coercion may outwardly comply while preserving faith, but a person free to leave must avoid voluntarily losing religious practice. — reasons: Tabatabai cites 16:106 and 3:28 for coerced dissimulation, 4:97 against self-imposed helplessness, and rejects proposed explanations that coercion itself causes apostasy or that dissimulation was once barred.
18:19/q46 How do later variant readings shape the threatened discovery? [وَلَا يُشْعِرَنَّ بِكُمْ أَحَدًا · readings] (turns on: The following verse's verb has an active/passive variant and disputed pronoun antecedent.)
  18:19/q46/p1 Zayd ibn Ali reads yuẓhirū passively in the following threat. — reasons: Abu Hayyan reports this reading as a continuation of the secrecy warning.
  18:19/q46/p2 The ordinary active form means the city people find or overpower the youths. — reasons: Abu Hayyan says the pronoun may denote inferred unbelievers, generic aḥad, or elided townspeople; al-Zamakhshari favors the townspeople, invoking 23:8 for plural after a generic.
18:19/q47 What does the scene show about the youths' fellowship? [بَيْنَهُمْ بِوَرِقِكُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: The notes draw communal ethics and an inward pedagogy from the plural dialogue.)
  18:19/q47/p1 They share inquiry, resources, responsibility and concern for one another's faith and safety. — reasons: Al-Biqai sees affectionate agreement in deferring knowledge to God; Tabatabai highlights equal delegation, communal money and tact. Alusi reads 'your silver' as shared even if one youth had none, and the narratives recall their pledge never to abandon each other.
  18:19/q47/p2 One fully developed member can learn on behalf of the group and awaken the others
