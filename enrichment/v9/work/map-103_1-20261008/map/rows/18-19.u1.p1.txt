18:19/q13 How many youths does the dialogue imply? [قَالَ قَآئِلٌۭ مِّنْهُمْ قَالُوا۟ · interpretation]
  18:19/q13/p1 At least seven: one asks and two plural replies require three speakers each. — reasons: Al-Nasafi attributes the inference to Ibn Abbas; Tabatabai gives the same count from the singular and two plurals.
18:19/q14 Why do they turn from duration to sending for food? [فَٱبْعَثُوٓا۟ أَحَدَكُم · interpretation] (turns on: Both hunger and fear shape the errand.)
  18:19/q14/p1 They woke hungry and redirected an unknowable discussion to an immediate need. — reasons: Al-Tabari says they needed food; al-Kashshaf, Abu Hayyan and Ibn Ashur call the pivot wiser than continuing debate; al-Qushayri says hunger followed a period without bodily appetites; Alusi points to fa as marking the redirect.
  18:19/q14/p2 The errand also let the envoy learn news about their pursuers. — reasons: Al-Tabari and al-Tha'labi report instructions to buy more food and investigate; they still believed Decianus sought them.
18:19/q15 Who was sent to the city? [أَحَدَكُم · referent] (turns on: Narrative reports name the envoy; the verse leaves him unnamed.)
  18:19/q15/p1 Yamlikha, also called Tamlikha, was the envoy. — reasons: Muqatil names Yamliha; al-Tabari transmits Muqatil and narrative forms; al-Tha'labi, al-Baghawi, al-Nasafi and others give variants. Al-Ghaznawi calls him the youngest; al-Wahidi has their leader Tamlikha speak earlier.
  18:19/q15/p2 An unnamed member of their group went; the command need not identify him. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says the group may address an unspecified delegate or omit the known name; the Quran's 'one of you' emphasizes membership.
  18:19/q15/p3 The wording 'one of you' avoids suggesting a specially honored chief. — reasons: Ibn al-Anbari contrasts aḥad with wāḥid; Alusi says the latter might imply their chief.
18:19/q16 How does 'with your silver' attach to the envoy? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ · grammar]
  18:19/q16/p1 It is circumstantial to 'one of you': the envoy goes carrying the silver. — reasons: Al-Samin describes the prepositional phrase as a circumstantial qualifier.
18:19/q17 What occupations do reports give the youths? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ · reports]
  18:19/q17/p1 They were money changers. — reasons: Abu Ja'far's report calls them money changers; al-Sadiq distinguishes changers in speech from dealers in coins.
18:19/q18 What is 'your silver'? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the noun denotes silver generally or minted coins in its lexical scope.)
  18:19/q18/p1 Silver, whether struck as dirhams or unminted; in context it was their money. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba and later authors cite the Arfaja silver-nose report to show unminted silver is included. Al-Asma'i's paper-leaf explanation is dismissed by Alusi. Al-Mawardi distinguishes a fatḥa livestock sense attested in poetry. Biqai develops a lexical family involving foliage, beauty, wealth and gathering; Islahi accordingly translates 'currency.'
  18:19/q18/p2 Specifically coined silver dirhams. — reasons: The wording and old coin reports support a struck currency; Ibn Ashur calls it a minted coin; several authors describe the king's stamped image. Alusi reports this narrower common explanation.
18:19/q19 How is bi-wariqikum read? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ · readings] (turns on: The variants concern vowel easing and the qāf-kāf contact; sources disagree on which assimilations are sound.)
  18:19/q19/p1 Fatḥa on wāw and sukūn on rāʾ lightens pronunciation. — reasons: Al-Farra names Asim and al-A'mash; most Kufans/Basrans, Abu Amr, Hamza and Abu Bakr are often named. Ibn Khalawayh, al-Nahhas and Qiraat-Er explain avoiding a heavy sequence of vowels.
  18:19/q19/p2 Assimilation of qāf into kāf, sometimes with a still rāʾ, is transmitted. — reasons: Al-Tabari reports a Meccan form; al-Zajjaj permits it and al-Farisi explains movement from the rear qāf to nearer kāf. Abu Hatim transmits Ibn Muhaysin; Ibn Jinni says possible, yet calls Abu Raja's form concealed articulation rather than full assimilation because full assimilation transfers the vowel. Abu Hayyan challenges a reported attribution to Ibn Kathir.
  18:19/q19/p3 Fatḥa on wāw and kasra on rāʾ, with qāf pronounced, is the common or original reading. — reasons: Al-Tabari prefers the original kasra pattern but accepts alternatives; Ibn Khalawayh calls kasra original. Ibn Mujahid, al-Farisi, al-Qurtubi and others name Ibn Kathir, Nafi, Ibn Amir, al-Kisa'i and Hafs; al-Tabari lists Medinan and some Iraqi readers. The forms are Arabic dialect variants with the same sense.
  18:19/q19/p4 Kasra on wāw, with varying rāʾ and assimilation, is a rare dialectal reading. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj judges kasra wāw least acceptable; al-Farra compares kibid and kalima. Ibn Jinni treats Abu Raja's form as concealed articulation; Zamakhshari and Razi object to two consecutive still letters in Ibn Muhaysin's form; Abu Hayyan cites a nonassimilated Zajjaj form.
  18:19/q19/p5 Ali reads bawāriqikum, a collective form relating to silver. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and al-Samin describe its pattern; Alusi mentions it.
18:19/q20 What does the demonstrative 'this' add to the silver? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ هَٰذِهِۦٓ · rhetoric]
  18:19/q20/p1 It points to money at hand, perhaps held out or handed to the envoy. — reasons: Bursevi infers a companion physically handed it over; Alusi says the speaker held it out. Tabatabai says the unusually emphatic mention foreshadows how the aged coin exposed them.
18:19/q21 What were the coins like and where had they come from? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ هَٰذِهِۦٓ · reports] (turns on: Narratives add the old ruler's imprint to the verse's bare mention of silver.)
  18:19/q21/p1 They carried old coins from the reign they fled, often bearing the persecuting king's image. — reasons: Al-Wahidi and Ibn Abbas say the stamped image was the old king's; al-Tabari dates the coins 309 years earlier; al-Tha'labi says Daqyanus' seal. Ibn Kathir says they brought and spent money when fleeing; Abu Hayyan says they brought it for expenses. Ibn Abbas likens the currency to camel hooves; later reports call the ruler Decius.
  18:19/q21/p2 Their coin was silver but its exact minting is not specified by the verse. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, al-Kashshaf and al-Razi allow coined and uncoined silver; al-Wahidi cites the old stamped-coin narrative as a separate report.
18:19/q22 Which city was meant? [ٱلْمَدِينَةِ · referent] (turns on: Reports vary in names and whether they denote one city over time.)
  18:19/q22/p1 Their former city was Ephesus, also reported as Daqsus or Daphsus and later called Tarsus. — reasons: Al-Tabari calls it Ephesus; al-Wahidi calls it Daqsus and present Tarsus; al-Baghawi, al-Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi and Alusi reconcile Ephesus as the former name with Tarsus as later name. Alusi says it was familiar home; Ibn Ashur calls it Ephesus. Al-Tha'labi's story spells Daphsus.
  18:19/q22/p2 Tarsus is the city's name. — reasons: Several sources state this directly without reconciling the older name.
  18:19/q22/p3 It was the city from which they fled; its specific name is unnecessary to the phrase. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi and Ibn Kathir identify it by relation to the youths; the Quran leaves the proper name unstated.
18:19/q23 Who or what does 'which of it' refer to? [أَيُّهَآ · referent] (turns on: Whether the city's people, its locations, or its foods are supplied to resolve the pronoun.)
  18:19/q23/p1 The city's inhabitants or vendors are understood: which person has the relevant food. — reasons: Al-Tabari, al-Zajjaj and al-Nahhas supply 'people'; al-Kashshaf compares 'ask the town' at 12:82; al-Nasafi, al-Samin, Bursevi and Tabatabai agree. Al-Tabari notes listeners understand food belongs to people.
  18:19/q23/p2 The pronoun can point directly to foods understood from context. — reasons: Al-Nahhas allows 'which food'; al-Samin says no elision is required; Abu Hayyan and Alusi present this alternative.
  18:19/q23/p3 It can refer grammatically to the city itself with an understood person or place. — reasons: Al-Tabari says the syntax attaches it to 'city' although the sense is inhabitants; Alusi lists city, residents, or food.
  18:19/q23/p4 It means which place in the city, such as a market stall. — reasons: Ibn Ashur makes place the referent and ṭaʿāman the specification of the comparative.
18:19/q24 How is 'let him look which has purer food' parsed? [فَلْيَنظُرْ أَيُّهَآ أَزْكَىٰ طَعَامًۭا · grammar] (turns on: The grammatical choice is interrogative versus relative ayyu and whether naẓar governs it or its clause.)
  18:19/q24/p1 Ayyuhā is an interrogative subject, azkā its predicate, and ṭaʿāman a specification. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj gives this parse; al-Wahidi cites Abu Ishaq; al-Razi and al-Nasafi follow; al-Samin and Alusi allow it.
  18:19/q24/p2 Ayy may be relative and function as the object, with an omitted predicate. — reasons: Abu Hayyan attributes this to Sibawayh; al-Samin and Alusi record the interrogative/relative options.
  18:19/q24/p3 The interrogative clause is what 'let him look' investigates. — reasons: Al-Akhfash says naẓar can be followed by a question without directly governing ayyu, illustrated with 'look, is Zayd more generous or Amr?'; al-Tabrisi calls the whole clause the object.
