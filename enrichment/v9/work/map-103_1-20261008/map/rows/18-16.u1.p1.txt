18:16/q10 What alternate wording is transmitted for 'what they worship except God'? [وَمَا يَعْبُدُونَ إِلَّا ٱللَّهَ · readings] (turns on: The wording of codex reports; Abu Hayyan judges both against the universally transmitted consonantal text.)
  18:16/q10/p1 Ibn Masud's codex says 'what they worship besides God.' — reasons: Qatada or Ibn Masud is cited across Yahya, Tabari, Thalabi, Baghawi, Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Durr, and Alusi. Abu Hayyan and Alusi's citation of al-Bahr say it is an explanatory gloss rather than Qurʾān because it conflicts with the transmitted consonantal text.
  18:16/q10/p2 Some copies instead say 'what they worship besides us.' — reasons: Harun reports this Ibn Masud codex variant; Abu Hayyan quotes it, and Alusi also records it. It too is described as an explanatory gloss rather than a canonical reading.
18:16/q11 What is the grammatical role of 'when you withdrew' relative to 'take refuge'? [وَإِذِ ٱعْتَزَلْتُمُوهُمْ فَأْوُۥٓا۟ · grammar] (turns on: Whether idh can carry a conditional force, or is temporal or causal with an explicit or omitted governor.)
  18:16/q11/p1 The cave command is the response to 'when,' treated like a conditional: when you withdraw, take refuge. — reasons: Farra is cited by Wahidi, Razi, Bursevi, and Alusi. Nahhas says the command in turn has 'your Lord will spread' as its answer. Alusi records the objection that idhā without mā is not properly conditional.
  18:16/q11/p2 The cave command reveals an omitted consequence: inward separation calls for bodily withdrawal. — reasons: Bursevi and Alusi give this analysis; Alusi reports a grammatical objection that the conditional force is weak without mā.
  18:16/q11/p3 'When' is temporal, governed by omitted words of speech or an implied command. — reasons: Nahhas supplies 'remember'; Samin supplies 'someone said'; Alusi allows temporal attachment to an unstated or stated command but calls Abu al-Baqa's omitted 'they said to one another' astonishing.
  18:16/q11/p4 'When' is causal: because they withdrew, they are told to take refuge. — reasons: Samin reports then rejects this analysis; Alusi allows a causal value linked to the command.
18:16/q12 What does 'take refuge in the cave' mean? [فَأْوُۥٓا۟ إِلَى ٱلْكَهْفِ · interpretation]
  18:16/q12/p1 Go to the cave and make it your place of shelter or dwelling. — reasons: Muqatil glosses awā as reaching a place, citing 18:63; Yahya, Wahidi's citation of Farra, Ibn Jawzi, Razi, Abu Hayyan, and others describe making the cave a home and remaining there. Thalabi says they planned it as a place to worship God; Study Quran says they hid from disbelievers.
18:16/q13 What does 'spread' mean in the promise? [يَنشُرْ · meaning]
  18:16/q13/p1 To extend or expand mercy over them. — reasons: Jishumi says nashr opposes folding, as with unfolding a book, reviving the dead, or a pleasant wind spreading blessing; Yahya glosses it as widening, while Wahidi and Alusi say spreading over them. Tabatabai also gives the lexical sense.
18:16/q14 What mercy is promised to them? [يَنشُرْ لَكُمْ رَبُّكُم مِّن رَّحْمَتِهِۦ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the benefits stressed are worldly sustenance and safety, broader divine care, or spiritual provision.)
  18:16/q14/p1 God will expand provision and protective care, including their sleep and relief from danger. — reasons: Muqatil calls their sleep the provision. Zajjaj, Ibn Jawzi, Nasafi and Baydawi gloss provision; Ibn Abbas in Wahidi and Tabrisi specifies relief from the king's oppression. Baydawi, Bursevi and Alusi include this world and the next; Alusi calls rabb their guardian who guided them to faith. Biqai says God fulfilled the hope by sleep and later awakening.
  18:16/q14/p2 The promise supplies spiritual provision arising from world renunciation and solitude. — reasons: Asad takes marfiq as concrete or abstract benefit but here reads it as spiritual provision.
18:16/q15 Why could they speak so confidently of God's help? [يَنشُرْ لَكُمْ رَبُّكُم وَيُهَيِّئْ لَكُم · theology] (turns on: Whether certainty is explained by faith, prior prophetic knowledge, or God's direct promise; Alusi rejects prophetic speculation and Islahi rejects a human assurance.)
  18:16/q15/p1 Their trust, hope, and certainty in God gave them confidence. — reasons: Kashshaf, Nasafi, Baydawi, Biqai, Bursevi, Alusi, and Tabatabai connect the assurance with trust. Abu Hayyan adds God would not abandon those brought from disbelief to faith. Bursevi cites a hadith on praying with certainty.
  18:16/q15/p2 A contemporary prophet told them, or one youth was a prophet receiving revelation. — reasons: Kashshaf offers both alternatives; Nasafi mentions a prophet's prior announcement. Alusi rejects both as conjectures without evidence.
  18:16/q15/p3 The assured promise is God's own speech or inspiration. — reasons: Islahi says certainty marks a divine promise, while a human could only ask or yearn as in 18:10; he compares the guidance to Moses' mother's inspiration.
18:16/q16 What does 'He will prepare' imply? [وَيُهَيِّئْ · meaning]
  18:16/q16/p1 God will arrange and make easy what benefits them. — reasons: Ibn Abbas in Abu Hayyan says the king's threats become easy to bear with kindness and comfort; Alusi glosses yuhayyiʾ as making easy and takes its omitted object as good or like benefit.
18:16/q17 How does 'from your affair' attach, and what does 'from' mean? [مِّنْ أَمْرِكُم · grammar] (turns on: Whether min expresses source, part, or replacement, and whether the phrase attaches to the verb or to marfiqan.)
  18:16/q17/p1 It attaches to 'prepare'; min marks source or a portion of their affair. — reasons: Samin gives both source and partitive possibilities, repeated by Alusi.
  18:16/q17/p2 Min means 'instead of': God prepares relief in place of their difficult situation. — reasons: Ibn al-Anbari cites a poem wishing for Zamzam in place of another water; Abu Hayyan notes the same line, and Alusi also invokes 9:38. Ibn Jawzi reports a poetic example of substitute.
  18:16/q17/p3 The phrase modifies marfiqan circumstantially, with an omitted governor. — reasons: Samin and Alusi record this alternative attachment.
18:16/q18 What does mirfaq mean in this promise? [مِّرْفَقًۭا · meaning] (turns on: Whether the general useful comfort is specified as food, escape, or replacement of hardship.)
  18:16/q18/p1 A useful means of comfort, ease, support, or beneficial accommodation. — reasons: Abu Ubayda, Akhfash, Tabari, Thalabi, and many later authors define practical support; Tabari ties it to distress and fear for life and faith. Mawrdi also offers spacious relief, and Tabatabai gentle treatment. Islahi calls it anything useful, Asad concrete or abstract benefit. Lexicographers also use it for the elbow, the bodily support joint.
  18:16/q18/p2 Specifically food or provisions, even breakfast. — reasons: Kalbi in Wahidi glosses breakfast, Suddi in Durr says food; Wahidi Wajiz says an easy source of food and Thalabi describes a comfortable provision.
  18:16/q18/p3 A way out, rescue, or deliverance. — reasons: Mawardi offers rescue; Wahidi reports others' gloss as an exit.
  18:16/q18/p4 A replacement of their hardship with a better situation. — reasons: Ibn al-Anbari is cited by Ibn Jawzi and Abu Hayyan, with a poetic use of substitute.
18:16/q19 How is mirfaq read? [مِّرْفَقًۭا · readings] (turns on: Regional and transmitted vocalization; sources disagree on some individual reader attributions, but Tabari accepts both main forms.)
  18:16/q19/p1 Kasra on mīm and fatḥa on fāʾ: mirfaq. — reasons: Five canonical readers Ibn Kathir, Abu Amr, Asim, Hamza and Kisai are repeatedly listed; Ibn Atiyya adds Hasan, Talha, Aʿmash, and Ibn Abi Ishaq. Tabari prefers this Iraqi form as common and eloquent, while accepting both. Farra says Aʿmash and Hasan use it; Abu Hayyan and Razi give transmission variants for Abu Amr or Asim.
  18:16/q19/p2 Fatḥa on mīm and kasra on fāʾ: marfiq. — reasons: Nafi and Ibn Amir, with Medina and Syria, recur in the lists; Farra also names Medina's readers and Asim, Baghawi adds Abu Jaʿfar, and Abu Hayyan and Alusi list Abu Jaʿfar, Aʿraj, Shayba, Humayd, Ibn Saʿdan and one Abu Amr report. Ibn Mujahid and Farisi report an Abu Bakr or Kisai transmission; Baydawi calls the form an anomalous verbal noun.
  18:16/q19/p3 Both mīm and fāʾ may be open. — reasons: Muʿadh permits this as a verbal noun in Abu Hayyan and Samin; Alusi also records it.
18:16/q20 Do the mirfaq forms differ in sense or formation? [مِّرْفَقًۭا · grammar] (turns on: Whether the vocalizations are equivalent dialects or mark different nouns; reports conflict even about Kisai's and Farra's usage.)
  18:16/q20/p1 Both are dialect forms usable for a helpful thing and, in many accounts, an elbow. — reasons: Farra cites a rajaz pairing the forms; Zajjaj, Qutrub, Abu al-Hasan, Ibn Atiyya, Razi, and Alusi allow both. Akhfash and Abu al-Hasan explain a noun like masjid, a dialect form, or rifq; Razi says derived from irtifāq. Farra says most Arabs prefer kasra for both, though some use fatḥa. Samin disputes a report that Farra knew only kasra by citing the transmitted Nafi and Syrian readings.
  18:16/q20/p2 The vowels distinguish the aid or facility from the body's elbow, though sources assign them differently. — reasons: Ibn Khalawayh, Abu Ubayda, Yunus, Farra on Hijaz speech, and Ibn Jawzi associate kasra with the elbow or comfort in differing ways; Kisai is quoted both as accepting only kasra for elbow (Tabari/Razi) and only open mīm (Abu Hayyan/Samin/Alusi).
