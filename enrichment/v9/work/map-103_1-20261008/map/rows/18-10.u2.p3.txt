18:10/q29 What explains the awe and flight associated with seeing the youths later in the story? [* · inward] (turns on: Whether emphasis falls on the divine awe manifested in them or the observer's standpoint.)
  18:10/q29/p1 Divine light, majesty and overpowering spiritual states in them cause the onlooker to flee. — reasons: Ibn Ata in Sulami says lights of the Real surround them; Husayn points to their states despite their nearness; Jafar calls the awe God's power, care and guardianship. Another voice distinguishes angelic honoring lights from human outward and inward guidance, comparing Satan fleeing Umar.
  18:10/q29/p2 Flight follows seeing them from the human self; seeing from God yields unity and firmness. — reasons: Jafar contrasts human and divine perspectives and compares the Prophet's flight from unbelievers; Ibn Ata says the Prophet beholds God and is beheld from Him, so merely created sight turns away.
18:10/q30 How does this verse connect to the surrounding account? [* · links] (turns on: The notes connect the entry prayer to the story's structure, later speech, and the Moses narrative.)
  18:10/q30/p1 It opens a short summary of their flight, prayer, sleep and awakening before the fuller story. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says the outline shows their protection as support for faith and that the episode is not God's most astonishing sign. Tabatabai identifies three summary elements, then later discovery and mosque; Islahi says this Quranic narrative pattern protects the main lesson from incidental details. Asad says the prayer previews verse 13 onward.
  18:10/q30/p2 Their later confidence that God will grant mercy and ease recalls this entry prayer. — reasons: Tabatabai links their advice after leaving the people and gods with the gifts first requested on entering the cave. Damghani and Ibn al-Jawzi's Nuzha group the prayer's mercy with the mercy mentioned later at 18:16, glossing it as provision.
  18:10/q30/p3 The certainty of the promise of mercy and provision in verse 16 marks it as God's speech. — reasons: Islahi rejects exegetes who attribute that promise to the youths: a humble speaker could express it only as prayer or yearning, as in verse 10.
  18:10/q30/p4 Khiḍr's mercy and knowledge and Moses' sound judgment echo the youths' two requests. — reasons: Study Quran compares the gift to Khidr at 18:60 and Moses' request for sound judgment with the cave youths' prayer.
18:10/q31 Why was the Cave story related to the Prophet? [* · reports]
  18:10/q31/p1 Quraysh tested him with questions obtained from Jewish scholars. — reasons: Abu Hayyan names al-Nadr and Uqba and questions about the youths, a world traveler and the spirit; another report says answering two while withholding one would confirm prophethood. Kuranyolu says Meccans heard the story from Jewish scholars in Medina. Al-Sadiq in Tabatabai says three envoys went to Najran and asked also about Moses' companion, Dhul-Qarnayn and the Hour.
18:10/q32 What larger themes does the account serve? [* · links] (turns on: The story bears on resurrection, faith under persecution and the sura's presentation of revelation.)
  18:10/q32/p1 It demonstrates resurrection and the coming Hour. — reasons: Kuranyolu says long sleep and awakening make these ideas easier to grasp; the townspeople's recognition is part of the instructive narrative.
  18:10/q32/p2 It supports faith and places this sign within the sura's larger praise of revelation. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says protection supports their faith; Abu Hayyan links this sura's praise of the upright warning Quran, its warning against claims of a divine son and its promise to believers, with the preceding sura's truth of revelation and humility at 17:105.
18:10/q33 How should the many historical reports about the cave be treated? [* · reports]
  18:10/q33/p1 Their particulars diverge, while the Quran selects the details needed for guidance. — reasons: Tabatabai lists disagreements on rulers, date, number, dog, names and titles; he attributes them to widespread Christian and Jewish storytelling and Muslim collection of outside reports via converts such as Wahb and Ka'b, later elevated to prophetic reports. The Quran omits ancestry, occupations, locations and names because guidance does not depend on them. Kuranyolu calls number guesses baseless; Christian reports differ on number, dog and sleep duration, and parallels occur in Indian, Talmudic and Christian stories.
18:10/q34 What is the adjacent verse's “raqim”? [* · links] (turns on: The word may derive from writing, but a transmitted uncertainty leaves a building possible.)
  18:10/q34/p1 It is something written or inscribed, perhaps a book or a stone tablet of the youths' story. — reasons: Ibn Kathir prefers Ibn Jarir's derivation from inscription, comparing qatil and jarih. Ibn Abbas and Abd al-Rahman ibn Zayd say book, the latter citing 83:9; Sa'id ibn Jubayr describes a stone tablet at the cave entrance.
  18:10/q34/p2 It may be a building rather than a book; its meaning was uncertain to Ibn Abbas. — reasons: Ibn Abbas says he knew the Quran except hannan, awwah and raqim, and through Ikrima says he cannot decide between book and building.
18:10/q35 What does “cave” mean and who are its companions? [ٱلْكَهْفِ · meaning]
  18:10/q35/p1 It is a large, broad mountain cave, associated with the long-sleeping youths. — reasons: Kuranyolu supplies the lexical size and says the title refers to people said to sleep there for years and awaken; Ibn Ashur says their refuge became their dwelling.
18:10/q36 What effect does dropping the hamza after “when” have? [إِذْ · readings]
  18:10/q36/p1 It smooths liaison without changing the meaning. — reasons: Qiraat-ER describes elision after the dhāl of idh.
18:10/q37 What is the status of the report about reciting Surat al-Kahf? [* · reports]
  18:10/q37/p1 A report promises light for reciting its beginning and end, and greater light for the whole sura. — reasons: Thalabi transmits it from Muadh as a saying of the Prophet: head-to-foot light for the ends and earth-to-heaven light for the whole.
18:10/q38 How does Sibawayh parse the similar form fatīya in a war poem and its grammatical parallels? [ٱلْفِتْيَةُ · grammar] (turns on: The reported parses differ on whether the poem's fatīya is a nominative predicate or an accusative state.)
  18:10/q38/p1 In the cited war poem, fatīya can be nominative: war's first stage is a maiden. — reasons: Sibawayh reports a parse taking the first stage as a maiden, with feminine agreement as in 'some of his fingers went.' He aligns nominative fatīya with nominative qafīzān in the wheat-price example; his Friday and spring-month examples make times the best days or periods. Another quantity construction likewise allows nominative.
  18:10/q38/p2 It can be accusative as a state: war is maiden-like in its first period. — reasons: Sibawayh reports parses describing war as a maiden at its initial time or its first phases as maiden-like, and aligns accusative fatīya with qafīzayn. His examples make accusatives states understood with idh kāna or idhā kāna; comparisons, attributed day and night states, and the necessarily accusative qāʾiman illustrate this. He also explains a Friday arrival by its slowest kind and permits accusative or circumstantial quantity expressions.
18:10/q39 What is the lexical sense of mercy in their request? [رَحْمَةًۭ · meaning]
  18:10/q39/p1 Mercy is a blessing granted to one in need, with a root sense of tenderness. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi's Nuzha defines it as a blessing for someone in need and reports Ibn Fāris's connection of raḥima, raḥm, marḥama and raḥma with tenderness.
18:10/q40 How does grammatical attachment affect the suitability of a pause in this verse? [* · grammar]
  18:10/q40/p1 A pause is less suitable where a dependent is more tightly attached to its governing word. — reasons: Burhan states this as a general principle for judging pauses by the strength of grammatical attachment.
18:10/q41 How can the past-tense refuge narrative and the ensuing prayer be coordinated? [أَوَى فَقَالُوا۟ · grammar]
  18:10/q41/p1 They can be coordinated as clauses; the narrow rule for syntactic verb-to-verb following applies only to imperfect verbs in subjunctive or jussive form. — reasons: Burhan reports the author of al-Mustawfa's narrow rule, then answers the objection from past-tense narrative followed by prayer by distinguishing clause coordination from inflectional following of verbs that share case or mood.
18:10/q42 What is reported about pausing on the hamza of 'prepare'? [وَهَيِّئْ · readings]
  18:10/q42/p1 Full articulation of this hamza at a pause and deletion of the substituted long-vowel letter for jussive syntax are rejected. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari applies the preceding rule to hayyiʾ and yuhayyiʾ, along with nabbiʾ, iqraʾ and nashaʾ, and rejects both proposed treatments.
18:10/q43 How is the related expression min ladunhu elsewhere in the sura vocalized? [مِن لَّدُنكَ · readings] (turns on: The reports distinguish vowels and connecting letters in a related ladun expression.)
  18:10/q43/p1 Abu Bakr gives the dāl sukūn with a hint of ḍamma, kasra on the nūn and hāʾ, and a connecting yāʾ. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari reports Abu Bakr's detailed vocalization of the related form.
  18:10/q43/p2 In a route from Abu Bakr, the hāʾ has kasra without a connecting vowel. — reasons: Nafṭawayh transmits this route through al-Ṣarīfīnī and Yaḥyā, as does Khalaf from Yaḥyā.
  18:10/q43/p3 The remaining readers give the dāl and hāʾ ḍamma and leave the nūn unvocalized; Ibn Kathir connects the hāʾ with wāw. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari reports the majority vocalization and Ibn Kathir's regular connecting wāw.
