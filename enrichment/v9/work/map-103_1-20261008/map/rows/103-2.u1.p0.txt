## THE CURRENT MAP
103:2/q01 What is the grammatical answer to the oath? [إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَفِى خُسْرٍ · grammar]
  103:2/q01/p1 The declaration that humanity is in loss answers the oath by time. — reasons: Jishumi identifies the oath wāw and this clause as its answer; Ibn al-Jawzi reports al-Zajjaj; Study Quran takes verses 2–3 as the answer and compares 95:5–6; Corpus Coranicum describes an opening oath followed by rebuke.
103:2/q02 Whom does al-insān designate? [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the definite singular is generic, as the following exception suggests, or points to a particular unbeliever or occasion.)
  103:2/q02/p1 Humanity as a whole, with believers subsequently excepted. — reasons: Kaʿb includes Adam and his descendants; Abu Ubayda and al-Zajjaj call it a generic noun. The following exception proves collective scope for Jishumi, Razi, Samin, Elmalili and others. Bint al-Shati says limiting it to named Meccans would make that exception fail. Alusi says named losers are examples, not exclusive. Study Quran allows a historical emphasis on humanity's final phase; Elmalili especially includes the last era.
  103:2/q02/p2 Disbelievers, whose exclusion is made clear by the next verse. — reasons: Wahidi reports Muqatil's reading of the unbeliever astray until death; the exception is Bagawi's reason to limit the term. Wahidi specifies one working outside God's obedience and forfeiting a place in Paradise. Ibn Abbas is transmitted by Abu Salih. Bint al-Shati objects that the exception cannot be taken from an exclusively named group.
  103:2/q02/p3 Abu Lahab in particular. — reasons: Muqatil says the verse was revealed about ʿAbd al-ʿUzza ibn ʿAbd al-Muttalib; Tustari says he lost all his days. Razi repeats Muqatil's report.
  103:2/q02/p4 Abu Jahl in particular. — reasons: Jishumi reports this occasion; Razi mentions an unspecified raised report; Ibn Abbas identifies Abu Jahl ibn Hisham in Durr and Tabatabai. Thalabi's Prophetic report and a sermon give the same identification; Alusi treats it as illustrative.
  103:2/q02/p5 A group of Meccan opponents. — reasons: Ibn Abbas in Razi names al-Walid, al-As and al-Aswad ibn Abd al-Muttalib; al-Dahhak in Qurtubi gives two men named al-Aswad. Tabrisi names al-Walid and Abu Jahl as examples; Bint al-Shati lists reported names.
103:2/q03 How can singular al-insān be followed by an exception for many believers? [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · grammar]
  103:2/q03/p1 A singular generic noun denotes the human collective. — reasons: Abu Ubayda's Majaz compares 70:19–22, 69:47 and a line of al-Nabigha; Tabari reports the same construction elsewhere. Qurtubi compares generic al-nafs and its exceptions in a legal verse. Abu Hayyan and Samin infer generic scope from the exception.
103:2/q04 What wording and vowel readings of this verse are transmitted? [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ خُسْرٍ · readings] (turns on: Which transmitted wording or vocalization is used, and whether an explanatory addition belongs to canonical recitation.)
  103:2/q04/p1 The received codex reads the present wording, with a still sīn in khusr. — reasons: Thalabi prefers the general reciters and codices; Qurtubi says Ali's explanatory addition is not Qur'an for recitation under the Uthmanic codex. Jishumi, Abu Hayyan and Samin attest the still sīn as the majority form.
  103:2/q04/p2 Khusr is pronounced with ḍamma on the sīn. — reasons: Reported from al-Aʿraj, Asim, Talha, Isa al-Thaqafi, Ibn Hurmuz, Zayd ibn Ali and an Asim transmission through Abu Bakr and Harun. Qurtubi compares the parallel vowel variants of ʿusr and yusr.
  103:2/q04/p3 An explanatory recitation adds that humanity remains in loss until time's end, sometimes also adding time's calamities. — reasons: Ali is cited by Tabari, Thalabi, Mawardi, Qurtubi and Elmalili; Thalabi also attributes the time's-end addition to Ibn Masud, and Tabari reports Qatada. The variants differ over whether calamities are included. Qurtubi rejects this as canonical Qur'an.
  103:2/q04/p4 Ibn Masud's codex says God created humanity in loss. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya records the wording of ʿAbd Allah ibn Masud's codex.
103:2/q05 What is the force of inna and la-fī? [إِنَّ لَفِى · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the phrase depicts an eventual outcome or a continuing condition.)
  103:2/q05/p1 Inna emphasizes the assertion and fī portrays the human as immersed and surrounded by loss. — reasons: Razi assigns emphasis to inna and describes loss encompassing a person from every side.
  103:2/q05/p2 La-fī can speak of an inevitable route toward future loss. — reasons: Razi compares 4:10, where eating orphans' property is expressed as fire already in bellies because that will be its outcome.
  103:2/q05/p3 La-fī can speak of continual, inescapable loss in passing time. — reasons: For Razi each passing hour is capital rarely preserved: sin wastes it, permissible pursuits leave no enduring trace, and even worship may fall short of higher humility and knowledge.
103:2/q06 What does the indefinite khusr convey? [خُسْرٍ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether indefiniteness signals magnitude, a distinct or unrestricted category, or relative smallness.)
  103:2/q06/p1 It magnifies a loss whose full extent is known only to God. — reasons: Razi prefers this because sins oppose God and immense blessings; Baydawi, Bursevi and others also intensify the loss. Bint al-Shati cites Razi's awe explanation, but rejects treating intensity as its sole force.
  103:2/q06/p2 It marks a distinctive kind or range of loss, beyond ordinary financial or social losses. — reasons: Bursevi, Alusi, Elmalili and Tabatabai mention a particular kind; Ibn Ashur adds multiple kinds and dramatic universality, comparing afterlife loss. Bint al-Shati understands it as open-ended and unrestricted rather than simple amplification.
  103:2/q06/p3 It can belittle human loss in comparison with Satan's. — reasons: Razi reports this consoling possibility: others are more disobedient, though he prefers magnification.
103:2/q07 What does khusr mean, and what is lost? [خُسْرٍ · meaning] (turns on: Whether loss is read as a lexical diminution, unbelief, punishment, squandered life, spiritual deprivation, bodily decline or loss across both worlds.)
  103:2/q07/p1 Loss, ruin, diminishment, or destruction of one's capital. — reasons: Abu Ubayda, Ibn Qutayba, Tabari, Suddi, Ibn Shajara, Akhfash and others give these overlapping lexical glosses; Ibn al-Jawzi says khusr and khusran are equivalent. Wahidi likens it to lost capital. Razi and Raghib note that loss may be predicated of a person or a transaction; Kashshaf compares the nominal forms. Bagawi and Ibn al-Jawzi identify the self as the greatest capital.
  103:2/q07/p2 Misguidance and disbelief that persist until death without faith. — reasons: Mujahid glosses loss as misguidance; Wahidi reports Muqatil's worldly explanation, and Razi links the particular-unbeliever interpretation to misguidance and belief as profit. Muqatil says Abu Lahab remains in error until Fire.
  103:2/q07/p3 Punishment for sins, especially Fire and forfeited home, family or property in Paradise. — reasons: Farra, Mawardi and Qurtubi cite punishment, with Qurtubi comparing 65:9; Nahhas calls entry into Fire the greatest loss. Wahidi adds the forfeited Paradise station. Hasan adds void deeds; Jishumi sees lasting punishment as unsurpassable. Tabrisi and Qur'an 39:15 are used for loss of self and family at Judgment.
  103:2/q07/p4 Evil or deprivation. — reasons: Zayd ibn Aslam gives evil in Mawardi; Ibn Zayd gives evil in Qurtubi, which also reports deprivation and observes that the senses are close.
  103:2/q07/p5 The lost capacity to serve God and attain spiritual nearness. — reasons: Razi says deprivation of serving the Lord is the central loss, beside which Paradise and Hell are secondary, citing 51:56. Qushayri distinguishes deeds whose loss is evident at the end from present loss of states: contraction after expansion, veiling after nearness and resort to concessions after a higher path. Qashani and Study Quran describe losing innate guidance or attachment to God.
  103:2/q07/p6 Decline of bodily strength with age, while believers' earlier deeds keep being recorded. — reasons: Ibrahim is transmitted by Thalabi, Bagawi and Qurtubi, who compare 95:4–6; Study Quran also reports a historical reading in which later generations decline from earlier ones.
  103:2/q07/p7 Loss in both worldly life and the Hereafter, culminating in loss of dignity and the soul. — reasons: Study Quran cites 35:39, 22:11 and 39:15, noting that a person may lose what is dear without material poverty. Qutb makes earthly profit negligible beside God's pleasure, Paradise and human dignity; Abu Hayyan calls losing both worlds the greatest loss.
103:2/q08 Why does ordinary human life tend toward loss? [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَفِى خُسْرٍ · interpretation] (turns on: How finite life and human choices produce loss or enduring gain.)
  103:2/q08/p1 Life and time are finite capital; misuse or failure to earn lasting good spends them irretrievably. — reasons: Bagawi calls self and lifetime the greatest capital. Razi says sin, permissible activity and even incomplete worship miss enduring gain; Elmalili compares every breath to an entrusted loan and cites 25:62, 53:39 and 52:21. Poems about each passing day, his elegy and the image of death consuming life sharpen the point. Tabatabai calls earthly life a test (13:26; 21:35) in which belief and deeds determine the afterlife (53:39–41; 30:44; 41:46). Islahi stresses rapidly consumed life. Bint al-Shati's broad al-insan usage is distinguished from al-nas and jinn contexts.
