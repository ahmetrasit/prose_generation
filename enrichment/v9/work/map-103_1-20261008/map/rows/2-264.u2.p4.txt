2:264/q39 What wider rules of Qurʾānic recitation are cited with this verse's reading material? [رِئَآءَ صَفْوَانٍ · readings]
  2:264/q39/p1 Recitation follows transmitted practice from earlier readers. — reasons: Ibn al-Munkadir, al-Shaʿbī, ʿUrwa, and other teachers call recitation a transmitted sunnah; attributions also reach the two ʿUmars. Ibn Mujāhid corrects the route through al-Zuhrī in one report.
  2:264/q39/p2 Sound transmission, written form, analogy, and explicit documentation govern variant readings. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī rejects the unsound report of codex errors, affirms legitimate regional variants, surveys hamza spelling and stopping by script, records al-Hudhalī and al-Dānī, and gives explicit transmission priority over silence in manuals.
  2:264/q39/p3 Underlying alifs and their inclination are identified by morphology and inherited pronunciation. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazarī tests noun alifs by the dual and verb alifs by conjugation, including extended wāwī verbs, and lists faʿlā forms read with imāla. Al-Suyūṭī reports imāla studies, al-Dānī's dialect distribution, Ibrāhīm's alif/yāʾ equivalence, and Kūfan appeal to script.
2:264/q40 What broader principles of wording and rhetorical arrangement are cited beside the verse? [* · rhetoric]
  2:264/q40/p1 Question form, predication, circumstance, and negation depend on syntax and position. — reasons: Al-Jurjānī explains false concession in 6:143, questions implying an event, verb-first present questions, omitted subjects, second predicates, a satire's occasion, and how circumstantial addition or the placement of kull changes a negative claim; poetry and the Dhū al-Yadayn report illustrate.
  2:264/q40/p2 Nazm coordinates grammatical meanings, and rhetorical figures gain force from their syntactic relations. — reasons: Al-Jurjānī distinguishes reciting words from composing them, treats meaning as prior to word order, locates inimitability in syntactic arrangement, and uses 19:4 to show metaphor depends on predication; he rejects bare sound as the source of eloquence.
  2:264/q40/p3 Qurʾānic eloquence uses paronomasia and antithesis as well as context-specific images. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr cites the paired sounds at 6:26 and opposed verbs at 22:4 as examples of these arts.
2:264/q41 What transmission and scholarship reports accompany the source material on this verse? [* · reports]
  2:264/q41/p1 Early Qurʾān collectors, readers, and teachers are identified through competing reports. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr examines Abū Zayd's identity, Anas's Aws and Khazraj report, al-Zuhrī's death notice, Abū Bakr's leading recitation, ʿUthmān's one-rakʿa reading, ʿAlī's compilation, Ibn Masʿūd's knowledge, Sālim, Mujāhid's study with Ibn ʿAbbās, and ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr's nightly recitation.
  2:264/q41/p2 Ibn ʿAbbās's lexical question collection circulated in several forms and later scholarly accounts. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ recounts Nāfiʿ and Najda's demand for Arabic evidence, al-Suyūṭī's count of 190 questions with omissions, selections by Ibn al-Anbārī and al-Ṭabarānī, and her inability to locate a marked old copy.
2:264/q42 What theology of divine speech occurs in the collected source material? [* · theology]
  2:264/q42/p1 Divine speech is eternal, with inner and manifested levels distinguished. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī cites an angelic-hearing report, locates inner speech beyond material modality, describes manifested speech as modal, presents al-Ashʿarī's act and thing-spoken distinction, and explains unsounded mental letters and their relation to audible words, citing ʿUmar and Imām al-Ḥaramayn.
2:264/q43 What wider arguments about the Qurʾān's lasting eloquence occur in these sources? [* · rhetoric]
  2:264/q43/p1 The Qurʾān's linguistic challenge and enduring rational miracle establish its distinctive eloquence. — reasons: Al-Suyūṭī surveys inimitability treatises and explanations contrasting lasting Qurʾānic evidence with earlier sensory miracles; Ibn Ḥajar combines the explanations. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites Khālid ibn Ṣafwān, the first Arabs' inability to answer the challenge, and al-Ṭabarī on its continuing miracle.
2:264/q44 How do modern writers frame the study and renewal of Qurʾānic interpretation? [* · interpretation]
  2:264/q44/p1 Reform arose in religious schools but often remained within inherited methods; some writers sought broader critical tools. — reasons: Rifāʿī describes al-Azhar, Zaytuna, and Najaf reformers, their differing and sometimes controversial questions, critique of inherited authority, contemporary contextual reading, limits of traditional methods, and Amīn al-Khūlī's life, teaching, academy, journal, and writings.
  2:264/q44/p2 Sustained fresh ijtihād should answer present needs while maintaining living religious sources. — reasons: Y. Khūlī criticizes past-ism and extremism for denying contemporary reality, argues language keeps the community connected to sacred texts, and calls for renewed Qurʾān and Sunna research.
