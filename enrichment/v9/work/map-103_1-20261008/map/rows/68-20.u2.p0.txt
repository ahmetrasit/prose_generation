## THE CURRENT MAP
68:20/q01 What does أصبح convey here? [فَأَصْبَحَتْ · meaning]
  68:20/q01/p1 The garden entered the morning in its ruined state. — reasons: Al-Jishumī explicitly glosses aṣbaḥa as entering morning.
68:20/q02 What does كالصريم liken the ruined garden to? [كَٱلصَّرِيمِ · interpretation] (turns on: Which attested sense or referent of ṣarīm supplies the simile: darkness, a cut or barren state, dawn/day, ashes, sand, the named place, or the unusual gold gloss; the notes also differ over whether the trees themselves or only their fruit were removed.)
  68:20/q02/p1 A black, dark night: the burned garden was blackened. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, al-Farrāʾ, al-Zajjāj and others tie the color to burning. Ibn ʿAbbās through ʿAṭāʾ specifies the moonless last night of a month. Al-Thaʿlabī and al-Qurṭubī cite a poem about a long dark night that dawn does not clear; al-Ṭabarī also cites poetry. Shammar and others say night and day are both ṣarīm because each separates from the other. Another derivation makes night ṣarīm because darkness stops movement or sight; al-Wāḥidī notes that this fails to explain the day usage, and al-Qushayrī objects to it. Qatāda's report says God's command came at night; Qaṭr ibn Maymūn agrees. Some authors present this alongside other possible comparisons. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ cites al-Nābigha's use of the related aṣrām in a dark-night comparison and reports Abū ʿAmr al-Shaybānī's citation of Zuhayr as another witness for the night sense. She reports al-Rāghib's alternative image of trees blackened as though burned.
  68:20/q02/p2 A harvested orchard or crop with its fruit cut away and nothing left. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba says the fruit looked cut and harvested. Al-Qushayrī and al-Bursawī say the fire left a harvested field, and al-Bursawī specifies nothing remained. Al-Thawrī and al-Suddī describe dry harvested stubble. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Ālūsī explain ṣarīm as a passive-form adjective, 'cut'; al-Biqāʿī specifies trees stripped of fruit. ʿAṭāʾ argues that fruit removed while trees remain better fits a fire than trees physically cut down. The Study Quran renders it 'like a garden plucked.' Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports al-Rāghib's explanation of trees stripped of fruit and favors cutting or harvesting on contextual grounds: the owners had sworn to harvest the garden, not make it disappear.
  68:20/q02/p3 A white, bare dawn or day, like a dried and harvested field. — reasons: Al-Akhfash derives ṣarīm for dawn from its separation from night; al-Mubarrad says daylight with nothing left in the orchard. Al-Thawrī says it whitened like harvested grain; the opposing account says it turned black. Ibn ʿAbbās in al-Qurṭubī connects the day sense to vanished trees and crops and bare soil. Shammar and others explain both temporal senses from night and day cutting one another off.
  68:20/q02/p4 A detached, barren patch of sand or dune, including a reported Yemeni sandy tract. — reasons: Abū ʿUbayda, al-Muʾarrij and others use ṣarīm or ṣarīmah for sand severed from a larger dune. Al-Aṣmaʿī calls a large separated section ṣarāʾim and relates its barrenness to the charred fruitless orchard. Ibn ʿAbbās and later authors report a known barren sandy tract in Yemen; al-Muʾarrij emphasizes that nothing useful grows there. Some reports thus treat the tract as a known referent, others as a generic sand image.
  68:20/q02/p5 Black ashes, in a reported tribal dialect. — reasons: Attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās; the tribal name varies in the notes as Khudhaym, Khuzaima, Khuzaymī, Judhayma or Khuzaʿa. Ibn ʿĀshūr reports Judhayma or Khuzayma. Al-Elmālī additionally reports the orchard uprooted and burned black like ash.
  68:20/q02/p6 A garden from which all good was cut off, leaving nothing. — reasons: Al-Ḥasan and authors reporting him apply the root sense 'cut off' to its good rather than specifying its color or a physical harvest. Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī connect this to a passive sense of ṣarīm.
  68:20/q02/p7 Something wholly severed or cut off, without specifying a single physical comparison. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās calls ṣarīm equivalent to maṣrūm, as qatīl can mean maqtūl; al-Bayḍāwī likewise allows passive faʿīl. Al-Thaʿlabī says this is the basic meaning and explains night and dawn by their mutual separation. Al-Elmālī extends it to cut fruit, harvested land, a portion of night and barren sand. Al-Biqāʿī also mentions something severed from its destination. Al-Wāḥidī reports the alternative active-form derivation from darkness cutting off movement, but says it cannot account for day. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports Abū Ḥātim's general 'cut off' sense, al-Rāghib's derivation from ṣarm as severance, and Ibn al-Athīr's cutting gloss.
  68:20/q02/p8 Cut-down trees or an orchard severed at the roots and burned. — reasons: Qatāda takes it as an orchard physically cut down. Al-Rāzī says burned trees resembled fruitless, cut trees. Al-Elmālī transmits a stronger reading in which the whole orchard, not merely its fruit, was uprooted and burned black. ʿAṭāʾ objects that fire burns trees rather than making them resemble a harvested orchard and favors fruit removed while trees remain.
  68:20/q02/p9 Al-Ṣarīm, a particular Yemeni place called Ḍarwān near Ṣanʿāʾ. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī reports the place identification generally and through Saʿīd ibn Jubayr; the latter locates Ḍarwān six miles from Ṣanʿāʾ. Other authors describe a known barren Yemeni sand without this place name.
  68:20/q02/p10 A black jar, to which the burned garden was compared. — reasons: Ibn Kaysān supplies this comparison.
  68:20/q02/p11 Gold, as a reported sense of al-ṣarīm. — reasons: In a dialogue with Nāfiʿ ibn al-Azraq, Ibn ʿAbbās gives this sense and cites an anonymous poem using the word.
  68:20/q02/p12 The garden was like something gone or vanished. — reasons: Al-Itqān gives 'that which has gone' as a gloss and begins to introduce a poetic witness whose line is absent from the excerpt. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports Ibn ʿAbbās giving the same sense with a poetic usage; she rejects it in favor of cutting or harvesting because the owners intended to harvest, not make the garden disappear.
68:20/q03 Why can night be called ṣarīm? [كَٱلصَّرِيمِ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the name comes from the mutual separation of night and day or from an effect peculiar to darkness; day also being called ṣarīm is the stated objection to the latter.)
  68:20/q03/p1 Night and day are severed from one another, so each can bear the name. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī, Shammar, al-Jishumī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya and others explain the two temporal senses by mutual separation. Al-Qushayrī points to the day usage when objecting to a derivation based only on darkness stopping activity. Ibn Qutayba explicitly uses the mutual separation of night and day to explain both senses, as Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports; she also transmits Abū Ḥātim's same explanation.
  68:20/q03/p2 Darkness cuts off activity or sight, hence night bears the name. — reasons: A linguist reported by al-Wāḥidī derives it as an active form from darkness restricting movement, while al-Rāzī also mentions the cutting off of sight. Al-Wāḥidī says it fails to explain the day sense; al-Qushayrī rejects it on that same ground.
68:20/q04 What happened to the garden overnight? [* · reports]
  68:20/q04/p1 A divine visitation or calamity struck while the owners slept and burned or devastated the orchard. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī notes that a ṭāʾif ordinarily arrives at night unless daytime is specified. Al-Farrāʾ describes a fire and says the sons first thought they had lost their way, then understood their refusal of the poor. Al-Qushayrī says a calamity from heaven burned its fruits. Ibn Kathīr, al-Durr and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī also describe a nocturnal divine visitation; al-Durr transmits Qatāda and Qaṭr ibn Maymūn. Al-Elmālī calls the result a scorched husk. Iṣlāḥī says attribution to God marks a wholly unexpected, instantaneous, miraculous devastation.
68:20/q05 What do the owners' loss and the ruined garden teach? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The notes draw complementary lessons from the same loss: deprivation for wrongdoing, repentance after a trial, and the reversal of a sworn plan.)
  68:20/q05/p1 Their refusal and sin caused the loss; sin may deprive a person of expected provision. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ says the owners recognized their refusal to help the poor as the cause. Al-Biqāʿī says heirs who sought everything and withheld charity lost the orchard entirely. A report from Ibn Masʿūd and reports attributed to the Prophet apply the warning that sin withholds prepared provision to the owners' lost crop; the latter also mentions lost knowledge and missed night prayer.
  68:20/q05/p2 The loss could be punishment or a trial that became a grace through repentance. — reasons: Al-Jishumī gives both possibilities because the owners repented. Ibn ʿAṭiyya likewise says repentance remained open to the survivors, as it did for the brothers.
  68:20/q05/p3 Their sworn plan was fulfilled against them, unexpectedly and by another hand. — reasons: Al-Elmālī says the oath came true in a harmful way they neither intended nor executed. Iṣlāḥī says punishment can ruin a plan secured by pledges just as people wake to enact it.
68:20/q06 How does this episode address Quraysh and reassure the Prophet? [* · links]
  68:20/q06/p1 Quraysh can likewise be punished in this life despite apparent security, while repentance remains possible; God's swift ruin reassures the Prophet. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya compares Quraysh's possible worldly punishment and the brothers' opportunity to repent. Iṣlāḥī says 'from your Lord' reassures the Prophet amid Quraysh's ease, and the parable answers those who call him possessed because they feel secure.
68:20/q07 What rhetorical effects connect this wording to the surrounding account? [فَأَصْبَحَتْ كَٱلصَّرِيمِ · rhetoric]
  68:20/q07/p1 The morning verb contrasts with the possible night sense of ṣarīm; ṣarīm echoes the earlier verb for harvesting. — rea
