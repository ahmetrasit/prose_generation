## THE CURRENT MAP
2:264/q01 Why address believers and repeat the warning against spoiled charity? [يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ لَا تُبْطِلُوا۟ صَدَقَٰتِكُم · rhetoric]
  2:264/q01/p1 The direct address strengthens a renewed prohibition because reproach and harm are especially damaging in charity. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says earlier conditions did not suffice, the repetition stresses the wrong, and charity is singled out because reproach is especially ugly there. Al-Ālūsī notes a shift from third person to direct address and says either vice spoils the gift. Jishumī says the parable reinforces the immediately preceding warning after charity was discussed. Ibn ʿĀshūr explains iltifāt as a meaningful shift in address that renews attention, quoting Ibn Jinnī's description of it as Arabic bravery.
  2:264/q01/p2 The warning implies that ostentation, reproach, and harm are traits believers must avoid. — reasons: Al-Bayḍāwī says the vices are insinuated to be qualities of disbelievers; al-Biqāʿī calls the description a severe warning. Al-Ālūsī treats the closing denial of guidance as completing the warning. Jishumī explicitly glosses the address as those who believe God and His messenger.
  2:264/q01/p3 The warning precedes the positive incentive because preventing harm takes priority; its repeated image makes the point clearer. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī relates the order of prohibition, incentive, and renewed warning.
2:264/q02 What does it mean for reminders or harm to invalidate charity? [لَا تُبْطِلُوا۟ صَدَقَٰتِكُم بِٱلْمَنِّ وَٱلْأَذَىٰ · theology] (turns on: Whether reward first accrues and can later be erased, or the gift lacked sincere intention and was never accepted; other accounts distinguish merit or future increase from prior reward.)
  2:264/q02/p1 The particular gift loses its reward through the donor's reproach or harm. — reasons: Muqātil and al-Ṭabarī say injury and reproach cost the reward; Ibn Zayd likens total loss to washed soil and cites 2:270, 2:272. Al-Qāḍī ʿAbd al-Jabbār stresses that a completed act cannot be undone, only future recompense. Muʿtazilites say a formerly meritorious act loses reward later. Ibn Kathīr says its reward cannot outweigh the sin. Al-Biqāʿī says the wrong can match or exceed reward. Al-Qurṭubī limits the loss to this gift, not unrelated deeds; Bursevī distinguishes lost reward from the completed act. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the analogy's show-off was invalid from the start, whereas the believers' gift is voided later, and the verse does not settle whether charity cancels other sins. Qatāda says God disliked this behavior for believers; al-Ṭabrisī says a deed outside its commanded form earns no reward. Al-Biqāʿī says the comparison presents the show-off at his worst, without belief. Al-Dāmaghānī calls ibṭāl making charity fruitless and compares 47:33. Jishumī argues the completed act cannot be undone, so its reward is cancelled; total loss, the parable, and the final clause support his doctrine of iḥbāṭ.
  2:264/q02/p2 The gift never qualified as accepted charity because its eventual reproach reveals corrupt intention. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya prefers this account: reproach exposes a defective intention, but does not invalidate an unrelated sound gift; the majority say God does not accept a gift He knows will be reproached over, while another account has an angel marked not to record it. Al-Rāzī's school says the command forbids giving in a null form and argues that later events cannot retroactively erase earlier merit; he prefers outward dust that never adheres to stone. Abū Ḥayyān prefers that image because dust merely rests on stone. Bursevī grounds reward in divine gift and the transaction with God (73:20; 9:111). Al-Ālūsī says the reading departs from the apparent wording though the simile supports it.
  2:264/q02/p3 Reminders erase the gift's excellence or its effect in the recipient's heart, while a sincere giver's reward remains. — reasons: Al-Māwardī distinguishes the show-off's lost reward from the sincere donor's lost merit and also proposes loss of effect on the recipient. Al-Qurṭubī reports and prefers the excellence reading over another account of reward's timing.
  2:264/q02/p4 Only reward accruing after reproach stops; earlier reward remains and further multiplication ceases. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī reports that otherwise charity continues to grow like a mountain.
  2:264/q02/p5 Theological cancellation of prior merit by a later sin is incoherent; good and blameworthy consequences can coexist. — reasons: Al-Rāzī's school argues that if merits conflict a later one has no priority, and that a past, present, or future merit cannot be erased by a later event without circular causation. He rejects Abū Hāshim's mutual balancing as contradictory and Abū ʿAlī al-Jubbāʾī's one-way erasure as contrary to 99:7 and justice. He argues selecting parts of reward for cancellation is arbitrary, and illustrates coexisting praise and blame with a servant who saves his master but exposes property to theft. He rejects a grave sin erasing decades of faith and compares faith after long unbelief.
  2:264/q02/p6 A grave sin may outweigh and erase prior obedient deeds. — reasons: The Muʿtazilite claim in al-Rāzī says immense divine gifts make disobedience exceptionally grave; ʿAbd Allāh ibn Abī Zakariyyā reports hearing that one ostentatious deed can cancel earlier deeds.
2:264/q03 Who receives the reproach called mann, and what does it mean? [بِٱلْمَنِّ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the boast is addressed to the recipient or to God.)
  2:264/q03/p1 It is reminding the recipient of a favor, claiming credit, or boasting about a gift. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj defines the reminder and credit claim; al-Wāḥidī gives boasting. Al-Rāzī calls the recipient reading clearer, and Abū Ḥayyān prefers it as the apparent and majority meaning. Jishumī reports the petitioner as one possible person reminded.
  2:264/q03/p2 It is reminding God of one's charitable act, while the injury falls on the recipient. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās is repeatedly cited for this division; al-Rāzī says it is possible but the recipient reading is clearer. Al-Dāmaghānī says the donor reminds God of the gift while injuring the recipient; Jishumī also reports this direction of the reminder.
  2:264/q03/p3 The giver may boast of the gift before other Muslims. — reasons: Jishumī reports Muslims as a third possible audience of mann, besides God and the beggar.
2:264/q04 What is the injury called adhā? [وَٱلْأَذَىٰ · meaning]
  2:264/q04/p1 It is rebuking, reproaching, or hurting the recipient. — reasons: Muqātil says reproach injures the recipient; al-Zajjāj and al-Wāḥidī specify rebuke. Ibn ʿAbbās directs the harm toward the petitioner even when directing mann toward God. Al-Dāmaghānī and Jishumī explicitly put the resulting harm on the gift's recipient.
2:264/q05 What practical and reported warnings concern gifts followed by reproach? [لَا تُبْطِلُوا۟ صَدَقَٰتِكُم بِٱلْمَنِّ وَٱلْأَذَىٰ · law]
  2:264/q05/p1 Withholding a gift is better than giving it and then injuring its recipient. — reasons: Al-Ḍaḥḥāk gives this practical judgment.
  2:264/q05/p2 A reproached gift loses gratitude and reward; donors should avoid recounting favors. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī reports Arabic labels: a reproached gift is a black hand, an unsolicited gift white, and a requested gift green; al-Naḥḥās also notes this idiom. A rhetorician distinguishes lost gratitude from self-admiration's lost reward; Ibn Sīrīn stops a man enumerating favors. A Prophetic report recites this verse after warning that reproach erases gratitude and reward, without grading.
  2:264/q05/p3 A charity that loses reward has different consequences for voluntary and obligatory giving. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī says voluntary secret charity is as though never done, whereas spoiled obligatory alms bring a threat as if the duty were unperformed.
  2:264/q05/p4 Giving obligatory alms through others can help avoid praise, reminders, and repayment. — reasons: Mālik disliked giving obligatory alms to relatives for these risks, preferred strangers, and preferred another distributor under an unjust ruler.
  2:264/q05/p5 Reproach can also forfeit the credit of spending on a campaign. — reasons: Abū Hānī al-Khawlānī and ʿAmr ibn Ḥurayth relay a fighter who curses his commander and campaign after hardship and vows not to return; his spending yields no credit.
  2:264/q05/p6 Prophetic warnings place the reproacher of gifts among grave offenders. — reasons: Reports name habitual wine drinkers and those who mistreat parents alongside reproachers as barred from Paradise or denied God's gaze; another adds belief in magic and soothsaying. Ibn ʿAbbās says he was troubled until finding this verse on reproach and the wine verse 5:90; one report says he took comfort in sinners' ability to repent.
2:264/q06 How does the comparison introduced by kā work grammatically? [كَٱلَّذِى يُنفِقُ مَالَهُۥ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the implied comparison is between kinds of invalidation or between the persons' states.)
  2:264/q06/p1 It modifies an omitted noun for the act or its invalidation: do not invalidate charity as the show-off does. — reasons: Al-Mahdawī supplies 'the invalidation of'; al-Rāzī says both deeds lack God's pleasure. Al-Nasafī makes the kāf an adjective of an omitted verbal noun. Al-Samīn adds Sībawayh's circumstantial analysis from the implicit verbal noun. Ibn ʿĀshūr alternatively supplies spending with an omitted possessive.
  2:264/q06/p2 It is circumstantial from the addressed donors: do not invalidate gifts while resembling the showy spender. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī calls the kāf accusative as a state of the subject in تبطلوا; Ibn ʿĀshūr treats it as an established circumstantial expression. Al-Ālūsī says this favors invalidity from the outset. Al-Suyūṭī argues the kāf is circumstantial from the plural subject without ellipsis and rejects al-Makkī's elided verbal-noun analysis.
