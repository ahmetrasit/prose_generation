## THE CURRENT MAP
78:14/q01 What does the plural in “We sent down” convey? [وَأَنزَلْنَا · rhetoric]
  78:14/q01/p1 It signals the majesty and collectivity of the Divine Essence, Names, and Attributes. — reasons: Bursevi explains the divine plural in these terms.
78:14/q02 What are the muʿṣirāt? [ٱلْمُعْصِرَٰتِ · referent] (turns on: The immediate source of rain ('from' clouds or sky) versus winds as the means of drawing it out; some explanations turn on whether the name describes imminent rainfall, squeezing, relief, or whirlwinds.)
  78:14/q02/p1 Rain-bearing clouds, heavy with water and ready to release it. — reasons: Al-Tabari, al-Nahhas, al-Qurtubi, and Ibn Kathir favor clouds because rain comes from them and the verse says 'from'; Ibn Kathir cites 30:48. Al-Farra, Ibn Qutayba, al-Razi, al-Zamakhshari and others liken impending rain to a girl approaching menstruation; Abu al-Najm's verse is cited by al-Tha'labi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi. Al-Azhari disputes al-Farra's precise definition: Abu Ubayd's informants say a girl has reached maturity, al-Kisa'i says approaching twenty, and Ibn al-A'rabi says the moment of menstruation, relating it to seclusion/refuge. Al-Wahidi and Bursevi compare ready grain; al-Samin notes the active form suits imminence, since otherwise a passive participle would be expected for clouds pressed by winds, and reports al-Zamakhshari's entry-into-a-state explanation. Abu Ishaq derives the name from clouds squeezing out water; al-Mubarrad says they retain and release water gradually, with 'asr also meaning refuge. Ibn Kaysan links the term to relief and 12:49; al-Samin questions the form-IV derivation. Al-Azhari prefers rain clouds over whirlwinds, which do not bring rain. Al-Biqai adds that these clouds cool the sun and enable food and drink. Ibn Kathir reports al-Tabari's preference; Islahi says water-laden and rain-producing clouds do not differ essentially.
  78:14/q02/p2 Winds that squeeze, form, drive, or draw rain from clouds. — reasons: Ibn Zayd cites 30:48 for winds raising clouds; al-Wahidi reports a rain-bringing wind, while al-Baghawi describes winds compressing clouds. Mujahid and others also describe whirlwinds or cyclonic winds. Al-Azhari, al-Tabrisi and Ibn al-Jawzi take min instrumentally as bi; al-Tabari objects that winds do not contain water, and al-Qurtubi prefers clouds as rain's immediate source. Al-Zamakhshari says winds form clouds and release moisture and cautiously reports that winds carry water from heaven. Al-Alusi says they compress clouds until rain falls. Bursevi rejects a causative derivation because winds themselves do the squeezing. Ibn Kathir cites reports from Ibn Abbas through al-Awfi and Ibn Abi Hatim.
  78:14/q02/p3 The heavens or sky from which water descends. — reasons: Al-Hasan, Qatada and other named authorities identify the heavens. Al-Samin explains that water passes from heaven to clouds, as if the heavens enabled their pressing. Al-Nahhas, as quoted by al-Qurtubi, says sky, wind, and cloud explanations may converge because winds pollinate clouds; Ibn Kathir calls the heaven view unusual, while al-Alusi objects that the heavens are not squeezed and rejects the proposed reconciliation as strained.
  78:14/q02/p4 Clouds specifically characterized by spiraling winds or whirlwinds. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba reports clouds possessing spiraling winds; al-Mu'arrikh and al-Mazini give the same description. Al-Wahidi's report from al-Mazini says a cloud has made an i'sar when a strong dust whirlwind rises; al-Alusi says such clouds necessarily bring rain. Al-Azhari counters that whirlwinds themselves are not rain-bearing.
  78:14/q02/p5 Specifically the south wind. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi attributes this specification to Zayd ibn Aslam.
78:14/q03 How is “from” construed in the rain phrase? [مِنَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether min names the place water comes from or the winds that cause its release; the bi variant favors the latter.)
  78:14/q03/p1 It marks the source or starting point of the descending water. — reasons: Al-Tabari, al-Nahhas, al-Qurtubi and Ibn Kathir use the ordinary 'from' sense to support clouds; al-Samin calls it the starting point. Al-Alusi reports an origin construal even when discussing the wind reading. Bursevi says descent from sky can pass through clouds.
  78:14/q03/p2 It has an instrumental or causal force like bi, rain coming by means of winds. — reasons: Al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi's al-Azhari, al-Tabrisi, Ibn al-Jawzi and al-Tabatabai expressly explain min as bi for winds. Abu Hayyan calls it attributing rain to its cause; al-Samin distinguishes the causal bi reading from the starting-point min reading. Al-Alusi reports causal and origin construals.
78:14/q04 What preposition is read before al-muʿṣirāt? [مِنَ ٱلْمُعْصِرَٰتِ · readings] (turns on: The codices' min is read as source; reported bi is generally taken as cause or instrument and often supports the wind interpretation.)
  78:14/q04/p1 The written codices read min al-muʿṣirāt. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi distinguishes the codices' min from reports of bi; al-Samin gives min a starting-point sense.
  78:14/q04/p2 Some readers have bi-l-muʿṣirāt instead. — reasons: Al-Tabari reports this from Ikrima and Qatada; Ibn Atiyya names Ibn al-Zubayr, Ibn Abbas, al-Fadl ibn Abbas, Qatada and Ikrima; Abu Hayyan adds Abd Allah ibn Yazid, and al-Samin names him. Al-Qurtubi names Ibn Abbas and Ikrima. Al-Jishumi compares 97:4. Durr reports Fadl ibn Abbas's codex, Ibn Abbas through Qatada, and Mujahid's bi reading. Al-Alusi calls bi causal or instrumental; Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan say it supports winds, though al-Zamakhshari, reported by al-Samin, permits clouds too, like giving from or by one's hand.
78:14/q05 How is the rain brought down and sustained? [وَأَنزَلْنَا مَآءًۭ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the notes describe wind transporting heavenly water, rain passing from heaven through clouds, or the sun raising terrestrial water into clouds.)
  78:14/q05/p1 Winds gather, carry, and drive water or water-filled clouds until rain falls. — reasons: Muqatil says winds draw water from the lowest heaven's sea of provisions (51:22), raise and join clouds, then release rain with wind. Ibn Masud says winds collect water and drive it into clouds until full like a milch camel; al-Tha'labi likens the falling scattered drops to water drawn from a pregnant she-camel. Durr gives Ibn Masud's full-udder image and heavy showers distributed by wind; Nasafi reports winds carrying sky water to clouds.
  78:14/q05/p2 Rain descends from the heavens through clouds. — reasons: Qatada and al-Hasan report descent from the sky. Al-Samin explains the sky-to-cloud passage; Bursevi says this reconciles sky descent with cloud descent, and Nasafi relays a report of winds carrying sky water to clouds.
  78:14/q05/p3 Celestial causes, including the sun lifting water from earth and sea, may produce rain clouds. — reasons: Bursevi offers this as another way to understand descent from the sky.
78:14/q06 What does thajjāj mean here? [ثَجَّاجًۭا · meaning] (turns on: Whether thajj itself names continuous pouring, with abundance as a consequence, or means sheer abundance; al-Tabari disputes the latter as Arabic usage.)
  78:14/q06/p1 Rain pouring copiously, forcefully, or in successive continuous flows. — reasons: Mujahid, Qatada, Ibn Abbas and others gloss it as pouring; al-Tabari stresses continuous succession, like sacrificial blood, and cites the Prophetic phrase about loud talbiya and sacrifice. Al-Nahhas likewise favors repeated pouring with the hadith. Al-Tha'labi, al-Tabrisi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, al-Biqai, Bursevi, al-Alusi, Elmalili, Tabatabai and Islahi add abundance, force, rapidity or repeated waves. Al-Samin cites a poem for copious forceful flow, and Durr an unnamed poem about black clouds. Ibn Kathir cites the report of a woman with prolonged bleeding; al-Razi mentions Ibn Abbas's epithet for copious speech. Al-Zajjaj takes the water as pouring itself; al-Alusi dismisses an objection that clouds could only release a little water as relative comparison. Muqatil links the copious successive rain to clouds; al-Qurtubi says Ibn Zayd's 'abundant' gives the same meaning.
  78:14/q06/p2 It means abundance of water or rain as such. — reasons: Ibn Zayd gives 'abundant'; al-Tha'labi attributes plentiful rain to him, and also reports Mujahid's abundant-rain gloss. Al-Tabari objects that Arabic thajj does not denote quantity alone; Ibn Kathir repeats that objection. Al-Qurtubi regards Ibn Zayd's gloss as the same meaning as copious pouring.
78:14/q07 Can the verb behind thajjāj be transitive or intransitive? [ثَجَّاجًۭا · grammar] (turns on: Whether the flowing water is the verb's subject or something poured by an agent; al-Alusi says the intransitive is more common.)
  78:14/q07/p1 It can be intransitive: the water flows or pours itself. — reasons: Al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, al-Biqai and Elmalili acknowledge this use. Al-Zajjaj's gloss makes the water pour itself. Al-Alusi prefers it as the more common construction.
  78:14/q07/p2 It can be transitive: someone pours the water. — reasons: Al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, al-Biqai and Elmalili acknowledge this construction; al-Alusi attributes it to al-Zajjaj while preferring the intransitive sense.
78:14/q08 What variant form is reported for thajjāj? [ثَجَّاجًۭا · readings] (turns on: The reported final consonant or form varies, while the notes connect the forms with flowing water.)
