12:36/q29 How is the hamza in 'tell us' read? [نَبِّئْنَا · readings] (turns on: How the hamza is articulated.)
  12:36/q29/p1 The standard reading retains the hamza. — reasons: Al-Nahhas says hamzated nabbiʾnā retains yāʾ unless hamza is lightened or replaced; Qiraat-ER records standard hamza. Ibn al-Jazarī details Abu Jaʿfar's disputed routes for tahqīq of nabbiʾnā; Abū Ṭāhir, al-Hudhalī, Abū al-ʿIzz, Abū al-ʿAlā, and Ibn Mihrān transmit or discuss retention. Al-Aṣbahānī's Warsh route excepts nabbiʾ forms from substitution.
  12:36/q29/p2 A ten-reader canonical variant elides the hamza. — reasons: Qiraat-ER says the meaning remains; al-Nahhas discusses lightening and replacement. Ibn al-Jazarī explains Abū Jaʿfar's replacement of silent hamza with a preceding-vowel-matched long vowel and records substitution of nabbiʾnā through some routes.
12:36/q30 Why is 'tell us' plural when each had his own dream? [نَبِّئْنَا · rhetoric]
  12:36/q30/p1 The plural narrates their separate requests collectively. — reasons: Bursevi and al-Alusi compare messengers separately addressed in 23:51; al-Tabataba'i says repeated 'said' replaces a repeated request.
12:36/q31 What does 'its interpretation' mean? [بِتَأْوِيلِهِۦٓ · meaning]
  12:36/q31/p1 It is the dream's interpretation: the outcome or end to which what was seen leads. — reasons: Al-Tabari and Abu Ubayd derive taʾwīl from return; Ibn Abbas glosses interpretation. Al-Razi and al-Biqaʿi stress the eventual result, while Mujahid speaks of expression of the dream. Al-Dāmaghānī and Ibn al-Jawzī give dream interpretation as a sense of taʾwīl; Ibn al-Jawzī also cites the dream's later fulfillment in 12:100.
12:36/q32 What does the pronoun in 'its interpretation' refer to? [بِتَأْوِيلِهِۦٓ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the singular pronoun summarizes both accounts or distributes over each dream.)
  12:36/q32/p1 It refers to the whole account or both visions, like a demonstrative 'that.' — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari and Abu Hayyan take the singular pronoun collectively; Ibn ʿAshur allows the mentioned matter or dream as a class. Al-Alusi takes what was seen.
  12:36/q32/p2 Each man means his own dream. — reasons: Al-Samin reports this alternative; al-Tabataba'i says the pronoun contextually refers to what each saw.
12:36/q33 What did the prisoners mean by calling Joseph one of the beneficent? [إِنَّا نَرَىٰكَ مِنَ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether they praise observed conduct, intellectual skill, or the favor they hope he will perform.)
  12:36/q33/p1 He was kind to fellow prisoners and visibly virtuous. — reasons: Al-Tabari prefers this practical-kindness sense, explaining that interpretation would be a further favor; al-Dahhak, Qatada and many others mention treating sick people, consoling grief, making room, gathering aid, worship, and good speech. Al-Tabataba'i says signs of a pure heart made them trust his judgment; Islahi defines muhsin as one who acts before God.
  12:36/q33/p2 He was learned or skilled at interpreting dreams. — reasons: Al-Farra says learned; Ibn al-Anbari supplies 'in knowledge' and recalls his teaching; al-Zajjaj says skilled in interpretation. Ibn ʿAtiyya and Abu Hayyan call this the majority view. Ibn ʿAshur defines ihsan as proficiency and says they inferred ability from intelligence without proof. Asad calls it figurative usage.
  12:36/q33/p3 The praise is a polite, conditional appeal: interpret our dreams and thereby do us good. — reasons: Ibn Ishaq and al-Farra read the praise prospectively; al-Tabari answers that the requested interpretation is kindness like Joseph's other acts. Qushayri says it seeks his beneficence.
12:36/q34 What specific acts made Joseph known as beneficent in prison? [ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ · reports]
  12:36/q34/p1 He cared for sick and distressed inmates, aided the needy, made space, urged patience and worshipped God. — reasons: Al-Dahhak adds practical aid and intercession; Qatada adds hope of reward, cleansing and devotion; al-Mawardi lists accepting excuses and putting others first; Ibn Kathir adds honesty and trustworthiness. Al-Tabataba'i reports borrowing for needy people and helping the weak. Al-Biqaʿi says inmates felt blessed by his company.
12:36/q35 Why did Joseph postpone interpreting the dreams? [نَبِّئْنَا بِتَأْوِيلِهِۦٓ · interpretation]
  12:36/q35/p1 He knew one interpretation would mean suffering and first diverted to a prophetic sign and monotheism. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabi, Baghawi and al-Wajiz connect the delay to the baker's fate. Al-Biqaʿi says Joseph used their receptiveness to counsel worship of God and rejection of partners first; al-Tabrisi invokes calling with wisdom (16:125).
12:36/q36 What lesson does Joseph's response give about teaching? [* · interpretation]
  12:36/q36/p1 A teacher may give essential counsel before answering and mention expertise to guide listeners toward God. — reasons: Al-Biqaʿi draws this from Joseph's preaching and claim to know interpretations; al-Tabrisi links the address to wisdom in 16:125.
12:36/q37 What did Joseph mean by promising to interpret before food arrived? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the coming food is dream material or ordinary provision.)
  12:36/q37/p1 He claimed prophetic foreknowledge of the prisoners' food dreams. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabi says most commentators take the promise this way.
  12:36/q37/p2 He meant ordinary waking food: what it was, when and how much they ate. — reasons: The prisoners suspected divination, but Joseph attributed the knowledge to God. Al-Tabataba'i reports a different contextual pronoun interpretation as less likely.
12:36/q38 What became of the two interpretations and Joseph's request? [* · reports]
  12:36/q38/p1 The cupbearer returned to service and the baker was crucified; Joseph asked to be remembered to the master. — reasons: Al-Durr reports his predictions, their denial of having dreamed, and Joseph's answer that the matter was settled. Mujahid also reports his reply. Ikrima specifies three days until the cupbearer's release.
12:36/q39 What does the verse establish about dreams and their interpretation? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether any dream can disclose reality, and which causes distinguish truthful from ordinary dreams.)
  12:36/q39/p1 Some dreams truly disclose future or hidden events and can be interpreted. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj says denying all true dreams contradicts scripture and a prophetic report of one-fortieth of prophecy. Al-Razi cites a report of one forty-sixth and explains the soul's sleep-time access to higher realities. Al-Tabataba'i argues correspondence to future events is not chance and distinguishes bodily and emotional dreams from sound dreams.
  12:36/q39/p2 Dreams include self-talk, satanic dreams and true dreams; many arise from bodily, emotional or chance causes. — reasons: Al-Razi accepts the prophetic threefold report as rationally sound. Al-Tabataba'i says imagination recombines sensory images, affected by body, temperament and emotions; all dreams have causes, though many do not foretell external events. He grants naturalists' account for some dreams.
  12:36/q39/p3 All dreams are unreal. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i rejects this inference from ordinary bodily and emotional causes; he says some dreams relate to external truth.
12:36/q40 How can a true dream disclose reality, and when does it need interpretation? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether the perception is direct or mediated by symbolic transformations.)
  12:36/q40/p1 The sleeping soul can reach nonphysical causes, seeing them directly or as symbolic images. — reasons: Al-Razi says bodily distraction lessens in sleep. Al-Tabataba'i distinguishes physical, imaginal and intellectual worlds; a sound soul may perceive causes clearly, while symbols or opposites such as crown/honor and darkness/ignorance need return to their meanings. Tangled symbolic chains resist interpretation. Ibn Sirin's Ramadan caller example illustrates decoding.
  12:36/q40/p2 Some dreams are clear without symbolic interpretation. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i says clear dreams may reveal events directly and identifies these apart from interpretable symbols.
12:36/q41 What do reports say about falsely claiming a dream? [* · reports]
  12:36/q41/p1 A dream liar will be ordered on Judgment Day to tie grains together, an impossible task. — reasons: Ibn Abbas's version says two barley grains; al-Tirmidhi grades it hasan sahih. ʿAli's version says a grain and is graded hasan. Al-Thaʿlabi's accompanying report also warns that secret eavesdroppers receive molten lead in the ear.
12:36/q42 How does this prison scene connect with later events and the sura's design? [* · links]
  12:36/q42/p1 Dream interpretation in prison eventually leads to Joseph's release and rule. — reasons: Al-Wahidi says divine grace made both his interpretive skill and kindness means of rescue; Qushayri says companionship bore fruit when the forgetful cellmate later spoke at 12:46; Islahi links the correct interpretations to the king's dream and appointment.
  12:36/q42/p2 The prison's apparent humiliation became honor under God's direction of events. — reasons: Al-Biqaʿi cites al-Razi on gentleness mingled with trial and sees the prison fulfilling a prior promise. Asad contrasts Genesis and cites 2:216: people can dislike what benefits them. Al-Tabataba'i places the inmates' dreams beside Joseph's and the king's dreams and waking predictions in the sura.
