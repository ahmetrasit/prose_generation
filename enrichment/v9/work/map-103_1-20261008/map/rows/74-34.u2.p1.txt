 Atiyya says kalla rebukes disbelievers and rebels, then the oaths by moon, night and morning honor signs of the God who sustains all existence.
  74:34/q09/p2 It rejects claims that people can count God's hosts or overpower them. — reasons: The Study Quran reports this explanation from Al and Tabatabai, alongside its comparison with other moon and night-day oaths.
  74:34/q09/p3 It rejects the audience's denial or challenge over the delayed Judgment before giving proof. — reasons: Islahi says the denial is so objectionable that emphatic negation comes before the evidentiary oath; the oaths then answer disbelievers asking why the promised Judgment has not yet arrived.
74:34/q10 Which other Qur'anic oaths or passages echo this sequence? [* · links]
  74:34/q10/p1 Other passages swear by the moon and by the succession of night and day. — reasons: The Study Quran cites 84:18 and 91:2 for moon oaths and 81:17-18, 84:16-17, 89:1-4, 91:1-4 and 92:1-2 for related oaths; its note also lists 91:1-4 as a group. Islahi cites 81:17-18 for night yielding to morning as an image of Judgment and for the daily alternation as evidence of that Day. Bint al-Shati also invokes 89:1, 93:1, 92:1, and 53:1 when discussing oaths by dawn, sun, and night and their light-dark imagery.
74:34/q11 Which moon is meant in the preceding oath alongside night and dawn? [* · referent]
  74:34/q11/p1 The crescent after its third night. — reasons: Bursevi reports this specification from Abu al-Layth for the moon in the preceding oath.
74:34/q12 What ancillary material appears in the added source passages? [* · reports]
  74:34/q12/p1 Al-Damghani also lists senses of saqat: regret, falling into shirk, scattering, and literal falling. — reasons: Al-Damghani gives this separate lexical entry alongside the extracted dawn gloss.
  74:34/q12/p2 Al-Suyuti explains the abridgment, title, and prayer attached to his report collection. — reasons: Al-Suyuti says he condensed his multivolume isnad-based Tafsir Musnad to provide hadith texts without long chains while retaining text and reliable-book references. He titles it al-Durr al-manthur fi al-tafsir bi-l-ma'thur and asks God to guard its author against error and falsehood.
  74:34/q12/p3 The collection gives reports about al-Fatihah's Qur'anic status, codex inclusion, and Meccan revelation. — reasons: Ibrahim reports al-Aswad's affirmation that al-Fatihah is Qur'an, transmitted by Abd ibn Humayd. Reports from Muhammad ibn Sirin and Ibn al-Anbari say Ubayy wrote al-Fatihah, the two protective suras, and invocations whereas Ibn Masud wrote none. Uthman wrote al-Fatihah and the protective suras; Ibn Masud said he would have placed al-Fatihah at the beginning of everything had he written it. Ali says it came down in Mecca from a treasure beneath the Throne, transmitted by al-Wahidi and al-Tha'labi.
  74:34/q12/p4 Abu Maysarah reports the Prophet's account to Khadijah of hearing a voice while alone. — reasons: Abu Maysarah Amr ibn Shurahbil says the Prophet feared what the voice meant and Khadijah answered, 'God forbid.'
  74:34/q12/p5 The Itqan reports derivations and differing definitions of tafsir and ta'wil. — reasons: Al-Suyuti reports ta'wil derived from return or governance. Abu Ubayd and others identify tafsir with ta'wil; Ibn Habib objects to treating them as synonyms. Al-Raghib calls tafsir broader and word-focused, ta'wil meaning- and sentence-focused. Other accounts distinguish a word with one sense from choosing among several by evidence, or categorical divine intent from ranked possibilities (al-Maturidi), or lexical evidence from inward intended reality (Abu Talib al-Taghlibi, with his watchful-ambush example).
