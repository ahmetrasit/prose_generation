18:19/q43 What do the silver, city and food signify inwardly? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ ٱلْمَدِينَةِ أَزْكَىٰ طَعَامًۭا · inward] (turns on: The allegorical mappings differ: knowledge, intellect, or scrupulous livelihood.)
  18:19/q43/p1 Silver is innate first principles, the city a gathering place of knowledge, and food sound knowledge that nourishes the heart. — reasons: Qashani cites the saying that Muhammad is the city of knowledge and Ali its gate; he rejects empty dialectic and superficial appearances, with 88:7 as a contrast. One learner may then teach the rest, as 9:122 suggests.
  18:19/q43/p2 The envoy is thought among spiritual faculties, seeking intellect instead of illusion, imagination or sensation. — reasons: Qashani gives this alternate symbolic mapping of youths and city.
  18:19/q43/p3 Pure food signifies scrupulous lawful sustenance that preserves insight. — reasons: Alusi quotes Dhu al-Nun on not extinguishing gnosis with scruple; he criticizes a shaykh accepting money known to come from an oppressor.
18:19/q44 What inward instruction is drawn from acting gently and concealing the youths? [وَلْيَتَلَطَّفْ وَلَا يُشْعِرَنَّ بِكُمْ أَحَدًا · inward] (turns on: The inward application concerns spiritual training and the audience for hidden knowledge.)
  18:19/q44/p1 Choose an upright, verified guide and deal gently with spiritually mature people. — reasons: Qashani warns against pretenders; Sulami transmits Abu Uthman that gnostics receive gentleness and novices firmness; Yusuf ibn al-Husayn gives gnostics delicate food and ascetics ordinary food. Qushayri reports the same food distinction. Alusi criticizes a mandatory delicate-food rule because major exemplars lived simply.
  18:19/q44/p2 Conceal spiritual realities from people unable to receive them. — reasons: Qashani says ignorant people should not be told the youths' state or faith; Alusi says veiled people cannot perceive such light.
18:19/q45 Why must discovery be avoided, and what threat follows? [وَلَا يُشْعِرَنَّ بِكُمْ أَحَدًا · interpretation] (turns on: Sources parse the danger as discovery followed by bodily or verbal attack and coercion.)
  18:19/q45/p1 The youths feared being found by persecutors, stoned, or forced back to idolatry. — reasons: Yahya and the narrative sources say discovery would destroy their religious success; Tabatabai says stoning signals collective hostility. Abu Hayyan prefers literal stoning over verbal abuse and says 'return' can mean entering an old creed even without earlier membership. Tabatabai says forced participation, not merely verbal assent, was feared.
  18:19/q45/p2 The next verse's 'appearance over you' can mean discovering their location or gaining ascendancy. — reasons: Abu Hayyan gives both; Tabatabai cites al-Raghib's semantic path from visibility to finding and prevailing, preferring location discovery because it leads to capture.
  18:19/q45/p3 The threatened rajm can mean verbal abuse. — reasons: Hajjaj and Ibn Jubayr are reported with this gloss; Abu Hayyan favors stoning because the king meant to kill and earlier people used it against dissent.
  18:19/q45/p4 Someone trapped under coercion may outwardly comply while preserving faith, but a person free to leave must avoid voluntarily losing religious practice. — reasons: Tabatabai cites 16:106 and 3:28 for coerced dissimulation, 4:97 against self-imposed helplessness, and rejects proposed explanations that coercion itself causes apostasy or that dissimulation was once barred.
18:19/q46 How do later variant readings shape the threatened discovery? [وَلَا يُشْعِرَنَّ بِكُمْ أَحَدًا · readings] (turns on: The following verse's verb has an active/passive variant and disputed pronoun antecedent.)
  18:19/q46/p1 Zayd ibn Ali reads yuẓhirū passively in the following threat. — reasons: Abu Hayyan reports this reading as a continuation of the secrecy warning.
  18:19/q46/p2 The ordinary active form means the city people find or overpower the youths. — reasons: Abu Hayyan says the pronoun may denote inferred unbelievers, generic aḥad, or elided townspeople; al-Zamakhshari favors the townspeople, invoking 23:8 for plural after a generic.
18:19/q47 What does the scene show about the youths' fellowship? [بَيْنَهُمْ بِوَرِقِكُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: The notes draw communal ethics and an inward pedagogy from the plural dialogue.)
  18:19/q47/p1 They share inquiry, resources, responsibility and concern for one another's faith and safety. — reasons: Al-Biqai sees affectionate agreement in deferring knowledge to God; Tabatabai highlights equal delegation, communal money and tact. Alusi reads 'your silver' as shared even if one youth had none, and the narratives recall their pledge never to abandon each other.
  18:19/q47/p2 One fully developed member can learn on behalf of the group and awaken the others. — reasons: Qashani appeals to 9:122 for the sufficiency of one envoy in learning.
18:19/q48 How does the verse fit the surah's structure? [* · links] (turns on: The sources emphasize either the local narrative expansion or the larger surah purpose.)
  18:19/q48/p1 It expands the earlier compressed cave account and answers the surah's central challenge about resurrection. — reasons: Abu Hayyan links the sleep-and-awakening sequence to 18:11; Islahi says the present questioning expands the summary near 18:13; al-Biqai ties the story to Quraysh's consultation of Jewish questioners and says acceptance or denial of the sign exposes whether they seek guidance. Ibn Ashur calls the Cave and Dhu al-Qarnayn stories the surah's main purposes.
  18:19/q48/p2 The discourse here summarizes the questioning and then supplies its words. — reasons: Ibn Ashur and Alusi describe the summary-dialogue relation; Abu Hayyan says the surah first summarizes the episode and later retells it.
18:19/q49 Where is the Quran's textual midpoint counted? [وَلْيَتَلَطَّفْ · sciences] (turns on: Different counting conventions yield different midpoint markers.)
  18:19/q49/p1 The tāʾ in walyatalaṭṭaf is counted as the midpoint letter. — reasons: Bursevi mentions an old count; Ibn Ashur records the saying.
  18:19/q49/p2 The nūn in nakran at 18:74 is counted as midpoint. — reasons: Ibn Ashur cites Ibn Atiyya and gives this alternate reckoning.
  18:19/q49/p3 The midpoint of the thirty parts is at 18:75. — reasons: Ibn Ashur distinguishes the division into thirty from letter counts and calls the surah's placement divinely arranged.
18:19/q50 How reliable and complete are the historical cave reports? [* · reports] (turns on: The extant narratives supply different historical details, and the Quran does not settle them.)
  18:19/q50/p1 The reports disagree extensively, and the Quran selects only details needed for guidance. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya deliberately shortens conflicting accounts to sounder essentials. Tabatabai lists conflicting names, dates, lineage, rulers, occupations and cave sites; he attributes variation to the tale's spread among People of the Book and to Muslim uptake of converts' outside reports, including Wahb and Ka'b. He says the Quran omits biography and gives withdrawal, sleep, awakening, exposure and mosque in sequence.
  18:19/q50/p2 The broader story includes a later encounter with the cave or its remains. — reasons: Al-Tabari reports Ibn Abbas passing a cave whose bones had vanished; Study Quran reports another account of preserved bodies after a renewed sleep.
  18:19/q50/p3 Christian accounts and Muslim reports partly overlap but differ on numbers, dog and duration. — reasons: Tabatabai says most historians place events between Jesus and Muhammad, outside the two Testaments, while Christian preservation was extensive; he compares Christian accounts with Ibn Ishaq's report.
18:19/q51 Where was the cave located according to later identifications? [* · reports] (turns on: Archaeological and historical fit is debated without decisive identification.)
  18:19/q51/p1 Rujayb near Amman is the strongest proposed fit, though identification remains unproved. — reasons: Tabatabai points to Jordanian excavation and some Muslim reports while stating no proposed cave has decisive evidence; he places Amman at ancient Philadelphia under Roman control until Muslim conquest.
  18:19/q51/p2 Other proposals place it near Damascus, Petra, Scandinavia or Nakhchivan. — reasons: Tabatabai lists Mount Qasiyun, Petra, seven preserved Roman-looking bodies in Scandinavia and a Caucasus cave; he objects that the northern claims conflict with the Roman setting and reach.
