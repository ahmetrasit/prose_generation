12:41/q32 How may Arabic temporal nouns and related expressions attach to clauses? [* · grammar]
  12:41/q32/p1 Temporal nouns commonly govern verbal clauses; past expressions also take nominal clauses, whereas future expressions take verbs. — reasons: Sibawayh gives a day-clause and Quranic examples at 77:35 and 5:119, extends verbal attachment to mundhu, mudh and a dhu expression, and explains past and future patterns through idh and idha. Yunus reports that Arabs reject a future day expression followed by a nominal clause.
12:41/q33 Can the verb taqulu govern two accusative complements? [* · grammar]
  12:41/q33/p1 Yes; it can take two accusatives by analogy with taẓunnu. — reasons: Sibawayh gives the example of asking whether one says Zayd is departing.
12:41/q34 How do prophetic signs suit the arts of their audiences? [* · theology]
  12:41/q34/p1 Moses' signs suit an age of sorcery, Jesus' an age of medicine, and Muhammad's inimitable Quran an age of eloquence. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba names Moses' sea, hand, staff and water signs; Jesus' raising, healing and clay birds; and the Quran's challenge to humans and jinn.
12:41/q35 What features of Arabic speech make expression fit its occasion? [* · rhetoric]
  12:41/q35/p1 Orators vary length, repetition, concealment, clarity and polish to suit listeners and setting. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba relates the manner to situation, audience and dignity, says rougher passages can heighten finer ones, and illustrates varied brilliance through lights and a mixed necklace.
12:41/q36 What properties of Arabic letters and case endings are noted? [* · grammar]
  12:41/q36/p1 Arabic has twenty-eight letters, and its case endings can distinguish otherwise ambiguous subjects and objects. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba treats apparent additional sounds in other languages as intermediate articulations and says case endings both adorn and disambiguate speech.
12:41/q37 What other senses of qaḍā are illustrated outside this verse? [قُضِىَ · meaning]
  12:41/q37/p1 Elsewhere qaḍā can mean commanding, informing, fashioning, carrying out, making a covenant, judging, or giving a testament. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba cites 17:23, 17:4, 41:12, 20:72, 10:71 and Abu Dhu'ayb's poem. Ibn al-Jawzi adds the formation of heavens, the slaughter of death, Hell's closed gates, Muqatil's covenant gloss, judgment and Moses' testament.
12:41/q38 How many recitation modes were granted according to the cited reports? [* · readings] (turns on: Whether the three-mode report describes a final limit or a limited or earlier case.)
  12:41/q38/p1 Seven modes were granted after requests for additional ease. — reasons: Ibn Abbas, Umm Ayyub, Sulayman ibn Surad and Ubayy transmit versions of successive increase; the Hisham and Umar report approves both recitations, and Abu Hurayra gives a limit on reversing mercy and punishment.
  12:41/q38/p2 One report gives three modes, possibly an early stage or words with three attested forms. — reasons: Al-Zarkashi relays Samura's report and Abu Ubayd's judgment that seven-mode reports are mass transmitted; Abu Shama offers the two reconciliations.
12:41/q39 What are the seven aḥruf said to comprise? [* · readings] (turns on: Whether aḥruf name alternate expressions, subject categories, or types of textual variation.)
  12:41/q39/p1 They are alternative words, dialect forms or readings for one meaning, without changing lawful and forbidden rulings. — reasons: Ibn Shihab states the legal limit in al-Tabari, Ibn Atiyya and al-Zarkashi. Ibn Atiyya calls the synonym account weak and Ibn Shihab's statement possible but ambiguous; al-Zarkashi accepts varied languages and near synonyms.
  12:41/q39/p2 They are subject categories such as commands, prohibitions, promises, stories and parables. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya reports the category account; Qadi Abu Bakr transmits a prophetic report of seven doors and categories.
  12:41/q39/p3 A category account is unsuitable because these topics are not called aḥruf and legal meanings cannot be changed. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya rejects the subject-category proposal on these grounds.
  12:41/q39/p4 They may be classes of variation in vowel, syntax, letters, form, order, addition or omission. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya reports the author of al-Dala'il's scheme, illustrated from 101:5, 56:29, 50:19 and 38:23.
12:41/q40 What wider senses does al-amr bear in the cited Quranic examples? [ٱلْأَمْرُ · meaning]
  12:41/q40/p1 The word can denote a matter or condition, divine rule, revelation, command, resurrection, death, victory, caution, drowning, fertility, abundance, consultation, or sin and its consequence. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi catalogs these uses with examples including 2:109, 16:1, 7:54, 32:5, 3:154, 5:95, 11:97, 57:14, 7:110, 9:50, 11:43, 5:52, 4:58 and 17:16; some dispute whether the last is abundance or a command to obey.
12:41/q41 What can mathal mean, and how does a parable convey meaning? [* · meaning]
  12:41/q41/p1 Mathal can mean likeness, lesson or description; a concise comparison clarifies an obscure point without needing essential resemblance. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba supplies three senses; specialists distinguish replaceable equals from illustrative likeness, Tha'lab calls proverbs transmitted concise wisdom, and Ibn al-Jawzi cites comparisons in 14:24, 22:73 and 62:5.
12:41/q42 What senses of muḥṣanāt and related forms are distinguished? [* · meaning]
  12:41/q42/p1 The forms can describe chaste, protected or married women; Qur'anic muḥṣanāt can mean chaste or free women. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi distinguishes the horse term and cites Tha'lab's vowel distinction for chaste and married women; exegetes cite 4:24, 4:25, 5:5, 21:91, 24:4, 24:23 and 66:12.
12:41/q43 Should recitation keep the transmitted order within a surah? [* · law] (turns on: Which version of the report about Bilal is more reliable and what transmitted order requires.)
  12:41/q43/p1 Readers should preserve the revealed sequence and avoid mixing passages from different surahs. — reasons: Al-Hulaymi calls it etiquette, al-Bayhaqi attributes arrangement through the Prophet to Gabriel, Ibn Sirin favors God's ordering, and Qadi Abu Bakr reports consensus against alternating single verses. Abu Ubayd's Bilal report says to read a surah in order; al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi says mixed themes serve listeners and describes Bilal's preference for mercy passages.
  12:41/q43/p2 Mixing passages is allowed according to the stronger report that all three reciters did well. — reasons: The Prophet is reported to approve Abu Bakr, Umar and Bilal; Abu Ubayd judges this wording more reliable and consonant with scholarly transmission.
  12:41/q43/p3 Mixing passages is disliked. — reasons: Khalid ibn al-Walid apologized for doing so under the pressures of jihad; Abu Ubayd initially cites apparent disapproval of Bilal, Khalid's apology and Ibn Sirin's dislike, while acknowledging a stronger favorable report.
12:41/q44 Why is the agent omitted from the passive quḍiya? [قُضِىَ · rhetoric]
  12:41/q44/p1 Omitting the agent magnifies the one who decreed the matter. — reasons: Al-Zarkashi attributes this rhetorical effect to the passive wording and compares 11:44.
12:41/q45 Does aṣbaḥa add meaning when a state already existed? [* · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the morning reference has a temporal rhetorical purpose.)
  12:41/q45/p1 It is substantive if the state also held the prior evening; otherwise it may be extra. — reasons: Hazim gives this distinction and uses honey becoming sweet as an example of an extra use.
  12:41/q45/p2 Morning can mark when sufferers realize the extent of loss after a troubled night. — reasons: Al-Rummani rejects treating aṣbaḥa as extra in the cited context because sufferers await morning relief.
12:41/q46 Is a final hāʾ al-sakt reliably transmitted for tastaf tiyān? [تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ · readings]
  12:41/q46/p1 The isolated report of hāʾ in this verb is weak against the other transmitters. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari rejects Abu al-Hasan ibn Abi Bakr's lone report. His surrounding discussion limits acceptable hāʾ readings to attested authority, records disputed Ruways and Ya'qub forms, accepts both Ruways forms where sound, and notes Arabic permits hāʾ by usage and analogy.
12:41/q47 What does al-sijn mean in the prison address? [ٱلسِّجْنِ · meaning]
  12:41/q47/p1 With kasra, it denotes the place of confinement. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari says all readers agree on the kasra here, at 12:36 and 12:42, distinguishing the place noun from the verbal noun; he contrasts Ya'qub's fatḥa at 12:33.
12:41/q48 What does aḥad mean when annexed in aḥadukumā? [أَحَدُكُمَا · meaning]
  12:41/q48/p1 It may designate the first or one of the two. — reasons: Al-Suyuti cites the prisoner's dream interpretation as the example of the annexed form.
12:41/q49 Is this clause cited as an expression used proverbially? [قُضِىَ ٱلْأَمْرُ ٱلَّذِى فِيهِ تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ · rhetoric]
  12:41/q49/p1 Yes; it appears in a list of Qur'anic clauses used as proverbs. — reasons: Al-Suyuti reports Ja'far ibn Shams al-Khilafa's classification as irsal al-mathal.
