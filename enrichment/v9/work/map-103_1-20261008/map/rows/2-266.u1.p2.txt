  2:266/q27/p5 The disbeliever or hypocrite who expects benefit from worldly works but arrives with no good and no second chance. — reasons: Muqatil likens the garden to a disbeliever's deeds, stressing that he cannot return to youth to replant or to life to amend. Ibn Abbas contrasts the believer's stored eternal honor with a wealthy disbeliever's lasting punishment for boasting and doubting God's meeting. Al-Dahhak and Ibn Kathir also apply it to the unbeliever; Ibn Atiyya says the broad image covers any hypocrite or unbeliever with apparent works.
  2:266/q27/p6 More generally, it images any loss of needed deeds or blessing at the moment when recovery is impossible. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and Umar also speak simply of deeds; al-Hasan emphasizes maximum need at life's end. Ibn Atiyya says the wording can cover every applicable case, and al-Tabrisi accepts three named applications. Qatada cites 29:43 for understanding parables. Al-Razi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Biqai and Elmalili dwell on the anguish of lost livelihood and reward.
  2:266/q27/p7 It may also image a polity prepared for blessing but ruined by coercive injustice, unbelief, immorality and lost resolve. — reasons: Elmalili says this devastates worldly and afterlife prospects.
2:266/q28 How does this parable connect to its surrounding verses? [* · links] (turns on: How narrowly to tie the image to showmanship and how widely to read its place in the charity passage.)
  2:266/q28/p1 It continues the warning in 2:264 that reproach, harm or hypocrisy can nullify charity. — reasons: Al-Tabari links the bare stone to the burned garden and prefers the immediate charity context; Ibn Zayd, al-Thalabi, al-Baghawi, al-Wahidi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Ibn Atiyya, al-Qurtubi, al-Biqai, Ibn Ashur and al-Tabatabai likewise trace the warning to 2:264. Ibn Ashur contrasts the ruined garden with fruitful sincere spending at 2:261 and 2:265; al-Harali contrasts ruin from below at the root in the first garden with harm from above at the branches in this one.
  2:266/q28/p2 It is another specific parable for ostentatious giving beside the earlier stone image. — reasons: Al-Suddi's formulation is preferred by al-Tabari. Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi report al-Tabari's preference but prefer the broader immediate-context connection; Ibn Atiyya disputes treating it as a second separate showmanship parable.
  2:266/q28/p3 It leads into instruction about the form and recipients of obligatory and voluntary spending. — reasons: Elmalili describes the transition toward where, how and to whom to give.
2:266/q29 What happened in the report of Umar asking about the parable? [* · reports] (turns on: The reports vary in Ibn Abbas's wording and whether Umar or Ibn Abbas states the late-life analogy.)
  2:266/q29/p1 Umar pressed the Companions for an answer, Ibn Abbas interpreted deeds ruined after obedience by Satan-led sins, and Umar approved. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi describe Umar's anger when they deferred instead of saying whether they knew; Ibn Abi Hatim, al-Kashshaf, Ibn Kathir, al-Biqai, al-Durr, al-Alusi and Ibn Ashur transmit the wealthy worshipper explanation. Al-Qurtubi calls it an established Bukhari report, Ibn Kathir says al-Bukhari alone transmits this interpretation, al-Biqai specifies Bukhari's tafsir, and al-Alusi also names al-Hakim and Ibn Jarir. Al-Durr adds that Ibn Abbas first whispered his thought. Baghawi transmits a version through Ubayd ibn Umayr in which Ibn Abbas says show-off and Umar gives the wealthy worshipper analogy.
  2:266/q29/p2 Another telling has Ibn Abbas say a person ends a long good life with evil when deeds are most needed, and Umar accepts or marvels. — reasons: Al-Tabari and Ibn Atiyya transmit this formulation. Ibn Abi Mulayka attributes the late-life summary to Umar in Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi. Al-Durr gives several versions, including an evil final deed and persistent late sin; al-Alusi reports Ata's version.
2:266/q30 What other transmitted reports are brought to bear on this verse? [* · reports] (turns on: These are separate reports used as illustrations, not rival accounts of one event.)
  2:266/q30/p1 The Prophet prayed for his broadest provision in old age near life's end. — reasons: Ibn Kathir attributes the report to al-Hakim; al-Durr transmits it from Aisha.
  2:266/q30/p2 A Prophetic report associates intense heat with Hell's breath and the Fire's complaint. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya cites it to illuminate the wind's heat; al-Qurtubi adds delaying prayer in severe heat.
  2:266/q30/p3 Ikrima says every worldly kind of tree exists in Paradise, even colocynth. — reasons: Ibn Abi Hatim transmits the statement beside the garden's fruits.
  2:266/q30/p4 Ibn Abbas also describes a man who wastes his garden in youth and loses it in old age, when weak children cannot help restore or support him. — reasons: Ibn Kathir preserves this as an alternate Ibn Abbas report.
  2:266/q30/p5 Ali asked forgiveness for devotions whose intention his heart later contradicted. — reasons: Al-Qashani uses the prayer to illustrate later inner corruption of good action.
  2:266/q30/p6 The Prophet advised Muadh to make religion sincere so even little work suffices. — reasons: Bursavi cites the instruction given when Muadh was sent to Yemen.
2:266/q31 What inward process can destroy a deed or spiritual progress? [* · inward] (turns on: Whether the inner ruin is corruption of a deed's intention or reversal of a seeker's ascent.)
  2:266/q31/p1 Ego, self-regard or ostentation turns a good deed's inner motions into Satan's whirlwind, and its fire consumes the deed. — reasons: Al-Qashani includes charity done for God's pleasure before the ego reappears; he cites Ali's prayer about intentions later contradicted by the heart.
  2:266/q31/p2 A seeker rises toward the divine unseen realm, turns back to worldly illusion, and loses the effort. — reasons: Al-Baydawi likens the scattered effort to dust; Bursavi gives the same spiritual parallel.
2:266/q32 How should a giver protect work from the ruin pictured here? [* · interpretation]
  2:266/q32/p1 Keep sincerity at the root of worship and resist ostentation before and during action. — reasons: Bursavi quotes Qushayri's contrast of sincere and hypocritical spending, flourishing crops and ruined bargains. Bursavi says fruits depend on roots, names love of the world and immediate pleasure as chief causes, and counsels examining the heart before worship, removing showy thoughts and resisting them. He distinguishes expecting notice, desiring praise and status, then accepting that desire. Al-Tabatabai adds love of wealth and status, pride, conceit and miserliness, saying reflection exposes their harm.
2:266/q33 How is the wider spending passage organized? [* · links]
  2:266/q33/p1 It forms a connected teaching on the purpose, manner, quality, recipient and reward of giving. — reasons: Al-Tabatabai says the connected themes suggest revelation as one unit: seven-hundredfold or greater growth, sincere fruitfulness, loss through ostentation or harm, giving good wealth, aid to the needy detained in God's path, and great reward. He orders these as giving for God, without reproach or injury, from good property, to the needy, for reward.
