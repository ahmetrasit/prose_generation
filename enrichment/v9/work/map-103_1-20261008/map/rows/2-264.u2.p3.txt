2:264/q28 How does this verse relate to nearby teaching and other Qur'anic images? [* · links]
  2:264/q28/p1 It reverses the fertile seed and garden images of sincere spending in the surrounding passage. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr sets the fruitless rock against the seven-hundred-grain seed at 2:261; Study Quran contrasts ruined charity with giving for God's pleasure and secure faith.
  2:264/q28/p2 It echoes images of unbelieving deeds as ashes, a mirage, or scattered dust. — reasons: Sources cite 14:18, 24:39, and 25:23; Ibn ʿĀshūr distinguishes a concealed rock exposed by rain from merely swept-away soil in 14:18. Al-Zarkashī notices the reversed order of 'anything' and 'what they earned' between this verse and 14:18.
  2:264/q28/p3 The warning shifts from spending in God's path to needy persons, where personal contact makes injury likelier. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr links this to the prior discussion at 2:262.
  2:264/q28/p4 It anticipates the orchard owner's loss and shattered hopes. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī likens abusive donors' loss to the orchard owner's family loss; Ibn ʿĀshūr connects the barren rock to the person turning his hands over spending at 18:42.
  2:264/q28/p5 The parable centers acceptance on sincere intention. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says seeking God's face is necessary for acceptance and cites the widely transmitted report that deeds follow intentions.
2:264/q29 What is reported about the judgment of people who acted for public praise? [رِئَآءَ ٱلنَّاسِ · reports]
  2:264/q29/p1 They are sent to seek reward from the people whose praise they pursued. — reasons: ʿIkrima transmits Ibn ʿAbbās's caller saying God accepts no deed mixed with worldly aims. Maḥmūd ibn Labīd and Bursevī transmit the Prophet's identification of ostentation as lesser shirk and this judgment warning.
  2:264/q29/p2 A praised donor, warrior, and scholar or reciter receive no divine reward for their reputations. — reasons: The Abū Hurayra narrative is introduced through ʿAbd Allāh al-Madanī's report of repeated tears: the donor was called generous, the warrior brave, and the scholar learned as intended. Bursevī's hadith names reciter, martyr, and donor among the first judged and condemned to Hell.
  2:264/q29/p3 Early givers hid their charity to avoid public notice. — reasons: Bursevī reports donors seeking blind recipients, fastening money to a sleeping poor person's clothing, or leaving it to be found.
2:264/q30 What does the passage teach in an inward reading? [* · inward]
  2:264/q30/p1 Seeking any aim besides the Real corrupts devotion, and reminding a poor person reveals misplaced intention. — reasons: Al-Sarrī says adorning oneself with deeds turns good into sin, still worse when claiming their value. Bursevī applies 10:32 to ulterior aims; a giver seeking God feels indebted to the poor as a way to Him. He interprets the report that rich would perish without poor and reverses the upper-hand saying in the recipient's favor; inward harm is turning toward anything other than the Real.
  2:264/q30/p2 The mature servant seeks God himself without demanding wages or public fame. — reasons: An unnamed scholar compares ostentatious worship to a full sack of valueless stones. Rūmī's verses seek pure guidance and say the lover's joy, grief, service, and wage belong to the beloved; Saʿdī urges hidden humility; Ḥāfiẓ urges trust in the master's generosity. Bursevī says divine love cuts rival attachment to wealth, children, and life and closes with prayer for hope in God alone.
2:264/q31 How is the broader spending passage organized? [* · links]
  2:264/q31/p1 It likely forms one revealed unit ordered around purpose, manner, quality, recipients, and reward. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī traces giving for God, freedom from reproach, good wealth, needy people constrained in God's path, and multiplied recompense. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the Qurʾān's eloquent details are intended and calibrated to challengers; its wording can carry conventional, rhetorical, and contextually implicit meanings, including ellipses.
  2:264/q31/p2 The explanation here draws on earlier exegetes whose wording was not always filed under invalidating charity. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya notes the provenance and organization of the linguistic and interpretive material.
2:264/q32 Whom does the opening address as believers include? [يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ · referent] (turns on: Whether the address follows outward profession or actual belief.)
  2:264/q32/p1 It addresses hypocrites by their outward claim to belief. — reasons: Jishumī reports this view of the addressees.
  2:264/q32/p2 It addresses actual believers. — reasons: Jishumī calls this the apparent reading.
2:264/q33 Why is the Day of Judgment called the Last Day? [وَٱلْيَوْمِ ٱلْءَاخِرِ · meaning]
  2:264/q33/p1 It follows life in this world. — reasons: Jishumī explains the name by its succession to worldly life.
2:264/q34 What does ibṭāl mean in the prohibition, and what related senses are distinguished? [لَا تُبْطِلُوا۟ · meaning]
  2:264/q34/p1 It means nullifying a deed or making its reward fruitless. — reasons: Al-Dāmaghānī gives this sense and cites 47:33; Ibn al-Jawzī lists the Qurʾānic sense of bringing works to nothing and the underlying sense of perishing or collapse.
  2:264/q34/p2 The wider word bāṭil can also mean falsehood or injustice. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī reports falsehood and lies in 29:48, 40:78, 41:42, 45:27, and unjust property taking and bribery in 2:188 and 4:29.
2:264/q35 How do jurists distinguish an invalid act from a corrupt one? [لَا تُبْطِلُوا۟ · law]
  2:264/q35/p1 A void act never takes effect; a corrupt act exists with a missing condition and may have legal effects. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī reports the distinction and Ḥanafī examples: corrupt sale can transfer ownership through possession while a child's or insane person's void sale cannot; intercourse after corrupt sale has no fixed penalty and the child is free.
  2:264/q35/p2 Invalidation need not itself be forbidden, though deliberate ruin of worship is. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzī contrasts loss of ritual purity with deliberately ruining prayer, fasting, or pilgrimage.
2:264/q36 What does mathal mean when the rock image is introduced? [فَمَثَلُهُۥ كَمَثَلِ · meaning]
  2:264/q36/p1 It denotes an unusual or noteworthy state, quality, or story set forth descriptively. — reasons: Al-Zarkashī explains mathal this way and names this rock image as a descriptive example.
2:264/q37 Why is 'anything' placed before 'what they earned' here, unlike 14:18? [شَىْءٍۢ مِّمَّا كَسَبُوا۟ · rhetoric]
  2:264/q37/p1 The order suits a donor who gives rather than an earner, while 14:18 foregrounds workers' earnings. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī and both parallel accounts explain the contrast by the local contexts; he also lists other contextual Quranic order reversals.
2:264/q38 What general Arabic grammar is recorded alongside analysis of this verse? [* · grammar]
  2:264/q38/p1 Land names vary in gender and declension, while light native and foreign names behave differently. — reasons: Sībawayh gives light feminine names, foreign names, and regional alternatives for Wāsiṭ, Dābiq, Minā, Hajar, Ḥajar al-Yamāma, ʿUmān, al-Zāb, Irāb, Falaj, Qubāʾ, Ḥirāʾ, and Aḍākh; al-Khalīl discusses derived names and a fixed feminine dialect. Poetry attests several uses.
  2:264/q38/p2 Derived verb patterns can express finding a quality, seeking something, assuming a quality, or reinforcing another form. — reasons: Sībawayh explains istifʿāl for judgment and request, tafaʿʿul for self-assumed quality, and overlapping tafaʿʿala and istafʿala pairs.
  2:264/q38/p3 Nisba and inflection can restore or reshape an underlying short noun stem. — reasons: Sībawayh examines ذيت, ذية, بنت, أخت, ابن, كلتا, فم, and ذو مال; he uses plurals, duals, poetry from al-Farazdaq, and derived nisba forms to trace deleted or substituted radicals.
  2:264/q38/p4 The diminutive of a plural follows its small-number pattern or a diminutive singular plus a plural suffix. — reasons: Sībawayh lists four paucity patterns, al-Khalīl's treatment of dūr, and alternatives for forms such as fityān, fuqarāʾ, marābid, and mafātīḥ.
