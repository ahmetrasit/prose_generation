## THE CURRENT MAP
103:1/q01 What does al-ʿaṣr in the oath designate? [ٱلْعَصْرِ · referent] (turns on: Whether the unqualified word is inclusive or specifies a time of day, a prayer, a paired period, or a marked era; arguments use Arabic usage, poetry, report, context and analogous oaths.)
  103:1/q01/p1 Time or an age broadly, encompassing its changing periods and events. — reasons: Al-Tabari declines restriction without textual proof; al-Nahhas and Elmalılı likewise allow several Arabic senses. Ibn Abbas and others identify an age; Imru al-Qays and Ibn al-Sikkit's poetry attest the usage. Farāhī and Asad distinguish fleeting ʿaṣr from dahr; Cuypers calls it a general span without further grounds. Ibn Kathir calls this the well-known view. Bint al-Shati follows al-Tabari, Ibn Qayyim and most exegetes; Kur'an Yolu finds this best suited to the sūra. Al-Razi questions treating a reported expanded wording as Qur'an because that would invalidate prayer. Elmalılı cites a poet likening unseen time's motion toward death to a ship. Al-Damghani glosses al-ʿaṣr as time or its passage; al-Suyuti includes time as a whole among the oath's possible referents.
  103:1/q01/p2 Late afternoon, the day's declining period from after noon toward sunset. — reasons: Al-Hasan and Qatada give the afternoon interval; al-Mawardi cites poetry, while Ibn Ashur defines it by shadow length and al-Harith ibn Hilliza's verse. The declining sun and approaching night signify divine power (Tabrisi, Nasafi, Qatada), and the closing market images judgment (Razi from Hasan). Robinson connects merchants' reckoning of earnings with verse 2's loss. Corpus Coranicum favors a specific daytime by analogy with other opening oaths, though observes late afternoon is unusual among early Meccan oaths. Ubayy's Prophetic report says God swears by day's end. Durr also reports Ibn Abbas and Qatada saying an hour of daytime; Tustari records 'afternoon or time itself'. Al-Suyuti also gives the day's latter part as the oath's referent.
  103:1/q01/p3 The afternoon prayer, the middle prayer distinguished by special merit. — reasons: Muqatil calls it the middle and best prayer. Hafsa's 2:238 recension and a reported reading of 5:106 are cited by Zamakhshari, Razi and others; Qurtubi cites an authentic identifying report. A Prophetic report likens missing it to losing family and property. Its hour conflicts with trade and livelihood, and Razi says it closes daily worship as repentance closes deeds. Razi also cites a report about a false oath after Asr and explains that God swears by His prescribed noble command. Alusi rejects Razi's sinful-woman story as unreliable. Neuwirth links prayer-time oaths to liturgical experience, though Corpus Coranicum disputes the proposed hymn tone.
  103:1/q01/p4 The Prophet's era, honored by renewed prophethood and his community. — reasons: Mawardi and Qurtubi cite renewed prophethood. Razi and Elmalılı invoke the workers' parable: the final workers labor from Asr to sunset and receive double pay; compare oaths by the Prophet's place and life (90:2; 15:72). Elmalılı describes the mission, Hijra, victory, and extension of Muhammad's community to the end of time; the afternoon of human history follows earlier ages. Tabataba'i favors this because the next verses treat truth and perseverance. Bursevi cites a report of a day or half-day for the community and Najmiyya reports of the Prophet's primordial status.
  103:1/q01/p5 Night and day together, called the two ʿaṣrs. — reasons: Ibn Kaysan and others give the pair; Humayd ibn Thawr's poem uses the dual for day and night, as cited by Tha'labi, Wahidi, Qurtubi and others.
  103:1/q01/p6 The final hour of daytime, near sunset. — reasons: Qatada specifies the last hour, while Abu Muslim calls it one end of the day; this narrower reading is distinguished from the whole interval after noon.
  103:1/q01/p7 Morning and evening together, called the two ʿaṣrs. — reasons: Ibn al-Sikkit's and other poetic witnesses use the dual for morning and evening; Ibn Atiyya calls them the two cooler parts of day.
  103:1/q01/p8 The Lord of Time, implied as the object of the oath. — reasons: Baghawi cites comparable oaths and Qurtubi supplies the omitted 'Lord of the age'.
  103:1/q01/p9 The time of Adam's creation on Friday afternoon. — reasons: Biqai invokes a sound report in Muslim; Alusi reports this from Qatada. Elmalılı ties late creation to the world's closing day.
  103:1/q01/p10 Each person's lifetime, the finite capital in which one acts. — reasons: Biqai includes each individual life. Elmalılı's poems picture each passing day or breath consuming it; Islahi and Farāhī call it an investment whose melting-ice speed makes present duties urgent.
  103:1/q01/p11 The era of the Mahdi, when truth prevails fully. — reasons: Tabataba'i reports this from unnamed narrations.
  103:1/q01/p12 Time's vicissitudes or calamities. — reasons: Ali's interpretive recitation adds the vicissitudes of time and loss until its end; Jishumi reports this gloss but prefers time itself.
103:1/q02 What meanings and derivations does the word ʿaṣr have in Arabic? [ٱلْعَصْرِ · meaning] (turns on: The sources distinguish its concrete pressing root, wide Arabic uses, bounded temporal force and the definite article's scope.)
  103:1/q02/p1 Its root denotes squeezing or pressing, extended to time, confinement, and the extraction of an essence. — reasons: Jishumi describes wringing cloth, grape extract, deferred prayer, rain-bearing clouds and a rising dust column. Tabarsi applies the pressed-events image to time. Bint al-Shati adds press equipment, lineage and the hardship that presses out a person's essence; she cites 12:36, 12:49, 78:14 and 2:266. Biqai connects the sūra name to extracting the saved essence. Bint al-Shati reads human life as pressed and refined by trials, exposing its essence; she cites the root's occurrences in Yusuf's dream and harvest year and the whirlwind in al-Baqara, calls this Qur'anic form unique, and rejects al-Raghib's derivation of temporal ʿaṣr from what remains after squeezing. Her criticism of proposed links in other oaths cautions against assuming a simple analogy for this image.
  103:1/q02/p2 The noun also spans an age, day or night, a part of day, prayer, group and rain. — reasons: Elmalılı adds Turkish senses of the current era and eighty or a hundred years and notes vocalizations ʿaṣr, ʿiṣr and ʿuṣr. Wahidi says these temporal senses are valid Arabic usage.
  103:1/q02/p3 Unlike dahr's vast or unlimited duration, ʿaṣr marks a bounded, passing period. — reasons: Asad describes dahr as unbounded; Elmalılı cites Raghib's range from the universe's beginning to end, and Farāhī stresses swift passage, supported by Imru al-Qays and ʿUbayd ibn al-Abras. Bint al-Shati reports Razi's suggestion that 'asr avoids unbelievers' exalted dahr.
  103:1/q02/p4 The word's definite article can be generic or point to a particular known time. — reasons: Elmalılı says the generic article can gather all senses while the covenant article directs the first hearers to their own era; Ibn Ashur allows contextual indication, addition, or shared reference.
103:1/q03 How does the opening oath work, and what does it emphasize? [وَٱلْعَصْرِ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether wāw is explained as a conventional emphatic oath or as time's witness to the proposition.)
  103:1/q03/p1 The wāw introduces an oath whose answer affirms that humanity is in loss. — reasons: Jishumi identifies the oath particle and its answer; Elmalılı compares 93:1. Ibn Ashur says an oath confirms the assertion and its object displays God's creation, power and knowledge. Bint al-Shati says wāw magnifies what is sworn by and moves from perceptible reality to moral truth. Corpus Coranicum notes a possible pre-Islamic seer formula, a connection not yet studied adequately. Al-Zarkashi and al-Suyuti count oath openings as the fifth of the sūra-opening modes and list fifteen such sūras; al-Suyuti further classifies al-ʿAṣr as an oath followed by a proposition about human conduct or condition, namely humanity's loss.
  103:1/q03/p2 Time itself bears witness to humanity's loss. — reasons: Islahi gives wāw this witness force: history shows recompense, while the rapidly spent lifespan makes a person's deeds profitable or ruinous.
  103:1/q03/p3 A thematic link between an oath's object and its answer must be argued in each case, not assumed from other oaths. — reasons: Bint al-Shati questions proposed links between night and day and differing human striving, and between the falling star and the Prophet's freedom from error; these analogies cannot by themselves establish the link in al-ʿAṣr.
103:1/q04 Why is time or afternoon singled out for the oath? [وَٱلْعَصْرِ · interpretation] (turns on: The explanations appeal to divine signs, the day's closure, the prayer's status, the Prophet's era, historical recompense, or the value and moral use of time.)
  103:1/q04/p1 Time displays divine power and wondrous changes, so its oath invites reflection. — reasons: Alternating nights and days, shifting light and temperature, health and illness, wealth and poverty, and other events disclose God's power (Razi, Ibn Qayyim, Tabarsi). Razi cites 25:62 and 6:13. Bursevi relates the oath to dawn, sun, moon, night, day and forenoon oaths. Ibn Ashur adds the solar-earth cycle and daily social life. Bint al-Shati cautions against loading the verse with speculative philosophy and cites 28:71–72.
  103:1/q04/p2 Time is precious life capital; wasting it causes loss, while a final good act can gain lasting reward. — reasons: Mawardi cites the day's closing acts. Razi says a last moment of repentance can redeem a wasted life (25:62); al-Hasan compares closing markets to Judgment (21:1).
