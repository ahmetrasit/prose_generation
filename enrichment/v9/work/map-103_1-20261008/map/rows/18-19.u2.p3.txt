18:19/q30 What does 'let him not alert anyone to you' mean? [وَلَا يُشْعِرَنَّ بِكُمْ أَحَدًا · meaning] (turns on: The warning's scope differs when the envoy is captured.)
  18:19/q30/p1 He must tell no one about the group or its location and avoid conduct that reveals them. — reasons: Abu Ubayda and Ibn Qutayba derive shaʿara as knowing and connect 'poet' to perception; Ibn Ashur explains causative ishʿār and an omitted object that covers their existence, number and location; the emphatic nūn signals the danger. Al-Kashshaf, al-Nasafi, Bursevi and al-Biqai include unintended disclosure. Al-Damghānī's heading lists a king's cupbearer as another possible referent of aḥad, but the excerpt supplies no example for that identification.
  18:19/q30/p2 If he is caught, he must not expose his companions. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj, al-Tha'labi and Abu Ishaq construe the warning as protection of the others after the envoy is discovered. Al-Wahidi prefers that he not disclose them voluntarily, but reports a contrary oath if captured.
  18:19/q30/p3 If captured, he should guide captors to the other youths so they share martyrdom. — reasons: Al-Suddi and others report their pledge not to keep martyrdom to one person; al-Wahidi finds this more fitting than Abu Ishaq's reading while preferring voluntary secrecy.
  18:19/q30/p4 Here 'anyone' specifically denotes Diqyanus, the persecuting ruler. — reasons: Al-Damghānī identifies aḥad in the concealment warning with Diqyanus.
18:19/q31 How is 'let no one know' read grammatically? [وَلَا يُشْعِرَنَّ بِكُمْ أَحَدًا · readings] (turns on: The variant changes the verb's subject and case of aḥad.)
  18:19/q31/p1 Abu Salih, Yazid ibn al-Qa'qa', Qutayba and Abu Ja'far read an active form with aḥad nominative as subject. — reasons: Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and Alusi report this reading.
  18:19/q31/p2 The regular construction forbids the envoy to make anyone aware of them. — reasons: Ibn Ashur calls ishʿār a causative verb taking one object and notes the emphatic nūn; al-Kashshaf says the envoy can cause awareness even unintentionally. Abū Ḥātim's lexical comparison says aḥad is more exhaustive than wāḥid, normally human-specific in absence statements, usable for one or many and invariant for gender; he contrasts their affirmative uses, plurals and arithmetic behavior, citing 112:1, 33:32 and 69:47.
18:19/q32 How did the cave open and the sleepers awaken in the narrative reports? [بَعَثْنَٰهُمْ · reports] (turns on: Reports differ over how the sealed entrance was opened and include a separate later revival story.)
  18:19/q32/p1 A shepherd opened the cave as shelter for sheep; the youths awoke the following morning. — reasons: Al-Tabari attributes this to Wahb; Study Quran transmits it from al-Tabari; Ibn Atiyya records a shepherd as one account.
  18:19/q32/p2 The entrance had been sealed, collapsed, or later unsealed. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi says Decianus sealed it and Tamlikha noticed the stones removed; Ibn Atiyya says it may have collapsed, alternatively been opened by a shepherd.
  18:19/q32/p3 They awoke after the persecuting king and city had disappeared. — reasons: Al-Sadiq's account says the dog slept with them; al-Mawardi also reports Muqatil's statement that the dog slept.
  18:19/q32/p4 Olyas removed stones to make a sheep pen; the revived youths greeted each other and prayed. — reasons: Al-Tabari says terror kept others at a distance while they revived unchanged; the account gives a named agent of the opening.
  18:19/q32/p5 God restored their souls for a later greeting to Muhammad's companions, then they returned to sleep. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi reports the four Companions brought by Solomon's wind; a variant says the Mahdi will later greet and revive them.
18:19/q33 What did the youths say and fear immediately after awakening? [لِيَتَسَآءَلُوا۟ بَيْنَهُمْ · reports] (turns on: Narratives differ in post-awakening details and the state in which the youths were later found.)
  18:19/q33/p1 They simply estimated their sleep and made a food errand under a later just or Muslim king. — reasons: Abd Allah ibn Ubayd ibn Umayr and Ibn Abbas reports describe a just or Muslim ruler already reigning.
  18:19/q33/p2 They prayed and thought Decianus still sought them; some urged steadfastness even unto death. — reasons: Al-Tabari's account says Yemliha warned of forced sacrifice or death and Maksalimina vowed fidelity; al-Tha'labi reports the same with Tamlikha. Their pledge held them together in life and death.
  18:19/q33/p3 One report has the sleepers' souls gone when the king found their bodies; another says bones had vanished. — reasons: Qatada reports bodies without souls; Ibn Abbas says their bones had vanished after more than 300 years. Study Quran reports perfectly preserved bodies after they fell asleep again.
18:19/q34 What happened when the envoy reached the changed city? [إِلَى ٱلْمَدِينَةِ · reports] (turns on: Reports agree on a changed era and an old coin, but differ in officials, discovery details and the cave's outcome.)
  18:19/q34/p1 His ancient coin led sellers to suspect hidden treasure and take him before the authorities. — reasons: Al-Tabari names governors Arius/Areus and Astius; al-Tha'labi calls the first Armos. They bound or threatened him; no one knew him or his father. He asked about Decianus, learned he had died centuries earlier, and revealed companions in the cave. Ibn Abbas's report adds a baker and a scholar matching names to an old tablet.
  18:19/q34/p2 A righteous king or governors recognized the youths as a divine sign and went to the cave. — reasons: Wahb and Ikrima name a just or Muslim king; al-Tabari's narrative has Areus, Astius and the people accompany Yemliha; al-Tha'labi has Armos; reports differ whether terror prevented entry, whether they found preserved bodies, and whether a mosque or church was built (18:21).
  18:19/q34/p3 He saw public Christian signs and heard Jesus invoked, unlike the persecution he remembered. — reasons: Al-Tabari and al-Tha'labi describe his bewilderment and fear of madness; Ibn Atiyya says landmarks and clothing had changed. Al-Sadiq adds mutually unintelligible speech.
  18:19/q34/p4 The verse leaves the exact route of discovery unspecified. — reasons: Islahi says investigation eventually exposed their origins, perhaps through the market; Tabatabai says the old coin is a narrative key.
18:19/q35 Why did God awaken the youths in that era according to the reports? [بَعَثْنَٰهُمْ · reports] (turns on: The reports supply a specific royal crisis behind the broader resurrection lesson.)
  18:19/q35/p1 Their awakening was a sign of resurrection and God's protection, whether or not a king had prayed. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi likens it to resurrection; Kashshaf, Baydawi, Biqai, Ibn Ashur, Study Quran and Kuranyolu emphasize divine power. Tabatabai says persecution had passed and monotheists could receive the sign.
  18:19/q35/p2 Their appearance answered a dispute over resurrection of body and soul. — reasons: Ikrima says the community was divided; Ibn Ishaq names Theodosius, whose 68-year reign saw deniers of bodily resurrection gain ground. The king donned sackcloth, sat in ashes and prayed. God made the youths a sign confirming the Hour and bodily resurrection, preserving the king and believers. Al-Kashifi's version names Tindrous.
18:19/q36 What happened in the report of Muhammad's message to the sleepers? [بَعَثْنَٰهُمْ · reports] (turns on: The later visitation reports differ in visitor and timing.)
  18:19/q36/p1 Four eminent companions carried Muhammad's greeting; the revived youths accepted his prophethood and returned to sleep. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi reports Gabriel instructing Muhammad to send Abu Bakr, Umar, Ali and Abu Dharr on a cloth carried by Solomon's gentle wind. The dog greeted them; God restored the youths' souls; they answered, praised God, accepted Islam and sent greetings. A separate variant gives the Mahdi a later greeting. The Prophet prayed for loved ones and companions.
  18:19/q36/p2 The Mahdi will greet them, after which they will sleep until resurrection. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi presents this as a variant to the four-companion narrative.
18:19/q37 What legal rule about sending an agent is drawn from the errand? [فَٱبْعَثُوٓا۟ أَحَدَكُم · law] (turns on: Jurists agree on delegation for need but differ over an unexcused, present principal.)
  18:19/q37/p1 Agency is valid for delegable, lawful matters, including buying food. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya cites Ali appointing Aqil before Uthman; al-Qurtubi adds 9:60, 12:93, Urwa al-Bariqi, Jabir collecting fifteen wasqs at Khaybar, and Bukhari's Abd al-Rahman–Umayya protection contract. Ibn al-Arabi calls this passage the strongest proof. Al-Qurtubi bars agency for usurpation or unlawful acts. Ibn ʿĀshūr explicitly uses the youths' appointment of an envoy as an example of legal inference from Quranic narrative; he compares deriving guarantee or reward contracts from Joseph's story at 12:72.
