103:2/q09 Who escapes loss, and to what extent? [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَفِى خُسْرٍ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether exemption requires all four traits and whether a believer's sinful or incomplete action still entails a degree of loss.)
  103:2/q09/p1 The complete exception requires faith, righteous deeds, and mutual counsel in truth and patience. — reasons: Muhammad ibn Kaʿb says belief alone is insufficient and lists the successive conditions. Muqatil says those who do these things remain in the Gardens; Elmalili calls the four complete faith; Qutb calls them the single successful path in every age and the basis of a community. Al-Shafiʿi and others regard the surah as sufficient guidance. Tabari and Hasan compare another connected exception with this one.
  103:2/q09/p2 Believers who do righteous deeds are exempted from the loss. — reasons: Mujahid identifies the excepted believers; Muqatil and Thalabi also name belief and deeds. Razi says believers gain enduring happiness with their self and time. Ibn Ashur says good believers avoid loss when they do no opposite deed or repent; al-salihat includes commanded good and avoidance of sin. Islahi says deeds show faith has taken root, cites 4:136, and explains their outward, inward, worldly, religious, individual and collective scope through Farahi.
  103:2/q09/p3 Sinful believers incur a lesser, possibly temporary loss, though faith ultimately saves them. — reasons: Biqai distinguishes denial from losses proportionate to disobedience. Elmalili rejects the Murji'a claim that faith without action entails no loss; sinful believers whose evil outweighs good may experience Hell until cleansed. Ibn Ashur distinguishes eternal loss through denial from graded lesser losses and cites 11:114 on forgiveness of minor sins. Samarrai says mutual counsel prevents loss of further reward.
  103:2/q09/p4 Faith without action incurs no loss. — reasons: Elmalili attributes this to the Murji'a and explicitly rejects it in favor of graded loss for disobedient believers.
103:2/q10 How is the oath by time related to the human loss that follows? [إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَفِى خُسْرٍ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the oath evokes life's finite capital, the late hour and reckoning, or time's warning events.)
  103:2/q10/p1 Time is the valuable, vanishing opportunity in which human gain or loss is determined. — reasons: Elmalili says time contains changes, wonders, blessing, harm and the chance to act; its passing consumes life unless used for faith and reform. He says the oath honors time rather than blaming it, and its senses of restraint and bestowal evoke both loss and gain. Kuranyolu similarly says God swears by precious time; Islahi relates it to responsibility for life's blessing.
  103:2/q10/p2 Late afternoon evokes the closing human era or the end of life and the reckoning of what was earned. — reasons: Elmalili evokes creation late in the world's day, the era of Muhammad and the sorrow of returning without gain. Study Quran reads humanity in a final historical phase. Robinson relates the afternoon to merchants counting daily takings; Corpus Coranicum supports this through Q 104's contrast of wealth and damnation.
  103:2/q10/p3 Time's calamities and transformations warn that human loss continues to its end. — reasons: Elmalili explains Ali's interpretive dehr recitation as recurrent changes and calamities, while another view sees time's wonders as signs of the Creator's power.
103:2/q11 What is the verse's relation to nearby sūras and other passages? [* · links] (turns on: Which nearby warning or parallel supplies the strongest context for the verse.)
  103:2/q11/p1 It follows al-Takāthur's warning that worldly rivalry wastes life and obscures future accountability. — reasons: Abu Hayyan links 102:1,3 to distinction between believers and unbelievers. Ibn al-Zubayr, as quoted by Biqai, says accumulation narrows sight to the present; Biqai also connects human ignorance, ingratitude and imagined immortality across 100:6, 103:2 and 104:3. Elmalili, Islahi and Study Quran likewise link 102:1–2,8 with time's squandered blessing.
  103:2/q11/p2 Al-Tīn's human decline and exception parallel this verse, with bodily and spiritual emphases. — reasons: Ibrahim's aging account cites 95:4–6; Razi resolves an apparent reversal by assigning bodily states to al-Tin and the soul to al-Asr. Study Quran pairs 103:2–3 with 95:5–6.
  103:2/q11/p3 Sūrah 104 likewise contrasts accumulated worldly wealth with final damnation. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum uses 104:1–9 to strengthen Robinson's link between reckoned earnings and eschatological loss; Biqai cites 104:3 alongside 103:2.
  103:2/q11/p4 The same pattern of a generic human term followed by an exception appears elsewhere. — reasons: Tabari reports Hasan's comparison with an exception after Hell; Majaz cites 70:19–22 and 69:47; Qurtubi cites 70:19,22 in discussion of a generic life.
  103:2/q11/p5 Other scriptures and Qur'anic passages also frame loss eschatologically. — reasons: Torrey compares Luke 9:24 and Syriac Peshitta ḥsar, and collects Qur'anic khusr usage; Corpus Coranicum notes Philippians 3:7–8 and the early Meccan 79:12. The commentator rejects a proposed hymn-like resonance and refers to criticism of Neuwirth's hypothesis, citing 93:1–2.
103:2/q12 What does the sūra as a whole teach through this declaration of loss and its exception? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the focus is the sūra's comprehensive program or the function of its opening contrast.)
  103:2/q12/p1 It concisely teaches the path of individual and communal salvation. — reasons: Shafiʿi is repeatedly reported to say reflection on this sūra would suffice; another report says it contains all Qur'anic sciences. Biqai maps its clauses onto knowledge of God and afterlife, worship, obstacles and patience, invoking Ghazali's six Qur'anic themes and a basmala contrast of transient creation and God's enduring mercy. Islahi calls it a condensed guide to personal and collective life; Kuranyolu and Qutb see the full duties and community identity in its brevity.
  103:2/q12/p2 Its opening separates the doomed from the saved before identifying saving conduct. — reasons: Biqai says prevention of harm precedes benefit and likens the saved human essence to what is extracted by pressing, linking the name al-ʿAsr with humanity's nobility and 95's best proportion. Qutb emphasizes one successful route across the ages.
103:2/q13 How is this sūra situated in revelation? [* · sciences] (turns on: The differing reported settings, with most named authorities favoring Mecca.)
  103:2/q13/p1 It is Meccan, probably after Sūrah 94. — reasons: Abu Hayyan reports Ibn Abbas, Ibn al-Zubayr and the majority as Meccan; Ibn Kathir and Qutb affirm it. Tabatabai favors Meccan despite allowing either; Asad and Study Quran place it shortly after al-Sharh.
  103:2/q13/p2 It is Medinan. — reasons: Abu Hayyan attributes this view to Mujahid, Qatada and Muqatil; Tabatabai allows the possibility but favors Meccan.
103:2/q14 What reports describe the sūra's recitation or reception? [* · reports] (turns on: Different reports concern distinct acts of recitation and reception.)
  103:2/q14/p1 Two Companions recited it to one another before parting and then exchanged greetings. — reasons: Reported by Tabarani through Abu Madina in Ibn Kathir and repeated by Elmalili, Qutb and Study Quran; Qutb takes the practice as renewing commitment to its guidance.
  103:2/q14/p2 The Prophet interpreted the sūra's phrases by naming Abu Jahl and four Companions. — reasons: Thalabi transmits Ubayy's recitation to the Prophet and question for interpretation; its reported reply identifies loss with Abu Jahl and belief, deeds, truth and patience with Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali. A sermon attributed to Ali ibn Abd Allah ibn Abbas repeats the assignments.
  103:2/q14/p3 Musaylima answered a recitation of al-ʿAsr with an unconvincing parody. — reasons: Ibn Kathir reports Amr ibn al-As reciting it before conversion; Musaylima's response invoked al-wabr, a catlike small animal with notable ears. Ibn Kathir says even an idolater found the parody incoherent and notes al-Khara'iti's version.
