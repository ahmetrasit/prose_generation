18:10/q19 Does their prayer seek worldly good, afterlife good, or both? [رَحْمَةًۭ رَشَدًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: The scope of mercy and the relation between the prayer's two clauses.)
  18:10/q19/p1 It chiefly concerns their worldly predicament. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says they trusted their afterlife outcome, though mercy might include it; Abu Hayyan reports his view and Alusi reports it while rejecting the restriction. Tabatabai identifies the peril after flight from coercion and death.
  18:10/q19/p2 It asks for good in both worlds. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya allows afterlife mercy; Alusi rejects restricting it to worldly affairs; Ibn Ashur calls it comprehensive good in both worlds, and Biqai asks deliverance and guidance in both.
  18:10/q19/p3 Mercy and guidance may name one gift or two distinct requests. — reasons: Tabatabai says if mercy means divine support, the guidance clause explains it; if mercy means a specific divine gift, the clause adds another request.
18:10/q20 What does “prepare” mean? [وَهَيِّئْ · meaning]
  18:10/q20/p1 Set right, arrange or make easy the means to the desired result. — reasons: Wahidi glosses it as set right or prepare; Baghawi says make easy, Ibn Jawzi set right; Baydawi and Tabatabai define the root as bringing a thing into a formed state; Ibn Ashur says furnishing the means to an outcome. Muqatil uses facilitation.
18:10/q21 How is “prepare” read and explained phonologically? [وَهَيِّئْ · readings] (turns on: Whether hamza is retained, replaced with yāʾ, or removed by a further weak-letter process.)
  18:10/q21/p1 The common reading retains hamza after the doubled yāʾ. — reasons: Samin states this is the majority form; the alternative replaces or deletes the hamza.
  18:10/q21/p2 Abu Jaʿfar, Shayba and al-Zuhri read two yāʾs without hamza. — reasons: Abu Hayyan, Samin and Alusi say a quiescent hamza is replaced with yāʾ. Qiraat-ER argues the replacement could change the root from h-y-ʾ to h-w-y and shade the sense toward making desirable or inclining.
  18:10/q21/p3 A reading transmitted from ʿAsim lacks hamza by replacement or deletion. — reasons: Abu Hayyan cites Ibn Khalawayh's al-Aʿsha from Abu Bakr from ʿAsim: replacement is regular, while deletion is disputed but allowed in imperatives and jussives. Samin says the replacement may be treated as an original weak letter and then deleted, citing a poem; Alusi also gives both routes.
18:10/q22 How is the hamza of “prepare” written? [وَهَيِّئْ · grammar]
  18:10/q22/p1 Here it is seated on alif, though common spelling normally follows the preceding vowel. — reasons: Farra says Arabs sometimes use alif in every position and reports the ʿAbd Allah codex spelling شيأ and يستهزأون with alif. The more common book pattern uses vowel-conditioned seats, though the alif pattern reflects the hamza's origin. Al-Ghazi ibn Qays reports alif for the hamza of hayyiʾ and yuhayyiʾ in some codices to avoid excessive deletion after a doubled yāʾ. Al-Sakhawi says al-Dani lacked knowledge of the Syrian codex, and Ibn al-Jazari says he saw those forms there.
  18:10/q22/p2 The hamza is written according to the preceding vowel, with two yāʾ letters in hayyiʾ. — reasons: Al-Dani rejects alif as contrary to consensus; al-Shatibi and others specify two yāʾ letters. Suyuti states the vowel-conditioned hamza-seat rule and cites wa-hayyiʾ as its example.
18:10/q23 Why are the prepositional phrases placed before the requested object? [لَنَا مِنْ أَمْرِنَا · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether fronting chiefly highlights the beneficiaries and affair or recasts the whole affair as rightness.)
  18:10/q23/p1 Their fronting emphasizes the desired favor and brings it into view early. — reasons: Muqatil notes fronting; Alusi says it focuses the petition's object from the outset. Bursevi says both phrases attach to 'prepare.'
  18:10/q23/p2 The construction rhetorically extracts guidance from their affair, meaning make the whole affair right. — reasons: Alusi gives this as another rhetorical analysis; Zamakhshari's comparison to calling someone a lion is reported by Abu Hayyan.
18:10/q24 What is “our affair” in the prayer? [أَمْرِنَا · referent] (turns on: Whether the noun points narrowly to their migration or broadly to every aspect of their situation.)
  18:10/q24/p1 It is their flight from unbelievers and their effort to remain faithful. — reasons: Kashshaf, Baydawi, Nasafi, Bursevi and Alusi identify separation from unbelievers; Bursevi adds perseverance in obedience. Ibn Ashur includes faith and refuge, for which God prepared concealment, bodily preservation, long sleep and their later public sign. Tabatabai describes their peril after persecutors killed or coerced believers.
  18:10/q24/p2 It is their entire situation or all their affairs. — reasons: Kashshaf, Baydawi and Nasafi allow the whole affair to be ordered; Razi and Abu Hayyan report making the affair itself guided, with a simile to calling someone a lion.
18:10/q25 What is the right course they ask God to prepare? [رَشَدًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether right guidance refers chiefly to religious conduct, safe escape, sound judgment, facilitation, or the character of the whole affair.)
  18:10/q25/p1 Guidance to conduct pleasing to God, nearness to Him and steadfast faith. — reasons: Tabari links ease in what pleases God, escape from idolatry and sound action, citing 4:6; Zajjaj, Wahidi's Abu Ishaq, Ibn Jawzi, Baghawi, Thalabi, Tabrisi and Nasafi speak of nearness and pleasure. Wahidi's Ibn Abbas asks deeds directed to God's love. Abu Hayyan says steadiness in monotheism; Islahi calls this wise in peril.
  18:10/q25/p2 A safe escape and deliverance from their immediate peril. — reasons: Ibn Abbas, reported by Thalabi, Baghawi, Tabrisi, Qurtubi and Study Quran, says a safe exit from the cave. Nahhas says guidance by which they are saved; Ibn Atiyya calls it a good way out; Tabatabai a path to safety, while Ibn Kathir asks a sound outcome and Biqai deliverance. Damghani glosses rushd here as a way out of the youths' predicament.
  18:10/q25/p3 Correctness, sound judgment and the opposite of error. — reasons: Thalabi and Qurtubi preserve 'what is correct'; Wahidi says the root opposes error; Raghib in Tabatabai calls it guidance to the desired end; Asad says consciousness of right; Islahi says guided steadfastness; Ibn Ashur adds benefit and soundness, while Study Quran compares Moses' request for sound judgment.
  18:10/q25/p4 That God make their whole affair itself rightly guided. — reasons: Razi, Nasafi and Zamakhshari in Abu Hayyan offer this strong predication; Alusi calls it rhetorical extraction. Wahidi's 'people of meanings' posit an omitted 'thing of guidance,' then a further ellipsis: prepare what lets us attain guidance.
  18:10/q25/p5 Facilitation or divine enabling. — reasons: Muqatil glosses rushd as facilitation; Sahl in Sulami asks for the easiest route of divine enabling, and Baghawi says make easy.
18:10/q26 How is “right guidance” read here? [رَشَدًۭا · readings] (turns on: Both forms convey guidance, but the common form matches the surrounding cadence and has a different usage profile.)
  18:10/q26/p1 The common reading is rashadan, with fatḥa on both consonants. — reasons: Zajjaj, Nahhas, Ibn Atiyya, Samin and Alusi say it matches the verse endings; Abu Hayyan reports Ibn Atiyya's preference. Ibn Ashur says it is the only famous reading here and at 18:24, contrasting 2:256 and 4:6; at 18:66 the cadence favors ḍamma, though Abu Amr and Yaqub have fatḥa. He says fatḥa conveys good, benefit and soundness. Ibn al-Jazari reports unanimous opening of rāʾ and shīn in 18:10 and 18:24 and favors the cadence with nearby endings ʿajaban, ʿadadan and aḥadan (18:9, 18:12, 18:49). Suyuti says choosing between permissible vocal forms can serve the sura's cadence and rejects al-Farisi's preference for the other form.
  18:10/q26/p2 Abu Raja reads rushdan, with ḍamma on rāʾ and still shīn. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, Samin and Alusi report it; Alusi says its meaning is the same. Ibn Ashur says this form commonly concerns sound management of property, and contrasts its occurrence at 2:256, 4:6 and 18:66.
18:10/q27 What does their prayer teach about dependence on God? [رَبَّنَآ ءَاتِنَا وَهَيِّئْ · interpretation] (turns on: The notes draw complementary lessons from the appeal rather than dispute its wording.)
  18:10/q27/p1 It models helpless, sincere recourse to God and a request for a good outcome. — reasons: Qushayri says they disavow their own power and receive divine care; Tabatabai sees exhausted means; Ibn Kathir cites a transmitted prayer for every decree to end well, and a report from Busr says the Prophet sought good ends and protection from worldly disgrace and afterlife punishment. Study Quran compares the same hadith; Qurtubi says the Prophet prayed when troubled. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan recommend this verse as sufficient for believers' worldly needs; Islahi calls their request wise.
  18:10/q27/p2 Their prayer expresses God's lordship and refusal to invoke another deity. — reasons: Thalabi connects it with their declaration that God alone is Lord of heaven and earth and that another invocation would be an outrageous falsehood.
