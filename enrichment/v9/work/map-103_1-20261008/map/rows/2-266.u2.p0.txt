## THE CURRENT MAP
2:266/q01 What does the opening question convey? [أَيَوَدُّ أَحَدُكُمْ · rhetoric]
  2:266/q01/p1 It repudiates the prospect: no one would wish such a loss. — reasons: Al-Razi says the wish to avoid calamity is especially intense; Abu Hayyan says ahadukum distributes the question to each person; Bursavi distinguishes denying a wished prospect from denying an existing act, with two Arabic examples; Ibn Ashur compares the repudiating question at 49:12. Al-Biqai stresses the initially attractive orchard.
2:266/q02 What does the verb ayawaddu mean here? [أَيَوَدُّ · meaning]
  2:266/q02/p1 It means would love or wish. — reasons: Al-Razi defines wudd as complete love; al-Tabatabai adds an element of wishing. Jishumi relates wudud, love and will, distinguishing future-directed love from mawadda, which can concern past or future.
2:266/q03 How is the opening verb read? [أَيَوَدُّ · readings] (turns on: Which verb expresses the wish.)
  2:266/q03/p1 The transmitted wording is ayawaddu, would wish. — reasons: Al-Alusi contrasts a report of Umar's alternate wording.
  2:266/q03/p2 Umar is reported to have read yuhibbu, would love. — reasons: Al-Alusi reports the reading.
2:266/q04 What is the janna in the scene? [جَنَّةٌ · meaning] (turns on: Whether janna itself names the vegetation or the land containing it.)
  2:266/q04/p1 A densely planted orchard or its covering trees. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi and al-Tabatabai explain that dense growth shades and covers the earth; al-Mawardi calls it an orchard. Bursavi and al-Alusi say taking it as trees lets rivers run beneath them directly; al-Alusi prefers this. Al-Tabatabai compares 23:50 and other gardens with water below.
  2:266/q04/p2 The land containing the trees. — reasons: Bursavi and al-Alusi say an omitted reference to trees is then needed to explain rivers beneath it; al-Alusi finds the tree sense more fitting.
2:266/q05 What variant is reported for janna? [جَنَّةٌ · readings] (turns on: Singular or plural noun.)
  2:266/q05/p1 A singular garden. — reasons: The verse wording contrasted with al-Hasan's plural reading.
  2:266/q05/p2 Al-Hasan reads jannat, gardens, in the plural. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan transmit it; al-Kashshaf also notes a gardens reading.
2:266/q06 What do nakhil and anab denote and how are they formed? [نَّخِيلٍ أَعْنَابٍ · meaning]
  2:266/q06/p1 Nakhil denotes date palms, as a collective noun or plural; anab denotes grapes, and anab is plural of inaba. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and al-Samin give both analyses of nakhil and record inba as the singular of grapes, with inba as a further extended form; al-Alusi reports the same morphology. Al-Tabrisi says nakhla is a palm and can be masculine or feminine, citing 69:7 and 54:20; al-Raghib derives nakhil from selected or sifted trees and notes its pollination and uses.
2:266/q07 Why are palms and vines named before the other fruits? [نَّخِيلٍ أَعْنَابٍ كُلِّ ٱلثَّمَرَٰتِ · rhetoric]
  2:266/q07/p1 They are the finest, most useful or prized core trees; the garden also contains other fruit. — reasons: Al-Mawardi notes flowing water as the orchard's best feature; al-Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Bursavi, Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi cite the trees' excellence. Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi say the whole palm is useful whereas the vine's chief benefit is its grapes, explaining the switch from tree to fruit. Al-Biqai says naming them specifies an earlier generic garden, 2:265; al-Harali likens the useful palm to a believer and the spreading vine to a pious believer.
2:266/q08 What does min in min nakhil do? [مِّن نَّخِيلٍۢ وَأَعْنَابٍۢ · grammar]
  2:266/q08/p1 It explains the orchard's predominant type or contents without excluding other plants. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi calls min explanatory. Al-Tabatabai cites the subsequent all fruits as proof that palms and vines are not exhaustive. Al-Samin parses min nakhil as a nominative adjective of janna.
2:266/q09 What does the phrase about rivers beneath the garden describe? [تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ · grammar] (turns on: The antecedent of the pronoun and the syntactic role of the river clause.)
  2:266/q09/p1 It describes the garden, with beneath referring to its trees. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya explicitly identifies the trees. Al-Samin permits a nominative adjectival clause or an accusative circumstantial clause from the described garden. Al-Tabrisi permits the garden as antecedent; al-Tabatabai explains water runs beneath dense tree growth. Al-Tabrisi defines anhar as broad channels of flowing water and adds a lexical aside that tahut means people low under others' feet and unnoticed. Jishumi reports the trees as one possible referent. Al-Samarrai stresses that the rivers certainly flow, while the idea of gardens themselves flowing is not established by ordinary experience, even if possible in the afterlife.
  2:266/q09/p2 It may describe the date palms, with the pronoun returning to them. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi supplies this alternative antecedent.
  2:266/q09/p3 It may be the predicate of takuna. — reasons: Al-Samin reports this parse from Makki.
  2:266/q09/p4 The streams may flow beneath the garden's buildings. — reasons: Jishumi reports buildings as another possible referent for beneath.
2:266/q10 What do the running rivers add to the image? [تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the detail is treated as orchard abundance or as a Paradise echo.)
  2:266/q10/p1 They make the orchard fertile, valuable and secure, making its loss more devastating. — reasons: Al-Mawardi calls water the best orchard feature; al-Razi says rivers and diverse produce complete its beauty and utility. Al-Biqai contrasts this irrigated orchard with the rain-fed ridge of 2:265, noting that irrigation failure is costlier. Jishumi rehearses the flowing streams and every kind of fruit as part of the attractive scene. Al-Samarrai compares 47:15, where flow needs no separate assurance that river water is not stale.
  2:266/q10/p2 Running rivers also recall the recurring Paradise image. — reasons: Study Quran cites 2:25; al-Tabatabai compares 23:50 and gardens with water below.
2:266/q11 What does every fruit mean here? [مِن كُلِّ ٱلثَّمَرَٰتِ · meaning] (turns on: Whether thamarat names produce or the garden's benefits, and whether all is exhaustive.)
  2:266/q11/p1 Many different kinds of actual fruit, adding to palms and grapes. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi, al-Biqai and al-Baydawi take the clause as adding other varieties. Bursavi compares the broad expression in 47:15; al-Samin and al-Akhfash say exhaustive ownership is impossible. Al-Alusi rejects taking it literally as every individual fruit and rejects treating it merely as a generalizing restatement after the two named kinds. Jishumi explicitly describes every kind of fruit alongside the palms and vines.
  2:266/q11/p2 Fruits may stand for all the orchard's benefits. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf compares the two gardens in al-Kahf, 18:32 and 18:34; al-Alusi records this interpretation while favoring many actual varieties.
2:266/q12 How is min kull al-thamarat construed? [لَهُۥ فِيهَا مِن كُلِّ ٱلثَّمَرَٰتِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether min is licensed as an extra particle and whether all implies literal totality.)
  2:266/q12/p1 An omitted noun such as provision, fruit or food is understood before the prepositional phrase. — reasons: Abu Hayyan rejects extra min under Kufan and standard Basran conditions and supplies rizq or thamarat; al-Samin supplies rizq or fakiha, citing al-Nabigha and 37:164; Bursavi compares 37:164. Al-Alusi also gives this parse, with lahu as fronted predicate. Al-Samin notes that a prepositional phrase cannot literally be the subject. In a separate grammatical illustration at 6:100, Jurjani parses a fronted object and says fronting changes the scope of denial; his comparison supplies background for the significance of constituent placement, not an alternative parse of this verse.
  2:266/q12/p2 Min is extra, leaving the sense he has all kinds of fruit. — reasons: Al-Akhfash imposes no condition on extra min; al-Samin says all means abundance, since literal totality is impossible. Abu al-Baqa objects under both Sibawayh's and al-Akhfash's analyses if literal totality is implied, but permits it on the abundance sense. Abu Hayyan objects that neither Kufan nor normal Basran conditions are met.
2:266/q13 How does the past wording for old age fit the wish construction? [وَأَصَابَهُ ٱلْكِبَرُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether waw is circumstantial, or the past clause follows an implied law or the sense of the wish.)
  2:266/q13/p1 The waw introduces a circumstantial clause, with qad understood, describing the owner when he has the garden. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf, Ibn Atiyya, al-Razi, al-Baydawi, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, al-Qurtubi and al-Alusi give or permit this analysis. Al-Samin attaches it to ahadukum under yawaddu and compares 2:28 and 3:168; Abu Hayyan calls it the apparent reading.
  2:266/q13/p2 The past form has future force because wishes take an or law, whose constructions can interchange. — reasons: Al-Farra licenses the implied law after wudd and cites 30:51, 4:102, 68:9 and parallel mixing of forms at 52:40; Abu's reading of 4:102 has subjunctive fa-yamilu matching an, while indicative fa-yamiluna follows law. Al-Thalabi cites 3:30 and a poetry line; al-Wahidi cites 2:221, 30:51 and 68:9; al-Samin compares 11:98. Al-Tabari and Ibn al-Jawzi adopt the same account. Al-Alusi reports Abu Hayyan's objection that it would make old age wished, which he finds unconvincing. The Farra heading identifies Arabic and exegetical analysis of this passage. Jishumi transmits al-Farra's explanation that an and law interchange after yawaddu; he also reports Abu al-Hasan that an marks futurity while the sentence bears the sense of law, since wishing may concern past or future.
