[KASHSHAF:103:1/r5] KASHSHAF, d. 538 · author | reports | Alternatively, al-ʿaṣr means evening, like the morning oath, since both times show signs of God's power.
[KASHSHAF:103:1/r6] KASHSHAF, d. 538 · author | reports | It may instead mean time, whose passing contains many kinds of marvels.

[IBNATIYYA:103:1/r6] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · بعض العلماء | reports | Another interpretation assigns al-asr to morning and evening, the two cooler periods.
[IBNATIYYA:103:1/r7] IBNATIYYA, d. 542 · مقاتل | reports | Muqatil says al-asr is the middle prayer, by which God swears; reports identify it as the afternoon prayer and stress the loss of family and property for missing it.

[RAZI:103:1/r1] RAZI, d. 606 · قائل | reports | One reported interpretation takes al-ʿaṣr as time or the age (al-dahr).
[RAZI:103:1/r10] RAZI, d. 606 · أبو مسلم | reports | Abū Muslim interprets al-ʿaṣr as one of the day’s two ends, the late afternoon.
[RAZI:103:1/r11] RAZI, d. 606 · أبو مسلم | reports | Like dawn, evening displays divine power: dawn resembles resurrection, while evening resembles the world’s ruin in death; these are witnesses against the heedless person who fails to judge.
[RAZI:103:1/r12] RAZI, d. 606 · الحسن | reports | Al-Ḥasan compares afternoon to markets nearing closure: one who has not earned before going home faces dependents’ claims; likewise the world nears judgment while people remain unprepared and liable to answer for blessings and wrongs. | mentions 21:1
[RAZI:103:1/r13] RAZI, d. 606 · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | A Prophetic report says one who swears falsely after the afternoon prayer will not be addressed or regarded by God on Judgment Day; al-Rāzī cites it as evidence that this time is honored.
[RAZI:103:1/r14] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Al-ʿaṣr warns the loser while some daylight remains, urging repentance and recovery; in contrast, the morning oath concerns the successful and movement toward arrival.
[RAZI:103:1/r15] RAZI, d. 606 · بعض السلف | reports | An unnamed early Muslim said an ice seller taught him the sūra’s meaning by crying that his capital was melting: passing time consumes life without earnings and leaves one in loss. | mentions 103:2
[RAZI:103:1/r16] RAZI, d. 606 · مقاتل | reports | Muqātil interprets al-ʿaṣr as the afternoon prayer.
[RAZI:103:1/r17] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | One argument for the afternoon prayer interpretation is its identification with the “middle prayer” in 2:238, reportedly specified as afternoon prayer in Ḥafṣa’s codex. | mentions 2:238
[RAZI:103:1/r18] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | A second scriptural argument identifies the prayer mentioned in 5:106 as the afternoon prayer. | mentions 5:106
[RAZI:103:1/r19] RAZI, d. 606 · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | The Prophet likened missing the afternoon prayer to losing one’s family and property; this is cited to establish its merit.
[RAZI:103:1/r2] RAZI, d. 606 · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | rejects | A report says the Prophet swore by time and recited “by time and its vicissitudes”; al-Rāzī rejects that as Qurʾānic recitation because it would invalidate prayer, treating it as exegesis.
[RAZI:103:1/r20] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Performing the afternoon prayer is especially difficult because people are occupied with trade and livelihood late in the day.
[RAZI:103:1/r21] RAZI, d. 606 · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | A reported woman’s confession recounts adultery, killing her child in a vinegar jar, and selling the vinegar; the Prophet assigns punishments and rebukes her for having left the afternoon prayer, underscoring its gravity.
[RAZI:103:1/r22] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | The afternoon prayer closes the day’s acts of worship like repentance closes one’s deeds; because endings matter, it merits special emphasis, and performing it properly turns loss into profit, as the exception says. | mentions 103:3
[RAZI:103:1/r23] RAZI, d. 606 · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | The Prophet listed among three people whom God will neither regard, address, nor purify a man who falsely swears after the afternoon prayer.
[RAZI:103:1/r24] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Although prayer is a human act, God may swear by it because it is a noble institution He commanded, rather than because it is the worshippers’ act.
[RAZI:103:1/r25] RAZI, d. 606 · author | reports | A fourth interpretation takes al-ʿaṣr as the era of the Prophet and his community, based on a report comparing the wages of earlier religious communities and Muslims.
[RAZI:103:1/r26] RAZI, d. 606 · النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | The Prophet’s parable says Jews worked from dawn to noon for one qīrāṭ, Christians from noon to afternoon for one, and his community from afternoon to sunset for two; God calls the greater wage His bounty.
[RAZI:103:1/r27] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | The Prophet’s era, place, and lifespan are respectively sworn by in 103:1, 90:2, and 15:72; as his setting is honored, so is he, and the oath rebukes those who rejected his invitation. | mentions 90:2, 15:72
[RAZI:103:1/r3] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Time contains astonishing opposites, and its divisions and changing states make it impossible for reason straightforwardly to judge it either nonexistent or existent.
[RAZI:103:1/r4] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | A thousand wasted years can be redeemed by repentance in life’s final moment, making that moment more valuable than the preceding lifespan.
[RAZI:103:1/r5] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Because time is a fundamental blessing, the oath warns that night and day are opportunities people waste, as stated in 25:62. | mentions 25:62
[RAZI:103:1/r6] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | Qurʾān 6:12–13 pairs the ownership of places with what inhabits night and day; time is nobler than place, so the oath is by the nobler half of God’s dominion. | mentions 6:12, 6:13
[RAZI:103:1/r7] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | People blame time’s vicissitudes for their losses, but the oath shows time itself is a blessing without fault; the defective loser is the human being.
[RAZI:103:1/r8] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | As time passes, a person’s lifespan shrinks; without compensating gain, that loss explains the next verse’s judgment that humanity is in loss. | mentions 103:2
[RAZI:103:1/r9] RAZI, d. 606 · author | holds | The verse’s paradox is that people rejoice in passing days although each day cuts into their remaining lifespan; the cited poem expresses this.
