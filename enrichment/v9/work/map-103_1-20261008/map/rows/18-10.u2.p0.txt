## THE CURRENT MAP
18:10/q01 What governs the opening “when”? [إِذْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the earlier clause supplies a suitable governing word and whether the two events must be simultaneous.)
  18:10/q01/p1 An omitted command, “remember,” governs it. — reasons: Wahidi and Razi exclude attachment to the preceding question because the Prophet's time and the youths' retreat were far apart. Ibn Ashur calls this an explanatory restart; Abu Hayyan, Samin, Bursevi and Alusi also allow the ellipsis.
  18:10/q01/p2 It is governed by the preceding “wonder.” — reasons: Abu Hayyan, Samin, Bursevi and Alusi give this as a syntactic possibility; Bursevi calls it an adverbial attachment.
  18:10/q01/p3 It attaches to “were” in verse 9. — reasons: Ibn Ashur treats it as a temporal adverb linking the scene to the preceding clause; Alusi also allows this attachment.
  18:10/q01/p4 It attaches to “did you think” in verse 9. — reasons: Alusi argues against this because the Prophet's perception was not simultaneous with their retreat; Wahidi and Razi likewise reject attaching it to the preceding question because of the long interval.
18:10/q02 What does “the youths” designate? [ٱلْفِتْيَةُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the form is analyzed as a broken plural or collective, and how strictly youth limits its sense.)
  18:10/q02/p1 It denotes a small group of young men, the Companions of the Cave. — reasons: Abu Hayyan calls it a broken plural of paucity, fitting their few number; Ibn Ashur calls it a small-group plural of fully grown youths; Biqai says youth made them receptive to truth. Alusi identifies them as the Cave companions. Ibn Jawzi reports the usual sense as young men; Bursevi adds vigor and the metaphorical use for rulers even when old, and cites a prophetic preference for 'my young man' over 'my slave,' while Abu Yusuf allows bondage.
  18:10/q02/p2 It is a collective plural of fatā on a nonproductive pattern. — reasons: Zajjaj, Tabrisi and Ibn Jawzi compare ghulām/ghilma and ṣabī/ṣibya; Ibn Jawzi says the pattern cannot freely form analogues such as 'ravens' or 'wealthy men.' Ibn al-Sarraj, cited by Abu Hayyan, calls it a collective noun; Alusi reports other authorities likewise.
  18:10/q02/p3 Fatā can also mean a fully grown man. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, reported by Ibn Jawzi, cites the verse about believing women; Ibn Ashur describes a fully grown youth.
18:10/q03 Why does the verse repeat “the youths” as a noun? [ٱلْفِتْيَةُ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the explicit noun chiefly foregrounds youth or respects the order by which they became cave companions.)
  18:10/q03/p1 It draws attention to their youth and its significance. — reasons: Alusi says the explicit noun highlights their youth; Ibn Ashur says their close ages evoke mature courage, sound judgment, steadfastness and defense of truth, while Biqai sees youth as receptive to truth.
  18:10/q03/p2 It avoids calling them owners of the cave before they took refuge there. — reasons: Alusi says cave ownership results from their retreat, so a pronoun referring back to the earlier title would presuppose it.
18:10/q04 Who were the youths socially, and how many were there? [ٱلْفِتْيَةُ · reports] (turns on: The divergent historical reports give different numbers and social roles; the Quran supplies neither their number nor their lineage.)
  18:10/q04/p1 They were young Roman nobles or sons of prominent families. — reasons: Ibn Ishaq through Tabari calls them freeborn nobles; Tabari's Ubayd report calls them richly dressed princes, with gold ornaments in Abu Hayyan and Alusi. Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi call them sons of nobles; Baydawi, Bursevi and Study Quran report the same setting. Mujahid says one was still downy-bearded. Ibn Ishaq's narrative gives eight.
  18:10/q04/p2 They were close associates or ministers of the king. — reasons: Tabrisi's Ubayd account calls them close associates; Study Quran reports courtiers, and Tabatabai lists ministers advising the king among variant reports.
  18:10/q04/p3 They numbered eight. — reasons: Ibn Abbas in Tabari names the eldest spokesman Mكسلمينا and seven others; Tabari's king confronts eight, and Ibn Ishaq's account in Baghawi and Thalabi has eight elite youths.
  18:10/q04/p4 They numbered seven, including a shepherd in one account. — reasons: Ibn Abbas's account in Qurtubi has seven young secret worshippers and a shepherd with a dog; Tabatabai lists seven including a shepherd among reports.
  18:10/q04/p5 Reports also call them scholars or bathhouse workers. — reasons: Tabatabai lists these alternative social identities; Wahb's bathhouse account concerns a disciple who worked there and converted the youths.
18:10/q05 When and within which faith did the youths live? [ٱلْفِتْيَةُ · reports] (turns on: Reports place them before or after Jesus, while the Quran itself omits the date.)
  18:10/q05/p1 They followed Jesus and lived after him. — reasons: Amr in Tabari says they followed Jesus; Ibn Ishaq's account makes them Christian believers under a pagan Roman ruler, as do Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi and Study Quran. Tabrisi says they followed Jesus after the Gospel community had passed. Tabatabai says most historians date them between Jesus and Muhammad and that Christians preserved the story widely.
  18:10/q05/p2 They entered the cave before Jesus. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba says Jesus disclosed their story and they rose between Jesus and Muhammad; Ibn Atiyya, Tabrisi, Qurtubi and Abu Hayyan preserve the alternative pre-Jesus chronology.
  18:10/q05/p3 The Quran leaves their religious affiliation and date unspecified. — reasons: Kuranyolu says reports link them to Jesus but the Quran selects only instructive details, with no date or place; Tabatabai says the Quran omits historical particulars that do not serve guidance.
18:10/q06 What persecution led to their flight? [ٱلْفِتْيَةُ أَوَى · reports] (turns on: Reports differ on the ruler's religion and the immediate cause of pursuit.)
  18:10/q06/p1 An idolatrous ruler demanded worship of idols and threatened believers with death. — reasons: Yahya says they feared death for faith. Ibn Ishaq names Deqianus/Decius and describes forced sacrifice, mutilation and believers displayed on city walls; Tabari says informers found their prostration, the king summoned eight, stripped their rank and delayed punishment. Qurtubi and Baydawi report refusal and flight; Tabrisi calls the king Daqiyanus in Ephesus. Names vary Daqyus, Daqliyus and Daqinus; Study Quran notes Decius as a possible source of the name.
  18:10/q06/p2 A Magian or Zoroastrian king imposed his religion. — reasons: Tabrisi reports this alternative to an idol-worshiping king, also noted by Study Quran from Christian sources.
  18:10/q06/p3 One youth was accused of a crime and the group fled the ruler's pursuit. — reasons: Tabari gives this as a separate reported explanation.
18:10/q07 How did the youths come together and leave for the cave? [أَوَى ٱلْفِتْيَةُ · reports] (turns on: Different transmitted narratives give different occasions, escape devices and sequences.)
  18:10/q07/p1 The young believers met by chance while leaving an idolatrous festival, tested one another's faith, and fled together. — reasons: Ubayd ibn Umayr in Tabari and Ibn Atiyya describes independent faith, cautious pairing and disclosure; Mawardi's Mujahid and Tabrisi describe their chance meeting. Some accounts add a hunting dog and 309 years' sleep. Islahi says recognition of truth made them disregard danger.
  18:10/q07/p2 They escaped under cover of play or a hunt. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi preserve a polo-ball ruse or flight during a public festival; Tabatabai's report says they feigned hunting. These accounts differ on whether the king knew of their resistance before escape.
  18:10/q07/p3 After the king delayed punishment, they gathered money and fled to a mountain cave. — reasons: Tabari says Deqianus removed their insignia, gave them time to reconsider, and each took money, gave some away and left with a dog; Qurtubi recounts the reprieve. Ibn Ishaq says they had already prayed, fasted and given alms under persecution.
  18:10/q07/p4 A disciple of Jesus converted them at a bathhouse, then fled with them after two deaths were blamed on the group. — reasons: Wahb's report says the king's son and a woman or prostitute died there; a farmer and dog joined, and the king later sealed the cave.
  18:10/q07/p5 Accounts differ on whether they planned together or met by chance, and whether the ruler knew before they fled. — reasons: Tabatabai explicitly records these axes of variation.
  18:10/q07/p6 Tamlikha, named as the leader, proposed taking refuge in the cave. — reasons: Suyuti names him as the spokesman who proposed the cave; the same note says he spoke after their awakening.
18:10/q08 What happened after they took shelter in the cave? [أَوَى ٱلْكَهْفِ · reports] (turns on: The accounts vary in discovery, sealing and later reopening, while agreeing on extraordinary sleep.)
  18:10/q08/p1 They worshipped there, obtained food secretly, feared the king's search, and were sealed inside. — reasons: Tabari says Yemliha entered town disguised as a poor man, returned with news and a meal, and the youths prostrated and prayed for safety. Amr says the people sealed them and a later Christian king reopened the cave. Study Quran says the king expected hunger and thirst to kill them. Tabari also recounts the king's search and his stated willingness to forgive repentance.
  18:10/q08/p2 They slept for 309 years, later awoke and debated how long they had slept. — reasons: Ubayd's Tabari report gives 309 years; Amr says they guessed a day or part of one. Mawardi's Mujahid gives three hundred plus nine, and Ibn Qutayba places their raising between Jesus and Muhammad. Tabatabai summarizes their flight, sleep, awakening and eventual discovery. Suyuti names Tamlikha as the one who said God knew how long they had stayed after awakening.
