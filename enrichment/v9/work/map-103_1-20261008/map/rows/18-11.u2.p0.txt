## THE CURRENT MAP
18:11/q01 What does the opening fa connect the sleep to? [فَضَرَبْنَا · links] (turns on: Whether the conjunction resumes the youths' prayer or their entry into the cave.)
  18:11/q01/p1 Their preceding prayer for mercy and guidance was answered by the long sleep. — reasons: Alusi compares the response to Zachariah's prayer at 21:90; sleep, turning, and awakening answer 18:10. Ibn Ashur says this favor saved them from torture, confirmed their truth, and became a sign. Bursevi includes their turning as a hidden grace. Qurtubi reports the harm of their people was diverted.
  18:11/q01/p2 The fa connects to their taking refuge in the cave: God promptly gave more than they expected. — reasons: Ibn Ashur offers this alternate connection to 18:10.
18:11/q02 What does God striking over their ears mean? [فَضَرَبْنَا عَلَىٰٓ ءَاذَانِهِمْ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the wording directly denotes sleep, a barrier to hearing that preserves sleep, or a broader cutoff from outside sounds.)
  18:11/q02/p1 God imposed sleep upon the youths in the cave. — reasons: Muqatil says their hearing was sealed for 309 years; Yahya, al-Farra, Ibn Qutayba, Tabari, al-Tha'labi, al-Wahidi, Jishumi, al-Tabrisi, Ibn Kathir, and others read the action as making them sleep. Ibn Qutayba cites Abu Dharr's same expression. Al-Tha'labi calls the sleep overpowering. Ibn Atiyya says it attached firmly, as do Arabic expressions for imposed humiliation, tax, or levies. Al-Majma's explanation is preferred by Tabataba'i over Zamakhshari's paraphrase. Study Quran reports the deep-slumber reading. Asad also reports that classical commentators read the veiled ears as putting the youths to sleep. Ibn Qutayba says rendering the expression as putting them to sleep conveys its intended meaning, since a word-for-word rendering of striking ears would be unintelligible.
  18:11/q02/p2 God barred sound from their ears, making their sleep deep and undisturbed. — reasons: Zajjaj explains that sound wakes sleepers. Mawardi stresses that blocking hearing preserves sleep rather than causing it. Wahidi cites Ibn Abbas's 'struck with sleep' wording. Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi, Alusi, Ibn Ashur, and others understand a veil or barrier; ordinary sounds cannot rouse them. Ibn Ashur contrasts covering the ears with ordinary closing of eyes. Tabataba'i reports Zamakhshari's account; Study Quran reports that incoming sounds were prevented.
  18:11/q02/p3 The expression means preventing the ears from functioning, as an idiom for stopping someone's action. — reasons: Qutrub compares a ruler stopping subjects or a master stopping a servant's trade by 'striking' their hands. Alusi objects that bare auditory prevention does not establish sleep or fit the resurrection setting, though its proponents may intend sleep. Al-Rummani, al-Baqillani, and al-Burhan explain that sensation through the ears was suspended without the youths becoming deaf; al-Burhan calls the expression metaphorical.
  18:11/q02/p4 They were cut off, physically or figuratively, from the sounds and bustle outside. — reasons: Asad leaves both physical and metaphorical separation open while also reporting the classical sleep interpretation.
18:11/q03 What rhetorical picture does the ear expression convey? [فَضَرَبْنَا عَلَىٰٓ ءَاذَانِهِمْ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether its force is an imposed covering, a representation of deep sleep, or tender lulling.)
  18:11/q03/p1 It pictures sleep as something firmly imposed or a cover laid over the ears. — reasons: Tabari compares striking someone with paralysis. Tha'labi and Wahidi cite al-Aswad ibn Ya'fur's barriers over the earth; Jishumi notes the poet was blind. Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, and Samin cite al-Farazdaq's line; Abu Hayyan also cites al-Aswad and an unnamed poet, and compares humiliation, poll tax, conscription, and fixed traits. Alusi mentions a struck lock and pitched tent; Ibn Ashur cites imposed humiliation at 2:61 and also mentions 2:26. Alusi answers al-Qutb's objection to metonymy and offers an implicit metaphor; other investigators prefer a representational metaphor. Qurtubi and Tabrisi independently transmit al-Aswad's verse; Tabrisi uses it for the image of confinement. Al-Rummani compares the silencing of their ears to striking a book shut. Al-Burhan calls the absence of ear sensation a metaphor and explains allusive metaphor through an unnamed borrowed image hinted at by an attribute, such as predation implying a lion for a brave man; it also reports al-Sakkaki's broader account of figurative predication.
  18:11/q03/p2 It is a notably eloquent Qur'anic idiom whose full verbal force cannot be conveyed in translation. — reasons: Tha'labi calls it unmatched; Qurtubi says its eloquence challenged the Arabs. Tabrisi and Tabataba'i say translation cannot preserve its wording. Ibn Ashur calls its metonymy unknown before the Qur'an and an aspect of inimitability. Ibn Qutayba says a literal translation of striking the ears is unintelligible, whereas 'putting them to sleep' preserves the sense. Al-Burhan cites Ibn Faris's example of this compact phrase, which requires explanatory expansion in another language; it says translation loses Arabic expressive precision and even an Arabic paraphrase cannot replace the challenged wording. Al-Qaffal, as reported by al-Burhan, says no complete Persian translation is possible, though tafsir can convey some intended meaning.
  18:11/q03/p3 It evokes a mother gently tapping a child near the ear to lull it to sleep. — reasons: Tabataba'i suggests tender maternal care. Islahi traces an affectionate lulling sense of the idiom and calls the verse an eloquent image of loving care.
18:11/q04 What is understood as the omitted object of 'struck'? [فَضَرَبْنَا عَلَىٰٓ ءَاذَانِهِمْ · grammar]
  18:11/q04/p1 A veil, covering, or barrier over the ears is understood. — reasons: Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, Samin, and Ibn Ashur supply the omitted object; Abu Hayyan compares pitching a tent over one's wife. Alusi analyses the covering as an implicit metaphor.
18:11/q05 Why are the ears singled out? [ءَاذَانِهِمْ · rhetoric]
  18:11/q05/p1 Hearing is what commonly wakes a sleeper; suspending it makes the sleep especially deep. — reasons: Zajjaj supplies the causal point; Ibn Atiyya, Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, Bursevi, and Alusi repeat it. Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi cite the prophetic report of Satan urinating in the ear of one who sleeps through the night; Abu Hayyan says this signifies heavy sleep without rising. Alusi says blocked eyes would suggest only sleep's onset, since sights do not rouse a sleeper. Samin says striking ears brings sleep. Al-Rummani contrasts ears with eyes: closed eyes may reopen, whereas stopping hearing prevents awakening.
18:11/q06 What inward meaning is drawn from the blocked ears and sleep? [ءَاذَانِهِمْ · inward] (turns on: The notes offer different spiritual applications: exclusive perception of God, transformation of the self, heedlessness, and retreat from human impressions.)
  18:11/q06/p1 Their hearing and sight were withdrawn from all but God. — reasons: Sulami reports 'some' saying they heard and saw only Him, so no creature or harm had a share in them. Another Sulami report says the covered ears also gave rest from creatures.
  18:11/q06/p2 Their human qualities gave way to sacred qualities until God returned them to bodily life. — reasons: Ibn Ata, as reported by Sulami, says their outward and inward were sanctified and held in God's grasp, then returned to bodily form and attributes at raising.
  18:11/q06/p3 The sleep signifies spiritual heedlessness during bodily existence until maturity or death. — reasons: Qashani cites the prophetic saying that people are asleep and awaken when they die; he relates this to immersion in bodily management until voluntary or natural death.
  18:11/q06/p4 For retreat seekers, shutting the senses protects the heart and opens it to divine knowledge. — reasons: Bursevi's Ta'wilat Najmiyya blocks human speech from impressing the heart, erases earlier impressions through repeated 'There is no god but God,' and describes divine manifestation, annihilation of self, and preservation through God. Alusi also reports allegorizing the sleep as absorption in God and annihilation in all besides Him.
18:11/q07 What do reports say happened as the youths entered the cave? [فِى ٱلْكَهْفِ · reports] (turns on: Accounts vary in when sleep began and in surrounding details.)
  18:11/q07/p1 God put them to sleep in the cave, with the dog and their provisions beside them. — reasons: Tabari places sleep after their meal and conversation, with the dog at the entrance. Tha'labi puts it at sunset, provisions by their heads. A report from Abu Abd Allah says they entered with the dog, slept until the king and city disappeared, then awoke thinking a day or part of one had passed. Tabataba'i summarizes the protected cave sleep.
  18:11/q07/p2 They slept as soon as they entered the cave, hiding them from the kingdom. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya reports this chronology.
  18:11/q07/p3 The youths fled an idol worshipping tyrant with a shepherd and his dog. — reasons: Abu Abd Allah's report has the tyrant kill dissenters; the youths feigned a hunting trip and the shepherd joined their faith. Tha'labi adds that Tamlikha warned them of Daqyanus, after which they prostrated and sought protection; the king later could not find them and leaders accused them of rebellion.
18:11/q08 How was the cave sealed and later opened? [ٱلْكَهْفِ · reports] (turns on: Reports differ on the king's reason for sealing and on who later opened the cave.)
  18:11/q08/p1 Daqyanus sealed it so the youths would die of hunger and thirst. — reasons: Tha'labi says he believed them awake; Ibn Atiyya reports the searchers' advice. Wahb's report in Tha'labi and Ibn Jawzi says the king's men feared entering, so they blocked the entrance.
