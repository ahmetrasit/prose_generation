  2:264/q22/p5 Only the explicitly stated failure matters; any cleansing benefit of rain does not defeat the analogy. — reasons: Al-Rāzī answers the objection that rain cleans rock; he rejects al-Qāḍī's recourse to benefits of dust and relies on the stated likeness.
2:264/q23 To whom does the plural 'they cannot' refer? [لَّا يَقْدِرُونَ · referent] (turns on: Whether the plural resumes the generic show-off, the addressed believers, an implied planter, or all analogous donors.)
  2:264/q23/p1 It refers by meaning to the class of showy spenders despite the earlier singular relative. — reasons: Al-Kashshāf says الذي and من alternate; al-Bayḍāwī cites poetry shifting from singular الذي to plural هم; al-Samīn cites 2:17 and allows an independent or circumstantial clause. Ibn ʿAṭiyya objects to reversing Arabic's normal form-then-meaning order; al-Samīn disputes the rule.
  2:264/q23/p2 It returns to the believers addressed earlier, shifting from direct address to third person. — reasons: Al-Qaffāl cites a similar shift at 10:22; al-Samīn calls the return distant.
  2:264/q23/p3 It refers to any would-be planter unable to retrieve seed from the washed-off soil. — reasons: Al-Qaffāl uses the implied seed to show the donor likewise cannot benefit; al-Biqāʿī says elliptical correspondence joins the represented spender and the planter.
  2:264/q23/p4 It encompasses show-offs, disbelievers, and reproachful donors together. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī says all fail to benefit when reward is needed, since the spending was for other than God.
2:264/q24 How does the 'they cannot' clause attach syntactically? [لَّا يَقْدِرُونَ عَلَىٰ شَىْءٍۢ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the clause is syntactically independent or a circumstantial state despite the intervening likeness.)
  2:264/q24/p1 It may be independent, explanatory, or a summary of the parable. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says it can explain the comparison or answer an implied question; Ibn ʿĀshūr lists explanatory resumption and summary among deliberately flexible attachments.
  2:264/q24/p2 It may be circumstantial from the spender or the figurative rock. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī and al-Samīn permit a state clause; Abū al-Baqāʾ objects that فمثله intervenes, while al-Samīn says the intervening comparison reinforces the point. Al-Ālūsī calls al-Samīn's analysis weak; Ibn ʿĀshūr also offers a state with omitted relative pronoun.
2:264/q25 What do the spenders fail to obtain? [لَّا يَقْدِرُونَ عَلَىٰ شَىْءٍۢ مِّمَّا كَسَبُوا۟ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether incapacity means no recoverable reward or failure to use earnings rightly.)
  2:264/q25/p1 They cannot obtain reward or benefit from their spending at judgment. — reasons: Muqātil compares windblown ashes at 14:18; al-Kashshāf and Bursevī cite scattered dust at 25:23. Ibn Abī Ḥātim says the money yields neither reward nor help on Judgment Day; Ibn ʿĀshūr adds that donors cannot retrieve the gift itself.
  2:264/q25/p2 They cannot direct their earnings to their proper end, having spent fruitlessly for the afterlife. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the subsequent denial of guidance to disbelievers suggests this possible sense.
2:264/q26 Why is spending called what they 'earned'? [مِّمَّا كَسَبُوا۟ · rhetoric]
  2:264/q26/p1 They spent hoping to acquire recompense, so their spending is called earnings. — reasons: Al-Māwardī, al-Qurṭubī, and Abū Ḥayyān explain the intended return; al-Samīn simply notes charity is termed acquisition.
2:264/q27 What does God not guiding disbelievers mean here? [وَٱللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِى ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلْكَٰفِرِينَ · theology] (turns on: Whether guidance means right conduct now, divine recompense, Paradise and special success, or the fate of those who die unbelieving.)
  2:264/q27/p1 He does not guide persistent unbelievers to right spending or good deeds while they prefer falsehood. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī says their preference for falsehood prevents right spending; al-Nasafī limits it to their persistence in disbelief. Abū Ḥayyān says it may mean no guidance in their deeds while unbelieving and that this favors a disbeliever in the parable.
  2:264/q27/p2 He does not make them guided because of their disbelief. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj and Abū Isḥāq attribute the lack of guidance to disbelief.
  2:264/q27/p3 He does not make guidance the reward of disbelief. — reasons: The alternative appears alongside the causative account in al-Zajjāj and al-Wāḥidī.
  2:264/q27/p4 He does not guide unbelievers into Paradise by their works or give them believers' special care. — reasons: Al-Ṭabrisī reports Paradise and extra care/success readings. Al-Rāzī reports a Muʿtazilite account of being led away from reward and Paradise by one's own choices, with removal of faith as another gloss.
  2:264/q27/p5 The saying generalizes why the show-off's act belongs to unbelief. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī connects the closing rule with his reading of unbelief in the promised reward.
  2:264/q27/p6 It refers to those who die unbelieving. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya records this as one possible referent.
2:264/q28 How does this verse relate to nearby teaching and other Qur'anic images? [* · links]
  2:264/q28/p1 It reverses the fertile seed and garden images of sincere spending in the surrounding passage. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr sets the fruitless rock against the seven-hundred-grain seed at 2:261; Study Quran contrasts ruined charity with giving for God's pleasure and secure faith.
  2:264/q28/p2 It echoes images of unbelieving deeds as ashes, a mirage, or scattered dust. — reasons: Sources cite 14:18, 24:39, and 25:23; Ibn ʿĀshūr distinguishes a concealed rock exposed by rain from merely swept-away soil in 14:18.
  2:264/q28/p3 The warning shifts from spending in God's path to needy persons, where personal contact makes injury likelier. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr links this to the prior discussion at 2:262.
  2:264/q28/p4 It anticipates the orchard owner's loss and shattered hopes. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī likens abusive donors' loss to the orchard owner's family loss; Ibn ʿĀshūr connects the barren rock to the person turning his hands over spending at 18:42.
  2:264/q28/p5 The parable centers acceptance on sincere intention. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says seeking God's face is necessary for acceptance and cites the widely transmitted report that deeds follow intentions.
2:264/q29 What is reported about the judgment of people who acted for public praise? [رِئَآءَ ٱلنَّاسِ · reports]
  2:264/q29/p1 They are sent to seek reward from the people whose praise they pursued. — reasons: ʿIkrima transmits Ibn ʿAbbās's caller saying God accepts no deed mixed with worldly aims. Maḥmūd ibn Labīd and Bursevī transmit the Prophet's identification of ostentation as lesser shirk and this judgment warning.
  2:264/q29/p2 A praised donor, warrior, and scholar or reciter receive no divine reward for their reputations. — reasons: The Abū Hurayra narrative is introduced through ʿAbd Allāh al-Madanī's report of repeated tears: the donor was called generous, the warrior brave, and the scholar learned as intended. Bursevī's hadith names reciter, martyr, and donor among the first judged and condemned to Hell.
  2:264/q29/p3 Early givers hid their charity to avoid public notice. — reasons: Bursevī reports donors seeking blind recipients, fastening money to a sleeping poor person's clothing, or leaving it to be found.
2:264/q30 What does the passage teach in an inward reading? [* · inward]
  2:264/q30/p1 Seeking any aim besides the Real corrupts devotion, and reminding a poor person reveals misplaced intention. — reasons: Al-Sarrī says adorning oneself with deeds turns good into sin, still worse when claiming their value. Bursevī applies 10:32 to ulterior aims; a giver seeking God feels indebted to the poor as a way to Him. He interprets the report that rich would perish without poor and reverses the upper-hand saying in the recipient's favor; inward harm is turning toward anything other than the Real.
  2:264/q30/p2 The mature servant seeks God himself without demanding wages or public fame. — reasons: An unnamed scholar compares ostentatious worship to a full sack of valueless stones. Rūmī's verses seek pure guidance and say the lover's joy, grief, service, and wage belong to the beloved; Saʿdī urges hidden humility; Ḥāfiẓ urges trust in the master's generosity. Bursevī says divine love cuts rival attachment to wealth, children, and life and closes with prayer for hope in God alone.
