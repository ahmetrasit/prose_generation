## THE CURRENT MAP
12:41/q01 Why does Joseph again call them his prison companions? [يَٰصَىٰحِبَىِ ٱلسِّجْنِ · rhetoric] (turns on: Which rhetorical effect of the renewed address is emphasized.)
  12:41/q01/p1 The address renews their attention and marks the importance of his answer. — reasons: Abu Hayyan says Joseph returns to their interpretation after the more important teaching about religion; al-Alusi says interpretation is a new topic after his proof of knowledge; Ibn Ashur says the vocative stresses the interpretation.
  12:41/q01/p2 The address invokes shared hardship and friendship. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i says shared imprisonment removes worldly pretension and softens the heart.
12:41/q02 Which prisoner receives each predicted fate? [أَحَدُكُمَا ٱلْءَاخَرُ · referent]
  12:41/q02/p1 The cupbearer returns to royal wine service, and the baker is crucified and eaten by birds. — reasons: Muqatil, al-Thalabi, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi, al-Tabrisi, and others specify three more prison days; some say release and crucifixion occur on the fourth. The cupbearer dreamed of grapes or a vine, the baker of bread baskets and birds. Ibn Ishaq, Mujahid and later reports name them variously Nabu, Nabwa or Banu, and Mujlith, Majlatha, Majlath, Malhab or Mujlab; the name forms vary. Al-Mawardi calls the condemned man the king's baker; the Imami report calls both king's servants. Study Quran says the baker's head is eaten after death and for his crime.
12:41/q03 Why are the two prisoners not named in the prediction? [أَحَدُكُمَا ٱلْءَاخَرُ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the verse reproduces Joseph's veiled speech or condenses a more explicit speech.)
  12:41/q03/p1 Joseph deliberately veiled the identities to spare the condemned man. — reasons: Ibn Kathir says this spared the other prisoner; al-Biqa'i says each could hope for restoration unless he asked directly; al-Alusi invokes trust in the interpretation and good companionship; Ibn Ashur says the condemned man could grasp his fate gradually.
  12:41/q03/p2 The verse may abbreviate Joseph's direct assignment of each fate. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says the verse may condense the original speech in which Joseph named each man's outcome.
12:41/q04 How does the cupbearer's dream yield the timing of his restoration? [فَيَسْقِى رَبَّهُۥ خَمْرًۭا · interpretation]
  12:41/q04/p1 Three grape clusters or vine branches mean three more prison days before his return to service. — reasons: Muqatil gives three days. Al-Thalabi speaks of three clusters; al-Kalbi in al-Wahidi of a vine with three branches and pressed grapes; al-Tabrisi specifies release on the fourth day. Al-Zamakhshari and al-Alusi take the vine as the king and favorable standing. Ikrima's report in al-Durr describes planting a seed, its growth into clusters, pressing, and serving the king.
12:41/q05 How do the baker's baskets yield the timing of his crucifixion? [فَيُصْلَبُ فَتَأْكُلُ ٱلطَّيْرُ مِن رَّأْسِهِۦ · interpretation]
  12:41/q05/p1 Three baskets mean three more days before execution and the birds eating his head. — reasons: Al-Thalabi says crucifixion is on the fourth day; al-Kalbi, Ibn al-Sa'ib, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, and al-Alusi link the three baskets to three days. Al-Baghawi says release after three days is followed by crucifixion. Al-Mawardi notes that birds had eaten bread from the dream baskets.
12:41/q06 How is فيسقي read? [فَيَسْقِى · readings] (turns on: The verb's active or passive morphology and, for the passive, who receives the drink.)
  12:41/q06/p1 The common active reading derives from saqā: he gives his master wine. — reasons: Abu Hayyan calls it the majority reading; al-Samin describes its open yāʾ and derivation from saqā. Al-Durr records Ikrima reciting the active wording.
  12:41/q06/p2 An active reading derives from asqā, with the same drink-serving sense. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan report the variant; al-Samin attributes a damma to Ikrima and compares 56:69; al-Alusi also reports it.
  12:41/q06/p3 The passive reading yusqā rabbahu has the master or king given wine. — reasons: Ikrima and al-Jahdari are named by Ibn Jinni, Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and al-Samin; Abu Hayyan explicitly identifies the king as recipient. Al-Samin says rabbahu is nominative and notes al-Zamakhshari attributes the reading only to Ikrima. Ibn Jinni sees parallel favorable and punitive passive outcomes. Al-Alusi also reports a passive with riyyihi as a second object.
  12:41/q06/p4 The passive reading means the cupbearer himself is given drink. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari glosses Ikrima's passive as his being given drink to satisfy him; Ibn Atiyya also says he is given wine to drink.
12:41/q07 Do saqā and asqā have the same meaning? [فَيَسْقِى · meaning] (turns on: Whether Arab usage treats the two forms as synonyms or distinguishes direct drinking from provision of water.)
  12:41/q07/p1 They can both mean giving a person a drink. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya calls them synonymous; al-Qurtubi reports some linguists' synonymy; Abu Hayyan cites the Lawami author; al-Samin compares the reading at 56:69; al-Alusi allows this shared sense.
  12:41/q07/p2 Saqā gives a drink directly, whereas asqā provides water or irrigation. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj states the distinction; al-Nahhas says most linguists make it, cites Qur'anic form against a poem claiming synonymy, and prefers it. Al-Qurtubi cites al-Nahhas with 77:27; Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi call it the usual distinction.
12:41/q08 Who is the cupbearer's rabb? [رَبَّهُۥ · referent]
  12:41/q08/p1 His master, the king of Egypt. — reasons: Al-Tabari and Ibn al-Sa'ib gloss rabb as master or king. Al-Tabrisi compares 'lord of a house' or estate. Al-Razi infers the prisoners served the king from this expression and the request to be remembered in 12:42. Abu Hayyan attributes the master gloss to Ibn Atiyya; Ibn Zayd uses lord or master.
12:41/q09 What does the passive form of crucifixion convey? [فَيُصْلَبُ · rhetoric]
  12:41/q09/p1 It points to the king's sole authority to crucify. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i says only the king can order or carry out this punishment.
12:41/q10 How do the paired fate clauses work rhetorically? [فَيَسْقِى رَبَّهُۥ خَمْرًۭا فَيُصْلَبُ فَتَأْكُلُ ٱلطَّيْرُ مِن رَّأْسِهِۦ · rhetoric]
  12:41/q10/p1 Each stated outcome implies the opposite counterpart left unspoken for the other prisoner. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i describes ellipsis across the paired clauses.
12:41/q11 What does quḍiya mean here? [قُضِىَ · meaning]
  12:41/q11/p1 It means completed, settled, or fully executed. — reasons: Al-Jishumi derives the sense from completing and executing, compares lammā quḍiya and the naming of a qāḍī. Al-Tabari says God's decree has taken effect; al-Alusi glosses it as completed or settled. Ibn Qutayba traces qaḍā to determination and completion, cites a poem about completing affairs, and explains qāḍī as one who settles disputes; he compares the decreed death in 39:42. Al-Damghani says the prison interpretation is settled or due. Ibn al-Jawzi distinguishes completion in 6:60, 20:114 and 28:28–29, settlement in 6:58, 10:47, 10:54 and 39:69, and irrevocable decree here and in 19:21, 34:14 and 39:42.
12:41/q12 What is the 'matter' that has been decided? [ٱلْأَمْرُ · referent] (turns on: Whether the inquiry's object is the dreams and their interpretation, the prisoners' eventual fates, or the accusation underlying both cases.)
  12:41/q12/p1 The men's eventual fates: one will be saved and one killed. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari, Abu Hayyan, and al-Samin connect the singular matter to their shared imprisonment or accusation; al-Baydawi says they sought their shared outcome. Al-Nasafi and Ibn Ashur take it as what would happen to them. Al-Alusi prefers this as less strained: they asked about dreams in order to learn their fate, so fī is causal; he says inquiry can treat dream and outcome together.
  12:41/q12/p2 The requested interpretation or the dreams as the object of inquiry has been settled. — reasons: Al-Mawardi gives the question and answer as finished; Ibn al-Jawzi says the interpretation is settled whether the dreams were true or false. Ibn Abbas, Ibn Masud and Qatada in al-Wahidi say Joseph only finished the interpretation. Al-Alusi's investigator says a question concerns the dream as an incident, compares the king's request in 12:43, and says 'matter' magnifies obscure dreams; singular matches the prisoners' own wording. Al-Alusi objects that dream and outcome need not be separated this way.
  12:41/q12/p3 The matter is the accusation or crime for which both men were imprisoned. — reasons: Al-Alusi reports this view with an omitted term meaning the consequence of that accusation. Al-Zamakhshari and Abu Hayyan likewise invoke their shared poisoning accusation, while reading the decided matter as its outcome.
12:41/q13 Why is 'matter' singular for two dreams? [ٱلْأَمْرُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the singular gathers a shared case and fate or echoes the prisoners' phrasing.)
  12:41/q13/p1 The prisoners share one case or sought one combined outcome. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari identifies the poisoning accusation and the opposed results; al-Baydawi says their inquiry was about the shared case; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin similarly identify the joint accusation and final outcome.
  12:41/q13/p2 The singular follows the prisoners' own singular wording for their dream inquiry. — reasons: Al-Alusi's investigator says they asked about the image of an outcome, not their crime or its cause.
