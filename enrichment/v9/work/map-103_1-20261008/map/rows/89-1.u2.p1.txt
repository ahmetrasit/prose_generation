89:1/q09 Why is dawn chosen as an oath sign? [وَٱلْفَجْرِ · rhetoric] (turns on: The sources stress divine order, resurrection, particular rites, moral symbolism and the oaths' evidentiary role.)
  89:1/q09/p1 Its ordered light and benefit show the Creator's sole power. — reasons: Jishumi calls these signs proof of the Creator; Razi notes that dawn sends people and animals seeking provision. Islahi says God alone brings forth and removes, grants respite and harmonizes opposing forces (21:22); 28:71-72 illustrates His power over perpetual night or day. Elmalili sees changing time as lordship. Qutb portrays creation joyfully waking and praying. Corpus Coranicum cites a likely authentic Umayya poem greeting the Lord each dawn, suggesting a cultic connotation.
  89:1/q09/p2 Its daily return after dark images resurrection and judgment. — reasons: Razi and Bursevi compare dawn's spreading life with rising again. Biqai likens waking from sleep's lesser death to return, work and eventual recompense. Islahi invokes a waking supplication, 74:32-34, moral incompleteness without judgment and 68:35-36; ease and hardship prepare for 89:28. Kuran Yolu cites waking living things and 81:18.
  89:1/q09/p3 The particular pilgrimage dawn carries collective joy and major rites. — reasons: Elmalili prefers its collective joy; the Dhu al-Hijja nights, Arafah and sacrifice sequence fits the sura and urges worship during limited life. Biqai mentions entry to the sanctuary and release from consecration; Bursevi cites Arafah supplication and Adha rites; Qurtubi links the dawn to pilgrimage completion. Corpus Coranicum finds a cultic ten-night interpretation persuasive despite objections to Neuwirth's liturgical reading. Al-Suyuti says these times contain the great rites of pilgrimage and humble worship, pointing to the honor of what Muhammad and Abraham brought.
  89:1/q09/p4 Darkness yielding to light prompts moral reflection and repentance. — reasons: Bint al-Shati reads dawn, crescent and night as truth emerging after tyranny, with wealth and poverty tests and the tyrants' and tranquil soul's outcomes to follow. Bursevi urges washing sins with tears. Qutb pictures a joyful waking creation. Bint al-Shati reports Zamakhshari's admonitory reading of 89:6 and 89:14.
  89:1/q09/p5 The oath objects are evidence for the sura's claim. — reasons: Islahi says identify the claim and compare parallel Quranic passages. Bint al-Shati rejects making every object a symbol of honor; she reports Tabari's caution against unsupported interpretation and Razi's and Zamakhshari's criticism of speculation. Elmalili notes disagreement on definiteness, article scope and oath objects. Bint al-Shati argues that discerning a created thing's purpose does not establish its greatness; interpreters must attend to restrictive wording such as 'when it settles' or 'when it falls.' She asks what oath-waw conveys to an Arab listener beyond an explicit 'I swear by' construction.
  89:1/q09/p6 The oath magnifies the thing sworn by, inviting inquiry into its greatness. — reasons: Bint al-Shati reports this as the view of most exegetes.
89:1/q10 How does dawn relate to the following and parallel oaths? [وَٱلْفَجْرِ · links] (turns on: The next oath supports different calendars; other sources read the series through cosmic contrasts.)
  89:1/q10/p1 Dawn and departing night are the chief contrast; intervening oaths develop it. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says the blessed ten are a subset of night. Corpus Coranicum compares 74:34, 81:18, 91:1-3, 92:2 and 93:1 and notes that the ten uniquely quantify night. Islahi calls first and last primary and the middle secondary. Tabatabai glosses dawn, even, odd and withdrawing night; al-Aswad pairs familiar dawn with Dhu al-Hijja's ten.
  89:1/q10/p2 Cosmic oppositions anticipate moral oppositions and final recompense. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum compares 91:9-10 and 92:4-10, plus similar oath openings in 91-93, 95, 100 and 103. Bint al-Shati sees truth after tyranny; Islahi says worldly pairs call for an afterlife settling good and evil.
  89:1/q10/p3 The formula resembles Quranic oaths and may echo pre-Islamic soothsayer language. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum calls the soothsayer link probable but under-studied, compares 100:1-5, points to discussion of 93:1-2 and lists similar sura openings. Islahi compares receding night and appearing morning in 74:32-34. Al-Zarkashi and al-Suyuti each count fifteen suras beginning with an oath, including al-Fajr, and classify oath as the fifth form of sura opening.
  89:1/q10/p4 Dawn and ten nights point to Dhu al-Hijja and sacrifice rites. — reasons: Al-Dahhak and Ibn al-Qayyim argue from the next oath. A Jabir report identifies the dawn and ten with the ten of sacrifice; Elmalili reports Tabari endorsing it and prefers the ten nights, Arafah and Sacrifice Day sequence.
  89:1/q10/p5 Dawn and ten nights point to Muharram's first days. — reasons: Study Quran explicitly pairs Muharram's beginning and ten nights; Qatada calls it the year's first day.
  89:1/q10/p6 Dawn and ten nights point to Ramadan's final ten and first revelation. — reasons: Study Quran cites 97:1.
89:1/q11 How does this opening connect to the sura and al-Ghashiya? [* · links] (turns on: The sources stress judgment, moral testing, divine power and the basmala.)
  89:1/q11/p1 It supports return, reckoning and divine watchfulness announced at al-Ghashiya's end. — reasons: Jishumi links the prior chapter's return and judgment to this chapter's warning and encouragement. Biqai repeatedly cites 88:26, waking from death-like sleep and work before recompense; pilgrimage clothing, talbiya and sacred travel evoke resurrection. Islahi says the prior signs of divine power, wisdom and providence require accountability. Alusi reports Suyuti's view that the oath affirms al-Ghashiya's promise and threat and parallels its opening questions.
  89:1/q11/p2 It prepares tyrants' stories, human tests and the tranquil soul at judgment. — reasons: Alusi connects al-Ghashiya's two classes of faces (88:2, 88:8) with deniers and the blessed soul (89:27). Study Quran traces oaths, tyrants, fortune, stinginess and judgment. Tabatabai says worldly attachment causes tyranny and ingratitude; wealth and poverty test preparation for the next life, while the tranquil soul stays submitted through gain and loss. Bint al-Shati anticipates these oppositions in the natural contrasts. Qutb describes Meccan scenes, rhythms, rhyme, warning, history and moral correction.
  89:1/q11/p3 The signs establish sole divine rule and the need for a just afterlife. — reasons: Islahi says the Prophet warns through heavenly and earthly signs even when some cannot see; harmonized opposites require one sovereign (21:22), and unrequited good and evil would make creation purposeless (68:35-36). Alternating day and night show even the powerful cannot escape God (28:71-72).
  89:1/q11/p4 The basmala evokes mercy, lovers' self-offering and gathering at resurrection. — reasons: Qushayri says reciting satisfies worshippers while lovers offer life; the basmala unsettles the poor, disorients the enamored and brings mercy to lovers. Biqai portrays God reassembling dispersed people, giving guidance and granting His allies contentment and gardens.
89:1/q12 What inward meaning is given to dawn? [ٱلْفَجْرِ · inward] (turns on: These readings transpose dawn's light to prophetic, devotional and eschatological experience.)
  89:1/q12/p1 Muhammad, through whom faith and obedience shine and unbelief disappears. — reasons: Tustari adds the lights of both worlds; Ibn Ata as reported by Sulami stresses faith dispelling unbelief.
  89:1/q12/p2 At the great resurrection, divine light appearing in the soul's night. — reasons: Qashani relates ten to dormant senses during disclosure, pair to witness and witnessed, odd to oneness after duality and night to disappearing selfhood.
  89:1/q12/p3 At death's lesser resurrection, the sun rising from the west. — reasons: Qashani relates ten to dimmed senses, pair to spirit and body, odd to separated spirit and night to bodily darkness dispersing.
  89:1/q12/p4 The saints' opening worship or lovers' communion with God. — reasons: Qushayri reports the former and Kashifi through Bursevi the latter.
89:1/q13 Where and when was the sura revealed, and how many verses has it? [* · sciences] (turns on: Most call it Meccan, with a Medinan report; verse counts vary.)
  89:1/q13/p1 Meccan, often placed early after al-Layl. — reasons: Kashshaf gives thirty verses with twenty-nine as an alternative. Durr cites Ibn Abbas and Ibn al-Zubayr for Meccan revelation and thirty verses. Alusi says this is the majority, while regional verse counts differ. Bint al-Shati places it tenth, after al-Layl and before al-Duha; Study Quran says probably tenth. Tabatabai infers Meccan origin from context; Qutb calls it Meccan. Al-Zarkashi's reported revelation order puts al-Fajr ninth, whereas Jabir ibn Zayd's order reported by al-Suyuti puts it tenth. Al-Bayhaqi transmits Ikrima and al-Hasan's Meccan attribution; al-Suyuti calls the preceding chain good, and Ibn Abbas places al-Fajr after al-Layl and before al-Duha. Qatada's Medinan list omits al-Fajr. Bint al-Shati observes that suras opening with oath-waw are Meccan. Ibn Kathir reports general address-form criteria from Ibrahim ibn Alqama and Maymun ibn Mihran, notes that some material can be classified separately from its sura or revealed twice, and favors sound evidence. Al-Suyuti also reports broader chronologies and disputed claims about first Meccan and Medinan revelations and Ibn al-Hassar's agreement categories.
