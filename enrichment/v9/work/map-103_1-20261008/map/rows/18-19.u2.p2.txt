18:19/q23 Who or what does 'which of it' refer to? [أَيُّهَآ · referent] (turns on: Whether the city's people, its locations, or its foods are supplied to resolve the pronoun.)
  18:19/q23/p1 The city's inhabitants or vendors are understood: which person has the relevant food. — reasons: Al-Tabari, al-Zajjaj and al-Nahhas supply 'people'; al-Kashshaf compares 'ask the town' at 12:82; al-Nasafi, al-Samin, Bursevi and Tabatabai agree. Al-Tabari notes listeners understand food belongs to people.
  18:19/q23/p2 The pronoun can point directly to foods understood from context. — reasons: Al-Nahhas allows 'which food'; al-Samin says no elision is required; Abu Hayyan and Alusi present this alternative.
  18:19/q23/p3 It can refer grammatically to the city itself with an understood person or place. — reasons: Al-Tabari says the syntax attaches it to 'city' although the sense is inhabitants; Alusi lists city, residents, or food.
  18:19/q23/p4 It means which place in the city, such as a market stall. — reasons: Ibn Ashur makes place the referent and ṭaʿāman the specification of the comparative.
18:19/q24 How is 'let him look which has purer food' parsed? [فَلْيَنظُرْ أَيُّهَآ أَزْكَىٰ طَعَامًۭا · grammar] (turns on: The grammatical choice is interrogative versus relative ayyu and whether naẓar governs it or its clause.)
  18:19/q24/p1 Ayyuhā is an interrogative subject, azkā its predicate, and ṭaʿāman a specification. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj gives this parse; al-Wahidi cites Abu Ishaq; al-Razi and al-Nasafi follow; al-Samin and Alusi allow it. Al-Burhān reports al-Saʿdī's and grammarians' differing rules for a comparative annexed to its genus, then identifies azkā ṭaʿāman as a tamyīz construction scholars must explain within those rules. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says the specification explicitly states the criterion of comparison, unlike an unqualified superlative.
  18:19/q24/p2 Ayy may be relative and function as the object, with an omitted predicate. — reasons: Abu Hayyan attributes this to Sibawayh; al-Samin and Alusi record the interrogative/relative options.
  18:19/q24/p3 The interrogative clause is what 'let him look' investigates. — reasons: Al-Akhfash says naẓar can be followed by a question without directly governing ayyu, illustrated with 'look, is Zayd more generous or Amr?'; al-Tabrisi calls the whole clause the object. Sībawayh says the interrogative clause in 'let him look which has purer food' occupies the verb's object position while retaining independent internal syntax, as at 18:12. He distinguishes direct government when no question intervenes, illustrates other embedded-question positions and accusative Arab usage, and notes that intransitive naẓar and asking about someone leave the name within the question. Al-Itqān says the exegete must attend to particles, nouns, verbs and adverbs because their varying uses alter meaning and inference.
  18:19/q24/p4 Naẓar can mean seeing or mental consideration. — reasons: Abu Hayyan states both senses.
18:19/q25 What does 'purest food' mean? [أَزْكَىٰ طَعَامًۭا · meaning] (turns on: Zakā can signify purity, increase, goodness or benefit; the context is disputed between lawful source, taste, quality, quantity and price.)
  18:19/q25/p1 Lawful, pure food, especially properly slaughtered meat from believers and free of idolatrous sacrifice. — reasons: Sa'id ibn Jubayr and Ibn Abbas are repeatedly cited; al-Farra and al-Zajjaj explain that many townspeople were Magians, while hidden believers could slaughter lawfully. Al-Tabari prefers lawful food over mere abundance regardless of seller's stock. Ibn Kathir invokes 24:21 and 87:14 for purity and prefers wholesome purity. Reports also mention slaughtered pigs. Al-Damghānī glosses azkā in the sleepers' request as 'more lawful.'
  18:19/q25/p2 The more abundant food, or the vendor with the most food. — reasons: Ikrima gives the gloss; Abu Ubayda cites the seven-versus-three tribes line, repeated by al-Tha'labi; Ibn Qutayba derives it from increase. Al-Tabari accepts this Arabic usage but says it does not decide the context and prefers lawful purity.
  18:19/q25/p3 The tastiest, most pleasant, or wholesome food. — reasons: Muqatil, al-Dahhak and al-Kalbi give taste; Ibn Kathir prefers a wholesome/pure sense supported by purification verses, while distinguishing it from mere quantity.
  18:19/q25/p4 The better or finest food in quality. — reasons: Qatada gives this repeatedly; Muqatil ibn Hayyan gives best quality. Alusi judges Qatada's reading best; Ibn Ashur argues zakā conveys increased goodness and benefit.
  18:19/q25/p5 The cheaper food or best value. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba lists low price; al-Mawardi, Ibn Jawzi and Yaman ibn Ri'ab give it, and al-Kashshaf, al-Nasafi, Baydawi and Bursevi include it.
  18:19/q25/p6 Lawful food specifically means food not stolen, extorted, usurped or bought with injustice. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj offers the source-of-property sense; Mujahid links it to an oppressive ruler's markets; al-Dahhak says much local property was seized. Abu Ishaq combines this with lawful slaughter; al-Wahidi states both.
  18:19/q25/p7 More blessed or fruitful food. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and al-Qurtubi preserve this gloss; Alusi says zakā's increase may be otherworldly reward from lawfulness or material benefit.
  18:19/q25/p8 A gloss transmitted as arfaḍ. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi ascribes the wording to Yaman b. Ri'ab without further explanation.
18:19/q26 What specific food did they seek or buy? [طَعَامًۭا · reports] (turns on: Reports name different foods while the verse keeps the noun general.)
  18:19/q26/p1 Lawfully slaughtered meat, perhaps from a concealed believer. — reasons: Ibn Abbas, Sa'id and al-Farra point to Magian or idolatrous slaughter; al-Kashifi says hidden believers could supply meat.
  18:19/q26/p2 Raisins. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya transmits this possibility; Abu Hayyan and Alusi list it among proposed foods.
  18:19/q26/p3 Dates. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya gives it as an alternative to raisins; Abu Hayyan and Alusi also list it.
  18:19/q26/p4 Rice. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan and Alusi list it.
  18:19/q26/p5 Enough food for several people, without a specified item. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi adds the group's quantity to the generic noun.
18:19/q27 What does 'bring you provision from it' mean? [فَلْيَأْتِكُم بِرِزْقٍۢ مِّنْهُ · interpretation] (turns on: The antecedent of minhu may be selected food or the coins; both yield provisions.)
  18:19/q27/p1 Bring them some of the selected food for them to eat. — reasons: Al-Tabari's report and al-Mawardi give food; al-Tha'labi, al-Nasafi and Ibn Ashur call it sustenance. Tabatabai prefers the antecedent 'food' because silver would require an extra omitted pronoun.
  18:19/q27/p2 Bring lawful food for them to eat. — reasons: Al-Mawardi records a more specific gloss; Alusi says the provision should be lawful on either pronoun analysis.
  18:19/q27/p3 The pronoun 'from it' can refer to their silver, the source or substitute for the provision. — reasons: Alusi allows min to be partitive, source marking or substitution; Tabatabai calls this remote because an additional pronoun must be assumed.
18:19/q28 What does 'let him act gently' require? [وَلْيَتَلَطَّفْ · interpretation] (turns on: The verb's gentleness is applied to concealment, buying, seller relations, or the food's delicacy.)
  18:19/q28/p1 Move, shop and return discreetly, carefully and courteously so their presence stays hidden. — reasons: Muqatil and Yahya stress secrecy; al-Tabari, al-Tha'labi, al-Baghawi, al-Razi and Ibn Kathir specify roads, entry and purchases. Abu Hayyan prefers concealment from context; Tabatabai specifies smooth dealing to prevent quarrels and rejects generic transaction caution. Alusi includes courtesy; al-Qushayri emphasizes kind dealing with the seller.
  18:19/q28/p2 Bargain or inspect carefully to avoid a high price or being cheated. — reasons: Al-Mawardi gives low-price bargaining; al-Kashshaf, al-Nasafi and Baydawi give avoiding deception. Tabatabai argues this broad transactional sense lacks support from the following secrecy clause.
  18:19/q28/p3 Deal gently with the seller without haggling or quarrelling. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi offers this alternative; al-Qushayri and Alusi stress courtesy, and Tabatabai says a quarrel could betray them.
  18:19/q28/p4 Choose delicate food suited to recently fasting bodies or refined spiritual people. — reasons: Alusi records the bodily and spiritual explanations but rejects making delicate food a rule, citing simple diet and clothing of the Prophet and major Companions.
18:19/q29 How are the commands 'let him look' and 'let him act gently' read? [فَلْيَنظُرْ وَلْيَتَلَطَّفْ · readings] (turns on: The variation is vowel on the imperative lām and an attested passive verb.)
  18:19/q29/p1 The command lām is quiescent in the common reading; kasra preserves its original vowel. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj allows kasra in falyatikum; al-Nahhas notes it in li-ya'tikum and li-yatalaṭṭaf; Ibn Atiyya reports al-Hasan's kasra
