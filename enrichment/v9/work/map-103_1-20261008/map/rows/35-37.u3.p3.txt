35:37/q37 What ethical rule follows from the general denial of help to wrongdoers? [فَمَا لِلظَّٰلِمِينَ مِن نَّصِيرٍ · law]
  35:37/q37/p1 Helping an oppressor means restraining the injustice, since aiding it violates justice. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr rejects the pre-Islamic maxim of assisting one's brother whether oppressor or victim and cites the Prophet's correction that the oppressor is helped by being restrained.
35:37/q38 How does the verse's long respite figure in an argument for the Qur'an's inimitability? [نُعَمِّرْكُم ٱلنَّذِيرُ · theology]
  35:37/q38/p1 The challengers had ample time, yet failed to match the Qur'an because its production lies beyond human capacity and ordinary speech conventions. — reasons: Al-Bāqillānī cites the verse's prolonged respite for the challengers. He argues that a prophetic miracle is God's act, so human inability is a figurative description; God made a match contrary to ordinary custom. Had eloquent Arabs possessed the ability, they would have answered, but their failure showed recognition that the Qur'an exceeded their mastered measures, poetic meters and pleasing speech forms.
35:37/q39 Why is the longer form يَتَذَكَّرُ apt for this lifetime of reflection? [يَتَذَكَّرُ · rhetoric]
  35:37/q39/p1 Its length suits reflection extended through a lifetime, while a geminated alternative suggests a forceful reminder that startles the heart. — reasons: Al-Sāmarrāʾī contrasts yatadhakkar with yadhdhakkar: the longer form suits extended remembering, as at 79:35 and 89:23; the geminated form conveys a stronger awakening, compared with 8:57, 9:126 and 17:41.
35:37/q40 What Arabic ending patterns provide grammatical parallels for نَعْمَلْ and نَّصِيرٍ? [نَعْمَلْ نَّصِيرٍ · grammar]
  35:37/q40/p1 Poetic rhyme may alter final vowels or long letters, while hesitant ordinary speech may lengthen an ending or add kasra before resuming. — reasons: Sībawayh says poets may add a rhyme yāʾ after hāʾ, retain an alif as al-Khalīl recites, drop a wāw, or vocalize a still or jussive rhyme ending for meter, especially with kasra in genitive rhyme; he cites lines of Imruʾ al-Qays and Ṭarafa. In ordinary hesitation he reports lengthening after qāla and yaqūlu and kasra after qad or with tanwin, as in sayf-ni, to keep the utterance open. Sībawayh says sung rhyme prolongs endings with alif, yāʾ or wāw, with or without tanwīn, matching the preceding vowel because poetry is made for singing; he illustrates different inflections with lines of Imruʾ al-Qays (manzilī), Yazīd ibn al-Thaʿthariyya (maṣraʿā), al-Aʿshā (lāʾimū) and Jarīr (al-ʿitābā, al-khiyāmū, al-ayyāmā). He reports that Hijaz reciters retain these endings even without melody to mark verse, many Tamīmīs replace the length letter with nūn, as in al-ʿAjjāj's al-dharfan and annahajan, and a third practice drops prolongation, as in Jarīr's al-ʿitāb and al-Akhṭal's faʿal. When the preceding consonant is the rhyme, final yāʾ and wāw may be treated like appended length letters and even a weak radical may be omitted; al-Khalīl distinguishes yāʾ and wāw retained as the rhyme letter itself in yaqḍī and yaghzū, as qāf is in his cited line. Sībawayh says alif remains in yakhshā and yarḍā as in ordinary speech, unlike deletable yāʾ; al-Khalīl cites poetry retaining the alif of ṭayrā like taqaḍḍā and deleting the wāw of taqaddamū. Sībawayh says many of Qays and Asad even omit pronominal yāʾ and wāw, less often because they convey noun meaning; he cites omitted plural endings in ṣanaʿ, qanaʿ, jamaʿ and Ibn Muqbil's awjaf, and feminine endings in ʿAntara's takallam and al-Khazzāz ibn Lawdhān's idhhab, while the hāʾ in shattā ṭarāʾiquhu is retained because it is not a weak or length letter. He explains that a still or jussive rhyme ending can be moved and lengthened as poetic license, requiring kasra in genitive rhyme lest nominative or accusative rhyme be iqwāʾ; lines of Imruʾ al-Qays and Ṭarafa and Abū al-Najm's rajaz ending in ḥulī and maskanah illustrate this. In ordinary hesitation, Sībawayh adds examples of lengthening a final vowel (qālā, yaqūlū, al-ʿām) and using kasra on a final consonant (qadī, alī, sayfnī) to keep speech open while recollecting.
