18:19/q12 What does deferring their duration to God express? [رَبُّكُمْ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا لَبِثْتُمْ · theology] (turns on: The ordinary explanation concerns epistemic humility; the inward account treats time itself differently.)
  18:19/q12/p1 They acknowledge that only God knows the exact, long duration, and end an unresolvable dispute. — reasons: Muqatil says Maksalmina returns the matter to God; al-Qushayri says God placed them in that state; al-Kashshaf says signs or inspiration may have exposed their ignorance; al-Biqai calls it faithful fellowship; Tabatabai contrasts human inference from indicators with God's direct encompassing knowledge (96:5; 2:32). Sībawayh explains the hope in 20:44 as belonging to Moses and Aaron, whose knowledge of the outcome was limited; this supplies a parallel for speech limited to human knowledge.
  18:19/q12/p2 A leader urged them to stop disagreement because it can ruin a community. — reasons: Al-Nahhas transmits Ibn Abbas through Sa'id b. Jubayr; al-Durr repeats the leader's warning.
  18:19/q12/p3 Some spiritually realized sleepers understood that the interval had no independent reality. — reasons: Asad connects this to 18:12 and death and resurrection.
18:19/q13 How many youths does the dialogue imply? [قَالَ قَآئِلٌۭ مِّنْهُمْ قَالُوا۟ · interpretation]
  18:19/q13/p1 At least seven: one asks and two plural replies require three speakers each. — reasons: Al-Nasafi attributes the inference to Ibn Abbas; Tabatabai gives the same count from the singular and two plurals.
18:19/q14 Why do they turn from duration to sending for food? [فَٱبْعَثُوٓا۟ أَحَدَكُم · interpretation] (turns on: Both hunger and fear shape the errand.)
  18:19/q14/p1 They woke hungry and redirected an unknowable discussion to an immediate need. — reasons: Al-Tabari says they needed food; al-Kashshaf, Abu Hayyan and Ibn Ashur call the pivot wiser than continuing debate; al-Qushayri says hunger followed a period without bodily appetites; Alusi points to fa as marking the redirect. Al-Rāzī's general account distinguishes ends desired in themselves from means, assigns appetite its own pleasure, and says perceived pleasure draws action unless a stronger consideration intervenes; his examples include tasty food avoided for harm and immediate pain accepted to escape worse harm.
  18:19/q14/p2 The errand also let the envoy learn news about their pursuers. — reasons: Al-Tabari and al-Tha'labi report instructions to buy more food and investigate; they still believed Decianus sought them.
18:19/q15 Who was sent to the city? [أَحَدَكُم · referent] (turns on: Narrative reports name the envoy; the verse leaves him unnamed.)
  18:19/q15/p1 Yamlikha, also called Tamlikha, was the envoy. — reasons: Muqatil names Yamliha; al-Tabari transmits Muqatil and narrative forms; al-Tha'labi, al-Baghawi, al-Nasafi and others give variants. Al-Ghaznawi calls him the youngest; al-Wahidi has their leader Tamlikha speak earlier. Al-Damghānī names the envoy Malyakhā; al-Itqān names him Tamlikhā.
  18:19/q15/p2 An unnamed member of their group went; the command need not identify him. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says the group may address an unspecified delegate or omit the known name; the Quran's 'one of you' emphasizes membership.
  18:19/q15/p3 The wording 'one of you' avoids suggesting a specially honored chief. — reasons: Ibn al-Anbari contrasts aḥad with wāḥid; Alusi says the latter might imply their chief. Abū Ḥātim supplies wider lexical reasons for the choice of aḥad over wāḥid: its scope, gender invariance, collective possibility and restricted arithmetic use.
18:19/q16 How does 'with your silver' attach to the envoy? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ · grammar]
  18:19/q16/p1 It is circumstantial to 'one of you': the envoy goes carrying the silver. — reasons: Al-Samin describes the prepositional phrase as a circumstantial qualifier.
18:19/q17 What occupations do reports give the youths? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ · reports]
  18:19/q17/p1 They were money changers. — reasons: Abu Ja'far's report calls them money changers; al-Sadiq distinguishes changers in speech from dealers in coins.
18:19/q18 What is 'your silver'? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the noun denotes silver generally or minted coins in its lexical scope.)
  18:19/q18/p1 Silver, whether struck as dirhams or unminted; in context it was their money. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba and later authors cite the Arfaja silver-nose report to show unminted silver is included. Al-Asma'i's paper-leaf explanation is dismissed by Alusi. Al-Mawardi distinguishes a fatḥa livestock sense attested in poetry. Biqai develops a lexical family involving foliage, beauty, wealth and gathering; Islahi accordingly translates 'currency.'
  18:19/q18/p2 Specifically coined silver dirhams. — reasons: The wording and old coin reports support a struck currency; Ibn Ashur calls it a minted coin; several authors describe the king's stamped image. Alusi reports this narrower common explanation.
18:19/q19 How is bi-wariqikum read? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ · readings] (turns on: The variants concern vowel easing and the qāf-kāf contact; sources disagree on which assimilations are sound.)
  18:19/q19/p1 Fatḥa on wāw and sukūn on rāʾ lightens pronunciation. — reasons: Al-Farra names Asim and al-A'mash; most Kufans/Basrans, Abu Amr, Hamza and Abu Bakr are often named. Ibn Khalawayh, al-Nahhas and Qiraat-Er explain avoiding a heavy sequence of vowels. Ibn al-Jazarī names Abū ʿAmr, Ḥamza, Khalaf, Abū Bakr and Rawḥ as leaving the rāʾ of biwariqikum unvocalized.
  18:19/q19/p2 Assimilation of qāf into kāf, sometimes with a still rāʾ, is transmitted. — reasons: Al-Tabari reports a Meccan form; al-Zajjaj permits it and al-Farisi explains movement from the rear qāf to nearer kāf. Abu Hatim transmits Ibn Muhaysin; Ibn Jinni says possible, yet calls Abu Raja's form concealed articulation rather than full assimilation because full assimilation transfers the vowel. Abu Hayyan challenges a reported attribution to Ibn Kathir. Ibn al-Jazarī reports two valid treatments of a quiescent qāf before kāf: Makkī retains its emphatic quality, while al-Dānī assimilates fully; Ibn al-Jazarī favors the latter by analogy with a moving qāf and warns against weakening an unassimilated qāf. He limits within-word qāf-to-kāf assimilation to forms with a moving preceding letter and following plural mīm.
  18:19/q19/p3 Fatḥa on wāw and kasra on rāʾ, with qāf pronounced, is the common or original reading. — reasons: Al-Tabari prefers the original kasra pattern but accepts alternatives; Ibn Khalawayh calls kasra original. Ibn Mujahid, al-Farisi, al-Qurtubi and others name Ibn Kathir, Nafi, Ibn Amir, al-Kisa'i and Hafs; al-Tabari lists Medinan and some Iraqi readers. The forms are Arabic dialect variants with the same sense. Ibn al-Jazarī explicitly lists biwariqikum among forms where qāf is pronounced distinctly, because its preceding letter is quiescent; he reports kasra on rāʾ for the remaining readers.
  18:19/q19/p4 Kasra on wāw, with varying rāʾ and assimilation, is a rare dialectal reading. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj judges kasra wāw least acceptable; al-Farra compares kibid and kalima. Ibn Jinni treats Abu Raja's form as concealed articulation; Zamakhshari and Razi object to two consecutive still letters in Ibn Muhaysin's form; Abu Hayyan cites a nonassimilated Zajjaj form.
  18:19/q19/p5 Ali reads bawāriqikum, a collective form relating to silver. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and al-Samin describe its pattern; Alusi mentions it.
18:19/q20 What does the demonstrative 'this' add to the silver? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ هَٰذِهِۦٓ · rhetoric]
  18:19/q20/p1 It points to money at hand, perhaps held out or handed to the envoy. — reasons: Bursevi infers a companion physically handed it over; Alusi says the speaker held it out. Tabatabai says the unusually emphatic mention foreshadows how the aged coin exposed them. Sībawayh observes that an identified person may be indicated by a bare proper name when a visible sign reveals him, as if 'this' were uttered; this supplies a general Arabic parallel for demonstrative pointing.
18:19/q21 What were the coins like and where had they come from? [بِوَرِقِكُمْ هَٰذِهِۦٓ · reports] (turns on: Narratives add the old ruler's imprint to the verse's bare mention of silver.)
  18:19/q21/p1 They carried old coins from the reign they fled, often bearing the persecuting king's image. — reasons: Al-Wahidi and Ibn Abbas say the stamped image was the old king's; al-Tabari dates the coins 309 years earlier; al-Tha'labi says Daqyanus' seal. Ibn Kathir says they brought and spent money when fleeing; Abu Hayyan says they brought it for expenses. Ibn Abbas likens the currency to camel hooves; later reports call the ruler Decius.
  18:19/q21/p2 Their coin was silver but its exact minting is not specified by the verse. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, al-Kashshaf and al-Razi allow coined and uncoined silver; al-Wahidi cites the old stamped-coin narrative as a separate report.
