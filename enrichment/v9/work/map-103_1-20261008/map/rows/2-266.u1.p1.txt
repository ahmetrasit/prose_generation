2:266/q16 Who are the weak offspring, and why mention them? [وَلَهُۥ ذُرِّيَّةٌۭ ضُعَفَآءُ · interpretation]
  2:266/q16/p1 Young, helpless dependents cannot work, support him or restore the orchard, intensifying his care and the loss. — reasons: Ibn Abbas calls them part of the parable. Al-Mawardi and Ibn al-Jawzi note a parent's special tenderness; al-Nasafi calls the garden their livelihood too. Al-Tabatabai says an elder with strong children could rely on them, unlike this owner. Ibn Ashur says daif can mean young, limited or poor, citing 2:282 and Abu Khalid al-Attabi's verse; Abu Hayyan also allows needy dependents. Bursavi and al-Alusi stress inability to earn.
2:266/q17 What forms are reported for the plural weak? [ضُعَفَآءُ · readings] (turns on: Equivalent plural forms and their readings.)
  2:266/q17/p1 Duafa is the verse form, a regular plural of daif. — reasons: Al-Akhfash compares fail plurals such as zurafa and zuraf. Al-Tabari compares 4:9; Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi recognize both plural patterns.
  2:266/q17/p2 Di'af or du'af is another regular plural form or variant reading. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf notes a weak offspring reading; al-Tabari cites 4:9 for diafa, while al-Akhfash, Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi give the alternate plural patterns.
2:266/q18 How is the clause about offspring attached? [وَلَهُۥ ذُرِّيَّةٌۭ ضُعَفَآءُ · grammar]
  2:266/q18/p1 It is circumstantial, describing the owner when old age strikes. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and al-Nasafi take the waw as circumstantial; al-Samin derives the circumstance from the pronoun in asabahu.
2:266/q19 What is an i'sar? [إِعْصَارٌۭ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the word itself is a general rotating wind, a hot samum, or a cold blast.)
  2:266/q19/p1 A fierce, rotating wind or whirlwind rising like a column, often lifting dust. — reasons: Al-Tabari gives its plural a'asir and cites Yazid ibn Mufarrigh; al-Zajjaj calls it zawba'a and cites an unnamed poet. Al-Thalabi cites al-Kumayt and Yazid ibn al-Muqri; Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Razi, al-Tabrisi, al-Samin and Ibn Abbas in al-Durr cite poetry or the wind proverb. Al-Jawhari adds umm zawba'a and says the name also belongs to a jinn chief. Al-Alusi describes colliding air currents, possible upward or downward motion and force enough to uproot trees or snatch boats. Al-Tabatabai describes a twisting dust column between earth and sky.
  2:266/q19/p2 It is specifically the deadly hot wind called samum. — reasons: Al-Suddi gives this gloss through al-Mawardi; Ibn Atiyya calls the whirlwind violent and scorching. Ibn Zayd and al-Suddi in al-Qurtubi describe the hot wind. Ibn Ashur identifies a violent hot wind likened to fire or samum.
  2:266/q19/p3 It is a cold blast or frost. — reasons: Al-Hasan gives this second gloss in Ibn Abi Hatim; al-Hasan and al-Dahhak elsewhere describe cold within the whirlwind.
2:266/q20 Why is the whirlwind called i'sar? [إِعْصَارٌۭ · meaning] (turns on: Which observed squeezing or twisting motivates the word; Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi disagree about the cloth derivation.)
  2:266/q20/p1 Its coiling motion resembles cloth being wrung. — reasons: Al-Mawardi cites a poem; al-Mahdawi gives the derivation in Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi. Ibn Atiyya calls it weak, while al-Qurtubi endorses it as observable; al-Samin, al-Alusi and al-Tabatabai also mention twisting cloth.
  2:266/q20/p2 The wind squeezes or compresses clouds or bodies. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya cites the wind proverb and relates mu'sirat either to pregnant rain clouds or clouds compressed by winds; he reports al-Anbari and Ibn Sida's alternatives, the latter taking mu'sirat as winds. Al-Qurtubi repeats the derivations. Abu Hayyan describes a hot cloud-compressing wind; al-Harali links the name to squeezing hidden contents.
2:266/q21 What is the fire in the whirlwind? [فِيهِ نَارٌۭ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the destructive fire is heat, cold, actual flame, lightning, or Hell's breath.)
  2:266/q21/p1 Scorching samum or exceptionally hot wind. — reasons: Ibn Abbas repeatedly calls it samum; Qatada calls the wind a carrier of it and al-Suddi distinguishes wind from its fire. Al-Tabari also transmits Ibn Abbas that jinn-creating samum is one seventieth of fire; Ibn Atiyya attributes this proportion to Ibn Masud and identifies the Fire as the afterlife's, while Abu Hayyan repeats it. Ibn Ashur says the wind is emphatically likened to fire. Al-Alusi says the indefinite fire magnifies it.
  2:266/q21/p2 Biting cold, frost or hail that burns and ruins the garden. — reasons: Al-Hasan gives bitter cold in Abd al-Razzaq and severe cold and hail in al-Tabari; al-Dahhak agrees. Al-Harali calls a cold edge a kind of fire.
  2:266/q21/p3 It may be literal fire carried by the wind. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya allows literal fire, and al-Tabrisi states that the wind's fire burns the garden.
  2:266/q21/p4 It may be lightning. — reasons: Abu Hayyan records this alternate explanation.
  2:266/q21/p5 Its heat or cold may be a breath of Hell. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya cites a Prophetic report about intense heat and the Fire's complaint; al-Qurtubi reports his view and the prayer-delaying report.
2:266/q22 How is fihi nar parsed? [فِيهِ نَارٌۭ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the locative itself modifies the whirlwind or belongs to a modifying clause.)
  2:266/q22/p1 Fihi is a modifying locative phrase and nar is its raised subject. — reasons: Al-Nasafi says the locative functions as an adjective of i'sar. Abu Hayyan says the masculine pronoun agrees with masculine i'sar. Al-Samin prefers this direct adjectival parse for simplicity.
  2:266/q22/p2 Fihi is the predicate of nar, and the entire clause modifies i'sar. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and al-Samin allow this analysis; al-Samin prefers the simpler direct locative adjective.
2:266/q23 What do the successive fa and burning verb convey? [فَأَصَابَهَآ فَٱحْتَرَقَتْ · grammar]
  2:266/q23/p1 The storm strikes after the garden has become useful, then the garden burns up as the intransitive result of burning it. — reasons: Abu Hayyan reads the successive fa as sequence after ripening; he and al-Samin distinguish intransitive ihtaraqa from transitive ahraqa. Al-Samin gives the burning then burned-up sequence; both cite the expression harqa nab and a poetic line with nominative or accusative nab. Ibn Abi Hatim reports Ibn Abbas's direct gloss that the garden itself burned. Ibn Kathir says fruit and trees were consumed. Abu al-Baqa instead coordinates the storm clause with the earlier adjectival garden description.
2:266/q24 What does thus refer back to and what are the signs explained? [كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ ٱللَّهُ لَكُمُ ٱلْءَايَٰتِ · referent] (turns on: How broad the backward reference is.)
  2:266/q24/p1 The preceding parables and disclosures, including spending, Abraham and the ruined town, clarify religious proofs. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj includes charity and struggle and says these establish oneness, messengership, reward and punishment. Al-Wahidi cites 2:258-259 for oneness; al-Tabrisi includes all preceding disclosures. Ibn Atiyya says thus points to the parables just described. Bursavi includes striving, spending, Abraham and Ezra. Al-Nasafi speaks of monotheism and religion. Ibn Ashur says concrete images bring abstract truths near, citing 2:143; Abu Hayyan lists allusion, simile, ellipsis, specification, parable and metaphor.
  2:266/q24/p2 It points specifically to what has just been mentioned. — reasons: Sa'id ibn Jubayr gives this gloss; al-Samin says the closing phrase was discussed earlier.
2:266/q25 What reflection does the closing appeal seek? [لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَفَكَّرُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Which lesson of the parable the reflective command highlights.)
  2:266/q25/p1 Understand the parables, take warning or guidance, and act or obey accordingly. — reasons: Mujahid glosses reflection as obedience; Qatada cites 29:43 that the learned understand parables. Al-Mawardi gives taking a lesson or finding guidance, while Ibn Kathir and al-Durr urge application. Al-Biqai and al-Harali say worship needs thought about beginnings and outcomes; a sage reverses the sequence of thought and work. Elmalili defines thought as arranging known matters to discover unknown ones and says to draw analogies and act. Ibn Atiyya explains perhaps as human hope that reflection prepares a person for thought.
  2:266/q25/p2 Think about the world's passing and the afterlife's permanence, then turn toward the latter. — reasons: Ibn Abbas contrasts the two realms; Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi draw the practical consequence of seeking what lasts rather than grieving over worldly loss.
  2:266/q25/p3 Turn back to God's majesty and lordship rather than taking other protectors. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi gives this further application.
2:266/q26 What does perhaps imply about God's intention? [لَعَلَّكُمْ · theology] (turns on: How a particle of hope is understood when spoken by God.)
  2:266/q26/p1 It expresses hope from the human perspective, since reflection may make one fit to understand. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya explains the phrasing this way.
  2:266/q26/p2 It implies that God intends all people to believe. — reasons: Al-Razi reports this Mu'tazilite argument from the apparent hope particle and notes that hope is inappropriate to God.
