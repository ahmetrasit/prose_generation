12:36/q14 Did both prisoners actually have the dreams they recounted? [إِنِّىٓ أَرَىٰنِىٓ وَقَالَ ٱلْءَاخَرُ إِنِّىٓ أَرَىٰنِىٓ · reports] (turns on: The transmitted accounts conflict; requests for interpretation and arānī are offered as evidence for genuine dreams.)
  12:36/q14/p1 Both truly dreamed. — reasons: Ibn Ishaq and Mujahid report actual dreams, and Ibn Abbas describes troubled waking; al-Zajjaj and al-Wahidi argue that requesting interpretation implies dreams because waking sight needs none. Al-Razi adds that arānī would otherwise be unnecessary. Ibn Kathir prefers this as the majority view.
  12:36/q14/p2 Neither dreamed; both invented accounts to test Joseph. — reasons: Ibn Masud, al-Suddi and al-Shaʿbi report fabrication; Mujahid also relates their later claim that they were joking, after which Joseph said the matter was settled. Al-Durr gives the subsequent interpretations and request to remember Joseph. Baghawi notes contrary reports.
  12:36/q14/p3 Only the baker, later crucified, lied; the cupbearer dreamed truthfully. — reasons: Abu Mijlaz and an Imam report identify the crucified man as the liar; Bursevi repeats this resolution.
12:36/q15 How does the first man's dream scene unfold? [إِنِّىٓ أَرَىٰنِىٓ أَعْصِرُ خَمْرًۭا · reports] (turns on: Reports vary in whether the vine was planted and in details of its branches and clusters.)
  12:36/q15/p1 He pressed grapes from three clusters into the king's cup and served him. — reasons: Muqatil, Mujahid and later transmitters give three clusters, sometimes on a three-branched vine or in a garden; Ibn Abbas also reports the cup for the king. Bursevi describes the service.
  12:36/q15/p2 He planted a vine, pressed its grapes, and served the king. — reasons: Ikrima adds that Joseph foretold release after three days and resumed wine service.
  12:36/q15/p3 The vision matches the cupbearer's ordinary occupation: pressing grapes for wine. — reasons: Al-Razi notes both visions mirror the men's professions; Study Quran connects this vision with the wine carrier and 12:41.
12:36/q16 How does 'I see myself' mark a dream rather than waking action? [أَرَىٰنِىٓ · rhetoric]
  12:36/q16/p1 It recounts a past dream scene; the sleep setting is understood. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj says sleep need not be named; Ibn al-Anbari says simple 'I press' would otherwise suffice. Al-Razi cites arānī plus the request for interpretation. Al-Tabataba'i notes the break before 'one of them said' separates the dream scene from prison entry.
12:36/q17 How does the verb 'I see myself' take its objects? [أَرَىٰنِىٓ · grammar] (turns on: Whether aṣiru fills a second object slot or states the dreamer's circumstance.)
  12:36/q17/p1 As a cognition verb, it allows subject and object pronouns to refer to the same person. — reasons: Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi contrast ordinary verbs, which need a separate reflexive expression; al-Samin adds that causative hamza yields a third object, citing 8:43.
  12:36/q17/p2 'I press' is the second object of the dream cognition verb. — reasons: Al-Samin reports this parsing.
  12:36/q17/p3 'I press' is a circumstantial clause. — reasons: Al-Samin reports this alternative.
12:36/q18 How is the yāʾ of the two 'I see myself' forms read? [أَرَىٰنِىٓ · readings] (turns on: Length of the final yāʾ in recitation.)
  12:36/q18/p1 The standard form has a short final yāʾ. — reasons: Qiraat-ER records the standard at both occurrences.
  12:36/q18/p2 A canonical variant extends the final yāʾ. — reasons: Qiraat-ER records the extension at both occurrences. Ibn al-Jazarī lists the two dream phrases among Yūsuf's open yāʾs and names the Medinan readers, Ibn Kathīr, and Abū ʿAmr in the relevant lists.
12:36/q19 How is the yāʾ of the two 'indeed I' forms read? [إِنِّىٓ · readings] (turns on: Length of the final yāʾ in recitation.)
  12:36/q19/p1 The standard form ends in a short yāʾ. — reasons: Qiraat-ER records this at both occurrences.
  12:36/q19/p2 A canonical recitational variant extends the yāʾ. — reasons: Qiraat-ER records this at both occurrences. Ibn al-Jazarī counts the two innī arānī expressions among the sūra's yāʾs before open hamza and records their opening among the Yūsuf forms.
12:36/q20 What does 'press' mean here? [أَعْصِرُ · meaning]
  12:36/q20/p1 It means squeezing something moist to extract its liquid. — reasons: Al-Layth distinguishes ʿaṣartu, doing it oneself, from iʿtaṣartu, having another do it; ʿuṣāra is extracted liquid. Al-Jishumi compares rain-bearing muʿṣirāt; Ibn ʿAshur explains ʿaṣīr as the product. Al-Biqaʿi likewise defines extraction. Ibn ʿAzīz glosses aʿṣiru as extract. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ traces ʿaṣr forms to pressure and extraction, comparing the vine, harvest year, rain clouds, whirlwind, and al-ʿAṣr.
12:36/q21 What does 'wine' mean as the thing pressed, and how is the wording explained? [أَعْصِرُ خَمْرًۭا · meaning] (turns on: Whether khamr directly names grapes, anticipates wine, abbreviates a construction, or names the intended product.)
  12:36/q21/p1 It means grapes in a dialect, especially Oman or Azd Oman. — reasons: Al-Asmaʿi's Bedouin called carried grapes khamr; al-Dahhak says the Quran uses several peoples' speech. Some name Ghassan and Azd Oman, while Ibn al-Qasim says Quraysh knew it. This explains the object directly. Al-Zarkashī relays Ibn Durayd through al-Fārisī on the Azd Oman lexical usage and a view denying metaphor on that basis; al-Suyūṭī reports al-Ḍaḥḥāk's Omani gloss.
  12:36/q21/p2 Grapes are called wine by anticipating what they will become. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj compares naming a thing by its eventual product; al-Wahidi cites cooking bricks from clay and molasses or juice. Ibn al-Anbari and many commentators take this as the common explanation. Al-Sijistani's separate evidence notes masculine khamr among eloquent speakers. Ibn Jinnī treats wine as the grapes' effect, named in place of its cause; al-Zarkashī describes the grapes by their future wine, and al-Suyūṭī compares other things named by their future state.
  12:36/q21/p3 An omitted noun is understood: grapes intended for wine. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj and later authors supply the missing grapes; al-Qurtubi calls it an omitted genitive relation and adds khamra/khamr/khumur inflection.
  12:36/q21/p4 Wine itself may be called pressed because grapes are pressed for its sake. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba compares saying 'I pressed oil' for olives; Ibn ʿAtiyya and Abu Hayyan permit the goal of pressing as its object. Al-Alusi says this differs from the common future-product explanation. Al-Zarkashī reports Ibn ʿAzīz's proposal that the transferred usage lies in the verb aʿṣiru, meaning to extract.
12:36/q22 What reading says 'grapes' in place of 'wine,' and what status does it have? [خَمْرًۭا · readings] (turns on: Whether the reported substitution is recited text or explanatory wording.)
  12:36/q22/p1 Ibn Masud and Ubayy are reported to read 'I press grapes.' — reasons: Al-Tabari and others cite Ibn Masud; Ibn ʿAtiyya and Abu Hayyan add Ubayy. Al-Durr says Ibn Masud swore the Prophet gave him this wording. The reading makes grapes explicit. The Muḥtasab preface specifically notes Ibn Jinnī's defense of Ibn ʿAbbās's ʿinaban wording.
  12:36/q22/p2 The 'grapes' wording is an explanatory gloss, not a canonical recitation. — reasons: Abu Hayyan argues the mutawatir reading for both is khamr and ʿinab conflicts with the codex; al-Samin says the gloss does not establish synonymy, comparing the explanatory tharīd. Al-Alusi agrees.
12:36/q23 What did the baker see above his head? [أَحْمِلُ فَوْقَ رَأْسِى خُبْزًۭا تَأْكُلُ ٱلطَّيْرُ مِنْهُ · reports] (turns on: Whether the report specifies three baskets, general bread, or tharīd.)
  12:36/q23/p1 Three baskets of bread or assorted food were above him, and birds ate from the upper one. — reasons: Muqatil mentions a top dish; others mention predatory birds. Reports vary between bread, mixed food and the exact basket arrangement; the upper basket is the one eaten. Ibn Abbas and Mujahid give the same overall scene.
  12:36/q23/p2 The scene is bread carried over his head and pecked or torn by birds. — reasons: Al-Baydawi and al-Tabataba'i keep the verse's simple detail; al-Razi says the scene matches the baker's trade. Al-Wajiz specifies birds of prey. Al-Dāmaghānī cites the bread above the head as an instance of literal carrying.
  12:36/q23/p3 The food may specifically have been tharīd. — reasons: Ibn ʿAtiyya cites Ibn Masud's codex; Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi treat its wording as an explanatory gloss, not a canonical reading.
  12:36/q23/p4 The mention of bread anticipates the grain that birds actually eat. — reasons: Al-Zarkashī says the dreamer names bread although birds eat grain, by naming it for what the grain will become.
12:36/q24 What does 'above' mean in the baker's dream? [فَوْقَ · meaning]
  12:36/q24/p1 It means on or at the top. — reasons: Muqatil cites 8:12 and 14:26 for uppermost sense; Bursevi cites 8:12's 'above the necks.' Al-Dāmaghānī explicitly gives physical elevation above the head as a sense of fawq.
12:36/q25 How is the baker's bread clause constructed? [أَحْمِلُ فَوْقَ رَأْسِى خُبْزًۭا تَأْكُلُ ٱلطَّيْرُ مِنْهُ · grammar]
  12:36/q25/p1 'Birds eat from it' describes the bread; 'above' may attach to carrying or stand circumstantially with bread. — reasons: Al-Samin gives both attachments for fawq.
12:36/q26 What does 'bread' denote here? [خُبْزًۭا · meaning]
  12:36/q26/p1 Prepared baked flour and water, a loaf. — reasons: Ibn ʿAshur defines the food; al-Biqaʿi calls it prepared food.
