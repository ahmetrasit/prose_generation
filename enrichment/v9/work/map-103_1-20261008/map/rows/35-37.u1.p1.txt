35:37/q14 What related reports describe ordinary human lifespans? [نُعَمِّرْكُم · reports] (turns on: The transmitted numbers and, for the sixty-to-seventy report, differing chains and assessments.)
  35:37/q14/p1 Most of the Prophet's community live or die between sixty and seventy, with few exceeding it. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Jishumī, al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Biqāʿī and al-Bursawī transmit this. Ibn Kathīr records a route he calls weak, another graded ḥasan gharīb by al-Tirmidhī, and al-Bazzār's warning that a route through ʿUthmān ibn Maṭar is weak.
  35:37/q14/p2 The community's span is fifty to sixty, with a prayer of mercy for those reaching seventy or eighty. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr transmits Ḥudhayfa's question and the Prophet's reply.
  35:37/q14/p3 Some physicians give a natural lifespan of 120 years, with growth until sixty and decline thereafter. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr adds a poet's illustration of loss of youthful pleasure at sixty.
  35:37/q14/p4 The Prophet most famously lived sixty-three years; other reports give sixty or sixty-five. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr records and ranks these reported ages.
35:37/q15 What other reported admonitions mark stages of life? [نُعَمِّرْكُم · reports]
  35:37/q15/p1 A caller warns at forty that harvest nears, at sixty to account for one's deeds, and at seventy to come to reckoning. — reasons: Al-Bursawī transmits this daily and nightly call.
  35:37/q15/p2 Worshippers intensified their devotion at forty or at the appearance of gray hair. — reasons: Al-Bursawī describes Companions and successors increasing night prayer, reducing social contact and leaving beds, while noting that death's unknown time makes no age safe.
35:37/q16 How is مَن تَذَكَّرَ read? [مَّا يَتَذَكَّرُ فِيهِ مَن تَذَكَّرَ · readings] (turns on: Al-Aʿmash's assimilation and the pronounced linking hamza distinguish the alternate reading.)
  35:37/q16/p1 The majority reads mā yatadhakkaru fīhi man tadhakkar. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān name the majority reading.
  35:37/q16/p2 Al-Aʿmash reads an assimilated form, reported as mā yudhakkaru or mā yadhkur with man adhkar or udhkira. — reasons: The reports vary in transliteration and vocalization; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī say it comes from idhkar and note the unusual pronunciation of hamzat al-waṣl in connected recitation; al-Samīn also records the assimilation and the Shaykh's observation.
35:37/q17 Who or what is ٱلنَّذِيرُ? [ٱلنَّذِيرُ · referent] (turns on: Whether the warning is delivered by a messenger or scripture, or embodied in life signs and reason; proponents of the Messenger cite other verses and binding scriptural proof, while gray-hair proponents cite usage, poetry and signs of death.)
  35:37/q17/p1 Muhammad, God's messenger, came as the warner. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Zayd cite 53:56; al-Wāḥidī cites 33:45 and 48:8; Ibn Jarīr as reported by Ibn Kathīr cites 43:77–78, 17:15 and 67:8–9. Al-Qurṭubī says prophetic warning ends excuses, citing 4:165 and 17:15. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the title highlights the relevant aspect of Muhammad's mission. Al-Zajjāj, al-Jishumī, al-Wāḥidī and Ibn Jarīr prefer this reading; al-Jishumī calls it literal. Al-Thaʿlabī, al-Baghawī and al-Bursawī say most commentators hold it.
  35:37/q17/p2 Gray hair warns of old age and approaching death. — reasons: ʿIkrima, Sufyān ibn ʿUyayna, Wakīʿ and others transmit this. Al-Qurṭubī says gray hair shows youth's play is past. Poets cited by al-Thaʿlabī, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Ṭabrisī, al-Qurṭubī and al-Ālūsī call it a warning; a transmitted saying makes each white hair warn its neighbor. Al-Kāshifī likens its whiteness to a life-flame waning. Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it good but says binding proof comes through scriptural warning. The notices differ on whether al-Ṭabarī reports or holds it.
  35:37/q17/p3 The Qur'an itself is the warner. — reasons: Al-Thaʿlabī attributes this to Zayd ibn ʿAlī, as do al-Jishumī and al-Ṭabrisī; other sources report it among the alternatives.
  35:37/q17/p4 Prophets generally are the warners, each to his own people and Muhammad to the later world. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān take the word generically; al-Ālūsī says the plural reading supports this. Al-Biqāʿī adds that messengers and revealed books reinforce what reason alone could not establish.
  35:37/q17/p5 Deaths of relatives, friends, or neighbors warn the living of their own death. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī cites two poems on repeated departures of companions; al-Bursawī and several others list deaths among the signs.
  35:37/q17/p6 Mature reason or intellectual discernment itself warns a person. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī says it distinguishes reality and moral differences and directs work toward the afterlife. Al-Bayḍāwī and al-Bursawī include reason, while al-Ṭabrisī and al-Ālūsī give completed maturity.
  35:37/q17/p7 Fever warns that death is approaching. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī cites a Prophetic description of fever as death's forerunner and al-Azharī's explanation that it is death's messenger.
  35:37/q17/p8 Any sign of departure from life can count as a warner. — reasons: Al-Kawāshī, cited by al-Bursawī, extends the expression to every such sign and calls for heedfulness.
  35:37/q17/p9 The warning is the subsidiary branches of religious rulings. — reasons: Elmalılı gives this explanation.
  35:37/q17/p10 The word means warning itself, as a verbal noun. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī includes 'warning' among the lexical possibilities, and Abū ʿAlī says a verbal noun is preferable if the referent is gray hair.
35:37/q18 How does ٱلنَّذِيرُ work grammatically for different referents? [ٱلنَّذِيرُ · grammar] (turns on: The grammatical form tracks whether the referent is a person or an impersonal sign.)
  35:37/q18/p1 It is an active participle when it names Muhammad. — reasons: Abū ʿAlī, cited by al-Wāḥidī, distinguishes the personal referent.
  35:37/q18/p2 It is better taken as a verbal noun when it names gray hair. — reasons: Abū ʿAlī makes this grammatical distinction because gray hair is a warning rather than a personal warner.
35:37/q19 How is وَجَآءَكُمُ ٱلنَّذِيرُ connected to the question before it? [وَجَآءَكُمُ ٱلنَّذِيرُ · grammar] (turns on: Whether coordination follows the rhetorical question's asserted meaning or the verb within the question.)
  35:37/q19/p1 It coordinates with the affirmative sense of the preceding rhetorical question: 'We gave you life, and a warner came.' — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān cites 28:18 and 94:1–2; al-Samīn cites 26:18 and 94:1–2. Al-Nasafī, al-Ālūsī and Ibn ʿĀshūr likewise say the interrogative form carries an assertion, allowing coordination.
  35:37/q19/p2 It coordinates directly with 'We gave you life.' — reasons: Al-Ālūsī reports this as a second syntactic parse.
35:37/q20 Is there a variant reading of ٱلنَّذِيرُ? [ٱلنَّذِيرُ · readings] (turns on: Singular versus plural wording and, in al-Qurṭubī's form, feminine plural agreement.)
  35:37/q20/p1 The verse is read with singular al-nadhīr. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says the singular suits the immediate point.
  35:37/q20/p2 An alternate reading has plural al-nudhur, 'warners.' — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī transmits the feminine plural verbal agreement wajāʾatkum al-nudhur; al-Samīn and Abū Ḥayyān also report the plural, which supports a generic prophetic referent.
35:37/q21 What does فَذُوقُوا۟ command, and why does it follow? [فَذُوقُوا۟ · interpretation]
  35:37/q21/p1 They must taste the punishment of Hell, with remorse, as the consequence of ignored lifetime and warning. — reasons: Al-Rāzī calls the command humiliating and signifying lasting punishment; al-Qurṭubī links it to failure to reflect. Ibn Kathīr links it to disobedience to prophets. Ibn ʿĀshūr says fāʾ marks consequence, 'punishment' is the omitted object, and the imperative conveys continuity and inability to escape. Al-Biqāʿī cites 17:56 and 77:36 for their helplessness.
35:37/q22 Who are ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ here? [ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ · referent] (turns on: Whether 'wrongdoers' is limited in context to the disbelievers in Hell or expresses a rule about all injustice.)
  35:37/q22/p1 The wrongdoers are the unbelievers or polytheists, who wronged themselves through disbelief and idolatry. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī, al-Bursawī and al-Ālūsī make disbelief explicit; Yaḥyā calls them polytheists. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls shirk the gravest injustice, violating God's oneness and harming the self, citing 31:13.
  35:37/q22/p2 The wording reaches every wrongdoer whenever wrongdoing is established. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says wrongdoing weakens as truth strengthens; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the general statement includes these disbelievers and all wrongdoers.
