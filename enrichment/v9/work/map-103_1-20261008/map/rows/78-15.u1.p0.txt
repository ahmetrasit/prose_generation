## THE CURRENT MAP
78:15/q01 What does the purpose clause say about sending rain? [لِّنُخْرِجَ بِهِۦ · grammar]
  78:15/q01/p1 Bringing forth crops is the purpose or consequence of sending the rain. — reasons: Elmalılı says the lām marks consequence and the bāʾ marks causation.
78:15/q02 Why say ‘bring forth’ rather than ‘grow’? [لِّنُخْرِجَ · rhetoric]
  78:15/q02/p1 ‘Bring forth’ foregrounds resurrection from the earth. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr contrasts Qāf 50:9, 50:11, where divine favor is foregrounded, growth is mentioned first, and resurrection is inferred afterward; he also cites 20:55.
78:15/q03 What role does rain play in producing the crops? [بِهِۦ · theology] (turns on: Whether water directly mediates the crops' production or the crops occur when water is present.)
  78:15/q03/p1 Rain is the means through which God brings forth the crops. — reasons: Alūsī reports this as the early authorities' account. Elmalılı says the bāʾ expresses causation. The Study Quran says God remains the primary cause and cites 21:30, 24:25, and 25:54 on life from water. Ibn Kathīr says God uses abundant, beneficial rain to grow stored grain, fresh plants, and diverse gardens.
  78:15/q03/p2 Crops come into being at the presence of water. — reasons: Alūsī reports this as the Ashʿarī account, in distinction from the early authorities' account of water as the direct means.
78:15/q04 What does ḥabb denote? [حَبًّۭا · meaning] (turns on: Whether ḥabb is ordinary crop grain, defined by its use or cultivation, or pearls formed from rain at sea.)
  78:15/q04/p1 Edible grain or crop seeds, commonly harvested and chiefly human food. — reasons: Muqātil includes wheat, barley, and sesame. Naḥḥās and Ṭabrisī describe grain enclosed in husks; al-Ḍaḥḥāk, reported by Māwardī, calls it harvested seed in a husk; Ibn al-Jawzī reports al-Zajjāj's wider criterion of whatever is harvested. Wāḥidī, Baghawī, Ṭabrisī's alternative, Ibn al-Jawzī's majority, Bayḍāwī, and Iṣlāḥī assign it to human food. Rāzī counts husked grain as one of three classes of terrestrial growth and says it is basic food. Qurṭubī, Nasafī, and Biqāʿī name wheat and barley; Biqāʿī calls it staple food. Abū Ḥayyān calls it staple food. Ālūsī says it is usually human food and more noble. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it a collective for edible seeds, including wheat, barley, millet, corn, rice, and pulses. Ibn Kathīr says stored grain serves people and livestock; Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the growth feeds people and other animals. The Study Quran names grain among the products God makes through rain.
  78:15/q04/p2 Grain useful as food for animals. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya contrasts this grain with nabāt, grass used fresh by people or animals, and says both kinds of benefit are named.
  78:15/q04/p3 What people sow. — reasons: Māwardī reports this as the counterpart to vegetation that people did not sow.
  78:15/q04/p4 Pearls produced in the sea by rain. — reasons: Māwardī and Ibn al-Jawzī report ʿIkrima: each drop of heavenly rain produces a pearl in the sea or grass on land.
78:15/q05 Why is grain named before vegetation and gardens? [حَبًّۭا وَنَبَاتًۭا · rhetoric]
  78:15/q05/p1 The sequence follows practical importance: staple grain first, animal fodder next, and less essential fruit last. — reasons: Rāzī says grain is basic food, animals need the plants, and orchard fruit is less essential. Abū Ḥayyān says grain leads because it is staple food. Biqāʿī says staple wheat and barley precede other growth for enjoyment and fodder. Ālūsī says grain is more noble and usually human food, even though it sprouts later.
78:15/q06 What does nabāt denote? [وَنَبَاتًۭا · meaning] (turns on: Whether nabāt is distinct grass or fodder, all vegetation including grain, or specifically unsown growth.)
  78:15/q06/p1 Vegetation such as grass, herbage, pasture, fodder, and other plants, especially growth eaten by animals. — reasons: Muqātil includes wild trees and grass of open country that renew yearly. Naḥḥās includes grass and fodder. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk, reported by Māwardī, says pasture for grazing; ʿIkrima, reported by Māwardī and Ibn al-Jawzī, says each rain drop produces grass on land or a pearl at sea. Wāḥidī, Baghawī, Ṭabrisī's alternative, Bayḍāwī, Biqāʿī, Ālūsī, and Iṣlāḥī assign vegetation to livestock, with Bayḍāwī specifying straw and grass. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the grass is eaten fresh by people or animals. Ṭabrisī includes herbage and other plants. Ibn al-Jawzī reports the majority's view that people and livestock eat it, and al-Zajjāj's description of pasture eaten by livestock. Rāzī says grasses lack husks or stems and cites 20:54 for grain and grass. Qurṭubī, Nasafī, and Ibn Kathīr describe fodder, pasture, or fresh edible plants; Ibn Kathīr adds varied fruit gardens. Ibn ʿĀshūr says it is useful vegetation whose seeds are not eaten, such as straw, clover, and grass. Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the resulting food serves people and other animals. The Study Quran names plants among the products God makes through rain.
  78:15/q06/p2 All growth, including grain itself. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says nabāt is broad enough to include every kind of growth, even the grain named before it.
  78:15/q06/p3 Growth that people did not sow. — reasons: Māwardī reports this as the counterpart to grain that people sow.
78:15/q07 What is the grammatical origin of nabāt? [وَنَبَاتًۭا · grammar]
  78:15/q07/p1 It began as a verbal noun and became a conventional noun for useful vegetation. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr cites 71:17 in discussing its original verbal-noun use; in its conventional use he specifies vegetation whose seeds are not eaten, such as straw, clover, and grass.
78:15/q08 How do grain, vegetation, and gardens classify the earth's produce? [حَبًّۭا وَنَبَاتًۭا · interpretation]
  78:15/q08/p1 Together they cover the kinds of growth brought out by rain. — reasons: Rāzī names three exhaustive terrestrial classes: grain with husks, grasses without husks or stems, and trees that form the gardens of the next verse; he says 20:54 names the first two. Ṭabrisī says grain in crop husks and herbage and plants encompass the earth's produce. Ibn Kathīr describes grain stored for people and livestock, fresh plants, and gardens with fruits of varied colors, flavors, and scents.
78:15/q09 How are the sky, earth, rain, and plants pictured together? [* · interpretation]
  78:15/q09/p1 They are pictured as a reproductive pair and its offspring. — reasons: Biqāʿī likens sky to a male, earth to a female, rainwater to semen, and vegetation to their offspring; he says springs and pools are implied.
78:15/q10 What do these signs show about resurrection and judgment? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The notes draw either an inference from God's creative power or one from the moral purpose of providence.)
  78:15/q10/p1 God's creative power makes resurrection and the afterlife credible. — reasons: Biqāʿī reports Muqātil's statement that each sign just mentioned is more astonishing than resurrection. Kur'an Yolu says the firm heavens, sun, rain clouds, and life-giving growth display God's power to create a new realm; it cites 2:29 and 67:3. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the verb ‘bring forth’ portrays resurrection from earth as this passage's main point, comparing Qāf 50:9 and 50:11 and 20:55.
  78:15/q10/p2 Providence, mercy, wisdom, and purpose require a fixed reckoning that rewards the God-fearing and punishes rebels. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says the signs throughout heaven and earth show humanity is not unchecked or unaccountable. Paired with al-Mursalāt, this sūra presents judgment as the necessary end of a purposeful world.
78:15/q11 How does the passage's address connect with al-Mursalāt? [* · links]
  78:15/q11/p1 Both sūras use providential signs and an affirmative-question style to confront deniers with judgment. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says providence in the heavens and earth establishes judgment as the necessary end of a purposeful world, with reward for the God-fearing and punishment for rebels. He says each verse reproaches deniers through affirmative questions, while glad tidings for believers are understood alongside the warning.
