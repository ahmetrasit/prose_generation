12:48/q13 What does tuḥṣinūn mean? [تُحْصِنُونَ · meaning]
  12:48/q13/p1 It means to protect, secure, save or store provisions. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba, Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatāda, Abū ʿUbayda and other exegetes give these close glosses. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Ṭabrisī, Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī explain iḥṣān through a protected place, enclosure or fortress. Al-Māwardī specifies protected storehouses. Al-Qurṭubī notes that Abū ʿUbayda's different wording has the same meaning. Al-Suddī's wording is to raise or save the remainder.
12:48/q14 How is qalīlan parsed? [قَلِيلًۭا · grammar]
  12:48/q14/p1 It is accusative as the exception. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī gives this grammatical analysis.
12:48/q15 What alternate wording is reported for 'what you put forward for them'? [مَا قَدَّمْتُمْ لَهُنَّ · readings]
  12:48/q15/p1 Al-Ṣādiq reports the wording 'what you bring near for them.' — reasons: The report from Abū ʿAbd Allāh in Ṭabāṭabāʾī presents this as the revealed wording.
12:48/q16 What farming and storage measures carry people through famine? [* · interpretation]
  12:48/q16/p1 Cultivate repeatedly, keep much of the good harvest in storage, protect grain in its ears, and ration it through the drought while retaining seed. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya says successive harvests and even meager famine produce support the people. Ibn al-Jawzī says grain lasts better in its ear and spoils less. The Study Quran says most of the first phase's harvest supplies the second. Ṭabāṭabāʾī reads repeated planting, keeping ears unthreshed and rationing into the dream's required measures. Ibn Kathīr says famine sowing yields no return, and Ibn ʿĀshūr says the wording urges ample stockpiling. Al-Biqāʿī says the reserve must last the famine.
12:48/q17 Does the passage permit storing food until it is needed? [* · law]
  12:48/q17/p1 Yes, storing food for a later need is permissible. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī derives this ruling from the passage's instruction to save grain.
12:48/q18 What does the king's dream show about an unbeliever's dream? [* · interpretation]
  12:48/q18/p1 An unbeliever's dream can be true and interpreted as seen, especially where a believer is concerned; this dream also served as a messenger's sign and miracle. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī draws these points from the king's dream and its fulfillment.
12:48/q19 What happened in the reported famine economy under Joseph? [* · reports]
  12:48/q19/p1 Joseph stockpiled harvests and sold food successively for money, valuables, livestock, servants, property, land and finally people's freedom; afterward he freed them and restored their possessions and kingship. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī transmits this from al-Riḍā and says he omits many similar economic reports because most do not directly help interpret these verses.
12:48/q20 What comparison is reported between this famine and Quraysh's hardship? [* · reports]
  12:48/q20/p1 The Prophet prayed that Quraysh suffer seven years like Joseph's famine; hardship began but ended before seven years. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports the comparison and the shorter duration.
12:48/q21 What happened after the seven famine years, and how is the following year's wording read? [* · links] (turns on: The reported reading specifies rain, rather than rescue, as the sense of the following year's wording.)
  12:48/q21/p1 The following year brought rain and renewed plenty. — reasons: Al-Qummī's report says Joseph predicted rain after the plentiful and famine years. Al-Ṣādiq explains the following year's yughāth by rain rather than rescue; al-ʿAyyāshī transmits the same explanation. Al-Wāḥidī and Qatāda refer to the further abundant year beyond the dream's fourteen years.
  12:48/q21/p2 Al-Ṣādiq reads the following year's verb passively as 'they are rained upon.' — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports al-Ṣādiq's passive reading and its transmission by al-ʿAyyāshī.
12:48/q22 What broader point does Joseph's story illustrate? [* · interpretation]
  12:48/q22/p1 God directs human affairs in ways people cannot foresee; they may hate what benefits them or love what harms them. — reasons: Asad contrasts this Qur'anic theme with the romantic account of Joseph in Genesis and cites Q 2:216.
