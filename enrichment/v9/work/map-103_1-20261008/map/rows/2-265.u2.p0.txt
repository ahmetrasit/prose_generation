## THE CURRENT MAP
2:265/q01 Who are the spenders and what do they seek? [ٱلَّذِينَ يُنفِقُونَ أَمْوَٰلَهُمُ ٱبْتِغَآءَ مَرْضَاتِ ٱللَّهِ · interpretation]
  2:265/q01/p1 They give sincerely in obedience to God to obtain His approval and reward, without display, reproach, or injury. — reasons: Muqatil and al-Tabari exclude reproach and harm; al-Tabari and al-Mawardi include aid to fighters and obedient Muslims. Al-Hasan excludes reputation. Al-Biqa'i excludes worldly motives and reads marḍāt as lasting satisfaction. Tabataba'i explains God's face as His satisfaction with obedience. Ibn Kathir compares the Ramadan report about faith and seeking reward. Jishumi says wealth is spent in works of goodness and worship should seek God's pleasure and nearness to Him.
2:265/q02 How are ابتغاء and تثبيتا construed grammatically? [ٱبْتِغَآءَ وَتَثْبِيتًۭا · grammar] (turns on: Whether coordinated تثبيتا can name a purpose of giving, and whether its implied object is God's reward.)
  2:265/q02/p1 Both are circumstantial verbal nouns describing the spenders' state. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya argues coordination makes this the sounder parse and rejects making strengthening the purpose of spending; Abu Hayyan reports and disputes Makki's purpose analysis. Ibn Ashur adopts the circumstantial parse.
  2:265/q02/p2 Both are accusatives of purpose, with تثبيتا coordinated to ابتغاء. — reasons: Al-Nahhas, al-Tabrisi and Makki offer the parse; Abu Hayyan allows it if the object of strengthening is God's reward, since securing reward can motivate spending. Ibn Atiyya objects that spending is not undertaken for the sake of being strengthened.
  2:265/q02/p3 The wording admits either purpose or circumstance; ابتغاء alone may be purposeful even if تثبيتا is circumstantial. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya accepts a purpose sense for ابتغاء alone; al-Samin and Abu Hayyan allow both analyses. Al-Qurtubi records both proposals. Jishumi separately parses ibtigha as an accusative object of yunfiqun or as accusative after an elided li-, both expressing seeking as the purpose.
2:265/q03 What does تثبيتا من أنفسهم mean? [وَتَثْبِيتًۭا مِّنْ أَنفُسِهِمْ · interpretation] (turns on: The causative force of tathbīt, its implied object and agent, and whether the wording can carry the sense of deliberation.)
  2:265/q03/p1 Inward faith, certainty, and belief in God's promised recompense strengthen their resolve to give. — reasons: Al-Tabari derives causative strengthening from Ibn Rawaha's verse and says the donors trust the promise; al-Zajjaj contrasts the hypocrite. Al-Sha'bi, Qatada, Abu Salih, Ibn Abbas and others transmit certainty. Al-Tabari, Ibn Atiyya, al-Nahhas and Tabataba'i dispute alternative constructions. Ibn Qutayba describes confirmation as realized from within the donors themselves; Jishumi also attributes the certainty-and-insight gloss to Sa'id ibn Jubayr, Ibn Zayd, al-Suddi and Abu Muslim.
  2:265/q03/p2 They deliberate over where and to whom to give, and test their motive before proceeding. — reasons: Mujahid and Ata emphasize proper allocation; Hasan gives only when the motive is wholly for God. Al-Wahidi and al-Tabrisi defend the sense as making oneself firm. Al-Tabari, Ibn Atiyya, al-Nahhas and Abu Hayyan object that deliberation requires tathabbatan or an explicit licensing verb; 73:8 is debated. Jishumi attributes careful selection of recipients to al-Hasan and Mujahid, paraphrasing it as firming oneself in the choice.
  2:265/q03/p3 They train or fortify themselves in obedience by giving up beloved wealth, making virtue habitual. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf says wealth is close to life and discipline restrains desire; giving wealth steadies part of the self and wealth plus life all of it, citing 61:11. Razi cites repeated action forming disposition, 14:27, 13:28, 76:9 and the Abu Bakr report. Bursevi cites Busiri and Rumi. Ibn Ashur stresses difficult practice. Jishumi reports al-Asamm and Abu Ali's gloss of preparing oneself to remain firm in obedience to the Lord.
  2:265/q03/p4 The self firmly preserves an already pure intention throughout the act of giving. — reasons: Tabataba'i makes the self both agent and implied object; the sequence excludes show from the outset and subsequent reproach or harm. Abu Hayyan likewise speaks of protecting the gift from corruption. Jishumi reports the Qadi's gloss as committing oneself to preserve the charity from anything that could corrupt it.
  2:265/q03/p5 They seek and expect reward for themselves. — reasons: Qatada and Ibn Abbas give this gloss. Al-Tabari objects that the root does not mean seeking reward unless the soul's hope is what strengthens its owner; al-Nahhas likewise calls it unfamiliar. Jishumi attributes the reward-seeking gloss to Qatada.
  2:265/q03/p6 Their giving makes their sincere faith apparent to other believers. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf and Razi invoke Mujahid's tabyīnan variant; Alusi repeats it. The reading describes proof of faith, rather than its internal strengthening.
  2:265/q03/p7 It lessens their sins. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi transmits Sa'id ibn Jubayr and Abu Malik.
  2:265/q03/p8 They give acknowledging that God watches them. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi reports al-Zajjaj.
  2:265/q03/p9 They give willingly, from generous intention. — reasons: Abu Hayyan reports satisfaction in giving zakat; Ibn Atiyya calls Qatada's generosity gloss close to certainty, and al-Durr transmits intention.
2:265/q04 How does من أنفسهم work? [مِّنْ أَنفُسِهِمْ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the self is part of what is strengthened, the source of strengthening, or the intended object.)
  2:265/q04/p1 من is partitive: giving wealth commits or steadies part of the self. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf uses the idiom of stirring some energy; 61:11 distinguishes wealth from wealth and life. Al-Biqa'i, Bursevi and Ibn Ashur likewise treat wealth as figuratively part of the self; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin attach min to the verbal noun.
  2:265/q04/p2 من marks source: confirmation or resolve arises from within their own selves. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf contrasts this with partitivity; al-Nasafi, Bursevi and Alusi locate conviction in the heart. Abu Hayyan and al-Samin say the self can be the semantic agent; Tabataba'i makes it both agent and object.
  2:265/q04/p3 من takes the place of ل or marks an object or an adjective of تثبيتا. — reasons: Abu al-Baqa supplies deeds as object and permits min as li; al-Samin also offers an adjectival attachment. Al-Jubba'i takes min as li for training oneself in obedience.
2:265/q05 Who or what is compared with the garden? [وَمَثَلُ ٱلَّذِينَ يُنفِقُونَ كَمَثَلِ جَنَّةٍۭ · grammar] (turns on: What implied term completes the comparison and whether its elements correspond one by one.)
  2:265/q05/p1 The sincere spending or its standing with God is like the fruitful garden. — reasons: Al-Wahidi supplies an omitted 'spending'; Ibn Atiyya permits that syntax. Al-Kashshaf and al-Nasafi stress the expenditure's growth or standing. Al-Biqa'i says the named spender implies the owner and the named garden implies the expenditure.
  2:265/q05/p2 The giver is like the garden or its planter, while greater and lesser gifts are like heavy and light rain. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya supplies a planter; al-Kashshaf gives a differentiated matching; al-Baydawi and al-Alusi allow the giver or the expenditure to be the primary term.
  2:265/q05/p3 The likeness is a composite of pure, growing expenditure and the fertile, fruitful garden. — reasons: Al-Alusi describes the common feature as growth with purity; the imagery includes both the soil and either amount of rain. Al-Jurjani's separate example of Bashshar's dust-and-swords simile shows how a likeness can be one inseparable scene rather than pairwise correspondences; his note does not expressly apply this to 2:265.
2:265/q06 What does جنة mean here? [جَنَّةٍۭ · meaning]
  2:265/q06/p1 An orchard or tree-covered garden. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj, Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi explain the root by trees concealing the ground; al-Qurtubi compares jinn and embryo. Al-Farra, al-Mubarrad and others distinguish a palm garden, janna, from a vineyard, firdaws. Jishumi attributes the palm-orchard versus vineyard distinction to al-Farra and derives janna from concealment.
2:265/q07 Is the image read as a garden or a grain? [جَنَّةٍۭ · readings] (turns on: A reported alternative word in the comparison.)
  2:265/q07/p1 جنة, a garden. — reasons: The standard wording is retained by the sources recording the variant. Jishumi identifies the established readers' consonants as jim and nun.
  2:265/q07/p2 حبة, a grain or seed. — reasons: Mujahid, Asim al-Jahdari and al-Jahdari are named for the variant; al-Kashshaf and al-Alusi also report it. Al-Samin relates its comparison to 2:261. Jishumi attributes the ha-and-ba form to al-Sulami.
2:265/q08 What does ربوة mean? [بِرَبْوَةٍ · meaning] (turns on: Whether rabā denotes geographical elevation, soil's swelling growth, or a low place.)
  2:265/q08/p1 Raised ground, often level and fertile, above its surroundings or runoff. — reasons: Mujahid and Hasan stress level elevation; Ibn Abbas and Dahhak say rivers do not flow through this garden, though al-Qurtubi notes hills may have water. Al-Yazidi specifies above the watercourse. The root rabā means rise or increase; al-A'sha's poem depicts upland gardens. Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi limit the no-rivers detail to this rain-fed setting. Al-Damghani allows level raised ground or a hill and also points to 23:50; Jishumi says the word has seven dialectal forms and attributes the elevation gloss to Ibn Abbas, al-Hasan, al-Dahhak, Mujahid, al-Suddi and al-Rabi'.
