## THE CURRENT MAP
12:43/q01 Who is the king, and what position does he hold? [ٱلْمَلِكُ · referent] (turns on: The sources give differing personal names or a proposed historical dynasty; none supplies a decisive test.)
  12:43/q01/p1 He is al-Rayyan ibn al-Walid, the Egyptian king. — reasons: Ibn Ishaq says he recognized a true vision without understanding it; al-Kashshaf, al-Nasafi, al-Qurtubi, and Abu Hayyan name him. Al-Alusi calls him an unbeliever; al-Biqa'i describes his political and administrative power.
  12:43/q01/p2 He is al-Walid ibn al-Rayyan, Egypt's ruler, with al-Aziz as minister. — reasons: Al-Mawardi gives the name; al-Tabrisi adds the minister.
  12:43/q01/p3 He is probably one of the Hyksos rulers of Egypt. — reasons: Asad proposes Arab origin and kinship with Hebrews to explain his confidence in Joseph and Israelite settlement. Kuranyolu places six Hyksos rulers around 1700–1580 BCE, entering by Sinai; the 'shepherd kings' label suggests Syrian Arabs. It suggests shared nomadic origins with Hebrews and contrasts Moses' later Coptic Pharaoh, citing 2:49.
  12:43/q01/p4 He is the chief king of Egypt. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi gives the political designation without a personal name.
12:43/q02 May an unbelieving ruler be called a king? [ٱلْمَلِكُ · law] (turns on: Whether a royal title implies rightful authority.)
  12:43/q02/p1 Yes, the verse itself calls the unbelieving Rayyan king. — reasons: Al-Alusi cites this title as evidence of permissibility.
  12:43/q02/p2 Some prohibit king or amir when the title implies rightful authority. — reasons: Al-Alusi reports their concern and Muhammad's letter calling Heraclius the 'great Roman.'
12:43/q03 What circumstances led to the king's dream and Joseph's release? [ٱلْمَلِكُ أَرَىٰ · reports] (turns on: The reports differ on whether Gabriel's prior announcement is included and what preceding events are named.)
  12:43/q03/p1 The king's dream arose as Joseph's release approached and became its means. — reasons: Mujahid says the released prisoner's forgotten promise delayed Joseph's release because Satan caused him to forget remembrance of his Lord. Al-Kalbi places seven prison years after five earlier years. Al-Mawardi says the dream also warned of drought for preparation. Al-Biqa'i says God provided another cause for Joseph's elevation after the forgotten request. Islahi links the earlier inmates' dreams and Joseph's interpretations to the king's consultation; God's plan brings unlikely means into being.
  12:43/q03/p2 Gabriel announced Joseph's coming release, power, and reunion before the king dreamed that night. — reasons: Wahb reports the appointed prison term ending. Al-Qurtubi reports Gabriel saying the dream would bring freedom and power.
12:43/q04 Does 'I see' describe a dream or a waking sight? [أَرَىٰ رُءْيَٰىَ · interpretation]
  12:43/q04/p1 It describes a dream seen in sleep. — reasons: Al-Farra says Arabic normally uses this wording for a dream and cites Abraham's explicit sleep vision in 37:102; al-Tabari notes the verse does not itself say 'asleep.' Al-Tabrisi reports this as the majority view. The king later calls it his ru'ya, as Abu Hayyan observes; Study Quran describes it as veridical.
12:43/q05 Why does the king use the present tense 'I see'? [أَرَىٰ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether present tense merely enlivens a past report or hints that the vision recurred.)
  12:43/q05/p1 He vividly recounts a past dream as though seeing it now. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says he contemplates the vision now; Abu Hayyan calls the scene present to the hearer; al-Biqa'i ties vividness to his alarm. Ibn al-Jawzi and al-Alusi explicitly read it as 'I saw.'
  12:43/q05/p2 The wording may indicate a repeated dream. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i compares the earlier prisoners' dreams.
12:43/q06 What did the king see, and how did the images unfold? [سَبْعَ بَقَرَٰتٍۢ سِمَانٍۢ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌۭ وَسَبْعَ سُنۢبُلَٰتٍ خُضْرٍۢ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَٰتٍۢ · reports]
  12:43/q06/p1 Seven lean cows consumed seven fat cows, and dry ears overcame green ears. — reasons: Al-Suddi says God showed a frightening vision; several accounts say fat cows emerged from a dry river before the lean cows swallowed them, sometimes without visible change, and report the green ears being overcome or consumed. Ibn Atiyya reports the lean cows emerged after the fat and disappeared inside their bellies. Al-Wahidi says none of the fat remained visible; ripe green ears were overcome. The reports vary in these details.
12:43/q07 What do the cows and ears in the dream signify? [سَبْعَ بَقَرَٰتٍۢ سِمَانٍۢ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌۭ وَسَبْعَ سُنۢبُلَٰتٍ خُضْرٍۢ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَٰتٍۢ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the images principally forecast years, prescribe famine management, or take meaning from contextual dream details.)
  12:43/q07/p1 They forecast fertile years followed by barren years and abundant harvests followed by drought. — reasons: Qatadah assigns fat and lean cows to fertile and barren years and green and dry ears to abundant harvests and drought. Ali says this is the meaning of fat and lean cattle seen in a town. Al-Mawardi calls the dream a warning enabling preparation; Joseph explains it in 12:47–49, as Study Quran notes.
  12:43/q07/p2 The dream chiefly depicts the practical measures needed to survive famine. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i makes fat cows provisions from repeated planting, lean cows shortage, their eating consumption of reserves, green ears stored grain, and dry ears preservation unthreshed. He says unnumbered dry ears fit duties rather than counted famine years.
  12:43/q07/p3 Cattle symbols vary with the dream's setting and details. — reasons: Ali says coastal sailing season can make cows arriving ships; otherwise they may be successive turmoil. Uniform yellow can suggest disease; varied colors, ugly horns, or fiery mouths can signal army, raid, or enemy. Al-Qurtubi adds wife, servant, crop yield, or year because cattle bear offspring and produce yield.
12:43/q08 What does 'fat' mean, and what forms does the word have? [سِمَانٍۢ · meaning]
  12:43/q08/p1 The cows are full of flesh and fat; siman is the plural of samin or samina. — reasons: Al-Nasafi calls it a regular plural; al-Samin notes samina and shared-gender samin like kiram. Abu Hayyan and al-Samin describe saman as noun or verbal noun from samina and samin as active adjective, with irregular vowel pairing; al-Alusi derives forms from samana and samn.
12:43/q09 What does 'fat' grammatically describe, and why? [سَبْعَ بَقَرَٰتٍۢ سِمَانٍۢ · grammar] (turns on: The scope of the adjective: the counted cows or the numeral, with a difference in emphasis.)
  12:43/q09/p1 It qualifies the cows, selecting seven of the fat kind. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf contrasts describing the numeral, which first identifies the count by cow-kind. Abu Hayyan cites the difference between 'three noble men' constructions; al-Samin says an adjective of the genitive classifier makes the counted set possess the quality. Al-Alusi prefers this to describing number and says a derived adjective cannot formally discriminate the count; al-Qurtubi observes another construction is possible outside Qur'anic wording. Al-Zarkashi cites 'seven fat cows' as an example of the rule for agreement of an adjective following the counted expression.
  12:43/q09/p2 Outside this wording, 'fat' can qualify the numeral itself. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi cites a comparable construction at 67:3; al-Tibi says describing the number before its discriminator stresses both count and quality, whereas naming the discriminator first focuses the description.
12:43/q10 Why is the lean cows' number followed directly by an adjective? [سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌۭ · grammar] (turns on: Whether an adjective may itself supply the numeral's counted kind or an understood 'cows' is needed.)
  12:43/q10/p1 The noun 'cows' is understood from the earlier phrase, and lean qualifies the seven. — reasons: Abu Hayyan says the earlier seven cows supply the omitted noun; numerals do not normally take mere adjectives as their genitive discriminator except poetic necessity or substantivized adjectives such as knights. Al-Kashshaf and al-Samin likewise distinguish substantivized nouns; al-Qutb says the prior cows identify the kind. Al-Baydawi ties the feminine form to the understood cows.
  12:43/q10/p2 The adjective may stand for cows as the numeral's discriminator, avoiding an omitted noun. — reasons: Al-Fara'id objects that numerical discriminators normally take genitive construction and offers this alternative; al-Qutb replies that when lean is so used it ceases to be an adjective in the relevant analysis.
12:43/q11 Why is the cows' eating expressed in the imperfect? [يَأْكُلُهُنَّ · rhetoric]
  12:43/q11/p1 It makes a past, startling scene present to the listener. — reasons: Al-Alusi says 'they eat' means 'they ate'; he parses the clause as circumstantial or adjectival.
12:43/q12 What does 'lean' mean? [عِجَافٌۭ · meaning]
  12:43/q12/p1 The cows are extremely emaciated, stripped of flesh and fat. — reasons: Al-Layth defines ajaf as loss of fat; Ibn Durayd describes thick bones bare of flesh. Ibn Atiyya and al-Samin cite a poem about famished Meccans. Al-Raghib relates the root to a thin blade, aversion from food or a person, and thin livestock.
12:43/q13 How is the form 'ijaf' derived and pluralized? [عِجَافٌۭ · grammar]
  12:43/q13/p1 It is an unusual plural of feminine ajfa', patterned after its opposite siman. — reasons: Al-Wahidi, al-Layth, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, al-Alusi, and al-Tabataba'i give analogy with siman; expected alternatives are 'ujuf or 'ujf. Ibn al-Sikkit from al-Farra reports a kasra variant in the root and parallels. Al-Qurtubi gives two verb patterns, 'ajafa ya'jafu and 'ajifa ya'jafu. Al-Tabrisi supplies masculine a'jaf and feminine ajfa'.
