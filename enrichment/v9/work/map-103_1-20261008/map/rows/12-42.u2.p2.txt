12:42/q19 Can prophets forget ordinary matters? [فَأَنسَىٰهُ ٱلشَّيْطَٰنُ · theology] (turns on: Whether the reported forgetting is ordinary and permissible or entails blameworthy satanic influence.)
  12:42/q19/p1 Yes; protection concerns conveying revelation, not every ordinary lapse. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi cites 12:45, 17:65 and hadith that Adam and the Prophet forgot; he uses this to answer an objection to the cupbearer reading. A'isha reports that the Prophet was reminded of a recitation he had omitted or forgotten. Ibn Mas'ud transmits his instruction to say one was made to forget a verse. Ibn Kathir says involuntary forgetting after diligence is no deficiency; neglect may cause it, and he reads the attribution of forgetting in 18:24 as naming the result for its cause.
  12:42/q19/p2 Joseph's sincerity and divine purification rule out blameworthy satanic access in this episode. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i rejects reports alleging grave lapses, while Ibn Kathir challenges their chains; Abu Hayyan says the stories should not be attributed to prophets.
12:42/q20 What inward reading is given to Joseph's request and continued imprisonment? [* · inward] (turns on: Which spiritual faculty or stage is represented by the forgotten messenger.)
  12:42/q20/p1 The request represents seeking stable spiritual existence through love, with a remnant of self that prolongs the journey. — reasons: Al-Qashani maps intoxicated love, spirit and heart toward unity, yet sees veiling rather than annihilation; ego or illusion can persist, and prison marks seclusion until subsistence through God begins.
  12:42/q20/p2 The alternative forgetting is the lover's loss of the love-messenger in bewildered witnessing. — reasons: Al-Qashani says unity makes the lover forget creation and self until annihilation ends and divine life restores distinction.
12:42/q21 What institutional lesson is drawn from Joseph's long imprisonment? [* · law]
  12:42/q21/p1 Prisons need regular judicial oversight so detainees do not disappear from official attention. — reasons: Ibn Ashur infers that the prison lacked a reliable register or periodic royal report, then says Islamic law requires a judge to review prisoners daily.
12:42/q22 What did Joseph's patience after imprisonment show? [* · reports]
  12:42/q22/p1 He interpreted the king's dream before asking for release and sought vindication before leaving prison. — reasons: Abd al-Razzaq transmits a Prophetic expression of admiration for his patience and generosity, while connecting the earlier request to longer confinement.
12:42/q23 How is the term of imprisonment connected to an earlier decree? [فَلَبِثَ فِى ٱلسِّجْنِ · links]
  12:42/q23/p1 The added years fulfill the earlier decision to imprison Joseph in 12:35. — reasons: Muqatil says the initial seven years and five later punitive years made twelve in all.
12:42/q24 What did Ali say about disciplining the soul in relation to this episode? [* · inward]
  12:42/q24/p1 The soul repeatedly scatters from discipline like a flock moving away from the shepherd. — reasons: Al-Tustari transmits Ali's comparison as an inward reflection on attention and self-discipline.
12:42/q25 Which prisoner was expected to survive? [ٱلَّذِى ظَنَّ أَنَّهُۥ نَاجٍۢ · referent]
  12:42/q25/p1 The surviving prisoner was the cupbearer. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi identifies him explicitly. Al-Suyuti also identifies the expected survivor as the cupbearer.
12:42/q26 Does a report of Joseph's request necessarily imply blame? [ٱذْكُرْنِى عِندَ رَبِّكَ · interpretation]
  12:42/q26/p1 Some scholars read Joseph's words without blame. — reasons: Al-Alusi reports this interpretive possibility, which accords with lawful use of means discussed elsewhere in the notes.
12:42/q27 How does al-Tabataba'i assess the al-Qummi account of the two prisoners? [* · reports]
  12:42/q27/p1 Its description of them as royal attendants assigned to guard Joseph is textually disordered. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i says it conflicts with the Qur'an's description of them as Joseph's prison companions.
12:42/q28 What meanings and derivations are given for shaytan? [ٱلشَّيْطَٰنُ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the nun belongs to the root and whether the word is derived from distance or burning/perishing.)
  12:42/q28/p1 The word comes from shatana, to be distant, with a radical nun; it describes estrangement from good and mercy. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba gives the fi'al pattern, expressions for distant places and a date stone, and Umayya's shatin as evidence for the radical nun; Tarafa's shatr supports distance. Ibn Atiyya prefers this root, citing al-A'sha, Sibawayh's tashaytana and Umayya; Ibn al-Jawzi records distance from good or depth in evil.
  12:42/q28/p2 An alternative derives it from shata, to blaze, or a root meaning to perish, with destruction through sin as its point. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya reports the burning derivation from a group but rejects it using tashaytana and Umayya's shatin. Ibn al-Jawzi reports the alternative root meaning to perish and poetry offered in its support.
  12:42/q28/p3 In general usage, it can name a rebellious being among jinn, humans, or animals. — reasons: Ibn Qutayba describes rebellious jinn and humans as leaving their group and connects shatir to leaving home. Ibn al-Jawzi quotes Abu Ubayda on rebellious jinn, humans and animals, and cites 6:112 and 6:121.
  12:42/q28/p4 In other cited verses it can designate consulted leaders or soothsayers, a snake, or particular named adversaries. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi reports these Qur'anic faces for 2:14, 37:65 and 25:29; for the last, reports differ between Umayya ibn Khalaf and Abu Jahl, with Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt named as the human.
12:42/q29 What senses can dhikr have in and around Joseph's request? [ٱذْكُرْنِى ذِكْرَ · meaning] (turns on: Whether remembrance is vocal, inward, or a broader figurative use.)
  12:42/q29/p1 It can mean spoken remembrance or telling another person about a matter, as Joseph asks the cupbearer to do. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi distinguishes remembrance on the tongue and cites 2:200, 3:191, 4:103 and 33:41; he names Joseph's request as the face of reporting or telling.
  12:42/q29/p2 It can mean remembrance in the heart, including keeping something in mind or regret after sin. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi gives the heart as a literal sense and cites 3:135 for recollection after sin; Ibn Jinni distinguishes a damma vocalization for inward remembrance from kasra for speech.
  12:42/q29/p3 It can denote a report or account, an admonition, or exalted rank or honor in other usage. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi cites accounts of earlier people in 18:83, 21:24 and 37:168, warnings in 6:44, 7:165, 36:19 and 50:45, and honor; he also records related use for bearing or counting male children.
12:42/q30 What verbal noun and related noun patterns are recorded around labitha? [فَلَبِثَ · grammar]
  12:42/q30/p1 Labitha has the verbal noun labthan and the participle labith; the cited comparisons show Arabic forming related acts and nouns in several patterns. — reasons: Sibawayh compares labitha labthan with amila amalan and cites labith; he then records intransitive makatha makthan, other verbal nouns, omitted prepositions with motion verbs, and examples of patterns for ordinary acts, illness, crafts, offices, states, periods, avoidance and remnants. These further examples are general morphological background, not separate interpretations of 12:42.
12:42/q31 How are disconnected questions with am formed in the cited Arabic grammar? [* · grammar]
  12:42/q31/p1 Disjunctive am starts a fresh question after a completed statement or question; an opening interrogative hamza can be omitted in poetry. — reasons: Sibawayh distinguishes this from asking which of two alternatives applies, compares a declarative about camels and sheep, cites al-Khalil on al-Akhtal, and gives poems by al-Aswad al-Tamimi and Umar ibn Abi Rabia for hamza omission. The notes state no particular application to the wording of 12:42.
12:42/q32 What can a speaker's character contribute to the force of speech? [وَقَالَ · rhetoric]
  12:42/q32/p1 A speaker's known character can make speech more forceful, while artificial speech may reveal what the speaker meant to hide. — reasons: Al-Baqillani compares al-Mutanabbi and al-Buhturi on courage and says Ibn al-Mutazz's and Abu Firas's boasts suit their personae. These are general literary comparisons; the notes do not expressly apply them to Joseph's words.
12:42/q33 What effect does placing a subject before or after a verb have in the cited rhetoric? [وَقَالَ · rhetoric]
  12:42/q33/p1 Verb-first order normally reports an expected occurrence, while subject-first order focuses or strengthens a claim about the agent, identity, or circumstance. — reasons: Al-Jurjani uses promises, praise, boasting and circumstantial clauses to explain emphasis. In questions he contrasts whether anyone came with whether a known visitor was a man, explains when a modified noun can be fronted, and uses the barking-dog proverb and negation with illa for identification. These are general examples without an express reading of 12:42.
