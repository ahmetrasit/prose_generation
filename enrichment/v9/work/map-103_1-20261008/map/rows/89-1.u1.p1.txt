89:1/q10 How does dawn relate to the following and parallel oaths? [وَٱلْفَجْرِ · links] (turns on: The next oath supports different calendars; other sources read the series through cosmic contrasts.)
  89:1/q10/p1 Dawn and departing night are the chief contrast; intervening oaths develop it. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says the blessed ten are a subset of night. Corpus Coranicum compares 74:34, 81:18, 91:1-3, 92:2 and 93:1 and notes that the ten uniquely quantify night. Islahi calls first and last primary and the middle secondary. Tabatabai glosses dawn, even, odd and withdrawing night; al-Aswad pairs familiar dawn with Dhu al-Hijja's ten.
  89:1/q10/p2 Cosmic oppositions anticipate moral oppositions and final recompense. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum compares 91:9-10 and 92:4-10, plus similar oath openings in 91-93, 95, 100 and 103. Bint al-Shati sees truth after tyranny; Islahi says worldly pairs call for an afterlife settling good and evil.
  89:1/q10/p3 The formula resembles Quranic oaths and may echo pre-Islamic soothsayer language. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum calls the soothsayer link probable but under-studied, compares 100:1-5, points to discussion of 93:1-2 and lists similar sura openings. Islahi compares receding night and appearing morning in 74:32-34.
  89:1/q10/p4 Dawn and ten nights point to Dhu al-Hijja and sacrifice rites. — reasons: Al-Dahhak and Ibn al-Qayyim argue from the next oath. A Jabir report identifies the dawn and ten with the ten of sacrifice; Elmalili reports Tabari endorsing it and prefers the ten nights, Arafah and Sacrifice Day sequence.
  89:1/q10/p5 Dawn and ten nights point to Muharram's first days. — reasons: Study Quran explicitly pairs Muharram's beginning and ten nights; Qatada calls it the year's first day.
  89:1/q10/p6 Dawn and ten nights point to Ramadan's final ten and first revelation. — reasons: Study Quran cites 97:1.
89:1/q11 How does this opening connect to the sura and al-Ghashiya? [* · links] (turns on: The sources stress judgment, moral testing, divine power and the basmala.)
  89:1/q11/p1 It supports return, reckoning and divine watchfulness announced at al-Ghashiya's end. — reasons: Jishumi links the prior chapter's return and judgment to this chapter's warning and encouragement. Biqai repeatedly cites 88:26, waking from death-like sleep and work before recompense; pilgrimage clothing, talbiya and sacred travel evoke resurrection. Islahi says the prior signs of divine power, wisdom and providence require accountability. Alusi reports Suyuti's view that the oath affirms al-Ghashiya's promise and threat and parallels its opening questions.
  89:1/q11/p2 It prepares tyrants' stories, human tests and the tranquil soul at judgment. — reasons: Alusi connects al-Ghashiya's two classes of faces (88:2, 88:8) with deniers and the blessed soul (89:27). Study Quran traces oaths, tyrants, fortune, stinginess and judgment. Tabatabai says worldly attachment causes tyranny and ingratitude; wealth and poverty test preparation for the next life, while the tranquil soul stays submitted through gain and loss. Bint al-Shati anticipates these oppositions in the natural contrasts. Qutb describes Meccan scenes, rhythms, rhyme, warning, history and moral correction.
  89:1/q11/p3 The signs establish sole divine rule and the need for a just afterlife. — reasons: Islahi says the Prophet warns through heavenly and earthly signs even when some cannot see; harmonized opposites require one sovereign (21:22), and unrequited good and evil would make creation purposeless (68:35-36). Alternating day and night show even the powerful cannot escape God (28:71-72).
  89:1/q11/p4 The basmala evokes mercy, lovers' self-offering and gathering at resurrection. — reasons: Qushayri says reciting satisfies worshippers while lovers offer life; the basmala unsettles the poor, disorients the enamored and brings mercy to lovers. Biqai portrays God reassembling dispersed people, giving guidance and granting His allies contentment and gardens.
89:1/q12 What inward meaning is given to dawn? [ٱلْفَجْرِ · inward] (turns on: These readings transpose dawn's light to prophetic, devotional and eschatological experience.)
  89:1/q12/p1 Muhammad, through whom faith and obedience shine and unbelief disappears. — reasons: Tustari adds the lights of both worlds; Ibn Ata as reported by Sulami stresses faith dispelling unbelief.
  89:1/q12/p2 At the great resurrection, divine light appearing in the soul's night. — reasons: Qashani relates ten to dormant senses during disclosure, pair to witness and witnessed, odd to oneness after duality and night to disappearing selfhood.
  89:1/q12/p3 At death's lesser resurrection, the sun rising from the west. — reasons: Qashani relates ten to dimmed senses, pair to spirit and body, odd to separated spirit and night to bodily darkness dispersing.
  89:1/q12/p4 The saints' opening worship or lovers' communion with God. — reasons: Qushayri reports the former and Kashifi through Bursevi the latter.
89:1/q13 Where and when was the sura revealed, and how many verses has it? [* · sciences] (turns on: Most call it Meccan, with a Medinan report; verse counts vary.)
  89:1/q13/p1 Meccan, often placed early after al-Layl. — reasons: Kashshaf gives thirty verses with twenty-nine as an alternative. Durr cites Ibn Abbas and Ibn al-Zubayr for Meccan revelation and thirty verses. Alusi says this is the majority, while regional verse counts differ. Bint al-Shati places it tenth, after al-Layl and before al-Duha; Study Quran says probably tenth. Tabatabai infers Meccan origin from context; Qutb calls it Meccan.
  89:1/q13/p2 Medinan. — reasons: Alusi attributes this to Ali ibn Abi Talha; Study Quran notes some commentators hold it.
89:1/q14 What reports concern reciting Surat al-Fajr? [* · reports] (turns on: The reports describe distinct merits and uses.)
  89:1/q14/p1 Recitation brings forgiveness; daily recitation brings light on Resurrection Day. — reasons: Jishumi transmits the attributed Prophetic promise through Ubayy ibn Kab.
  89:1/q14/p2 The Prophet recommended it among short suras for communal prayer. — reasons: Ibn Kathir and Durr recount a worshipper leaving Muadh's long prayer and the Prophet rebuking Muadh for driving people away; the suggested chapters also include 87:1, 91:1 and 92:1.
89:1/q15 What Muharram and Ashura reports accompany this dawn interpretation? [* · reports] (turns on: These are distinct accompanying reports; the eye-treatment report is explicitly weakened.)
  89:1/q15/p1 Muharram and Ashura are especially recommended for fasting. — reasons: Durr reports the Prophet calling Muharram God's month and best for fasting after Ramadan, with night prayer best after obligatory prayer. Ali recommends Muharram because of repentance and forgiveness. Reports commend an adjacent day to Ashura or ninth and tenth to differ from Jews. Ibn Shihab fasted while traveling because Ashura would pass; Abu Musa and Abu Hurayra connect its fast to Jewish festivity and prophets' practice.
  89:1/q15/p2 Generosity to one's family on Ashura brings provision through the year. — reasons: Durr gives reports through Jabir, Ibn Masud, Abu Said and Abu Hurayra and a traditional saying; Bayhaqi says individually weak chains support one another.
  89:1/q15/p3 Antimony on the eyes on Ashura prevents inflammation forever. — reasons: Durr reports it through Urwa from Ibn Abbas and notes Bayhaqi weakened it.
