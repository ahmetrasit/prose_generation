## THE CURRENT MAP
68:17/q01 Who is meant by 'We tested them'? [بَلَوْنَٰهُمْ · referent] (turns on: Whether the plural is confined to the historical deniers or includes their type.)
  68:17/q01/p1 The Meccan or Quraysh deniers addressed in the preceding passage. — reasons: Muqātil, al-Ṭabarī and many later authors identify the addressees as Meccans; Ibn ʿĀshūr links the pronoun to the preceding deniers (68:8, 14–15). Iṣlāḥī takes the preceding portrait to describe Quraysh leadership collectively.
  68:17/q01/p2 The deniers and, more broadly, people of the same contemptible oath-breaking character. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ explicitly extends the plural beyond its immediate deniers; her objection to a narrowly retrospective Badr reading leaves a general warning to tyrants.
68:17/q02 How were the people addressed tested? [بَلَوْنَٰهُمْ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the comparison names an experienced famine, a test by gifts and guidance, or the Badr reversal.)
  68:17/q02/p1 Through famine, drought and hunger, often linked to the Prophet's prayer for years like Joseph's. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj names disease, death and lost provisions; al-Qushayrī, Biqāʿī and others say they ate carrion; Bursawī adds hides, bones and blood. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Elmalılı give seven years. Abū Ḥayyān cites the Prophet's supplication. Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports the famine setting but observes that the owners' mutual blame does not parallel Mecca easily; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports the seven-year famine comparison.
  68:17/q02/p2 Through prosperity, secure trade, wealth, children and Muhammad's mission, which called for gratitude and right conduct. — reasons: Al-Rāzī sees blessings as a test of gratitude and obedience; Ibn ʿAṭiyya compares Muhammad's guidance with the brothers' inherited duties. Biqāʿī says ease made deniers disdain poor believers; Ibn ʿĀshūr details security and distant trade; Asad says affluence exceeded moral deserts. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ allows prosperity as a form of testing despite the more common hardship sense, and cites a prophetic report that prosperity too will test people after adversity.
  68:17/q02/p3 Through the defeat at Badr, when Quraysh's apparent power over opponents failed. — reasons: Ibn Jurayj relates Abū Jahl's order to bind captives in mountains rather than kill them; the owners' supposed control of the orchard supplies the likeness. Ṭabāṭabāʾī sees Badr as worldly proof that apparent causes fail if the passage preceded it. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports the comparison with a pre-Badr vow, then rejects it as reading an event about five years later into the passage.
68:17/q03 What does balāʾ mean here, and how can God test? [بَلَوْنَٰهُمْ بَلَوْنَآ · theology]
  68:17/q03/p1 A trial or affliction that exposes conduct; divine testing assigns duties without giving God new knowledge. — reasons: Jishumī says trial, testing and examination are synonymous and that acts become manifest although God knew them beforehand, so the expression is figurative of His treatment. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ distinguishes balāʾ as affliction or trial from ibtilāʾ as examination; Ṭabāṭabāʾī allows both test and affliction. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ notes ibtilāʾ in Last Day descriptions and surveys the agents of fitna: God, Satan, magic, hypocrites, disbelievers, and people testing each other. She endorses al-Rāghib's and Ibn al-Athīr's observation that balāʾ and fitna more commonly signal hardship, while acknowledging trials through prosperity and adversity together. She also cites a prophetic report that a trial through adversity will be followed by a trial through prosperity.
68:17/q04 How is 'as We tested' constructed? [كَمَا بَلَوْنَآ · grammar]
  68:17/q04/p1 The kāf qualifies an omitted cognate accusative; mā can be a verbal noun or a relative pronoun. — reasons: Al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī give both analyses of mā; al-Samīn explicitly calls the kāf an accusative adjective of the omitted verbal noun.
68:17/q05 What is the point of comparing their trial with the orchard owners' trial? [كَمَا بَلَوْنَآ أَصْحَٰبَ ٱلْجَنَّةِ · interpretation] (turns on: Which feature of the story the comparison selects: withdrawal of blessings, famine, or Badr.)
  68:17/q05/p1 Blessings can be withdrawn suddenly when their recipients respond with pride, ingratitude and denial of others' rights. — reasons: Muqātil calls the story a parable inviting Meccan unbelievers to return to God; Ibn ʿAṭiyya leaves repentance open to survivors. Ibn ʿĀshūr compares Cave and Qarun (18:32; 28:76); Elmalılı says the law spans generations. Iṣlāḥī stresses that Quraysh's peace and leaders' authority can vanish, making later grief futile; Kur'an Yolu cites 4:147, 14:7 and 31:12 for gratitude and its opposite.
  68:17/q05/p2 Mecca's famine parallels the garden's destruction as a worldly punishment. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj associates the trial with the Prophet's prayer against Muḍar; Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports seven years; al-Zamakhsharī says punishment of the afterlife is still greater. Ṭabāṭabāʾī notes the final mutual blame does not fit this comparison easily.
  68:17/q05/p3 The owners' overconfidence parallels Quraysh's presumed power before Badr. — reasons: Ibn Jurayj supplies Abū Jahl's proposal; Ṭabāṭabāʾī relates Badr to the failure of apparent causes. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ objects on chronology: Badr occurred about five years after this sūra.
68:17/q06 What does janna denote here? [ٱلْجَنَّةِ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the term is read only as the physical orchard or also as a social symbol.)
  68:17/q06/p1 An earthly orchard or garden. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ traces janna to concealment, comparing embryo, jinn, insanity, night and shield; she says Qur'anic context distinguishes earthly gardens from Paradise and counts singular, dual and plural uses. Elmalılı calls it an earthly paradise and also suggests a deeper image of homeland. Al-Dāmghānī explicitly glosses the garden here as an earthly orchard and compares the usage at 18:32.
  68:17/q06/p2 At a deeper level, a homeland held and managed collectively. — reasons: Elmalılı notes the plural owners, unlike the single owner in al-Kahf 18:35, to support a social or national parable.
68:17/q07 Is 'the garden' a particular known orchard or a generic parable? [ٱلْجَنَّةِ · referent] (turns on: Whether the definite article points to a known place or serves the parable generically.)
  68:17/q07/p1 A particular orchard known from accounts about Yemen and its owners. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī says the story was locally known; Ibn ʿĀshūr associates it with Darwan; Elmalılı says Arabs knew it. Qatāda says God has recounted and made clear these people's story.
  68:17/q07/p2 The definite article is generic, presenting a complete parable rather than identifying one historical orchard. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī explicitly rejects using the article to identify an orchard or owners in Yemen or Ṣanʿāʾ.
68:17/q08 Where was the garden said to be, and what was it called? [أَصْحَٰبَ ٱلْجَنَّةِ · reports] (turns on: Reported place names, distances and whether a name denotes the garden or its locality.)
  68:17/q08/p1 In Yemen near Ṣanʿāʾ, without a fixed name or distance. — reasons: Accounts vary between an orchard, fertile estate and farmland; some place it outside the city. Ibn Kathīr transmits Yemen or Abyssinia as alternate localities.
  68:17/q08/p2 At Darwān, Ḍarwān or Darawan near Ṣanʿāʾ. — reasons: This is variously the village, orchard or locality; reported distances differ: al-Māwardī and al-Wāḥidī give twelve miles, al-Qurṭubī one farsakh, al-Suhaylī several farsakhs, and Ibn ʿĀshūr says some call Darwan the garden's name.
  68:17/q08/p3 At Ṣarwān, Ṣurawān or Sawran near Ṣanʿāʾ. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī's report from Ibn Jarīr says six miles; al-Nasafī relates Ṣarwān to nearby Darwān. Spellings vary across the sources.
  68:17/q08/p4 Two farsakhs from Ṣanʿāʾ; one account calls it al-Sarim. — reasons: Muqātil gives al-Sarim, palms, crops and grapes; al-Kalbī's distance is repeated by al-Wāḥidī and al-Qurṭubī, and al-Kalbī says it burned.
  68:17/q08/p5 At Dar, six miles from Ṣanʿāʾ. — reasons: Al-Durr transmits this from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr.
  68:17/q08/p6 At al-Ridwan in Yemen, nine miles from Ṣanʿāʾ. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī transmits this from Abū Jaʿfar.
  68:17/q08/p7 In Abyssinia. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr reports this as one alternative to Yemen; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ mentions it among exegetical elaborations.
68:17/q09 Who were the garden owners by people, religion or kinship? [أَصْحَٰبَ ٱلْجَنَّةِ · referent] (turns on: Conflicting reports identify nationality, religion and historical period differently.)
  68:17/q09/p1 Heirs of a righteous father, commonly described as sons or brothers. — reasons: Muqātil calls their forebears righteous Muslims after Jesus; Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls the heirs brothers. Some accounts name three sons, while Ibn ʿAbbās in Ṭabāṭabāʾī names five; these number reports are set out separately below.
  68:17/q09/p2 Abyssinians or Ethiopians. — reasons: ʿIkrima and Ibn ʿAbbās relate a charitable father whose sons called him foolish and vowed to stop feeding the poor; al-Māwardī separately reports this identification.
  68:17/q09/p3 People of the Book, without a fixed Jewish or Christian identification. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the father was righteous and believing, without specifying Jewish or Christian; Ibn ʿAbbās is cited by al-Ṭabarī, al-Naḥḥās and al-Durr.
  68:17/q09/p4 Israelites, including an elder who gave away his annual surplus. — reasons: Qatāda's account gives the elder and sons; al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī report the identification as an alternative.
  68:17/q09/p5 A Muslim man or family of Thaqīf in Yemen. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī attributes Thaqīf to the exegetes; al-Rāzī reports a Muslim Thaqīf owner near Ṣanʿāʾ.
