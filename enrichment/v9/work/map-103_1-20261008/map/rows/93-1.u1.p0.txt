## THE CURRENT MAP
93:1/q01 What does al-ḍuḥā denote in this oath? [وَٱلضُّحَىٰ · meaning] (turns on: Whether the term denotes a whole day, a particular time or light, or an event or symbolic referent.)
  93:1/q01/p1 The whole daylight or daytime. — reasons: Tabari, Tha'labi, Baghawi, Tabrisi, Razi, Qurtubi, Nasafi, Kuranyolu and Shawkani appeal to its contrast with night; Baghawi, Kashshaf, Qurtubi and Alusi cite 7:98, Qurtubi also 7:97. Mujahid links it with exposure to sun. Alusi says the objection assumes an intensely dark night without proof. Bint al-Shati reports Tabari's choice of visible daylight, but cites 79:46, 20:59 and 91:1 against the whole-day sense.
  93:1/q01/p2 A defined forenoon or midmorning after sunrise. — reasons: Qatada and Muqatil describe temperate heat or cold. Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Alusi, Elmalili, Islahi and Samarrai describe the sun rising high and strong light. Alusi prefers a time over sunlight or sunrise because of the next oath. Bint al-Shati cites 79:46, 20:59 and 91:1 for a particular hour; Abduh calls it sunlight in the day's prime. Study Quran gives daybreak-to-noon as a wider range.
  93:1/q01/p3 The hour Moses' opponents prostrated. — reasons: Tha'labi, Kashshaf, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Nasafi, Bursawi, Alusi and Study Quran relate it to the gathering at 20:59. Razi sees a promise that God can honor Muhammad and turn his enemies' hearts. Bint al-Shati calls the reading anachronistic because these passages came later.
  93:1/q01/p4 The beginning or first hour of day. — reasons: Qatada calls it an hour or the start; Suddi and Muqatil specify the sun moving onward. Jishumi derives it from appearing in sunlight. Bursawi allows metonymy for the time or an omitted noun.
  93:1/q01/p5 Sunlight, brightness, or visible exposure. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya calls it the rise and strength of light; Tabari's daylight is visible sunlight; Ibn Kathir swears by forenoon and its light. Study Quran says the title most commonly means rising sunlight and can extend to all creation it reveals. Bint al-Shati says the root centers on visibility; Wahidi and Ibn Ashur refer to the parallel at 91:1. Yaqub derives aḍḥā from sheep sacrificed at this hour.
  93:1/q01/p6 The morning when God spoke to Moses. — reasons: Tha'labi, Qushayri, Qurtubi, Baydawi, Bursawi and Study Quran report this; Ja'far pairs it with the Night Journey, citing 7:143 and 17:1. Bint al-Shati objects that the cited passages were revealed later.
  93:1/q01/p7 The forenoon prayer. — reasons: Qushayri lists it; Study Quran notes the term also names a late-morning supererogatory prayer.
  93:1/q01/p8 The Prophet's radiant face. — reasons: Bursawi cites a Persian couplet pairing it with his dark hair; Elmalili reports the association. Bint al-Shati excludes such esoteric identifications.
  93:1/q01/p9 Sunrise itself. — reasons: Qutrub identifies it with sunrise.
  93:1/q01/p10 The dawn of the Muhammadan mission and truth. — reasons: Elmalili prefers a specific dawn of truth radiating mercy and light over a generic definite article.
93:1/q02 How is the final vowel of al-ḍuḥā written and explained? [وَٱلضُّحَىٰ · grammar] (turns on: Whether spelling follows spoken form, root origin or muṣḥaf and neighboring endings.)
  93:1/q02/p1 Its final alif is written in yāʾ shape in the muṣḥaf despite a wāw root. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says this follows neighboring endings, mostly yāʾ-derived, supports imāla in relevant dialects, and cites Ibn Malik. Nahhas notes inflected forms shift toward yāʾ.
  93:1/q02/p2 It should be written with final alif according to pronunciation. — reasons: Muhammad ibn Yazid derives it from ḍaḥā yaḍḥū and cites rāmā and ghazā. Nahhas rejects root-based Kufan spelling as inconsistent with speech and muṣḥaf. Abu Ishaq cites mā zakkā and rabayān against Qur'anic yarbū at 30:39.
  93:1/q02/p3 The yāʾ-root spelling convention was politically promoted. — reasons: Ali ibn Sulayman transmits an allegation that Akhfash and Kisa'i devised it for profit and influence and that Kisa'i's political power spread it.
93:1/q03 How is the ending of al-ḍuḥā recited? [وَٱلضُّحَىٰ · readings] (turns on: Different reader transmissions and whether the final alif behaves as yāʾ-derived.)
  93:1/q03/p1 Open pronunciation without imāla. — reasons: Ibn Kathir, Asim and Ibn Amir open endings here and in al-Layl; Ibn Khalawayh says the difference here is openness versus imāla. Some Nafi, Warsh and Qalun transmissions also open all, while one opens talāhā alone.
  93:1/q03/p2 Full imāla. — reasons: Kisa'i inclines these endings throughout; Hamza inclines yāʾ-origin forms but darkens wāw-origin forms. Abbas and a report from Abu Amr incline selected endings. Nahhas licenses inclination when a wāw root shifts to yāʾ. Farisi cites yāʾ-like behavior, derived verbs and nouns, and says rare inclined wāw forms do not overturn the general rule.
  93:1/q03/p3 Intermediate inclination. — reasons: Nahhas gives Medinan intermediate treatment; Farisi reports it for Nafi and Abu Amr, among variant transmissions. Abu Amr may follow the sūra's ending pattern.
93:1/q04 What is the grammatical object of the oath? [وَٱلضُّحَىٰ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the expressed time is itself the oath object or an omitted Lord is supplied.)
  93:1/q04/p1 An omitted 'Lord' is understood: by the Lord of morning and night. — reasons: Semantic scholars in Tha'labi and Qurtubi and a view in Tabrisi supply the ellipsis.
  93:1/q04/p2 Al-ḍuḥā itself is sworn by. — reasons: Ibn Khalawayh calls it an oath; Tabari identifies ḍuḥā as the oath object.
93:1/q05 Why is the forenoon singled out for an oath? [وَٱلضُّحَىٰ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether its sensory quality, a past event, or a divine sign supplies the point.)
  93:1/q05/p1 It recalls divine speech to Moses and the magicians' prostration. — reasons: Kashshaf, Razi, Nasafi, Bursawi, Alusi and Elmalili cite 20:59 and God's ability to honor Muhammad and change his enemies. Bint al-Shati rejects this appeal as anachronistic because the Moses passages were revealed later.
  93:1/q05/p2 It is the day's vigorous, bright and honored phase. — reasons: Razi says people gather and find ease after night, offering comfort after the revelation pause. Biqa'i calls it the brightest portion, suited to the most honored creature. Alusi calls it the sun's youthful daily honor; Islahi connects it with renewed work.
  93:1/q05/p3 Its light is a great divine sign and blessing. — reasons: Abduh emphasizes the light's grandeur. Ibn Kathir says the contrast shows Creator power. Ibn al-Qayyim appeals to lordship, wisdom and mercy, which Bint al-Shati says misses the occasion's abandonment claim.
93:1/q06 What does the oath form do rhetorically? [وَٱلضُّحَىٰ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the oath primarily magnifies its object or prepares the reader for an unseen point.)
  93:1/q06/p1 It glorifies what is sworn by as a great sign. — reasons: Bint al-Shati reports Ibn al-Qayyim's account as the prevailing earlier view; Abduh stresses the light's greatness.
  93:1/q06/p2 It directs attention to familiar senses to prepare for unseen meaning. — reasons: Bint al-Shati prefers this role for the wāw, and says any impression of glorification strengthens attention. The chosen sensory quality must suit context.
93:1/q07 What inward or symbolic reality is signified by ḍuḥā? [وَٱلضُّحَىٰ · inward] (turns on: The different spiritual correspondences assigned to daylight.)
  93:1/q07/p1 Spirit or God's unveiling of an inner secret. — reasons: Tustari identifies the spirit; Ibn Ata says God unveils the secret as the servant is occupied with calling people through attention to creation.
  93:1/q07/p2 The station of witnessing. — reasons: Junayd pairs it with night's inner-eye station or ghayn, clouding over the heart in a Prophetic report; Sulami and Bursawi transmit related forms.
  93:1/q07/p3 The Prophet's radiant face. — reasons: Bursawi's Persian couplet pairs face with hair. Bint al-Shati excludes this and other esoteric pairs including relatives, knowledge/forgiveness, and Islam's rise/estrangement.
  93:1/q07/p4 Divine disclosure, gentleness and beauty. — reasons: Kashf al-Asrar pairs it with night's veiling, severity and majesty.
  93:1/q07/p5 The Garden's light or the light of knowers' hearts. — reasons: Study Quran reports counterparts in the Fire's darkness and unbelievers' darkened hearts.
93:1/q08 What is the status and form of the ḍuḥā prayer? [وَٱلضُّحَىٰ · law] (turns on: The jurists differ over the number of units.)
  93:1/q08/p1 It is a sunnah from sun-high time until shortly before noon. — reasons: Bursawi calls this unanimous and cites a sound report that the Prophet prayed eight units in Umm Hani's house on the conquest of Mecca, having prayed it earlier too.
  93:1/q08/p2 Two or four units with one salutation. — reasons: Bursawi reports Abu Hanifa's prescription.
  93:1/q08/p3 No fixed upper limit. — reasons: Bursawi reports Malik's view.
  93:1/q08/p4 At least two, at most twelve units. — reasons: Bursawi reports Shafi'i's count.
  93:1/q08/p5 At least two, at most eight units. — reasons: Bursawi reports Ahmad's count, followed by most Shafi'i authorities and endorsed by Nawawi in al-Tahqiq.
  93:1/q08/p6 The two midmorning units may use al-Shams and al-Ḍuḥā. — reasons: Durr transmits Uqba ibn Amir's Prophetic instruction.
