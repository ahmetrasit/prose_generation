2:265/q09 How is ربوة read? [بِرَبْوَةٍ · readings] (turns on: Dialectal vowel and added-alif forms, with preference based on prevalence and plural morphology.)
  2:265/q09/p1 With ḍamma on rāʾ, rubwa. — reasons: Widely attributed to the Hijaz, Medina and Iraq; Abu Hatim, Abu Ubayd, Abu al-Hasan, al-Akhfash, al-Tabari and al-Tabrisi prefer it as common or because the plural rubā/rubā supports it. The same variation occurs in 23:50. Jishumi calls the damma form the more common Qurashi form and says the variation also occurs in 23:50.
  2:265/q09/p2 With fatḥa on rāʾ, rabwa. — reasons: Attributed to Asim and Ibn Amir and to Tamim; Ibn Khalawayh calls both principal forms eloquent dialects. Jishumi attributes this form to Asim and Ibn Amir in 23:50 as well.
  2:265/q09/p3 With kasra on rāʾ, ribwa. — reasons: Reported from Ibn Abbas and also Hasan, al-A'mash and Abu Ishaq; al-Tabari rejects it as a recitation. Jishumi transmits the kasra form from Ibn Abbas.
  2:265/q09/p4 Rabāwa with an added alif and fatḥa. — reasons: Attributed to Abu Ja'far, Abu Abd al-Rahman, Ibn Abbas and Abu Razin, with variants reported in 23:50.
  2:265/q09/p5 Rabāwa with added alif and kasra. — reasons: Attributed to al-Ashhab al-Uqayli; reports vary in spelling. Jishumi also transmits a ribawa form with kasra and added alif.
  2:265/q09/p6 A lengthened form with ḍamma. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi attributes this to Ubayy and Asim al-Jahdari.
2:265/q10 Why is the garden placed on a rabwa, and what setting is envisaged? [جَنَّةٍۭ بِرَبْوَةٍ · interpretation] (turns on: Elevation and water's agricultural effects, and whether a named region is intended.)
  2:265/q10/p1 Raised, fertile ground produces especially fine, abundant fruit. — reasons: Al-Tabari invokes richer soil and al-A'sha's upland verse. Al-Biqa'i cites sun and pollinating winds; Bursevi and Alusi cite mild, unstagnant air; al-Baydawi cites cool air. Ibn Atiyya rejects identifying Riyad al-Hazn as the precise setting, explaining Najd and Tihama differ; Abu Hayyan follows. Ibn Qutayba says orchards are generally best on heights; Jishumi describes a high, level site where streams run without flooding it and growth is greater.
  2:265/q10/p2 The image is the rain-fed, raised garden familiar in Arabia, with no stream in this instance. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya confines Ibn Abbas's no-rivers description to this garden, citing another raised place with a spring in 23:50; al-Qurtubi and Abu Hayyan agree the word need not exclude water universally.
  2:265/q10/p3 The fertile raised garden may have flowing water. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi and al-Baghawi describe level raised land supplied by water; Ibn Kathir reports streams in Ibn Abbas's and Dahhak's descriptions, while al-Qurtubi says a rabwa can have water. Jishumi describes streams running through the high, level site without inundating it.
  2:265/q10/p4 The parable's land is Egypt. — reasons: Zayd ibn Aslam says dew supports Egyptian soil and heavy rain doubles its yield. Al-Tha'labi's report instead says rain weakens it, a conflicting detail preserved here.
2:265/q11 What does وابل mean? [وَابِلٌۭ · meaning]
  2:265/q11/p1 A heavy, abundant downpour. — reasons: Al-Akhfash gives paired Arabic verb forms for raining and the adjective mobūla; al-Mawardi cites Adi ibn Zayd's verse; al-Tabari and al-Qurtubi describe intense rain.
2:265/q12 What is the syntax of أصابها وابل? [أَصَابَهَا وَابِلٌۭ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the rain clause is descriptive or circumstantial, and whether its antecedent is garden or ground.)
  2:265/q12/p1 It is a second adjective describing the garden. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and al-Samin put lasting location before temporary rainfall; al-Qurtubi says the pronoun points to the raised tract.
  2:265/q12/p2 It describes the rabwa, of which the garden is part. — reasons: Abu al-Baqa gives this alternative; al-Qurtubi takes the pronoun as the elevated ground.
  2:265/q12/p3 It is circumstantial from the garden or the pronoun within its location phrase. — reasons: Abu Hayyan allows a circumstantial clause; al-Samin specifies either the implicit pronoun or the now specified garden, with qad understood in the latter.
2:265/q13 What does آتت أكلها mean and how is آتت construed? [فَـَٔاتَتْ أُكُلَهَا · grammar] (turns on: Whether آتت retains 'gave' with an omitted recipient or means 'produced' with one object.)
  2:265/q13/p1 The garden gives its edible produce, with an implicit owner or people as first object and أكلها as second. — reasons: Al-Samin prefers the ordinary two-object construction and calls the giving metaphorical; Abu Hayyan supplies owner or people. Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi say the possessive is association, not garden ownership.
  2:265/q13/p2 آتت means produced or brought forth, taking أكلها as its sole object. — reasons: Abu al-Baqa offers this and Bursevi permits it; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin's al-Shaykh object that Arabic does not attest this meaning of آتت.
2:265/q14 What does أكلها mean? [أُكُلَهَا · meaning]
  2:265/q14/p1 Its edible fruit, crop, food or produce. — reasons: Al-Akhfash, al-Tabari and Abu al-Hasan distinguish food from the act of eating; the poet's meal/hunger line and 14:25 support edible produce. Al-Mufaddal broadens akl to what strengthens something, like yarn in a garment; Abu Zayd uses it for a person's livelihood. Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi explain the possessive as association. Jishumi distinguishes damma akl, food because it is eaten, from fatha akl, the act of eating; he reports al-Zajjaj's description of the fruit as what grows there and is eaten.
2:265/q15 How is أكلها read? [أُكُلَهَا · readings] (turns on: Pronunciation with or without the medial vowel; meaning remains produce.)
  2:265/q15/p1 The light form has sukūn on kāf in the feminine possessive. — reasons: Attributed to Ibn Kathir, Nafi and Abu Amr. Abu Amr differs from the first two for masculine and unpossessed forms; al-Khalawayh says lightening avoids consecutive dammas after suffixing. Qiraat-ER says the sense is unchanged.
  2:265/q15/p2 The heavy form keeps the kāf vowelled, with two ḍammas. — reasons: Attributed to Asim, Ibn Amir, Hamza and al-Kisa'i throughout; al-Khalawayh calls it the original form. Both pronunciations name produce. Jishumi calls the heavier form original.
2:265/q16 How much and how often does ضعفين signify? [ضِعْفَيْنِ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the dual counts amount, harvests, or repeated multiplication, and the baseline to which a double is compared.)
  2:265/q16/p1 Twice the ordinary or comparable garden's crop. — reasons: Most interpreters count an amount plus its equal; Ata and Ibn Abbas compare one year here with two elsewhere. Al-Qurtubi prefers twice the fruit of other land over two yearly harvests. The cause is the high ground plus heavy rain. Jishumi reports al-Zajjaj's account of a double as the original amount plus an addition; Abu Muslim compares the crop to this garden's customary yield, while al-Asamm and Abu Ali compare other gardens.
  2:265/q16/p2 Two harvests in one year. — reasons: Ikrima reports two fruitings; al-Tabrisi specifies six months apart and cites 14:25. Al-Qurtubi prefers a quantity comparison instead.
  2:265/q16/p3 Four times the ordinary crop. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i prefers this: each double is original plus its equal, so two doubles are four. Al-Tabrisi, al-Baydawi, Bursevi and others transmit the reckoning.
  2:265/q16/p4 Three times the ordinary crop. — reasons: Abu Muslim is reported by Abu Hayyan; Taj al-Qurra rejects it as unsupported in Arabic.
  2:265/q16/p5 Repeated or manyfold abundance, without an exact count. — reasons: Abu Hayyan favors repeated multiples to match multiplied good deeds; Ibn Ashur compares the dual to labbayk. Al-Alusi reports this reading.
2:265/q17 What grammatical role does ضعفين have? [ضِعْفَيْنِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether آتت has an omitted recipient and the doubled amount is a state, or the doubled amount is made its object.)
  2:265/q17/p1 It is a circumstantial accusative describing the crop as multiplied. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi and al-Baydawi parse it this way; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin reject treating it as a second object of آتت.
  2:265/q17/p2 It is the second object of آتت. — reasons: Reported as a proposal only; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin call it erroneous because the garden's produce is already the object.
2:265/q18 What does طل mean? [طَلٌّۭ · meaning] (turns on: Literal fine rain versus a reported figurative extension to dew.)
  2:265/q18/p1 Light, fine drizzle or gentle rain. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj describes persistent fine rain scarcely making runoff; al-Wahidi gives small drops and related forms; Juwair calls it rakk. Ibn Atiyya calls this common Arabic usage; al-Tabrisi records other lexical uses related to moisture. Al-Biqa'i describes dew as an early stage of rainfall. Jishumi contrasts tall's fine droplets with the larger, forceful drops of wabil and attributes gentle rain to al-Hasan, al-Dahhak, al-Rabi' and Qatada.
  2:265/q18/p2 Dew or nighttime moisture on plants. — reasons: Mujahid, Ibn Abbas, al-Suddi and others report dew; Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and al-Samin call this figurative compared with fine rain. Al-Qummi specifies nighttime dew. Jishumi attributes the dew gloss to al-Suddi.
