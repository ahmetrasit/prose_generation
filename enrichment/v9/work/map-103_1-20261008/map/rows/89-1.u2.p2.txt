89:1/q14 What reports concern reciting Surat al-Fajr? [* · reports] (turns on: The reports describe distinct merits and uses.)
  89:1/q14/p1 Recitation brings forgiveness; daily recitation brings light on Resurrection Day. — reasons: Jishumi transmits the attributed Prophetic promise through Ubayy ibn Kab.
  89:1/q14/p2 The Prophet recommended it among short suras for communal prayer. — reasons: Ibn Kathir and Durr recount a worshipper leaving Muadh's long prayer and the Prophet rebuking Muadh for driving people away; the suggested chapters also include 87:1, 91:1 and 92:1.
89:1/q15 What Muharram and Ashura reports accompany this dawn interpretation? [* · reports] (turns on: These are distinct accompanying reports; the eye-treatment report is explicitly weakened.)
  89:1/q15/p1 Muharram and Ashura are especially recommended for fasting. — reasons: Durr reports the Prophet calling Muharram God's month and best for fasting after Ramadan, with night prayer best after obligatory prayer. Ali recommends Muharram because of repentance and forgiveness. Reports commend an adjacent day to Ashura or ninth and tenth to differ from Jews. Ibn Shihab fasted while traveling because Ashura would pass; Abu Musa and Abu Hurayra connect its fast to Jewish festivity and prophets' practice.
  89:1/q15/p2 Generosity to one's family on Ashura brings provision through the year. — reasons: Durr gives reports through Jabir, Ibn Masud, Abu Said and Abu Hurayra and a traditional saying; Bayhaqi says individually weak chains support one another.
  89:1/q15/p3 Antimony on the eyes on Ashura prevents inflammation forever. — reasons: Durr reports it through Urwa from Ibn Abbas and notes Bayhaqi weakened it.
89:1/q16 What do attached reports say the ten nights, even and odd denote? [* · reports] (turns on: They differ over even and odd: pilgrimage days or creation and God.)
  89:1/q16/p1 The ten are Dhu al-Hijja's first ten, odd is Arafah and even is Sacrifice Day. — reasons: Thalabi and Durr transmit Jabir's report from the Prophet.
  89:1/q16/p2 The ten are the ten of sacrifice, even is created pairs and odd is God. — reasons: A report through Atiyya and Abu Said links pairs to 78:8 and God's oneness to 112:1; Durr gives a related report citing 78:8.
89:1/q17 How do poetic comparisons picture dawn against night? [ٱلْفَجْرِ · rhetoric]
  89:1/q17/p1 Dawn appears as a small bright mark, band, or trim against a much wider dark field. — reasons: Jurjani says the resemblance to a horse's blaze, black silk's white mark, Ibn al-Mutazz's brocade, or trim on night rests on contrast, not equal size or brightness. He notes a poet's intensified ink-black night and compares the principle with sun, mirror, and coin similes that select shape, gleam, or color; such comparisons can be reversed.
89:1/q18 What does the Gabriel visit report say in the surrounding revelation discussion? [* · reports]
  89:1/q18/p1 Gabriel appeared to Umm Salama as Dihya al-Kalbi and was later identified by the Prophet. — reasons: Abu Uthman reports the visit from Usama ibn Zayd; Ibn Kathir says Bukhari and Muslim transmit additional routes.
89:1/q19 How is al-Fajr counted among short verses, and how are verse boundaries determined? [* · sciences] (turns on: Different counting conventions and judgments about the shortest verse.)
  89:1/q19/p1 Al-Fajr is a one-word verse and among the shortest verses, often paired with al-Duha. — reasons: Al-Zarkashi counts al-Fajr, al-Duha, and al-Asr as one-word verses, says al-Duha followed by al-Fajr is shortest, and compares one-word openings for verse-ending sound. Al-Suyuti reports an authority pairing al-Duha and al-Fajr as shortest. Ibn Ashur says al-Fajr implies an oath verb, so a verse can be complete by ellipsis.
  89:1/q19/p2 'Then he looked' is shortest; Amma, al-Fajr, and al-Duha are equal in shortness. — reasons: Al-Mawsili gives this alternative ranking, reported by al-Suyuti.
  89:1/q19/p3 Verse boundaries are transmitted, with differences arising from pauses and continuation. — reasons: Al-Zarkashi reports al-Zamakhshari saying boundaries are fixed by revelation rather than analogy, and says a prophetic pause could be mistaken for a boundary when he sometimes continued. Ibn Ashur says the Companions knew transmitted divisions but reports can differ over where adjoining verses divide; he compares conventionally counted opening letters.
89:1/q20 What is the shortest Quranic word, and what counts as a word? [* · sciences] (turns on: What is counted as an independent word.)
  89:1/q20/p1 The one-letter preposition bi is shorter than two-letter candidates. — reasons: Al-Zarkashi rejects al-Dani's two-letter minimum and notes that literal sense, figurative sense, written form, and convention can yield differing word counts; he otherwise describes a word as a single utterance of two to ten letters.
89:1/q21 How do sound and meaning govern the endings of al-Fajr? [* · rhetoric]
  89:1/q21/p1 The endings may match in sound when the wording remains natural and led by meaning. — reasons: Al-Zarkashi reports al-Zamakhshari's warning against sound ornament that distorts structure or exclusivity and says natural identical or close fawasil are praiseworthy; the ya in yasr is dropped for the endings. Bint al-Shati reports the same standard and al-Rummani's dispute over saj terminology, with al-Khafaji's explanation for calling such endings fawasil.
89:1/q22 How should al-fajr's consonants be articulated in recitation? [ٱلْفَجْرِ · readings] (turns on: The final ra changes with connected kasra and stopping.)
  89:1/q22/p1 Its jim exemplifies the strong, voiced qalqala consonants. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari says a quiescent ba and the other qalqala letters retain plosive strength and voicing; he names al-fajr as an example. His surrounding articulation discussion also treats emphatic alifs, smooth initial hamza, and light ba before emphatics.
  89:1/q22/p2 Its final ra is light with kasra in connected speech, but emphatic at a stop is preferred. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari says every reader lightens a kasra-bearing ra, citing al-fajr, and prefers tafkhim when stopping on it. He contrasts ra with imala or intermediate reading against full fath and surveys moving, quiescent, rounded, and kasra-bearing ra; for other rounded ra after kasra or silent ya he reports competing emphatic and light treatments, preferring the latter, with disputed examples.
89:1/q23 What pause or basmala is used between al-Fajr and the following sura? [* · readings] (turns on: The reader's usual practice in joining or pausing between suras.)
  89:1/q23/p1 Some readers who generally join suras make a brief pause at this transition. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari lists this among four transitions, to avoid awkward adjoining phrases such as 'Enter My garden, No'; special sakt presumes joining elsewhere.
  89:1/q23/p2 Some readers who generally pause use the basmala at this transition. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari names Ibn Ghalbun, Makki, and others; he says special basmala presumes pausing elsewhere and cannot be inserted where transmitted joining excludes it.
  89:1/q23/p3 Most authorities make no special exception for this transition. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari names Faris ibn Ahmad, Ibn Sufyan, Abu al-Tahir, the Iraqis, and al-Dani among those making no distinction.
89:1/q24 How long is the vowel when stopping with incidental sukun as in al-fajr? [ٱلْفَجْرِ · readings] (turns on: Whether stopping sukun counts like a necessary second sukun.)
  89:1/q24/p1 Fully extend it as a meeting of two sukuns. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari reports early Egyptian teachers through al-Dani, al-Shatibi's choice, and Ibn al-Fahham's exceptional full extension route from Hisham; he reasons that necessary-sukun madd receives full extension.
  89:1/q24/p2 Use medium length because the stopping sukun is incidental. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari attributes this to Ibn Mujahid, his students, al-Dani, and other routes, balancing the two sukuns with the accidental pause.
  89:1/q24/p3 Shorten it because the incidental stopping sukun does not count. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari reports Abu al-Hasan al-Husri's view and examples al-qadr and al-fajr.
89:1/q25 Where does al-Fajr stand in codex ordering and textual-history reports? [* · sciences] (turns on: How reported personal codices relate to the received order and collection.)
  89:1/q25/p1 Codex reports place al-Fajr differently, while the present verse and sura order is held to be prophetic. — reasons: Al-Suyuti reports Ibn Masud placing al-Fajr before al-Asr among the mufassal suras; Alusi gives a codex order placing it after al-Tahrim. Alusi holds the received verse and sura order prophetic and the present 114-sura collection accepted, while describing Ibn Masud's 112-sura and Ubayy's 115-sura codices. He says Quranic material was memorized and separately written in the Prophet's lifetime, rejecting proof of a single bound codex then; he also reports disputed additional text and recitation reports.
89:1/q26 Is al-Fajr classified as containing abrogation? [* · sciences] (turns on: Whether the broad sura categories withstand the detailed abrogation cases.)
  89:1/q26/p1 One classification says it contains neither abrogating nor abrogated material. — reasons: Al-Suyuti reports this classification, which places al-Asr in a different category containing both.
  89:1/q26/p2 That four-part sura classification is open to question. — reasons: Al-Suyuti rejects uncritical use of it; his surrounding discussion distinguishes several kinds of changed obligation and three kinds of abrogation, including the debated nursing report and cases where a ruling ends but recitation remains.
