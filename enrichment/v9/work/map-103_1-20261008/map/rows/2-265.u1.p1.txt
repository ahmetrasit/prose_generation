  2:265/q10/p1 Raised, fertile ground produces especially fine, abundant fruit. — reasons: Al-Tabari invokes richer soil and al-A'sha's upland verse. Al-Biqa'i cites sun and pollinating winds; Bursevi and Alusi cite mild, unstagnant air; al-Baydawi cites cool air. Ibn Atiyya rejects identifying Riyad al-Hazn as the precise setting, explaining Najd and Tihama differ; Abu Hayyan follows.
  2:265/q10/p2 The image is the rain-fed, raised garden familiar in Arabia, with no stream in this instance. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya confines Ibn Abbas's no-rivers description to this garden, citing another raised place with a spring in 23:50; al-Qurtubi and Abu Hayyan agree the word need not exclude water universally.
  2:265/q10/p3 The fertile raised garden may have flowing water. — reasons: Al-Tha'labi and al-Baghawi describe level raised land supplied by water; Ibn Kathir reports streams in Ibn Abbas's and Dahhak's descriptions, while al-Qurtubi says a rabwa can have water.
  2:265/q10/p4 The parable's land is Egypt. — reasons: Zayd ibn Aslam says dew supports Egyptian soil and heavy rain doubles its yield. Al-Tha'labi's report instead says rain weakens it, a conflicting detail preserved here.
2:265/q11 What does وابل mean? [وَابِلٌۭ · meaning]
  2:265/q11/p1 A heavy, abundant downpour. — reasons: Al-Akhfash gives paired Arabic verb forms for raining and the adjective mobūla; al-Mawardi cites Adi ibn Zayd's verse; al-Tabari and al-Qurtubi describe intense rain.
2:265/q12 What is the syntax of أصابها وابل? [أَصَابَهَا وَابِلٌۭ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the rain clause is descriptive or circumstantial, and whether its antecedent is garden or ground.)
  2:265/q12/p1 It is a second adjective describing the garden. — reasons: Abu Hayyan and al-Samin put lasting location before temporary rainfall; al-Qurtubi says the pronoun points to the raised tract.
  2:265/q12/p2 It describes the rabwa, of which the garden is part. — reasons: Abu al-Baqa gives this alternative; al-Qurtubi takes the pronoun as the elevated ground.
  2:265/q12/p3 It is circumstantial from the garden or the pronoun within its location phrase. — reasons: Abu Hayyan allows a circumstantial clause; al-Samin specifies either the implicit pronoun or the now specified garden, with qad understood in the latter.
2:265/q13 What does آتت أكلها mean and how is آتت construed? [فَـَٔاتَتْ أُكُلَهَا · grammar] (turns on: Whether آتت retains 'gave' with an omitted recipient or means 'produced' with one object.)
  2:265/q13/p1 The garden gives its edible produce, with an implicit owner or people as first object and أكلها as second. — reasons: Al-Samin prefers the ordinary two-object construction and calls the giving metaphorical; Abu Hayyan supplies owner or people. Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi say the possessive is association, not garden ownership.
  2:265/q13/p2 آتت means produced or brought forth, taking أكلها as its sole object. — reasons: Abu al-Baqa offers this and Bursevi permits it; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin's al-Shaykh object that Arabic does not attest this meaning of آتت.
2:265/q14 What does أكلها mean? [أُكُلَهَا · meaning]
  2:265/q14/p1 Its edible fruit, crop, food or produce. — reasons: Al-Akhfash, al-Tabari and Abu al-Hasan distinguish food from the act of eating; the poet's meal/hunger line and 14:25 support edible produce. Al-Mufaddal broadens akl to what strengthens something, like yarn in a garment; Abu Zayd uses it for a person's livelihood. Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi explain the possessive as association.
2:265/q15 How is أكلها read? [أُكُلَهَا · readings] (turns on: Pronunciation with or without the medial vowel; meaning remains produce.)
  2:265/q15/p1 The light form has sukūn on kāf in the feminine possessive. — reasons: Attributed to Ibn Kathir, Nafi and Abu Amr. Abu Amr differs from the first two for masculine and unpossessed forms; al-Khalawayh says lightening avoids consecutive dammas after suffixing. Qiraat-ER says the sense is unchanged.
  2:265/q15/p2 The heavy form keeps the kāf vowelled, with two ḍammas. — reasons: Attributed to Asim, Ibn Amir, Hamza and al-Kisa'i throughout; al-Khalawayh calls it the original form. Both pronunciations name produce.
2:265/q16 How much and how often does ضعفين signify? [ضِعْفَيْنِ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the dual counts amount, harvests, or repeated multiplication, and the baseline to which a double is compared.)
  2:265/q16/p1 Twice the ordinary or comparable garden's crop. — reasons: Most interpreters count an amount plus its equal; Ata and Ibn Abbas compare one year here with two elsewhere. Al-Qurtubi prefers twice the fruit of other land over two yearly harvests. The cause is the high ground plus heavy rain.
  2:265/q16/p2 Two harvests in one year. — reasons: Ikrima reports two fruitings; al-Tabrisi specifies six months apart and cites 14:25. Al-Qurtubi prefers a quantity comparison instead.
  2:265/q16/p3 Four times the ordinary crop. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i prefers this: each double is original plus its equal, so two doubles are four. Al-Tabrisi, al-Baydawi, Bursevi and others transmit the reckoning.
  2:265/q16/p4 Three times the ordinary crop. — reasons: Abu Muslim is reported by Abu Hayyan; Taj al-Qurra rejects it as unsupported in Arabic.
  2:265/q16/p5 Repeated or manyfold abundance, without an exact count. — reasons: Abu Hayyan favors repeated multiples to match multiplied good deeds; Ibn Ashur compares the dual to labbayk. Al-Alusi reports this reading.
2:265/q17 What grammatical role does ضعفين have? [ضِعْفَيْنِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether آتت has an omitted recipient and the doubled amount is a state, or the doubled amount is made its object.)
  2:265/q17/p1 It is a circumstantial accusative describing the crop as multiplied. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi and al-Baydawi parse it this way; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin reject treating it as a second object of آتت.
  2:265/q17/p2 It is the second object of آتت. — reasons: Reported as a proposal only; Abu Hayyan and al-Samin call it erroneous because the garden's produce is already the object.
2:265/q18 What does طل mean? [طَلٌّۭ · meaning] (turns on: Literal fine rain versus a reported figurative extension to dew.)
  2:265/q18/p1 Light, fine drizzle or gentle rain. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj describes persistent fine rain scarcely making runoff; al-Wahidi gives small drops and related forms; Juwair calls it rakk. Ibn Atiyya calls this common Arabic usage; al-Tabrisi records other lexical uses related to moisture. Al-Biqa'i describes dew as an early stage of rainfall.
  2:265/q18/p2 Dew or nighttime moisture on plants. — reasons: Mujahid, Ibn Abbas, al-Suddi and others report dew; Ibn Atiyya, Abu Hayyan and al-Samin call this figurative compared with fine rain. Al-Qummi specifies nighttime dew.
2:265/q19 How does فإن لم يصبها وابل فطل work grammatically? [فَإِن لَّمْ يُصِبْهَا وَابِلٌۭ فَطَلٌّۭ · grammar] (turns on: Which constituent is omitted after the conditional, and whether any inversion is necessary.)
  2:265/q19/p1 An omitted verb or predicate completes the condition: if no downpour strikes, drizzle does or suffices. — reasons: Al-Farra and al-Tabari supply kāna or a prior event and cite a poem; al-Wahidi compares a one-or-two-slave example. Al-Mubarrad supplies 'rain is enough'; al-Zajjaj says 'what reaches it is light rain.' Abu Hayyan and al-Samin set out subject/predicate and omitted-verb parses, with Imru al-Qays's line as parallel.
  2:265/q19/p2 The order is inverted, so yielding follows either downpour or drizzle. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan and al-Samin report and reject this as unnecessary; ordinary order already conveys the sense.
2:265/q20 What happens if only طل reaches the garden? [فَإِن لَّمْ يُصِبْهَا وَابِلٌۭ فَطَلٌّۭ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether light moisture preserves the same multiplied crop or produces a smaller yet reliable one.)
  2:265/q20/p1 The fertile garden still gives a full or doubled yield; little moisture suffices. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf invokes fertile soil; al-Baghawi says steady light dew can do a downpour's work. Razi and Abu Hayyan offer full-yield possibilities; al-Wahidi and al-Nasafi say the crop remains abundant.
  2:265/q20/p2 The garden yields less than under heavy rain, but still yields something beneficial. — reasons: Al-Mawardi argues dew-fed crops are weaker than rain-fed crops; Razi allows this alternative; Ibn Ashur links varying yield to varying sincerity.
2:265/q21 What does the orchard teach about sincere giving? [* · interpretation]
  2:265/q21/p1 Sincere charity or a believer's deed remains fruitful and multiplied, whether the gift is large or small. — reasons: Qatada says God's promise never fails; al-Suddi connects doubled fruit to doubled reward. Al-Mawardi urges giving even a little. Razi, al-Alusi and Ibn Ashur say degree depends on sincerity, effort, attachment to wealth, recipient need and accompanying circumstances. Al-Qummi's lawful-date report and al-Qurtubi's Abu Hurayra report depict God nurturing a date to mountain size.
2:265/q22 What does God's seeing deeds convey? [وَٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ · interpretation]
  2:265/q22/p1 God knows outward deeds and inward motives, records and recompenses them; the close promises reward and warns against display. — reasons: Al-Tabari distinguishes sincere from hurtful giving and warns against neglected obligations; Ibn Atiyya, al-Qurtubi and others see both promise and warning. Razi notes amount and manner; al-Biqa'i urges improvement of inward and outward work. Al-Khattabi says basir's form functions as an active participle.
2:265/q23 How is تعملون read, and whom does it address? [تَعْمَلُونَ · readings] (turns on: A change in grammatical person and the antecedent of the third-person pronoun.)
  2:265/q23/p1 Second-person تعملون addresses the audience directly. — reasons: The majority reading shifts from third-person narration to direct address, making God's knowledge a promise and warning.
