12:41/q15 Does interpretation itself make a dream's predicted event binding? [قُضِىَ ٱلْأَمْرُ · theology] (turns on: Whether interpretation normally fixes a dream's fulfillment or Joseph's fulfilled words were a special prophetic confirmation.)
  12:41/q15/p1 A dream is suspended until interpreted and then follows its first interpretation, even if invented. — reasons: Abu Ruzayn's Prophetic report adds that a dream is one forty-sixth of prophecy and should be shared only with a discerning person; Anas reports it follows the first interpreter. Ibn Kathir cites Ahmad through Muawiya ibn Hayda and Abu Ya'la through Anas and extends the rule to invented dreams. Al-Alusi repeats the bird's-wing maxim: once told and interpreted, it falls. Al-Durr's Ibn Masud report says the test dreams' interpretation fixed the outcome.
  12:41/q15/p2 A false interpretation does not bind someone; Joseph's case was exceptional divine confirmation. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi says God made Joseph's interpretation occur to confirm his prophethood. His Qatada report has Umar predict alternating faith and apostasy, ending in unbelief, from a claimed dream of alternating growth and drought; the man then denied dreaming. Al-Qurtubi treats Umar as an exceptional divinely inspired person and cites two further accurate intuitions.
12:41/q16 What did the prisoners say after hearing Joseph's interpretation? [قُضِىَ ٱلْأَمْرُ ٱلَّذِى فِيهِ تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ · reports] (turns on: Whether both men invented the dreams, both truly dreamed and later denied it, or only one dreamer lied or recanted.)
  12:41/q16/p1 Both admitted they had fabricated dreams to test Joseph or play with him. — reasons: Ibn Masud reports this through al-Tabari, Ibn Abi Hatim, al-Thalabi, al-Baghawi, Ibn Kathir, al-Durr and al-Alusi; some say they had seen nothing, and Joseph replied the decision stood. Al-Zamakhshari reports the test; Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan include it among alternatives. Al-Alusi suggests the cupbearer could have lied to protect his companion.
  12:41/q16/p2 Both had real dreams but denied them after the unwelcome interpretation. — reasons: Mujahid says the true predictions had already taken effect when the youths claimed play. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan report real dreams followed by denial; al-Razi gives displeasure with the answer as a motive. Al-Suddi reports panic; Ibn Kathir's Ibn Masud report includes their later denial. Al-Baydawi says they accused Joseph of lying.
  12:41/q16/p3 Only the condemned baker denied or invented his dream. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj says the condemned man denied dreaming; al-Mawardi reports his protest that they were playing. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan preserve the only-condemned-man variant; al-Nasafi says the baker denied. An Imami report says the second prisoner admitted inventing his dream; the report of Abu Jafar identifies him as the baker. Al-Alusi also reports this variant.
  12:41/q16/p4 One prisoner lied about a dream, without identifying which. — reasons: Abu Majliz gives the unspecified one-prisoner report.
  12:41/q16/p5 The men denied having seen dreams, without a settled account of whether they had fabricated them. — reasons: Al-Farra reports denial after the crucifixion forecast; al-Tabari's Ibn Masud report, Ibn Abi Hatim's al-Suddi and Ibn Masud reports, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, al-Biqa'i and al-Durr's Qatada also describe denial. Al-Biqa'i says Joseph rebutted the implication of ignorance or error; al-Alusi allows his answer to rebut denial or confirm interpretation.
12:41/q17 What does the inquiry verb تستفتيان mean? [تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ · meaning]
  12:41/q17/p1 It means asking for a fatwa or an answer resolving the matter. — reasons: Al-Jishumi calls istifta asking for a legal opinion and says a fatwa answers the question itself, not its reasoning. Al-Rummani in al-Biqa'i distinguishes an answer about meaning from one giving the underlying cause. Ibn Ashur describes a fatwa as an explanation resolving doubt and says some connect aftā, whose hamza remains, to fatā, youth, because clarity strengthens the listener. Al-Wahidi points to his earlier gloss in al-Nisa.
12:41/q18 Why is the inquiry expressed in the present tense? [تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ · rhetoric]
  12:41/q18/p1 It keeps their request ongoing until Joseph completes the answer. — reasons: Al-Alusi's cited investigator says the wording magnifies the obscure, weighty dreams as an affair requiring inquiry.
12:41/q19 How do these two dreams connect to the king's later dream? [* · links]
  12:41/q19/p1 They may foreshadow its fertile and barren images. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i compares pressed grapes with the king's green ears and fat cattle, and birds eating the baker's bread with dry ears and lean cattle in 12:43.
12:41/q20 What inward sense is assigned to the cupbearer's fate? [فَيَسْقِى رَبَّهُۥ خَمْرًۭا · inward]
  12:41/q20/p1 After restraint from association, attachment to pleasures is imposed on the spirit. — reasons: Al-Qashani gives this inward reading of the cupbearer's fate.
12:41/q21 What inward sense is assigned to the baker's crucifixion? [فَيُصْلَبُ فَتَأْكُلُ ٱلطَّيْرُ مِن رَّأْسِهِۦ · inward]
  12:41/q21/p1 Desire is suppressed and fixed to vegetative nature until it dies and the soul's powers fulfill divine rights. — reasons: Al-Qashani says imagination and animal faculties can no longer act through the bound desire.
12:41/q22 What inward sense is assigned to the declared outcome? [قُضِىَ ٱلْأَمْرُ · inward]
  12:41/q22/p1 It marks Joseph's approach to God and sainthood emerging through annihilation in God. — reasons: Al-Qashani gives this inward reading of their declared outcome.
12:41/q23 How does Joseph's interpretation fit his preaching to the prisoners? [* · links]
  12:41/q23/p1 He used their question as an opportunity to preach monotheism before interpreting their dreams. — reasons: Islahi says prophets seize opportunities to invite others to God's message. Kuranyolu reports Ibn Ashur's account of Egyptians having about thirty deities representing natural forces or stars, giving the religious context.
12:41/q24 What larger lesson does the Joseph story illustrate? [* · links]
  12:41/q24/p1 God directs human affairs beyond people's understanding; they may hate what benefits them or love what harms them. — reasons: Asad contrasts this Qur'anic purpose with Genesis' romantic account of Joseph's fortunes and cites 2:216.
12:41/q25 What does the source's cited form related to عشوة mean? [* · meaning]
  12:41/q25/p1 It may be a plural of عشوة, meaning darkness. — reasons: Ibn Jinni says the darkness has distinct portions, motivating the plural.
12:41/q26 What does Qushayri draw from the prisoners' shared condition and opposed fates? [* · interpretation]
  12:41/q26/p1 They shared a question and imprisonment, but one was killed while the other gained favor and elevation. — reasons: Al-Qushayri highlights their opposed outcomes despite their shared situation.
12:41/q27 What does the isolated Ibn Jurayj citation add about the inquiry? [تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ · reports]
  12:41/q27/p1 It repeats the inquiry phrase without offering another gloss. — reasons: Al-Tabari records Ibn Jurayj's quotation only.
12:41/q28 Why did Joseph delay interpreting the dreams? [* · reports]
  12:41/q28/p1 He knew one interpretation foretold execution and first spoke about food to prompt a repeated request. — reasons: Ibn Abi Hatim reports Ibn Jurayj citing Muhammad ibn Abbas: a royal meal was sent to the prisoner intended for death.
12:41/q29 What do the prisoners ask Joseph to interpret? [تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ · interpretation]
  12:41/q29/p1 Their visions, which point to one man's rescue and the other's destruction. — reasons: Al-Alusi says the inquiry requests interpretation of their visions and the two predictions give their opposed outcomes.
12:41/q30 How forceful is the declaration that the matter has been decided? [قُضِىَ ٱلْأَمْرُ · interpretation]
  12:41/q30/p1 The requested outcome is inevitable. — reasons: Al-Suddi, as reported by Ibn Abi Hatim, glosses Joseph's declaration as inevitability. Ibn al-Jawzi explicitly assigns this verse the sense of an irrevocably determined decree, comparing 19:21, 34:14 and 39:42.

He prefers the subjunctive in one example to keep both clauses unified.
