[ALUSI:103:1/r11] ALUSI, d. 1270 · الإمام | rejects | Al-Imām recounts a woman's adultery, killing her child, and sale of vinegar, followed by a Prophetic answer about punishments and neglected ʿAṣr; al-Ālūsī says hadith authorities do not rely on this account.
[ALUSI:103:1/r12] ALUSI, d. 1270 · قتادة | reports | Qatāda takes ʿaṣr as the afternoon period, an oath by the time of Adam's creation on Friday and by its signs of divine power, like al-Ḍuḥā.
[ALUSI:103:1/r13] ALUSI, d. 1270 · الزجاج | reports | Al-Zajjāj says ʿaṣr can mean day or night, citing Ḥumayd ibn Thawr's verse about the two ʿaṣrs.
[ALUSI:103:1/r14] ALUSI, d. 1270 · قيل | reports | Another view takes ʿaṣr as morning and evening together; on this and the day-or-night view, the oath is by one of the two without specifying which.
[ALUSI:103:1/r15] ALUSI, d. 1270 · قيل | reports | Some take the oath by the era of Prophethood, especially the Prophet's lifetime, the noblest era because of his honor.
[ALUSI:103:1/r16] ALUSI, d. 1270 · قيل | reports | Another view extends the Prophet's lifetime to the Hour, likening the remaining history of his community to the afternoon span before sunset.
[ALUSI:103:1/r17] ALUSI, d. 1270 · سالم بن عبد الله | reports | Salim reports from his father that the Muslim community's remaining time among earlier nations is like the interval from ʿAṣr to sunset; al-Bukhārī transmits it.
[ALUSI:103:1/r18] ALUSI, d. 1270 · ابن عباس | reports | Ibn ʿAbbās says ʿaṣr means time itself, encompassing extraordinary events and thus reminding people of blessings and their opposites.
[ALUSI:103:1/r19] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | The oath recalls time's blessings and opposites, but the next verse assigns loss to people, not time; this rebuts blaming time for events, though using ʿaṣr for dahr seems unclear. | mentions 103:2
[ALUSI:103:1/r2] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | reports | Al-Ālūsī reports Hibat Allāh’s view that half the sūra came in Mecca about al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil and half in Medina about ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy.
[ALUSI:103:1/r3] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | The sūra has three verses, without disagreement.
[ALUSI:103:1/r4] ALUSI, d. 1270 · الشافعي | reports | Al-Shāfiʿī says that even if no other sūra had been revealed, this one would suffice because it contains all Qur'anic sciences.
[ALUSI:103:1/r5] ALUSI, d. 1270 · أبي حذيفة | reports | Abū Ḥudhayfa, a Companion, reports that two Companions would recite this sūra to each other before parting and exchanging salām; al-Ṭabarānī and al-Bayhaqī transmit it.
[ALUSI:103:1/r6] ALUSI, d. 1270 · author | holds | Placed after al-Takāthur, this sūra alludes to the state of people whom rivalry in abundance did not distract.
[ALUSI:103:1/r7] ALUSI, d. 1270 · مقاتل | reports | Muqātil says the oath is by the afternoon prayer, which most authorities identify as the middle prayer, supported by a Prophetic report and Ḥafṣa's codex.
[ALUSI:103:1/r8] ALUSI, d. 1270 · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | A Prophetic report says the opponents distracted them from the middle prayer, namely the afternoon prayer.
[ALUSI:103:1/r9] ALUSI, d. 1270 · رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم | reports | A hadith likens missing the afternoon prayer to losing one's family and property.
[ALUSI:103:3/r13] ALUSI, d. 1270 · علي | reports | A report attributes to ʿAlī a reading adding nawāʾib al-dahr and extending the loss statement to the end of time.

[ALUSI:103:3/r8] ALUSI, d. 1270 · سلام وهرون وابن موسى عن أبي عمرو | reports | Salām, Hārūn, and Ibn Mūsā from Abū ʿAmr read al-ʿaṣr with a kasra on ṣād and al-ṣabr with a kasra on bāʾ; Ibn ʿAṭiyya allows this only at pause by vowel transfer.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r1] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The surah is called Surat al-Asr in many Qurans, most tafsirs, and a fifth-century Kufic Qayrawani codex; some tafsirs and Sahih al-Bukhari title it Surat wa-l-Asr, following its first word.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r10] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Whichever sense of al-asr is intended, it must denote a state that points to a divine act, established through an implied adjunct, contextual clue, or prior reference; as time it recalls God's power in creation and momentous blessings.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r11] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | The most familiar sense is the late-afternoon period, from when an object's shadow equals its noon shadow plus its length until it reaches twice its length beyond the noon shadow, around yellowing and the start of evening.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r12] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · الحارث بن حلزة | reports | Al-Harith ibn Hilliza's verse uses asran for the late-afternoon period approaching evening.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r13] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Late afternoon recalls the nearing end of day, the sun and earth, Earth's orbit producing night and day, and changing atmospheric conditions, like the oaths by duha, night, day, and dawn.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r14] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | It is when people prepare to stop work in fields, gardens, and markets, recalling social order and God's inspiration of work rhythms; they return home to family, and the time is a blessing.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r15] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | The day's decline also hints at human life nearing its end after youth, maturity, and old age; on the general-time reading, the article means every age.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r16] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | Al-asr can mean the highly honored afternoon prayer, possibly the middle prayer in the cited verse; two hadith reports warn of missing it and of swearing a false sales oath after it. | mentions 2:238
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r17] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | On the prayer reading, the article is definite by reference and the word has become a conventional name, like al-aqaba.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r18] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | Al-asr can mean an era identified by a generation, ruler, prophet, or religion, such as the eras of Ibrahim, Alexander, or Jahiliyya; it could here mean the Prophet's own era.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r19] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | On the Prophet's-era reading, the article refers to the present era, like saying today; the oath resembles God's oath by the Prophet's life and by the sacred city where he is present, as Fakhr explains. | mentions 15:72, 90:2
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r2] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | Most authorities consider it Meccan; Qatada, Mujahid, and Muqatil are reported to consider it Medinan.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r20] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | Another possibility is the whole Islamic era, the final religious age of this world; a Prophetic parable compares Muslims, Jews, and Christians with workers hired through a day, with Muslims completing the remaining time after afternoon and receiving both groups' full wages.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r21] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | holds | That parable may be connected to the surah's symbolic reference to the Islamic era.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r22] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | reports | Al-asr may also mean all time; Ibn Atiyya reports Ubayy ibn Ka'b asking the Prophet about it and receiving the answer that the Lord swears by the latter part of the day.
[IBNASHUR:103:1/r23] IBNASHUR, d. 1393 · author | prefers | Reading al-asr as the Islamic era especially suits the surah, which distinguishes people in that era by disbelief or faith and by how fully they practice Islam; pre-Islamic peoples differed in access to messengers and held superseded or idolatrous religions. | mentions 3:85
