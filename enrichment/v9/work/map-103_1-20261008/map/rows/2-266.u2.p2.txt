2:266/q24 What does thus refer back to and what are the signs explained? [كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ ٱللَّهُ لَكُمُ ٱلْءَايَٰتِ · referent] (turns on: How broad the backward reference is.)
  2:266/q24/p1 The preceding parables and disclosures, including spending, Abraham and the ruined town, clarify religious proofs. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj includes charity and struggle and says these establish oneness, messengership, reward and punishment. Al-Wahidi cites 2:258-259 for oneness; al-Tabrisi includes all preceding disclosures. Ibn Atiyya says thus points to the parables just described. Bursavi includes striving, spending, Abraham and Ezra. Al-Nasafi speaks of monotheism and religion. Ibn Ashur says concrete images bring abstract truths near, citing 2:143; Abu Hayyan lists allusion, simile, ellipsis, specification, parable and metaphor. Jishumi says the comparative kaf likens this clarification to the preceding contrast of the ostentatious and sincere giver at 2:264-265; he also reports that the clarified signs are proofs pointing to the truth.
  2:266/q24/p2 It points specifically to what has just been mentioned. — reasons: Sa'id ibn Jubayr gives this gloss; al-Samin says the closing phrase was discussed earlier.
2:266/q25 What reflection does the closing appeal seek? [لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَفَكَّرُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Which lesson of the parable the reflective command highlights.)
  2:266/q25/p1 Understand the parables, take warning or guidance, and act or obey accordingly. — reasons: Mujahid glosses reflection as obedience; Qatada cites 29:43 that the learned understand parables. Al-Mawardi gives taking a lesson or finding guidance, while Ibn Kathir and al-Durr urge application. Al-Biqai and al-Harali say worship needs thought about beginnings and outcomes; a sage reverses the sequence of thought and work. Elmalili defines thought as arranging known matters to discover unknown ones and says to draw analogies and act. Ibn Atiyya explains perhaps as human hope that reflection prepares a person for thought. Jishumi says reflection should yield caution and piety; he reports Abu Muslim that parables invite comparison of examples. He also says clarification should lead to knowledge and belief.
  2:266/q25/p2 Think about the world's passing and the afterlife's permanence, then turn toward the latter. — reasons: Ibn Abbas contrasts the two realms; Abu Hayyan and al-Alusi draw the practical consequence of seeking what lasts rather than grieving over worldly loss.
  2:266/q25/p3 Turn back to God's majesty and lordship rather than taking other protectors. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi gives this further application.
2:266/q26 What does perhaps imply about God's intention? [لَعَلَّكُمْ · theology] (turns on: How a particle of hope is understood when spoken by God.)
  2:266/q26/p1 It expresses hope from the human perspective, since reflection may make one fit to understand. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya explains the phrasing this way.
  2:266/q26/p2 It implies that God intends all people to believe. — reasons: Al-Razi reports this Mu'tazilite argument from the apparent hope particle and notes that hope is inappropriate to God. Jishumi reports Abu Ali's argument that making the signs clear so people know and believe rebuts the Mujabbira claim that they were clarified to mislead.
2:266/q27 What does the ruined garden represent? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The context of spending, the scope of the image, and whether ruin comes from insincere giving, harm, later sin, neglected obedience or unbelief.)
  2:266/q27/p1 Showy or insincere charity, whose apparent reward vanishes at Judgment when needed most. — reasons: Al-Suddi repeatedly identifies another parable for ostentatious spending. Al-Tabari calls him closest to correct; Ibn Atiyya reports but resists making it a second separate showmanship image. Al-Tabari describes worldly praise and protection as the garden's apparent fruit; al-Kashshaf and al-Razi widen this to deeds done without God's pleasure. Al-Razi cites 39:47 and 25:23. The old owner's helplessness images inability to repent or recover reward after death. Ibn Qutayba includes the show-off whose deeds God erased; Jishumi says such deeds bring no benefit when most needed and reports al-Suddi's specific application to showy spending.
  2:266/q27/p2 Charity spoiled by reminders, insults or injury to its recipient. — reasons: Ibn Zayd and al-Tabari ground this in 2:264; al-Tabari prefers the contextual connection, while Ibn Atiyya prefers Ibn Zayd over a separate showmanship parable. Al-Tabatabai says a spoiled deed cannot be made sound again. Ibn Ashur says this is the unstated counterpart to sincere giving; al-Biqai says abuse ruins the giver's hope.
  2:266/q27/p3 Good works begun sincerely but wiped out by later sin or a corrupt final act. — reasons: The Umar-Ibn Abbas exchange describes a wealthy worshipper drawn by Satan into sins; other tellings specify evil near death, shirk or a major sin, dying an unbeliever, or final wrongdoing represented by the fiery storm. Ibn Kathir and al-Alusi favor this scope; al-Alusi appeals to Ibn Abbas's standing and Umar's approval. Al-Tabrisi says this and the other major readings all fit. The work is most needed at death or Judgment, when restoration is impossible. Jishumi reports Ibn Abbas's succinct reading of a believer whose deeds end in corruption.
  2:266/q27/p4 Neglect of obedience while absorbed in worldly life, discovered with regret after death. — reasons: Mujahid repeatedly gives this sense; Qatada and al-Rabi warn of dying in error and sin. Ibn Jurayj adds a youth and wealth wasted without action until both disappear. Al-Tabrisi says this and the other major readings fit the wording. Jishumi reports Mujahid's emphasis on worldly pleasures displacing obedience and ending in severe regret.
  2:266/q27/p5 The disbeliever or hypocrite who expects benefit from worldly works but arrives with no good and no second chance. — reasons: Muqatil likens the garden to a disbeliever's deeds, stressing that he cannot return to youth to replant or to life to amend. Ibn Abbas contrasts the believer's stored eternal honor with a wealthy disbeliever's lasting punishment for boasting and doubting God's meeting. Al-Dahhak and Ibn Kathir also apply it to the unbeliever; Ibn Atiyya says the broad image covers any hypocrite or unbeliever with apparent works. Ibn Qutayba includes the hypocrite, and Jishumi likewise says hypocritical deeds are lost when their owner most needs them.
  2:266/q27/p6 More generally, it images any loss of needed deeds or blessing at the moment when recovery is impossible. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and Umar also speak simply of deeds; al-Hasan emphasizes maximum need at life's end. Ibn Atiyya says the wording can cover every applicable case, and al-Tabrisi accepts three named applications. Qatada cites 29:43 for understanding parables. Al-Razi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Biqai and Elmalili dwell on the anguish of lost livelihood and reward. Ibn Qutayba transmits Ibn Abbas and others on the exact crisis of age, weakness and dependents. Jishumi reports the general gloss of irrecoverable blessing and al-Asamm's Judgment Day comparison: people perish if they then lack needed good deeds.
  2:266/q27/p7 It may also image a polity prepared for blessing but ruined by coercive injustice, unbelief, immorality and lost resolve. — reasons: Elmalili says this devastates worldly and afterlife prospects.
2:266/q28 How does this parable connect to its surrounding verses? [* · links] (turns on: How narrowly to tie the image to showmanship and how widely to read its place in the charity passage.)
  2:266/q28/p1 It continues the warning in 2:264 that reproach, harm or hypocrisy can nullify charity. — reasons: Al-Tabari links the bare stone to the burned garden and prefers the immediate charity context; Ibn Zayd, al-Thalabi, al-Baghawi, al-Wahidi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Ibn Atiyya, al-Qurtubi, al-Biqai, Ibn Ashur and al-Tabatabai likewise trace the warning to 2:264. Ibn Ashur contrasts the ruined garden with fruitful sincere spending at 2:261 and 2:265; al-Harali contrasts ruin from below at the root in the first garden with harm from above at the branches in this one. Jishumi reports the connection to preserving charity against invalidation and al-Asamm's link with the preceding believer doing good at 2:265; he explicitly compares the show-off and sincere giver at 2:264-265.
  2:266/q28/p2 It is another specific parable for ostentatious giving beside the earlier stone image. — reasons: Al-Suddi's formulation is preferred by al-Tabari. Ibn Atiyya and al-Qurtubi report al-Tabari's preference but prefer the broader immediate-context connection; Ibn Atiyya disputes treating it as a second separate showmanship parable. Jishumi reports al-Suddi's specific link to the show-off whose benefit fails when most needed.
  2:266/q28/p3 It leads into instruction about the form and recipients of obligatory and voluntary spending. — reasons: Elmalili describes the transition toward where, how and to whom to give.
