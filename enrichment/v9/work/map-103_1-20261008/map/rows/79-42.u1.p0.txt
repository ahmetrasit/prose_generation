## THE CURRENT MAP
79:42/q01 Who asks the Prophet about the Hour? [يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ · referent]
  79:42/q01/p1 The disbelievers, specifically Quraysh or the Meccan polytheists, ask him. — reasons: Muqatil names Quraysh; Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan say Quraysh repeatedly questioned him. Ibn Ashur identifies them with the earlier disbelievers asking about restoration (79:10). Qurtubi attributes the identification to Ibn Abbas; Tabatabai reports it through a weak chain. Tabari calls them deniers.
79:42/q02 Why do they ask when the Hour will come? [يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether their demand primarily expresses denial through ridicule or impatience for the announced event; Razi gives both possibilities.)
  79:42/q02/p1 They taunt the Prophet and cast doubt on resurrection, rather than seek its date sincerely. — reasons: Razi says their sarcasm could make followers think resurrection baseless; he also offers impatient demand as another motive, citing 42:18. Qurtubi attributes the mocking question to Ibn Abbas. Ibn Ashur says they take delay as disproof, wrongly likening divine anger to an impetuous human's immediate retaliation (18:58), and cites their demand for haste (10:48). Bursevi says they invoke terrifying descriptions mockingly. Kuranyolu says they try to put the Prophet in difficulty and deem the event impossible; Study Quran reports that information was not their aim. Islahi calls it teasing; Ibn Ashur and Tabatabai say it followed warnings about resurrection.
  79:42/q02/p2 They impatiently demand the Hour's time or hasten its arrival. — reasons: Razi gives impatience as an alternative to sarcastic denial and cites 42:18. Ibn Ashur cites their demand that it be hastened (10:48). Kuranyolu says they sometimes mockingly wished it would arrive soon because they considered it impossible.
79:42/q03 What does the imperfect 'they ask' convey? [يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ · rhetoric]
  79:42/q03/p1 The question is recurring, pressed repeatedly after the Prophet's warnings. — reasons: Ibn Ashur explicitly bases recurrence on the imperfect verb. Tabatabai likewise says the form depicts repeated demands after they heard of resurrection. Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan independently describe repeated Qurayshi questioning; Abu Hayyan links it to the Prophet's warnings.
79:42/q04 What events are reported around this verse's revelation? [يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ · reports] (turns on: The reports emphasize different actors and consequences: the mockers' questions, the Prophet's prior mentions, or his stopping after revelation.)
  79:42/q04/p1 Repeated, mocking questions by Meccan polytheists prompted its revelation. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says Quraysh pressed the Prophet. Qurtubi attributes the mocking occasion to Ibn Abbas. Abu Hayyan says their questions followed his warnings. Tabatabai transmits the occasion from Ibn Abbas but notes its chain is weak. Ibn Ashur treats the question as a response to repeated demands.
  79:42/q04/p2 The Prophet had repeatedly mentioned the Hour until this warning passage was revealed. — reasons: Tabari reports this from Tariq ibn Shihab. Razi also reports Aisha's account that the Prophet kept mentioning the Hour and being asked about it until the verse descended.
  79:42/q04/p3 After repeated questions about the Hour, the Prophet stopped addressing its timing when this verse was revealed. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya reports this from Aisha; Razi also invokes Aisha's account of the questioning up to revelation.
79:42/q05 What does 'the Hour' denote here, and how is the term used? [ٱلسَّاعَةِ · meaning]
  79:42/q05/p1 Here the definite Hour is the decisive event of resurrection and the Afterlife. — reasons: Tabari calls it the Resurrection, as do Wahidi, Ibn Ashur, Biqai and others. Bint al-Shati says sa'ah originally means a portion of time, later conventionally sixty minutes; the indefinite Qur'anic word means a short unspecified span, while the definite form consistently denotes the eschatological event (30:55; 16:61; 7:34; 34:30; 10:49; 10:45; 46:35). In ordinary Arabic the definite noun can mean the present moment, and later named a timekeeping instrument. She says Qur'anic verbs of coming and rising make this Hour active and momentous, marking changed cosmic order (6:31; 6:40; 12:107; 22:55; 43:66; 30:12; 30:14; 30:55; 20:15; 45:37).
79:42/q06 Why call it 'the Hour' here after 'the Great Calamity'? [ٱلسَّاعَةِ · rhetoric]
  79:42/q06/p1 The changed name gives the new sentence independent force and varies the expression. — reasons: Ibn Ashur contrasts the title here with 'the Great Calamity' in 79:34.
79:42/q07 What does 'ayyāna' ask? [أَيَّانَ · meaning]
  79:42/q07/p1 It asks when, seeking the time of a future occurrence. — reasons: Zajjaj says it asks the time of the Hour's occurrence and arising. Jishumi compares ayyan for time with ayn for place. Ibn Ashur says it asks for a specific time, with an insinuation that the event is distant or impossible; Islahi contrasts future time with place. Ibn Qutayba says signs precede the Hour. Others explain the whole phrase as asking when it occurs; Wahidi and Ibn Jawzi refer to the discussion at 7:187.
79:42/q08 Is 'ayyāna' read with another initial vowel? [أَيَّانَ · readings]
  79:42/q08/p1 Al-A'raj reads 'iyāna' with kasra on the initial alif. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya reports the variant from al-A'raj without giving an argument for it.
79:42/q09 What does 'mursāhā' mean in the question? [مُرْسَىٰهَا · meaning] (turns on: Whether mursā is the act of establishing the Hour, its endpoint or resting place, or the time fixed for that establishment; Alusi raises the when-versus-place difficulty for the endpoint gloss.)
  79:42/q09/p1 The Hour's occurrence or establishment: its arrival becomes fixed and manifest, like a ship reaching its mooring. — reasons: Farra explains irsa' through a moving ship that stops, and compares the standing of justice or truth; Tabari repeats the ship, manifestation and fixedness comparison. Nahhas and Qurtubi transmit Farra. Ibn Qutayba notes signs precede the arrival. Ibn Atiyya, Ibn Ashur, Islahi and Study Quran use the anchoring image; Study Quran reports that some infer nearness from it. Nasafi says God establishes it. Jishumi allows a verbal noun for anchoring. Tabatabai calls marsa a verbal noun of settling. Elmali allows the verbal noun of bringing to rest. Qurtubi says the explanations are close and refers to 7:187. Wahidi and Ibn Jawzi also direct readers to 7:187.
  79:42/q09/p2 Its endpoint or resting place, compared with a ship's anchorage at the end of its course. — reasons: Kashshaf, Razi, Baydawi and Abu Hayyan offer this as an alternative. Qurtubi attributes the endpoint gloss to Abu Ubayda and Ibn Abbas and says it is close to the establishment gloss. Alusi notes that place seems mismatched with a question about when, then reads the distant day metaphorically as a traveler. Elmali allows a place noun meaning endpoint.
  79:42/q09/p3 The appointed time of the Hour's establishment or anchoring. — reasons: Jishumi allows a time of anchoring alongside the source noun. Al-Rabi ibn Anas, reported by Qurtubi, glosses it as when its time will be. Bursevi speaks of fixing its time and supplies the grammatical estimate 'at what time is its appointed establishment' because a time cannot directly predicate an event. Alusi calls it a time noun from rasa, fixed or firm like mountains. Elmali allows a time noun meaning endpoint.
79:42/q10 How does this question connect with the surrounding passage and sūra? [* · links]
  79:42/q10/p1 After establishing resurrection and its consequences, the sūra turns to the deniers' demand for its date and the reply that God knows it. — reasons: Razi says the preceding argument established possibility and reality of resurrection and described its general and moral states. Ibn Ashur says punishment and the Great Calamity of 79:34 lead into the timing question, calling it an explanatory resumption; his account of the reply says the eventual wait will seem only part of a day. Biqai says the sūra's aim is resurrection and gathering, its oaths bringing the matter to a climax after preceding sūras. Bint al-Shati says the warning culminates in God's exclusive final knowledge. Tabatabai calls the passage an answer to repeated questioning. Islahi says it reassures the Prophet, directs him to warn, and describes the later short seeming stay. Study Quran says the closing verses answer the taunt over the Prophet's ignorance.
79:42/q11 How do names used for this sūra fit its subject? [* · links]
  79:42/q11/p1 Its names point to the soul-taking angels, the Pharaoh and Moses account, and the overwhelming resurrection scene. — reasons: Biqai says al-Nazi'at points to the angels extracting souls and then to Pharaoh and Moses; al-Sahirah and al-Tammah also suit the oath and the sūra's progression toward resurrection and gathering.
79:42/q12 Who knows the Hour's time, and what is the Prophet to do with the question? [* · interpretation]
  79:42/q12/p1 Only God knows its appointed time; the Prophet's task is to warn, while the Hour's signs and eventual arrival suffice for preparation. — reasons: Muqatil invokes 27:65 for God's exclusive knowledge of the unseen. Jishumi, Ibn Kathir (citing 7:187), Tabatabai and the Qummi gloss say its time belongs to God, beyond creation. Ibn Kathir reports Gabriel's question and the Prophet's answer that asker and asked know no more than one another. Razi says the final messenger has supplied signs of nearness and should direct preparation rather than a date. Islahi says the Prophet should not be troubled: he warns the heedful, and on seeing the Hour people will think their stay brief. Ibn Ashur says the reply addresses both their apparent request and denial from delay: the Hour will occur, and waiting will seem part of a day. Kuranyolu says the Prophet wished he could answer in hope of their belief. Tabatabai rejects a Bedouin report that he dated 'their Hour' by the youngest person'
