18:16/q20 Do the mirfaq forms differ in sense or formation? [مِّرْفَقًۭا · grammar] (turns on: Whether the vocalizations are equivalent dialects or mark different nouns; reports conflict even about Kisai's and Farra's usage.)
  18:16/q20/p1 Both are dialect forms usable for a helpful thing and, in many accounts, an elbow. — reasons: Farra cites a rajaz pairing the forms; Zajjaj, Qutrub, Abu al-Hasan, Ibn Atiyya, Razi, and Alusi allow both. Akhfash and Abu al-Hasan explain a noun like masjid, a dialect form, or rifq; Razi says derived from irtifāq. Farra says most Arabs prefer kasra for both, though some use fatḥa. Samin disputes a report that Farra knew only kasra by citing the transmitted Nafi and Syrian readings.
  18:16/q20/p2 The vowels distinguish the aid or facility from the body's elbow, though sources assign them differently. — reasons: Ibn Khalawayh, Abu Ubayda, Yunus, Farra on Hijaz speech, and Ibn Jawzi associate kasra with the elbow or comfort in differing ways; Kisai is quoted both as accepting only kasra for elbow (Tabari/Razi) and only open mīm (Abu Hayyan/Samin/Alusi). Abu Hatim makes the open form a place noun, Abu Zayd a verbal noun. Mawrdi distinguishes received benefit from benefit extended; Qiraat-ER notes instrumental, locative, or nominal patterns.
  18:16/q20/p3 Only the kasra-on-mīm form is known or fluent across senses. — reasons: Asmaʿi says he knows only kasra; Nahhas reports him across matter, hand, and all senses. Farra is reported similarly by Abu Hayyan and Alusi, though other Farra reports allow both. Nahhas calls kasra the fluent form; Samin objects that Nafi and Syrian transmission proves open mīm exists.
18:16/q21 Why is 'for you' placed before the promised provision? [لَكُم وَيُهَيِّئْ لَكُم · rhetoric]
  18:16/q21/p1 It first signals that the coming provision benefits them and builds anticipation. — reasons: Alusi explains the fronting and links it to the youths' trust, hope, and certainty.
18:16/q22 What does their flight teach about migration or seclusion under persecution? [وَإِذِ ٱعْتَزَلْتُمُوهُمْ فَأْوُۥٓا۟ إِلَى ٱلْكَهْفِ · law] (turns on: Whether the specific flight from coercion supports a general rule of solitary life.)
  18:16/q22/p1 Fleeing to protect faith is warranted when remaining demands disbelief or endangers belief. — reasons: Tabrisi and Alusi condemn staying where one must profess disbelief. Ibn Kathir cites the sheep-in-mountains hadith for escape from trials but says ordinary seclusion forfeits communal prayers. Tabatabai sees their escape from forced conversion and stoning, allowing earlier taqiyya. Biqai notes they were outnumbered.
  18:16/q22/p2 The verse commends general withdrawal or isolation from the world. — reasons: Bursevi's quoted continuation praises the pleasant corner of seclusion; inward authorities take retreat as spiritual discipline. Islahi objects that the youths faced mortal danger and cites the Prophet's refuge in Thawr, not monastic withdrawal.
18:16/q23 What inward meaning is given to leaving the people and entering the cave? [ٱعْتَزَلْتُمُوهُمْ إِلَى ٱلْكَهْفِ · inward] (turns on: Whether the cave signifies solitary devotion, bodily discipline, or the path of divine union.)
  18:16/q23/p1 Leave everything besides God, including bad company and desires, and retreat into solitude with Him. — reasons: Qushayri says detachment yields union and divine shelter; Bursevi cites al-Hujandi's line that union follows severance. Alusi compares the Prophet's solitude in Hira. Bursevi describes sincere reliance and taking guidance from a perfected shaykh; his Mathnawi couplet warns solitary travel breeds conceit and error, while the cave youths' direct divine training was rare. A hadith about God's direct education of the Prophet illustrates the exception.
  18:16/q23/p2 Leave attachment to self and faculties; the body becomes a disciplined cave for knowledge, action, and transformation. — reasons: Qashani calls it voluntary death to lower impulses; 6:122 supplies the image of life and light after death, and a saying about Abu Bakr as a dead man walking means death to self. His alternative describes abandoning idols and desires, limiting bodily excess, receiving celestial aid, and guiding others; he also associates the cave with the Mahdi before emergence and Jesus's descent.
  18:16/q23/p3 Special mercy is God's drawing seekers into His attributes, and the prepared benefit is a path to union. — reasons: Bursevi transmits the Najmiyya distinction between special favor and general mercy shared by believers, unbelievers, jinn, humans, and animals.
18:16/q24 Does separation in belief require social separation or affect marriage? [ٱعْتَزَلْتُمُوهُمْ · law]
  18:16/q24/p1 Inward or doctrinal separation entails physical separation; marriage between Sunnis and Muʿtazilites is impermissible. — reasons: Bursevi invokes Rasteghfani's ruling and cites his explicit answer on marriage.
18:16/q25 How are this promise and the cave episode linked to other verses? [يَنشُرْ لَكُمْ رَبُّكُم وَيُهَيِّئْ لَكُم مِّنْ أَمْرِكُم مِّرْفَقًۭا · links] (turns on: Which cited passage best illuminates the promise: the earlier prayer, God's provision rule, or another cave refuge.)
  18:16/q25/p1 The promised mercy and ease answer the youths' earlier request for mercy and right guidance in 18:10. — reasons: Tabatabai explicitly equates the gifts; Study Quran says the ease answers the request for sound judgment. Kuran Yolu says God accepted the prayer by causing long sleep; Elmalili links sleep to 18:10–11.
  18:16/q25/p2 God's aid here accords with al-Talaq's promise to provide for those who fear Him and move toward Him. — reasons: Islahi cites 65:2–3 for God's supplying needed means.
  18:16/q25/p3 The refuge recalls the Prophet and Abu Bakr in Thawr, though Ibn Kathir ranks that story greater. — reasons: Ibn Kathir quotes the Prophet's reassurance and 9:40; Study Quran cites the Hijrah parallel and Islahi uses Thawr as an example of refuge from danger. Study Quran adds that the cave in both episodes symbolizes the heart's interior and miraculous sanctuary.
18:16/q26 What wider lesson does their reliance on God convey? [* · interpretation]
  18:16/q26/p1 Those who abandon idolatry for God may expect protection, needed means, and eventual mercy. — reasons: Qushayri links sincere reliance to divine shelter; Biqai says God protects whoever takes refuge in Him, proved by their long sleep and awakening. Islahi connects initiative and trust with 65:2–3; Elmalili says faith's light leads to mercy and success. Tabatabai says the Quran selects lessons for guidance rather than a full historical record.
18:16/q27 How does the Qurʾanic account relate to later historical reports? [* · reports]
  18:16/q27/p1 The reports vary widely, while the Qurʾān selects details needed for guidance. — reasons: Tabatabai lists differences over disclosure to the ruler, joint plans, shepherd and dog, discovery and death, dates, rulers, lineage, names, sleepers' number and duration. He says Christian and People-of-the-Book circulation and Muslim transmission through converts such as Wahb and Kaʿb reshaped accounts. He outlines the Quranic sequence: withdrawal and long sleep, awakening and exposure, discovery and a mosque. Names, occupations, dates and dog identity are omitted as irrelevant to guidance. Most historians place the story between Jesus and Muhammad; Christian versions differ from Muslim ones.
18:16/q28 What was the Raqim connected with their disappearance? [* · reports]
  18:16/q28/p1 A lead tablet on which the king recorded their names and predicted their great destiny. — reasons: Ibn Abbas and another report in Durr say the tablet was kept in the ruler's treasury; both connect it to 18:9.
18:16/q29 What other report concerns the youths' age or status? [* · reports]
  18:16/q29/p1 The youths illustrate that prophets were sent and scholars received learning while young. — reasons: Ibn Abbas is cited by Alusi, with Abraham, Moses, and the cave youths as examples from 21:60, 18:60, and 18:13.
18:16/q30 Why is the name Muʿtazila connected with withdrawal? [ٱعْتَزَلْتُمُوهُمْ · meaning] (turns on: Which historical act of separation gave the group its name.)
  18:16/q30/p1 The name refers to leaving al-Hasan's circle, associated with ʿAmr ibn ʿUbayd. — reasons: Jishumi says it followed al-Hasan's death; Tabrisi relates it to ʿAmr ibn ʿUbayd's circle leaving al-Hasan's gathering.
  18:16/q30/p2 The name means rejecting innovations and following the early community. — reasons: Jishumi records this alternative derivation.
18:16/q31 What recitation change affects the hamza of 'take refuge'? [فَأْوُۥٓا۟ · readings]
  18:16/q31/p1 Substitution of yāʾ for hamza softens the glottal stop without changing meaning. — reasons: Qiraat-ER describes the sound change.
