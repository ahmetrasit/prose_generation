## THE CURRENT MAP
89:1/q01 What does al-fajr mean as an Arabic word? [ٱلْفَجْرِ · meaning] (turns on: Whether splitting is the root idea or an instance of a broader emergence.)
  89:1/q01/p1 Morning light breaking from night, or the time when it appears. — reasons: Tustari gives the outward sense as morning. Zajjaj, Mawardi, Tabarisi, Ibn Jawzi and Bint al-Shati describe darkness cleft by light. Ibn Jawzi relates the root to water bursting out and fājir breaking from obedience. Bint al-Shati cites six Quranic dawn uses, 2:187's white thread and 17:78's sequence after night; she also explains ghasaq as darkness mingled with first morning light or lingering evening light. Islahi distinguishes fajr's transition from subh, which extends beyond sunrise and is qualified in oaths (74:34; 81:18). Kuran Yolu gives brightness or daybreak time.
  89:1/q01/p2 The root broadly denotes emergence or outburst, physically or figuratively. — reasons: Bint al-Shati prefers this to al-Raghib's narrower splitting derivation. She cites springs, gushing and volcanoes, metaphorical emergence in goodness, generosity or evil, and the four pre-Islamic Fijar battle days. She counts 24 Quranic root uses, ten verbs mostly about water; 75:5 is the sole non-water verb. Nouns name rivers in 17:91, a spring in 76:6, and wickedness opposite piety in 91:8 and other passages.
89:1/q02 What color is the light called fajr? [ٱلْفَجْرِ · meaning] (turns on: The Kufan and Medinan grammarians give different colors; the latter invoke garment usage.)
  89:1/q02/p1 White. — reasons: The Kufan grammarians call it whiteness; Elmalili describes first whiteness and Bint al-Shati cites 2:187's white thread.
  89:1/q02/p2 Red. — reasons: The Medinan grammarians cite Arab usage for a red-dyed garment.
89:1/q03 What does al-fajr designate in the oath? [ٱلْفَجْرِ · referent] (turns on: Whether the noun denotes daily dawn, a whole day or prayer, a particular date or place, or a bursting spring or other emergence.)
  89:1/q03/p1 The familiar dawn occurring every day, taken generally. — reasons: Ibn Abbas locates it in the east; al-Qurazi extends it daily until the world's end. Razi's Ibn Abbas report includes true and false dawn and says light ends night, sends creatures to provision and pictures resurrection (74:34; 81:18; 6:96). Alusi especially favors true dawn for its prayer and fasting role. Abu Hayyan, Ibn Ashur and Tabatabai prefer generic dawn; Ibn Ashur cites its coordination with night. Elmalili calls it the joyful move into light (81:18). Bint al-Shati uses 2:187 against extending it to the whole day; Muhammad Abduh likewise treats dawn and the ten nights generically. Baydawi compares 81:18 and Nasafi 74:34.
  89:1/q03/p2 The dawn of Sacrifice Day, the tenth of Dhu al-Hijja. — reasons: Mujahid excludes every ordinary dawn. Abu Muslim links it to offerings and the ten nights' culmination. Razi recalls Abrahamic rites and 37:107. Qurtubi says pilgrimage remains valid until this dawn and reports the last of the ten as pilgrims leave Jam. Biqai mentions entering the sanctuary, ending consecration and divine hospitality; Bursevi lists circumambulation, shaving and stoning. Elmalili favors the collective joy of a particular feast, while generic dawn includes it; he reports Tabari endorsing Jabir's ten-of-sacrifice report on exegetical agreement. Study Quran cites 2:196-197 and 22:27-29.
  89:1/q03/p3 The dawn prayer, in some reports including its Quran recitation. — reasons: Ibn Abbas reports run through Atiyya and others. Ibn Atiyya adds Zayd ibn Aslam and dawn's Quran. Razi says night and day angels gather to hear it (17:78). Ibn Kathir also reports Ikrima. Samin explains the sense through an omitted noun. Tabatabai calls such proposals poor.
  89:1/q03/p4 The first dawn of Muharram, opening the year. — reasons: Qatada calls it the year's first day; Qurtubi says the year then begins to emerge. Razi cites Muharram as God's greatest month and Ibn Abbas calling it the year's dawn, with recurring annual rites. Study Quran joins it to Muharram's first ten nights. Bursevi's Tabyan says New Year.
  89:1/q03/p5 The whole daytime, named for its beginning. — reasons: Repeated Ibn Abbas reports give the view. Mawardi and Ibn Jawzi explicitly explain part-for-whole naming; Razi compares 93:1 and 92:2. Bint al-Shati rejects it for night-ending dawn (2:187), and Tabatabai calls such proposals poor.
  89:1/q03/p6 The first dawn of Dhu al-Hijja. — reasons: Al-Dahhak ties it to the ten nights; Ibn Jawzi specifies first day. Razi says the month opens great pilgrimage worship. Bint al-Shati reports Ibn al-Qayyim arguing from the following ten-night oath.
  89:1/q03/p7 Springs or water breaking from rock. — reasons: Some commentaries and Ibn Abbas reports name springs; Razi stresses that flowing water sustains life. Bursevi's Tabyan includes Moses' stone. Tabatabai calls this among the poor proposals.
  89:1/q03/p8 Dawn at Muzdalifah or Jamʿ following its night. — reasons: Muqatil and Ikrima locate it there; Durr reports Ikrima's general morning view as well. Ibn Ashur specifies Sacrifice Day at Muzdalifah. Qurtubi calls it the day at Jamʿ.
  89:1/q03/p9 Friday's dawn. — reasons: Ikrima is cited by Ibn Atiyya and Alusi; Elmalili includes a blessed Friday. Bursevi calls Friday the poor person's pilgrimage.
  89:1/q03/p10 Arafah morning. — reasons: Bursevi links it to pilgrims' supplication on the plain and cites 'Hajj is Arafah.'
  89:1/q03/p11 A Ramadan dawn paired with its final ten nights and the first Quranic revelation. — reasons: Study Quran cites 97:1.
  89:1/q03/p12 The start of each year without a named month. — reasons: Muqatil is reported this way by Thalabi.
  89:1/q03/p13 Other miraculous or natural outbursts, including a she-camel, rain and tears. — reasons: Bursevi's Tabyan names water from the Prophet's fingers, Salih's she-camel from rock, rain and sinners' tears as distinct images; Bursevi turns tears into an appeal for repentance.
89:1/q04 Which dawn governs prayer and fasting? [ٱلْفَجْرِ · law]
  89:1/q04/p1 The horizontal true dawn; the earlier vertical false dawn is legally night. — reasons: Mawardi says the first narrow streak misleads and cites poetic al-musaddaq for truthful dawn. Jishumi, Bursevi, Alusi and Elmalili assign prayer and fasting rules only to spreading true dawn; Bursevi compares false dawn to a wolf's tail. Tabarisi distinguishes the two. Alusi says the legal role makes true dawn especially apt for the oath.
89:1/q05 How is the oath phrase grammatically construed? [وَٱلْفَجْرِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the sworn object is named directly or an omitted head noun is supplied.)
  89:1/q05/p1 The oath wāw governs al-fajr in the genitive, directly swearing by dawn. — reasons: Nahhas gives the case analysis. Ibn al-Zubayr reports that God swears by dawn; Qurtubi counts five oaths.
  89:1/q05/p2 An omitted noun makes it an oath by the Lord of dawn. — reasons: Samin reports this; Bint al-Shati attributes supplied genitives to Tabari, Naysaburi and Zamakhshari.
  89:1/q05/p3 An omitted noun makes it an oath by dawn prayer or Quran of dawn. — reasons: Samin gives prayer and Bint al-Shati reports Quran of dawn as another completion.
89:1/q06 Who may swear by a created dawn? [وَٱلْفَجْرِ · law]
  89:1/q06/p1 God may swear by any created thing; humans may swear only by God. — reasons: Maymun ibn Mihran draws this rule from the divine oath.
89:1/q07 Is al-fajr recited with verse-final tanwin? [ٱلْفَجْرِ · readings] (turns on: Whether verse endings are pronounced like poetic pauses; grammarians also dispute the form's name.)
  89:1/q07/p1 Most readers leave al-fajr without tanwin. — reasons: Samin says they likewise leave al-watr and yasr without it.
  89:1/q07/p2 Abu al-Dinar al-Aʿrabi adds poetic pausal tanwin to al-fajr, al-watr and yasr. — reasons: Kashshaf says it substitutes for a lengthening letter and also reports Ibn Abbas's separate annexed layālin ʿashr. Abu Hayyan adduces a poem and grammatical discussion of open rhyme and ordinary pause. Ibn Khalawayh calls it tanwin al-tarannum, even on verbs and definite nouns. Samin notes some prefer tanwin tark al-tarannum and distinguish disputed tanwin al-ghali on closed rhymes; he cites 33:66-67, 33:10 and 13:9.
89:1/q08 What is the answer to the opening oath? [وَٱلْفَجْرِ · grammar] (turns on: Whether verse 14 states the response, ensuing stories imply an omitted one, or the preceding sura supplies one.)
  89:1/q08/p1 The expressed answer is that the Lord is surely watchful (89:14). — reasons: Zajjaj, Tabarisi, Ibn al-Anbari and Razi identify it; Bint al-Shati reports Razi treating intervening material as parenthetical. Ibn Masud says the oaths affirm watchfulness. Kuran Yolu adds knowledge of deeds and requital.
  89:1/q08/p2 An unstated answer implies resurrection, judgment, punishment or requital. — reasons: Tabarisi suggests seizing oppressors or vindicating victims. Samin infers each person's recompense from earlier peoples and reports Zamakhshari's 'He will surely punish' from 89:6 and 89:13. Kuran Yolu gives resurrection and judgment. Bint al-Shati reports Ibn al-Qayyim's implicit answer in what befell Abraham and Muhammad.
  89:1/q08/p3 The omitted answer is the end of al-Ghashiya: return to God for reckoning. — reasons: Samin reports the Shaykh's appeal to 88:25-26. Bint al-Shati reports Abu Hayyan and Muhammad Abduh arguing from printed adjacency; she rejects this because al-Ghashiya was revealed 58 suras after al-Fajr.
