18:10/q10 What are the youths' names, and how were they recorded? [ٱلْفِتْيَةُ · reports] (turns on: Transmission of the foreign names is weak, and reports differ on who inscribed the record and where it was kept.)
  18:10/q10/p1 Reports give foreign names, but their transmission is weak. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya and Qurtubi note weak transmission; Tabari's list includes Makselminya or Maksalmina as eldest spokesman, Majseliniya, Tamlikha or Yemliha as the one sent with money, Martus, Kashutunus, Birunus, Dinamus and Yatunus. Spellings vary.
  18:10/q10/p2 Their identities and story were inscribed on metal tablets. — reasons: Tabari says two secret believers Bedros and Ronas placed lead tablets in a sealed copper chest by the cave, hoping later believers would find them. Ibn Atiyya reports lead or copper tablets by two believing judges, hidden at their homes or in a building over the cave; Ubayd's report says people recorded their names and lineage.
18:10/q11 Where was their cave? [ٱلْكَهْفِ · reports] (turns on: Geographic identifications depend on divergent reports and archaeological signs; none is conclusive in Tabatabai's judgment.)
  18:10/q11/p1 It was Khayram in Mount Yanjalus or Banjulus near Ephesus. — reasons: Thalabi and Baghawi give variant forms of the mountain name; reports in Ibn Ishaq and Study Quran place the youths in Ephesus.
  18:10/q11/p2 Rujayb near Amman best fits the site. — reasons: Tabatabai prefers Jordanian archaeological signs and some Muslim reports after centuries of ruin and forgetting; he notes Amman's predecessor Philadelphia was a major Roman city.
  18:10/q11/p3 Other proposed sites include Qasiyun, Petra, Scandinavia and Nakhchivan. — reasons: Tabatabai lists them, but argues no proposed cave is identified conclusively; Scandinavian and Caucasian claims conflict with Roman geography.
  18:10/q11/p4 The Quran does not locate the event. — reasons: Kuranyolu says the place and date are not supplied; Tabatabai says such details are omitted because they do not determine guidance.
18:10/q12 What does “took refuge” mean? [أَوَى · meaning]
  18:10/q12/p1 They went to the cave, settled there and made it their refuge or dwelling. — reasons: Zajjaj, Thalabi, Baghawi, Razi, Abu Hayyan, Alusi, Bursevi and Ibn Jawzi's report from Zajjaj use going and sheltering; Ibn Ashur says it means settling in a dwelling, comparing maʾwā in 10:8. Wahidi transmits Ibn Abbas's gloss of fleeing to it. Jishumi and Tabrisi explain the root as returning to a refuge.
18:10/q13 Why did they seek the cave, and what does their flight teach? [أَوَى ٱلْكَهْفِ · interpretation]
  18:10/q13/p1 They fled persecution to preserve their faith, making religious flight a model. — reasons: Yahya, Tabari, Nahhas, Wahidi, Mawardi's Hasan, Tabrisi, Ibn Kathir and Study Quran say they protected religion from an unbelieving people. Qurtubi says believers may leave family, homeland, wealth and friends, citing 16:41 and 9:20 and the Prophet's Companions; Biqai compares Muhammad and Abu Bakr at Thawr. Study Quran's Aj says they despaired of worldly kindness and sought God alone.
18:10/q14 What form of seclusion does their retreat commend? [أَوَى ٱلْكَهْفِ · law] (turns on: Capacity and social conditions determine which kind of seclusion is apt; a prophetic report values patient mixing.)
  18:10/q14/p1 Retreat from corruption is commendable, with mountains and caves its strongest form. — reasons: Qurtubi calls retreat prophetic and saintly, especially in fitna. He cites a Bukhari report on sheep in mountain heights, reports advising staying home and guarding one's tongue, weeping over sin, flight from peak to peak, and a shepherd whose prayer earns forgiveness. He prefers mountain retreat for those able; a reported year-180 permission for celibacy and mountain retreat also appears.
  18:10/q14/p2 Seclusion can be at home, by the coast or frontier, or within a mosque. — reasons: Qurtubi says mountains are named as common retreats, but any separation from people counts. He cites reports calling homes cloisters and Badr veterans who stayed home after Uthman's killing; scholars allow coastal and frontier posts, and a prophetic counsel advises staying home.
  18:10/q14/p3 One may remain among people while withdrawing from evil or keeping inward distance. — reasons: A group of scholars defines this as inward and practical separation; Ibn al-Mubarak joins remembrance and keeps silent otherwise. Wahb counsels a recluse that people need one another. Qurtubi says some can mix outwardly while distinct inwardly.
  18:10/q14/p4 Patiently mixing with people can be better than avoiding them. — reasons: Qurtubi reports the Prophet's comparison of a believer who bears people's harm with one who does not mix or bear it.
18:10/q15 What does the cave signify in inward readings? [ٱلْكَهْفِ · inward] (turns on: Inward interpreters map the physical cave to different dimensions of spiritual refuge.)
  18:10/q15/p1 It is outward shelter and inward divine care. — reasons: Qushayri says God took charge of the youths and brought spiritual states upon them while they were absent from themselves; Study Quran's Aj says they sought God's protection after despairing of the world.
  18:10/q15/p2 It symbolizes the body, where souls develop toward intellectual and practical perfection. — reasons: Qashani reads the youths' descent into bodily existence, attachment and tools as a path toward God.
  18:10/q15/p3 It symbolizes the heart. — reasons: Study Quran states this mystical reading.
  18:10/q15/p4 It symbolizes solitary encounter with God and escape from the self. — reasons: Bursevi's cited Taʾwilat Najmiyya contrasts their flight from Daqyanus with seekers' flight from existence's darkness to divine beauty and majesty; Alusi reports spiritual chivalry as abandoning everything besides God and ascending through blissful stations.
18:10/q16 What does the “so” before their prayer convey? [فَقَالُوا۟ · rhetoric]
  18:10/q16/p1 They prayed immediately on reaching the cave. — reasons: Ibn Ashur reads the fāʾ as indicating haste.
18:10/q17 What mercy did they request? [رَحْمَةًۭ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether mercy names one concrete need or an inclusive gift, and whether it is outward safety, religious aid or inward remembrance.)
  18:10/q17/p1 They sought God's broad help, including forgiveness, provision, protection, guidance, patience or knowledge. — reasons: Zajjaj and Wahidi give forgiveness and provision; Kashshaf, Baydawi, Nasafi, Bursevi and Study Quran add safety from enemies; Razi adds guidance, knowledge and patience. Alusi prefers an encompassing sense beyond these glosses. Ibn Ashur calls it abundant good in both worlds; Biqai calls it exceptional mercy and al-Harali's highest hidden order. Tabatabai gives divine support or God's exclusive gifts, especially guidance, citing 2:201, 3:194 and 3:8.
  18:10/q17/p2 They asked for provision. — reasons: Muqatil, al-Suddi, Ibn Jawzi, Ibn Atiyya and exegetes cited by Abu Hayyan give this concise gloss; Baghawi reports it as an alternative to guidance in religion.
  18:10/q17/p3 They asked for guidance in religion. — reasons: Baghawi gives this alternative; Tabatabai calls special guidance a gift reserved to God.
  18:10/q17/p4 They asked to be preserved through remembrance and gratitude. — reasons: Tustari says preservation in God's remembrance; Sahl in Sulami adds gratitude in every state; Study Quran transmits the remembrance gloss.
  18:10/q17/p5 They asked for rescue from persecution. — reasons: Nahhas says rescue from unbelievers; Thalabi says lifting persecution, Tabrisi rescue, Ibn Kathir concealment from their people, and Ibn Ashur protection from exile's hardship and humiliation.
18:10/q18 What does “from Your presence” add? [مِن لَّدُنكَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the phrase is a simple source marker or suggests special, unowed divine favor.)
  18:10/q18/p1 It asks for mercy directly from God's own stores and special bounty. — reasons: Yahya's Suddi glosses it as God's presence; Ibn Abbas in Wahidi says this makes them independent of others. Razi says it magnifies the gift fitting divine generosity; Bursevi imagines hidden treasuries beyond hostile sight; Ibn Ashur distinguishes special abundant mercy from universal mercy; Study Quran transmits the storehouse gloss. Abu Hayyan transmits Zamakhshari's gloss of this mercy as forgiveness, provision and safety from enemies.
  18:10/q18/p2 It emphasizes that mercy is a gratuitous favor, not something owed. — reasons: Alusi draws this theological implication from the phrase; Tabatabai sees desperate recourse after every other means failed.
  18:10/q18/p3 It simply means “from You.” — reasons: Ibn Jawzi gives the direct gloss; al-Suddi likewise says 'from God's presence.'
18:10/q19 Does their prayer seek worldly good, afterlife good, or both? [رَحْمَةًۭ رَشَدًۭا · interpretation] (turns on: The scope of mercy and the relation between the prayer's two clauses.)
  18:10/q19/p1 It chiefly concerns their worldly predicament. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya says they trusted their afterlife outcome, though mercy might include it; Abu Hayyan reports his view and Alusi reports it while rejecting the restriction. Tabatabai identifies the peril after flight from coercion and death.
  18:10/q19/p2 It asks for good in both worlds. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya
