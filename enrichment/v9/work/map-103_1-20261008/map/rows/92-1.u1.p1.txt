92:1/q11 What is the oath by the night doing? [وَٱلَّيْلِ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether the note explains the oath's argumentative function or its proposed literary antecedent.)
  92:1/q11/p1 It invokes a natural sign to establish the ensuing premise and direct attention to the surah's subject. — reasons: Al-Māwardī identifies the oath; Iṣlāḥī says Qurʾānic oaths by natural phenomena establish a premise. Küran Yolu says they show the worth of created things and their Creator's greatness while introducing the subject. Corpus Coranicum points to 93:1–2 for this oath type's structure and function.
  92:1/q11/p2 Its form probably draws on characteristic pre-Islamic soothsayers' expression. — reasons: Corpus Coranicum proposes the connection, notes that it is not sufficiently studied, and compares the oath sequence in 100:1–5.
92:1/q12 Who may swear by created things? [وَٱلَّيْلِ · law]
  92:1/q12/p1 God may swear by any creature; creatures may swear only by God. — reasons: Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports Imam al-Bāqir's answer to Muḥammad ibn Muslim and a parallel transmission in al-Faqīh through ʿAlī ibn Mahziyār from Imam al-Bāqir II.
92:1/q13 What origin of night and day do the sources describe? [ٱلَّيْلِ · reports] (turns on: Whether light and darkness are described as jointly created before differentiation, or night darkness as preceding solar daylight.)
  92:1/q13/p1 God first created light and darkness, then distinguished them as dark night and bright seeing day. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī reports this from Qatādah through Saʿīd.
  92:1/q13/p2 Night darkness was the solar system's original state; daylight followed the sun's creation, so reckoning began with nights. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr presents this as an allusion in the passage.
92:1/q14 What further inward or ominous meaning can the night's covering bear? [ٱلَّيْلِ يَغْشَىٰ · inward] (turns on: Which extended spiritual or eschatological effect is drawn from the covering image.)
  92:1/q14/p1 Night prayer and good deeds can turn spiritual light into sensory light after death. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī reports this from some interpreters.
  92:1/q14/p2 Its engulfing darkness can warn of punishment, ignorance, death agonies, or resurrection events. — reasons: Elmalılı offers a town or people engulfed by punishment, the age of ignorance around the Prophet's mission, death agonies, and resurrection scenes as possible images.
  92:1/q14/p3 It can signify passing away of existent things before abiding witness to God. — reasons: Elmalılı distinguishes this accidental darkness from primordial nonexistence, cites 55:26–27 and 28:88 on all perishing except God's face, and describes movement from annihilation to abiding witness.
92:1/q15 What was the surah's place and period of revelation? [* · sciences] (turns on: Whether the whole surah belongs to Mecca, Medina, or both; the notes also differ in confidence about the Meccan attribution.)
  92:1/q15/p1 It is Meccan, with Study Quran calling it early Meccan and often ninth in revelation order. — reasons: Al-Māwardī calls the Meccan attribution a consensus; Abū Ḥayyān reports it as his own view. Al-Ālūsī says most hold it. Study Quran adds the common ninth-place ordering.
  92:1/q15/p2 It is Medinan. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī attribute this to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥa; Study Quran reports the view without naming its holder.
  92:1/q15/p3 Part of it is Medinan and part Meccan. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān, al-Ālūsī and Study Quran report a divided setting.
92:1/q16 How many verses does the surah have? [* · sciences]
  92:1/q16/p1 Twenty-one verses. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says the count is unanimous.
92:1/q17 Why is the surah named al-Layl? [* · sciences] (turns on: Whether the explanation identifies the naming source or the significance al-Biqāʿī finds in it.)
  92:1/q17/p1 It is named after its opening oath by night. — reasons: Study Quran states the naming basis.
  92:1/q17/p2 The name signals night as frustration of ordinary desire through darkness and sleep, the sibling of death. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī explains the name's thematic force.
92:1/q18 What event occasioned the surah's revelation? [* · reports] (turns on: Which charitable act is connected with revelation and how the palm narrative's chain is assessed.)
  92:1/q18/p1 A believer acquired a palm from a stingy owner and gave it to a deprived household. — reasons: Ibn Abī Ḥātim's Ibn ʿAbbās report says fallen dates were withheld, the buyer traded forty palms for the tree, and a palm in Paradise was promised. Ibn Kathīr's Ibn ʿAbbās version adds that the owner snatched fruit from poor children, refused the Prophet's offer of a palm in Paradise, and another man donated the purchased tree; Ibn Kathīr says the story explains the opening contrast of giving and withholding. Al-Ālūsī identifies the buyer in al-Suddī's version as Abū al-Daḥdāḥ and the beneficiaries as orphans, but reports al-Suyūṭī's judgment that the Ibn ʿAbbās report is weak; Ibn Kathīr calls Ibn Abī Ḥātim's report very unusual.
  92:1/q18/p2 It concerned Abū Bakr's charitable action, including his purchase and freeing of Bilāl. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says most reports concern Abū Bakr, citing sound chains from Ibn Masʿūd, Ibn ʿAbbās and others. Ibn Masʿūd reports that Abū Bakr bought Bilāl from Umayya ibn Khalaf for a cloak and ten uqiyyas and freed him, after which the opening was revealed.
92:1/q19 What reward is reported for reciting this surah? [* · sciences]
  92:1/q19/p1 The reciter receives until contentment, is protected from hardship, and has ease made easy. — reasons: Al-Jishumī transmits this as a prophetic report from Ubayy.
92:1/q20 What recitation of al-Layl did the Prophet direct in prayer? [* · reports] (turns on: The reports describe different instructions and prayer settings.)
  92:1/q20/p1 Muʿādh was told to use shorter chapters, among them al-Layl, instead of prolonging prayer. — reasons: Al-Durr reports the Prophet's rebuke and his examples of chapters beginning with glorification, sun, dawn and night, mentioning 87:1, 91:1 and 92:1.
  92:1/q20/p2 The Night and Sun surahs were to be recited in the dawn prayer. — reasons: Al-Durr reports the Prophet's instruction to Ibn ʿAbbās.
92:1/q21 How were the endings such as يَغْشَىٰ read? [يَغْشَىٰ · readings] (turns on: The transmitted degree and distribution of imālah across the sūrah endings.)
  92:1/q21/p1 With open vowels, without imālah. — reasons: Al-Fārisī reports this for Ibn Kathīr, ʿĀṣim and Ibn ʿĀmir across the relevant endings of al-Layl and al-Ḍuḥā; some Nāfiʿ transmissions through Warsh and Qālūn also open them all.
  92:1/q21/p2 With full imālah of the endings. — reasons: Al-Fārisī reports this throughout for al-Kisāʾī; ʿAbbās transmits fully inclined forms from Abū ʿAmr for selected endings.
  92:1/q21/p3 With intermediate imālah. — reasons: Some Nāfiʿ transmissions give an intermediate pronunciation, and Abū ʿAmr is also reported to use intermediate imālah.
  92:1/q21/p4 With selective opening or inclination depending on the ending. — reasons: Another Nāfiʿ report opens only talāhā and inclines the rest; al-Fārisī records differing Abū ʿAmr transmissions across verse endings, including 79:30.
92:1/q22 How was the oath's male-and-female clause read? [* · readings] (turns on: Whether the early reported wording without mā remains the operative reading or was superseded by the agreed muṣḥaf wording.)
  92:1/q22/p1 It was recited as 'the male and the female' without mā. — reasons: Ibn Jinnī reports this from the Prophet, ʿAlī, Ibn Masʿūd, Abū al-Dardāʾ and Ibn ʿAbbās; he treats it as evidence for a variant making the pair genitive in apposition to mā. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites the two Ṣaḥīḥs' report from ʿAlqama: Abū al-Dardāʾ heard the Prophet recite Ibn Masʿūd's wording. He says al-Zamakhsharī called it the Prophet's reading, but explains it as an early permitted variant later replaced.
  92:1/q22/p2 The final agreed reading retains mā in the clause. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says this is the reciters' agreed final reading and the form written in Abū Bakr's muṣḥaf.
92:1/q23 How does this opening connect with the preceding surah? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the link lies in repeated oath wording, development of the moral polarity, or reversal of the prior narrative movement.)
  92:1/q23/p1 It echoes the preceding surah's night-and-day oath wording and temporal restrictions. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr links the time restrictions to explanations of the oaths in 91:3–4. Corpus Coranicum calls 92:1–2 especially close to 91:3–4 and compares early Meccan oath parallels in 93:1–2, 89:1–4, 84:17, 81:17–18 and 74:33–34; it notes that brightening day corresponds to ḍuḥā in 91:1 and 93:1. Elmalılı also points back to 91:4.
  92:1/q23/p2 It develops the prior surah's purified-versus-corrupted souls by showing divergent deeds and their outcomes. — reasons: Al-Jishumī says the believer/disbeliever contrast recurs. Abū Ḥayyān connects success and failure in 91:9–10 to the traits that cause them and to the warning of Fire. Al-Biqāʿī links purified and corrupted souls and Thamūd's fate to good and evil efforts under God's freely chosen governance. Iṣlāḥī says al-Layl identifies what corrupts the soul and how to protect and purify it; Study Quran says the first three verses echo the prior polarity before the opposing human ends.
