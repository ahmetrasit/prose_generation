12:43/q15 What is a sunbula, and what were the green and dry ears like? [سُنۢبُلَٰتٍ خُضْرٍۢ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَٰتٍۢ · meaning]
  12:43/q15/p1 The green ears bore grain; the dry ears were ripe, curled, and no longer green. — reasons: Al-Baydawi says the green ears had formed grain and the dry ones ripened; al-Alusi adds a report of curling. Al-Biqa'i calls sunbula a reed-like plant with an ordered grain cluster.
12:43/q16 How is 'seven ears' read? [سَبْعَ سُنۢبُلَٰتٍ · readings]
  12:43/q16/p1 Ja'far ibn Muhammad reads sab' sanabil. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi reports this in place of the verse's sunbulat.
12:43/q17 Why use this form and number of ears? [سَبْعَ سُنۢبُلَٰتٍ · rhetoric] (turns on: The notes offer distinct links to 2:261: numerical correspondence or a contrast in crop abundance.)
  12:43/q17/p1 The seven ears correspond to the seven cows and recall the seven-ear parable. — reasons: Ibn Ashur cites 2:261.
  12:43/q17/p2 The numeral modifies a plural of paucity because the dream signifies famine. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i contrasts the multiplied crop in 2:261.
  12:43/q17/p3 The paucity plural sunbulat suits a counted seven or a dream without abundance; the same number takes the abundance plural sanabil elsewhere. — reasons: Al-Zarkashi contrasts sunbulat here with sanabil in another seven-ear verse. Al-Samarra'i says paucity plurals normally denote few, as in contrasting plural pairs, and seven is a small counted set; he says this dream does not call for abundance and notes other plural pairs can distinguish meanings, such as eyes for sight and springs of water.
12:43/q18 Did the dry ears also number seven? [وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَٰتٍۢ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the vision's dry ears represent a counted period or unnumbered duties.)
  12:43/q18/p1 Yes, seven is omitted but supplied by the repeated pattern and 'others'. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf and al-Nasafi point to the recurring sevens; Abu Hayyan invokes the matching fat/lean and green/dry divisions; al-Biqa'i says the lean cattle have already suggested the count. Al-Tabari glosses 'others' as seven other ears. Al-Alusi says the earlier cows supply it.
  12:43/q18/p2 The dry ears are deliberately unnumbered because the vision describes famine duties, not counted famine years. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i says a forecast reading would require their number.
12:43/q19 How does 'other dry ears' attach grammatically? [وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَٰتٍۢ · grammar] (turns on: The coordinate's scope and whether an omitted numeral or noun is supplied without making the seven green ears also a different dry set.)
  12:43/q19/p1 It coordinates with 'seven', with an omitted seven and understood ears. — reasons: Al-Samin says the matching cow and ear groups supply the numeral; al-Kashshaf, Abu Hayyan, and al-Alusi reject treating 'others' as a modifier of the seven green ears, which would make the same seven both green and other. Al-Samin contrasts 'seven green and dry ears' and al-Kashshaf's standing/sitting men analogy. Al-Alusi prefers ellipsis or Ibn al-Hajib's withdrawal analysis, and argues 'others' is useful emphasis.
  12:43/q19/p2 It coordinates with 'green', leaving the noun 'ears' expressed for both colors. — reasons: Al-Fara'id argues coordination normally repeats the numeral, creating fourteen ears, while an ellipsis analysis might wrongly total seven; attaching at 'green' avoids supplying a new ears noun. Al-Alusi replies that neither repetition nor omission cures the contradiction once a seven-ear discriminator is fixed.
12:43/q20 Is the dry ears' overcoming of the green ears omitted in the wording? [وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَٰتٍۢ · rhetoric]
  12:43/q20/p1 Yes; the cow imagery supplies the action, later clarified by verse 48. — reasons: Al-Baydawi says the ears' state is omitted because cows already explain it; Islahi supplies 'the dry ears consume the green ears' from 12:48.
12:43/q21 Who are the addressed mala'? [ٱلْمَلَأُ · referent] (turns on: Whether the address identifies the court's rank or its interpretive professions; several sources include both.)
  12:43/q21/p1 They are the prominent courtiers or advisers, including leading scholars and sages. — reasons: Al-Tabari calls them nobles and associates; al-Zajjaj and Ibn al-Jawzi people consulted and followed; al-Kashshaf, al-Nasafi, and Elmali describe scholars and sages; Abu Hayyan the king's attending officials; Ibn Ashur distinguished people, citing 7:60. Study Quran says high-ranking entourage. Al-Biqa'i says the elite include diviners; al-Tabrisi and al-Alusi report a gathered specialist group.
  12:43/q21/p2 They are magicians, soothsayers, and other dream or omen specialists. — reasons: Al-Suddi lists sorcerers, soothsayers, physiognomists, and bird augurs; al-Qurtubi adds diviners and astrologers. Al-Tabrisi reports this as an alternative to 'elite'; Ibn Ashur cites the Torah's Pharaoh summoning magicians and sages, and observes Egyptian priestly dream interpretation.
12:43/q22 Why did the king seek an interpretation from his council? [يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلْمَلَأُ أَفْتُونِى فِى رُءْيَٰىَ · interpretation]
  12:43/q22/p1 He found the vision troubling or significant and did not know its meaning. — reasons: Ibn Ishaq and Ibn Abi Hatim say he recognized it as true; al-Mawardi says he announced it broadly so a knowledgeable hearer might explain. Al-Biqa'i says the address signals seriousness rather than a test. Islahi says he suspected his naive courtiers could not interpret it; Elmali says he wanted his difficulty resolved. Al-Alusi reports that calling it a fatwa honors the companions and magnifies the vision. Bint al-Shati says the king twice calls his vision ru'ya because it was clear to him, although his courtiers later call it confused dreams (12:44).
12:43/q23 What does 'give me a fatwa about my vision' mean? [أَفْتُونِى فِى رُءْيَٰىَ · meaning]
  12:43/q23/p1 Explain or interpret the dream and its outcome for me. — reasons: Al-Tabari and Ibn Ashur gloss ifta' as explanation; al-Qurtubi calls it a ruling or interpretation. Al-Tabataba'i reports al-Majma's distinction that a fatwa properly answers a ruling, though the word can answer the matter itself. Al-Wahidi refers to the earlier discussion in al-Nisa'; Ibn Ashur refers to the prison scene at 12:41.
12:43/q24 How does 'fi' connect the fatwa to the dream? [فِى رُءْيَٰىَ · grammar]
  12:43/q24/p1 It figuratively makes the explanation concern and attach to the dream. — reasons: Ibn Ashur compares an explanation attached to a concise matter.
12:43/q25 How is the hamza of 'aftuni' read? [أَفْتُونِى · readings]
  12:43/q25/p1 Readers vary the two hamzas, and some pronounce an intervening alif as waw. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya reports the variants without assigning individual readers. Ibn al-Jazari classifies the adjacent hamzas in al-mala'u aftuni as a damma followed by a fath, among eleven agreed Qur'anic sites of that pairing; 27:32 is another.
12:43/q26 How is 'my vision' or 'the vision' read? [رُءْيَٰىَ لِلرُّءْيَا · readings] (turns on: The variants concern hamza, imala, and final ya pronunciation; the notes assign no semantic difference.)
  12:43/q26/p1 The word has hamza elision and assimilation variants without change of meaning. — reasons: Qiraat-ER gives ruyaaya by hamza elision, pause with silent final ya, and ruyyaya by waw-to-ya assimilation; al-ru'ya likewise has hamzless and assimilated ruyya. Abu Ja'far's assimilated reading is reported by Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, and al-Alusi; grammarians call its phonological sequence irregular because the waw substitution is optional.
  12:43/q26/p2 Ru'ya admits full opening or imala. — reasons: Ibn Khalawayh says imala signals the feminine final alif corresponding to ta'; al-Kisa'i reportedly uses imala here but opening at ru'yaka in 12:5. He says this is mistaken as a case distinction, valid as dialect variation.
  12:43/q26/p3 The first-person ya of 'my vision' is vocalized in one canonical form. — reasons: Qiraat-ER contrasts it with its silence in the standard reading. Abu Amr reports that the Medinans, Ibn Kathir, and Abu Amr open the first-person ya in the two dream forms, among seven listed forms.
12:43/q27 What kinds of dreams can be true or interpretable? [رُءْيَٰىَ · theology] (turns on: Whether ordinary bodily and mental causes exhaust dreams, or some dreams truly disclose hidden or future realities.)
  12:43/q27/p1 Some dreams disclose real events and can be interpreted, while others arise from the sleeper or deception. — reasons: Al-Tabrisi distinguishes dreams from God or angels, Satanic dreams without interpretation, and dreams from beliefs and prior thoughts. Study Quran calls this vision veridical. Al-Tabataba'i says bodily state, temperament, emotion, chance, and preoccupation shape ordinary dreams; sound dreams disclose future or hidden facts. He argues common experience and noncoincidental fulfillment establish these, while naturalist accounts explain only some dreams. Bint al-Shati distinguishes singular ru'ya, used seven times for a true, clear vision, from plural ahlam, used three times for confused, mixed dreams; she says the king's fulfilled vision is twice called ru'ya here despite the courtiers' description in 12:44.
  12:43/q27/p2 All dreams lack real or scientific connection to external events. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i attributes this to European natural scientists and rejects the universal claim: bodily and emotional causes do not account for sound dreams. He notes some psychologists study dreams.
