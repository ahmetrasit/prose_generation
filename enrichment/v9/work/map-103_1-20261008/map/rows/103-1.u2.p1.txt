103:1/q05 How is al-ʿaṣr read? [ٱلْعَصْرِ · readings] (turns on: Whether the transferred kasra is confined to pause or transmitted in connected recitation.)
  103:1/q05/p1 The received reading keeps ṣād vowelless in pause; Salām's reading gives ṣād kasra by vowel transfer. — reasons: Ibn Atiyya limits Salām's al-ʿaṣri and al-ṣabri to pause; Razi attributes the sound to breath or another impediment. Abu al-Fadl says transfer marks inflection and avoids two vowelless letters; Samīn gives poetic examples and also ḍamma transfer. A rajaz line in fī al-ʿaṣr supports the form.
  103:1/q05/p2 Reports also allow the kasra in connected recitation. — reasons: Al-Hudhali transmits Abu ʿAmr routes for al-ʿaṣr, al-ṣabr, al-fajr and al-watr in pause and liaison; Ibn Khalawayh does not restrict al-ṣabri to pause. Samīn records an imāla-like variant on bāʾ.
103:1/q06 What expanded recitations of the verse are reported? [وَٱلْعَصْرِ · readings] (turns on: Whether the additions are transmitted interpretations or canonical text, and which expanded wording is attributed to whom.)
  103:1/q06/p1 Ali's recitation names the vicissitudes of time and extends human loss until time's end. — reasons: Tabari, Tha'labi and Elmalılı transmit the interpretive expansion with time's calamities; Razi refuses to treat an expanded report as Qur'anic wording because it would invalidate prayer.
  103:1/q06/p2 A recitation attributed to Ali and Ibn Masʿud adds loss until time's end without naming vicissitudes. — reasons: Tha'labi gives both attributions; the wording differs from his other Ali report.
  103:1/q06/p3 The standard codices and general reciters have the sound reading. — reasons: Tha'labi prefers it; Razi's prayer argument weighs against elevating the expanded wording to Qur'an.
103:1/q07 How long is an oath not to speak for an ʿaṣr? [ٱلْعَصْرِ · law] (turns on: The word's range and the role of the swearer's intention.)
  103:1/q07/p1 A year. — reasons: Malik adopts the most expansive duration, consistent with his stricter handling of oaths.
  103:1/q07/p2 An hour, unless the speaker intended a different duration. — reasons: Al-Shafi'i gives an hour. Ibn al-Arabi prefers this, allowing a plausible stated intention for an Arab swearer except the shortest interpretation.
103:1/q08 Does the word al-ʿaṣr have a theological implication about who causes events? [ٱلْعَصْرِ · theology] (turns on: Whether the oath is read as correcting causal blame or as prompting inquiry into time's nature.)
  103:1/q08/p1 God acts through time; time itself does not cause calamity or loss. — reasons: Qashani invokes 45:24 and the ungraded 'God is time' report; Bursevi also quotes the prohibition on reviling time. Razi, Alusi and Elmalılı put blame on human conduct rather than the age.
  103:1/q08/p2 Time's existence is philosophically difficult to characterize. — reasons: Razi notes that past and future are absent and the indivisible present resists simple existence claims; Elmalılı recounts philosophers' and theologians' debate. Bint al-Shati records a report likening time to something both existent and like nothing.
103:1/q09 How does this opening connect with other Qur'anic passages? [وَٱلْعَصْرِ · links] (turns on: The links draw on the preceding sūra, analogous oaths, parallels to the exception, and the sūra's own later clauses.)
  103:1/q09/p1 It follows al-Takāthur by exposing the loss caused by worldly distraction. — reasons: Jishumi says the prior warning against abundance leads to the demand for deeds; Abu Hayyan distinguishes believer and unbeliever. Biqai and Alusi continue the warning of 102; Study Quran cites 102:1 and 102:8 about wasted time and accountable blessings.
  103:1/q09/p2 It complements oaths by the forenoon and other day and night phases. — reasons: Zamakhshari, Nasafi and others pair afternoon with 93:1; Razi contrasts prior midmorning rising fortunes with afternoon decline. Bursevi cites oaths in 89, 91, 92 and 93; Corpus Coranicum observes this afternoon oath is unusual among early Meccan oaths.
  103:1/q09/p3 The exception here adds mutual counsel to faith and works mentioned elsewhere. — reasons: Samarrai says counsel in truth and patience prevents further loss of reward.
  103:1/q09/p4 A verse about protected life uses this sūra as a parallel for a generic statement with an exception. — reasons: Qurtubi compares the generic definite al-nafs and its legally qualified exception to generic human statements in 103:1–3, citing 70:19 and 70:22.
  103:1/q09/p5 Its message distinguishes those saved from those ruined, framing the whole sūra. — reasons: Biqai maps the clauses to knowledge of God and afterlife, worship, doubt and patience, and says preventing harm precedes benefit. Qutb calls faith, work and mutual counsel the one path of success; Study Quran extends the decline image to each generation. Bint al-Shati links the opening's pressing image to the later contrast between human loss and salvation.
103:1/q10 Was the sūra revealed in Mecca or Medina? [* · sciences] (turns on: The authorities' differing attributions; the notes supply no decisive historical test.)
  103:1/q10/p1 It was revealed in Mecca. — reasons: Ibn Abbas, Ibn al-Zubayr and most authorities support Mecca (Abu Hayyan, Alusi); Tabataba'i says it seems more likely Meccan, while Study Quran also reports this. Al-Suyuti cites al-Bayhaqi's transmission from Ikrima and al-Hasan naming al-ʿAṣr among the Meccan sūras.
  103:1/q10/p2 It was revealed in Medina. — reasons: Mawardi cites one narration from Ibn Abbas and Qatada; Abu Hayyan and Alusi add Mujahid and Muqatil. Tabataba'i says Medina remains possible.
103:1/q11 How many verses does the sūra have, and where is its first break? [* · sciences] (turns on: The position of the first verse boundary; the reported total count is three.)
  103:1/q11/p1 It has three verses, with the oath as its own first verse. — reasons: Alusi calls the three-verse count undisputed; Neuwirth favors an independent oath in line with other openings. Al-Zarkashi counts al-ʿAṣr as a one-word opening verse by analogy with al-Fajr and al-Ḍuḥā and their rhyme despite different lengths, and notes that a single utterance can form a verse. Al-Suyuti reports scholars adding wa-l-ʿaṣr to standalone verses; the counters other than the later Medinans count this opening separately.
  103:1/q11/p2 Medina II joins the oath to verse 2 in its division. — reasons: Spitaler reports this verse division. Al-Suyuti reports that the later Medinan count instead makes wa-tawāṣaw bi-l-ḥaqq a separate verse while all still total three. Al-Mawsili's scheme distinguishes disagreements in internal breaks from disagreements in totals.
  103:1/q11/p3 Under Abu ʿAmr al-Dani's one-word rule, wa-l-ʿaṣr would not form a standalone verse. — reasons: Al-Suyuti reports al-Dani's assertion that mudhāmmatān is the only word that constitutes an āya on its own; the report does not itself supply an alternative three-verse division for al-ʿAṣr.
103:1/q12 When in the revelation sequence did this sūra come? [* · sciences]
  103:1/q12/p1 It followed shortly after Sūra 94. — reasons: Asad states this; Study Quran calls the sequence likely.
  103:1/q12/p2 In Jābir ibn Zayd's reported Meccan sequence it is thirteenth, with al-Fajr tenth. — reasons: Al-Suyuti transmits Jābir ibn Zayd's order with these numbered placements.
