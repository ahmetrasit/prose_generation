## THE CURRENT MAP
68:19/q01 What does طاف عليها convey? [فَطَافَ عَلَيْهَا · meaning] (turns on: The first explanation emphasizes the verb's circling image; Ibn ʿĀshūr additionally draws a sense of domination or descent from its preposition.)
  68:19/q01/p1 The visitation passed around and encompassed the entire orchard. — reasons: Al-Rāghib (via Bursawī) derives ṭawf from circling something, originally to guard it, and extends it to a night visitor, jinn, image, or servant. Ibn ʿĀshūr takes the literal circling as an image of calamity affecting every part of the garden, comparing 7:201; al-Ālūsī and Elmalılı likewise say it encircled the orchard. Al-Kalbī (via al-Wāḥidī) explicitly makes عليها refer to the orchard.
  68:19/q01/p2 With على, the verb depicts the calamity as descending upon or taking control of the orchard. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr attributes this shade to the preposition على; al-Ālūsī also glosses the action as the affliction descending.
68:19/q02 What was the طائف that visited the garden? [طَآئِفٌۭ · referent] (turns on: Reports identify the unnamed visitor by different agents or kinds of disaster, including competing origins for the fire; Ibn ʿĀshūr says the verse leaves its kind unspecified, and al-Ālūsī rejects the relocation account.)
  68:19/q02/p1 A divine calamity, punishment, scourge, or destructive visitation. — reasons: Muqātil, Qatāda as reported by al-Māwardī and al-Ālūsī, and many commentators identify a punishment or calamity; al-Samīn says ṭāʾif usually signals harm. Al-Qushayrī says it came from heaven and burned the fruits. Ibn ʿAṭiyya allows fire or another destructive agent; Ibn ʿĀshūr says its precise kind is deliberately unnamed, while 68:20 shows its ruinous effect. Al-Biqāʿī calls the act slight for God despite its devastating effect.
  68:19/q02/p2 A command, decree, or event from God. — reasons: Ibn ʿAbbās calls it God's act or command in al-Ṭabarī, al-Māwardī, al-Wāḥidī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Durr; Abū Ẓabyān transmits the wording 'a command from God's command.' Qatāda also uses God's command in al-Wāḥidī and al-Durr; al-Ālūsī reports Ibn ʿAbbās's 'event,' and Elmalılı offers command or disaster.
  68:19/q02/p3 Fire sent from heaven burned the orchard. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ says the sons first thought they had lost the path, then recognized the consequence of denying the poor. Al-Zajjāj says the fire burned it all; al-Naḥḥās specifies its crops. Al-Kalbī via al-Wāḥidī says fire was sent from heaven; Ibn ʿAbbās via al-Wāḥidī says it surrounded and burned it. Al-Wāḥidī explicitly prefers Ibn ʿAbbās's account as the core explanation. Ibn ʿĀshūr also reports a fiery blast.
  68:19/q02/p4 A fiery column or tongue emerged from the garden's own valley and destroyed it. — reasons: Ibn Jurayj is reported with a neck or column of fire from the garden valley by al-Māwardī and with a tongue of fire from that valley by al-Ālūsī. Elmalılı reports that it burned the garden to its roots.
  68:19/q02/p5 A fiery mass or neck came from a valley of Hell. — reasons: Ibn Jurayj's report appears with this origin in al-Qurṭubī, Abū Ḥayyān, and al-Durr; this differs from reports locating its source in the garden's own valley.
  68:19/q02/p6 Gabriel uprooted the garden and carried it around the Kaʿba to the site of al-Ṭāʾif. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān says this accounts for the town's name and its water, trees, and grapes; al-Samīn and Bint al-Shāṭiʾ repeat relocation reports. Al-Ālūsī judges this and related relocation stories unsound.
  68:19/q02/p7 Satan was the visitor. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports this as an alternative to the Gabriel account.
68:19/q03 Must a طائف come at night? [طَآئِفٌۭ · meaning] (turns on: Whether 7:201 and explicit daytime Arab usage defeat al-Farrāʾ's proposed nighttime restriction.)
  68:19/q03/p1 Yes: in ordinary usage a طائف is a nighttime visitor or event. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ gives the restriction and cites Abū al-Jarrāḥ's poem, 'I visited it by day, not at night,' as an explicitly exceptional expression; he says Arabs can use daytime visitation idiomatically for an impossible or unexpected event. Al-Ṭabarī accepts ordinary nighttime use while allowing an explicit daytime phrase. Ibn ʿĀshūr connects al-Farrāʾ's claim with a dream specter but questions derivation from a part of the night. Several sources point to the owners' sleep as confirmation of night.
  68:19/q03/p2 No: a طائف is not limited to a nighttime occurrence. — reasons: Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn, and al-Biqāʿī counter al-Farrāʾ with 7:201, which uses the word for Satan's touch without a time restriction. Al-Ṭabarī also allows explicitly stated daytime visitation, citing Abū al-Jarrāḥ's line.
68:19/q04 How is طائف alternatively read? [طَآئِفٌۭ · readings]
  68:19/q04/p1 Al-Nakhaʿī reads طيف in place of طائف. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn, and al-Ālūsī transmit the reading; al-Samīn refers back to discussion of the two descriptions at 7:201.
68:19/q05 Why is طائف indefinite? [طَآئِفٌۭ · rhetoric]
  68:19/q05/p1 Its indefiniteness magnifies the calamity, while leaving its kind unnamed puts the focus on its effect. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr says the next verse, 68:20, reveals the magnitude of the ruin, and that attributing the event to an unnamed ṭāʾif works like a passive construction.
68:19/q06 What does من ربك attach to? [مِّن رَّبِّكَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether the prepositional phrase modifies the action or describes the visitor.)
  68:19/q06/p1 It attaches to the verb طاف. — reasons: Al-Samīn presents this as one grammatical analysis.
  68:19/q06/p2 It qualifies the noun طائف as an adjective. — reasons: Al-Samīn presents this as the other grammatical analysis.
68:19/q07 What does من ربك convey? [مِّن رَّبِّكَ · interpretation] (turns on: Sources distinguish the phrase's basic attribution of agency from its corrective and reassuring implications.)
  68:19/q07/p1 The affliction originated with and was sent by God. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī gives the origin explicitly. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it divine punishment for ingratitude. Muqātil, al-Bayḍāwī, Elmalılı, and the Study Quran also attribute the visitation to God.
  68:19/q07/p2 The wording frames the devastation as merciful correction meant to reform the Prophet's people. — reasons: Al-Biqāʿī says the destroying act is slight for God although disastrous for the orchard.
  68:19/q07/p3 It reassures the Prophet that sudden divine ruin can reach people who seem secure. — reasons: Iṣlāḥī says the disaster arrived unexpectedly and destroyed the orchard in an instant; amid Quraysh's apparent ease, the phrase reassures the Prophet.
68:19/q08 What does وهم نائمون indicate? [وَهُمْ نَآئِمُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether sleep gives the literal time of the event or depicts the owners' unawareness; al-Ālūsī favors the literal reading from context.)
  68:19/q08/p1 The owners were literally asleep when the visitation came at night. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās and al-Ālūsī call the phrase circumstantial; al-Ālūsī cites Qatāda and prefers literal sleep because it fits the surrounding verses. Ibn ʿĀshūr says it can specify the time or reinforce al-Farrāʾ's nighttime sense while depicting the scene. Bursawī calls sleep a light death, and death heavy sleep.
  68:19/q08/p2 Their sleep also describes utter heedlessness of the decree arriving upon them. — reasons: Bursawī says they were unaware of the decree; al-Ālūsī records this as an alternative interpretation.
68:19/q09 How does the following verse describe the garden's aftermath? [* · links] (turns on: Explanations of ṣarīm in the next verse differ among cutting, darkness, daylight, ash, and barren sand; the cutting and night/day senses are tied to the root's sense of separation.)
  68:19/q09/p1 It became like cut, harvested, or fruitless trees and stubble. — reasons: Al-Qushayrī says the fruits burned and the garden resembled a harvested field. Al-Samīn explains ṣarām as cutting dates and traces ṣarīm, separation, and a detached tract of sand to the root 'cut,' citing Imruʾ al-Qays and another poem; related forms denote a decisive cutter, a dry milch camel, a month or year nearing its end, and bad fortune. He also reports trees stripped of fruit. Ibn Kathīr attributes dry stubble to al-Thawrī and al-Suddī; Ṭabāṭabāʾī gives cut fruitless trees.
  68:19/q09/p2 It became black or dark like night after burning. — reasons: Ibn Kathīr attributes the dark night description to Ibn ʿAbbās. Qatāda and Qaṭr ibn Maymūn in al-Durr say the garden became like dark night; Ṭabāṭabāʾī gives this as a possibility. Al-Samīn reports 'night' for ṣarīm; al-Shammir explains the opposing names night and day by each parting from the other.
  68:19/q09/p3 The description can mean day or dawn. — reasons: Al-Samīn reports both senses for ṣarīm; al-Shammir says night and day receive opposing names because each separates from the other.
  68:19/q09/p4 It became ash. — reasons: Al-Samīn reports Ibn ʿAbbās's explanation that ṣarīm means ash in the language of Khuzayma.
  68:19/q09/p5 It became like a barren tract of sand. — reasons: Al-Samīn reports a known barren sand tract in Yemen; Ṭabāṭabāʾī also gives barren sand as a possible comparison. Al-Samīn relates ṣarīma, a broken-off sand tract, to the root of cutting and separation.
68:19/q10 What background is reported about the garden? [* · reports] (turns on: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ records different reported settings without choosing among them.)
  68:19/q10/p1 A righteous man owned it before leaving it to his people and town. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ summarizes this among exegetical elaborations.
  68:19/q10/p2 The garden was in Yemen near Ṣanʿāʾ. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports this location.
  68:19/q10/p3 The garden was in Abyssinia. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ gives this as another reported location.
  68:19/q10/p4 The garden's people were among the People of Scripture. — reasons: Bint al-Shāṭiʾ gives this as another background report.
