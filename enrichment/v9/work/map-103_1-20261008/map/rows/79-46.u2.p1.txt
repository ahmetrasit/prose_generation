79:46/q09 What lesson does the brief comparison convey? [* · interpretation] (turns on: The authors draw different lessons from the same perceived brevity: worldly transience, imminent judgment, or the gravity of Resurrection.)
  79:46/q09/p1 Worldly pleasures and life pass quickly, so the warning calls for preparation. — reasons: Al-Nahhas warns that worldly people soon leave pleasures. Elmalili says the cautious escape the Hour's terrors while the heedless meet lasting calamity; Kur'an Yolu contrasts short life with everlasting afterlife.
  79:46/q09/p2 The Hour is near or should not be dismissed as distant. — reasons: Abu Hayyan says the comparison brings the Hour close. Islahi says judgment will make the worldly stay seem only an evening or morning, so delay is no ground for dismissing it; Alusi connects this to the challengers' impatient question.
  79:46/q09/p3 The Resurrection is tremendous and terrifying. — reasons: Jishumi says these verses demonstrate its great condition and many terrors; al-Wahidi al-Wajiz and al-Nasafi say its terror makes the stay seem short.
79:46/q10 How does this comparison relate to other Quranic statements about time at judgment? [* · links]
  79:46/q10/p1 It echoes statements that people will reckon their past as an hour, a day, or part of one. — reasons: Al-Mawardi and al-Qurtubi cite 10:45; al-Wahidi al-Basit, al-Baghawi, al-Kashshaf, al-Razi, al-Baydawi and al-Nasafi invoke 46:35. Al-Biqai quotes the judgment scene of an hour and connects the day-or-part-day reply to 23:112–113. Study Quran lists 10:45, 17:52, 20:103–104, 23:112–114, 30:55 and 46:35; Asad cites 2:259, 17:52, 18:19, 20:103, 23:112 and 30:55. Al-Tabatabai cites 23:114 while disputing an earthly-life referent.
79:46/q11 How does this closing comparison answer the earlier question about the Hour? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the preceding question is treated as a request for its unknown date, an impatient taunt, or a warning whose fulfillment will seem near; the reports differ on asking versus mentioning it.)
  79:46/q11/p1 It confirms the warning: the Hour will arrive swiftly and the Prophet's task is warning, while God knows its time. — reasons: Alusi prefers a short stay after the warning or threat and says the sentence may be circumstantial or independent. Islahi says timing demands should not trouble the Prophet. Alusi rejects construing 79:43 as a substitute for their question or a quotation of it, because 79:44 directly assigns knowledge of the endpoint to God; Ibn Jurayj, reported by al-Suyuti, also glosses that endpoint as God's knowledge.
  79:46/q11/p2 It rebuts disbelievers who mockingly or impatiently demand to know when the Hour will come. — reasons: Alusi ties the rebuke to 79:42 and prefers an interval after the warning. Study Quran says the final verses answer taunts over the Prophet's ignorance of the date.
  79:46/q11/p3 Earlier questions about the Hour ended with revelation assigning its timing to God. — reasons: Aisha reports that the Prophet was repeatedly asked until 79:43–44 came and then stopped asking; al-Hakim authenticated this report. Tariq ibn Shihab instead reports that he often mentioned the Hour, then stopped mentioning it after those verses. Aisha also reports the Prophet telling visiting Bedouins that if their youngest lived a century their own Hour would have come.
79:46/q12 What reading or construction is reported for mundhir in the surrounding warning? [* · readings]
  79:46/q12/p1 Several readers use mundhir with tanwin, governing its object. — reasons: Alusi reports Abu Jaʿfar, Shaybah, Khalid al-Hadhdhaʾ, Ibn Hurmuz, Isa, Talha, Ibn Muhaysin, Ibn Miqsam and Abu Amr through al-Mundhir; he says this is the original construction, while genitive attachment can lighten the expression.
79:46/q13 How does the verse serve the surah's overall design and transition? [* · links]
  79:46/q13/p1 It closes the surah's proof of resurrection and human destiny, then returns to the opening and leads into the next surah. — reasons: Al-Biqai says the surah establishes resurrection and gathering after preceding surahs' full exposition, with the oaths bringing the matter to a climax. Its name al-Naziʿat evokes angels extracting souls and the Pharaoh–Moses account; al-Sahirah and al-Tammah also fit the flow. The close returns to the opening through resurrection, then links fear and remembrance to 80:1.
79:46/q14 What does the passage imply about the experience of the afterlife? [* · theology] (turns on: Whether the verse's account of seeing and sensing the Hour entails sensory experience.)
  79:46/q14/p1 The afterlife involves felt experience; a merely spiritual account of comfort or pain is rejected. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi says the passage answers those who claim only spiritual comfort or pain without sensation.
  79:46/q14/p2 Comfort and pain in the afterlife are only spiritual, without sensation. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi identifies and rejects this claim.
79:46/q15 What inward meanings are given to the evening and morning? [عَشِيَّةً أَوْ ضُحَىٰهَا · inward] (turns on: Which paired spiritual phases the two short spans symbolize.)
  79:46/q15/p1 They evoke the setting of truth's light in bodies or its rising from the west; unity makes worldly existence seem imagined. — reasons: Al-Qashani reports this possible inward reading.
  79:46/q15/p2 They signify the bodily and spiritual worlds, passed in two steps before arrival. — reasons: Al-Qashani reports that sensation and intellect conceal these two worlds.
79:46/q16 What recitation virtue is reported for Surat al-Naziʿat? [* · reports]
  79:46/q16/p1 Its reciter will be held in the grave or at resurrection only as long as an obligatory prayer, then enter Paradise. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf reports both grave and resurrection; al-Baydawi reports resurrection and supplies no grading.
79:46/q17 How are the verses' brief-time expressions used in a reported prayer? [* · reports]
  79:46/q17/p1 A prayer for success invokes the briefness-of-time verses alongside mercy, forgiveness, safety, good deeds, Paradise and rescue from Hell. — reasons: Al-Suyuti transmits the prayer from Anas.
79:46/q18 How do Arabic temporal nouns such as ʿashiyyah and ḍuḥā behave in context? [عَشِيَّةً ضُحَىٰهَا · grammar]
  79:46/q18/p1 ʿAshiyyah stays indefinite yet can mean a contextually specified evening; specified ʿashiyyah and ḍuḥā function as accusative time adverbs. — reasons: Sībawayh says ʿashiyyah remains indefinite like ṣabāḥan and masāʾ, though context can identify the day. He explicitly cites this verse for the accusative adverbial use, alongside saḥar, ḍaḥwa, ʿatma, and other time expressions.
  79:46/q18/p2 Some Arabs omit tanwin from ʿashiyyah, by analogy with ghadwa. — reasons: Sībawayh records this usage from some Arabs.
  79:46/q18/p3 Other time expressions vary in definiteness, adverbial use, and context-dependent ellipsis. — reasons: Sībawayh reports Abu ʿAmr on definite ghadwa and bukra, al-Khalil's usage with al-yawm, and Abu al-Khattab's same-day or next-day bukra with 19:62; he also discusses saḥar, event-time answers, omitted kāna in temporal clauses, fixed lā ʿalayka, and dialectal dhā ṣabāḥ.
79:46/q19 What diminutive and plural forms of the verse's time nouns are attested in Arabic? [عَشِيَّةً ضُحَىٰهَا · grammar]
  79:46/q19/p1 Time nouns have regular and irregular diminutives, and plural forms can divide a period into successive parts. — reasons: Sībawayh gives ʿashīshiyyah/ʿashiyyān for ʿashiyyah, ḍuḥayyā for ḍuḥā with a verse of al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī, and other time examples. He explains that diminutives shorten the interval between times, not the time itself; al-Khalil explains aṣīlālā and evening plurals. Sībawayh contrasts personal names and other irregular diminutives.
79:46/q20 What Arabic hamza substitutions and deletions are described alongside the opening word's hamza? [كَأَنَّهُمْ · grammar]
  79:46/q20/p1 Hamza may be replaced by a matching long vowel or deleted in particular lexical, dialectal, and poetic settings. — reasons: Sībawayh distinguishes replacement before a final hamza and radical from extra-letter hamza, explains lightening after each vowel and poetic meter, and gives dialectal examples including Huthayl, nabī/bariyyah, word-boundary substitution, and deletion amid difficult consonant sequences. These are general Arabic examples; he does not specify a variant of كأنهم.
79:46/q21 What case and ellipsis patterns occur in Arabic comparisons mentioning ʿashiyyah? [عَشِيَّةً · grammar]
  79:46/q21/p1 After lā, a term may follow syntactic position or the particle's case; poetic lā ka-l-ʿashiyyati examples can imply an omitted verb. — reasons: Sībawayh and al-Khalil explain positional nominative and alternative accusative in lā constructions. Jarir's lā ka-l-ʿashiyyati requires an understood verb because an evening cannot be a visitor; Sībawayh treats kāf as a preposition and cites Dhu al-Rumma, Imruʾ al-Qays, and other comparisons. These examples concern Arabic usage, not this verse's syntax.
79:46/q22 How can Arabic illā express an exception in the verse's language? [إِلَّا · grammar]
  79:46/q22/p1 An excepted term can be accusative as a corrective 'but' or nominative as an identification with the preceding category. — reasons: Sībawayh contrasts Hijazi accusative with Tamim nominative, citing donkey and verbal-noun examples, al-Khalil's poetic analogy, and examples at 52:28 and 36:43. These establish general uses of illā; he does not assign one of those dialectal constructions specifically to 79:46.
