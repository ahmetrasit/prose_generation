q14 What does Joseph's imprisonment teach about coercion to commit adultery? [لَيَسْجُنُنَّهُۥ · law] (turns on: The degree of coercion: prison threat versus severe beating, and the treatment of sin and penalty.)
  12:35/q14/p1 Threat of imprisonment does not permit adultery, by consensus. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi cites Joseph's refusal despite the threat and his prayer for protection from the women's plot.
  12:35/q14/p2 Under severe beating, the sound view removes the sin of coerced adultery; the claim that its penalty remains is weak. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi distinguishes severe beating from imprisonment and argues from 22:78 that God does not combine two torments.
  12:35/q14/p3 The legal penalty remains even under severe beating. — reasons: Al-Qurtubi reports this claim but calls it weak because God does not combine two torments, citing 22:78.
12:35/q15 How was Joseph publicly taken to prison? [لَيَسْجُنُنَّهُۥ · reports]
  12:35/q15/p1 He was paraded on a donkey through Egypt's markets while a drum and callers announced a false charge against him. — reasons: Ibn Abbas says the announcement accused the Hebrew of desiring his master and deserving prison; Abu Salih says Ibn Abbas wept whenever recounting the humiliation.
12:35/q16 What did Joseph do during and after imprisonment? [لَيَسْجُنُنَّهُۥ · reports]
  12:35/q16/p1 In prison he comforted, cared for, and interpreted dreams for others, proclaimed his prophetic lineage, and called people to monotheism. — reasons: Muqatil gives the acts of care and descent through Jacob, Isaac and Abraham; al-Tabataba'i says prison is where he first identifies his Abrahamic family and teaches monotheism.
  12:35/q16/p2 He refused the king's later summons until his innocence was established and he could leave with his reputation clear. — reasons: Ibn Kathir links this to 12:50.
  12:35/q16/p3 The baker and cupbearer were also jailed over suspicion of a poisoning plot against the king. — reasons: Al-Durr says the baker tried to poison the king and the cupbearer was suspected of collusion.
12:35/q17 Why did God allow Joseph's imprisonment, and what did it reveal? [* · theology] (turns on: The sources give differing theological accounts of divine purpose and human agency, while also recounting human injustice.)
  12:35/q17/p1 God made prison a punishment or expiation for Joseph's earlier inclination toward the woman. — reasons: Al-Tabari, al-Tha'labi, al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi and al-Qurtubi state purification from desire. Ibn Abbas counts three lapses: inclination led to prison, asking the cupbearer prolonged it, and accusing the brothers of theft brought their accusation concerning Benjamin; Ibn Atiyya cites 12:24, 12:42, 12:70 and 12:77.
  12:35/q17/p2 God used an unjust prison to protect Joseph's faith, defeat the women's plot, and raise him toward authority. — reasons: Al-Biqa'i says divine power overturns expected causes and establishes Joseph's rank; al-Jishumi's report from al-Asamm says the women gained only his apparent confinement, not religious corruption. Al-Tabataba'i says imprisonment brings him near the king and Egyptian authority. Al-Islahi says Joseph valued faith and character above hardship; Wahb reports Gabriel's message that his resistance through grief, slavery and prison made him among the purified and righteous.
  12:35/q17/p3 The plotting and imprisonment were the people's own acts, not acts created by God. — reasons: Al-Jishumi states this agency claim even while reporting that the women failed to corrupt Joseph.
12:35/q18 Was Joseph's earlier prayer a request to be imprisoned? [* · links] (turns on: Whether the preceding response answers Joseph's request for protection or a supposed request for prison; the later decision wording is the cited test.)
  12:35/q18/p1 No; the response to his prayer was protection from sin, and only afterward did they decide to imprison him. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i uses the sequence of the preceding verse and this verse to reject the claim that God granted a prayer for imprisonment.
  12:35/q18/p2 Yes; the response granted a prayer for imprisonment. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i reports this interpretation only to reject it on the wording of the sequence.
12:35/q19 How does this turn in Joseph's story connect to the wider narrative? [* · links]
  12:35/q19/p1 The imprisonment begins another trial and becomes the route to Joseph's eventual vindication and authority. — reasons: Al-Tabataba'i links the years in prison with monotheist teaching, disclosure of his family, and his later proximity to the king; Ibn Kathir points to the later summons in 12:50 and Joseph's insistence on exoneration. Al-Islahi calls it another trial that Joseph desired over surrendering his faith. Asad compares the Qur'anic account with Genesis and invokes 2:216: people may dislike what benefits them, while God's direction of affairs is hidden.
12:35/q20 What inward meaning is given to Joseph's imprisonment? [* · inward] (turns on: The two inward readings map prison to different inner subjects: spirit in seclusion, or heart held by law and protection.)
  12:35/q20/p1 It is the spirit's seclusion, agreed by spirit, soul, faculties, intellect and thought for vision, defeat of illusion, guidance and the authority of thought. — reasons: Al-Qashani connects spiritual retreat with failed attraction and the inner faculties' agreement.
  12:35/q20/p2 The heart is confined by religious law and divine protection when it resists worldly and lower-self prompting. — reasons: Al-Ta'wilat al-Najmiyya, as cited by Bursavi, says even a prophet's heart would incline without divine protection.
12:35/q21 What general moral or historical point does the imprisonment illustrate? [* · interpretation]
  12:35/q21/p1 It shows wrongful social power and women's plotting: a compliant husband jailed an innocent man to protect appearances. — reasons: Abu Hayyan stresses a husband yielding authority despite his wife's treachery; al-Biqa'i calls the choice unjust. Islahi observes that masters had unrestricted power over enslaved people and that the Aziz's royal guard office removed obstacles. Kuranyolu says the household displaced blame onto the enslaved Joseph. Asad cites Zamakhshari's camel-reins simile for the wife's control.
12:35/q22 Which terms in this narrative does Abu Hayyan explain lexically? [* · meaning]
  12:35/q22/p1 His opening list treats imprisonment alongside trade, gentle entreaty, seeking marriage, locking, tearing cloth, and master or chief. — reasons: Abu Hayyan lists these lexical explanations for the surrounding narrative without a further argument in this note.
