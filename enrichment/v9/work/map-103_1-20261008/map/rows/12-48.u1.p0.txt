## THE CURRENT MAP
12:48/q01 What does 'after that' refer to, and why use this wording? [مِنۢ بَعْدِ ذَٰلِكَ · rhetoric]
  12:48/q01/p1 The hard years follow the preceding seven years; the demonstrative magnifies that preceding period. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī says 'after that' rather than 'after them' magnifies the earlier period.
12:48/q02 What are the seven severe ones, and what makes them severe? [سَبْعٌۭ شِدَادٌۭ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether shidād primarily describes the hardship experienced during drought or extends the image of aggressive, devouring years.)
  12:48/q02/p1 They are seven barren years of drought and famine, severe because people suffer hardship in them. — reasons: Maqātil, al-Ṭabarī, al-Thaʿlabī and others identify a seven-year famine following fertility. Al-Māwardī and al-Baghawī locate the severity in people's hardship; al-Bursawī calls it worse for them than captivity or killing. Al-Ṭabrisī says shidda, solidity and difficulty are near synonyms and reports seven uses, including time, anger, pain, drink and the body. Ibn ʿĀshūr calls describing years as severe a rational metaphor. Ibn ʿAṭiyya reports Arabic famine descriptions of a year stripping everything bare and drying stars, flesh and bone. Ibn Kathīr adds that sowing in those years yields nothing. Al-Biqāʿī links their seven-year duration to the dream imagery in 12:36 and 12:43. The Study Quran calls them the second phase of extreme dearth.
  12:48/q02/p2 The severe years are pictured as attacking things that pounce on the people and their stores. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī says this sense of shidād better suits the following image of the years devouring stored food, and elaborates the image as ravenous predators.
12:48/q03 What noun is understood after 'seven severe'? [سَبْعٌۭ شِدَادٌۭ · grammar]
  12:48/q03/p1 The omitted counted noun is 'years,' supplied by the preceding mention of seven years. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī each derive the elided noun from the earlier wording.
12:48/q04 How did Joseph recognize the first severe year? [سَبْعٌۭ شِدَادٌۭ · reports]
  12:48/q04/p1 A man who had previously left part of his food ate his whole allotment, showing Joseph that the famine had begun. — reasons: Ibn Zayd says the man ordinarily saved half his portion. Zayd ibn Aslam, as reported by al-Māwardī, al-Ṭabrisī and al-Qurṭubī, says Joseph served food for two; the accounts vary in whether this is described as eating an entire or double portion.
12:48/q05 How do the dream's cows and ears relate to these years? [سَبْعٌۭ شِدَادٌۭ يَأْكُلْنَ · links] (turns on: Whether the dream images primarily signify successive kinds of years or also prescribe the planting and preservation measures needed during them.)
  12:48/q05/p1 The lean cows and dry ears signify barren years, and their eating the fat cows depicts famine consuming stores from prosperous years. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī, Ibn Kathīr and al-Biqāʿī identify the lean cows and dry ears with drought; al-Biqāʿī cites the dream imagery in 12:36 and 12:43. Ibn ʿĀshūr maps cows to agricultural years, fatness to fertility, leanness to drought, ears to provisions, and seven dry ears to stores accumulated over seven years. Al-Bayḍāwī says the figurative eating follows the dream symbol. Al-Bursawī clarifies that thin cows did not literally eat fat ones. Qatāda says the first seven fertile and next seven dry years are indicated by the dream. Al-Qummī's report recounts the king's cows and ears and Joseph's interpretation.
  12:48/q05/p2 The cows picture yearly provisions and the ears picture the grain and the work needed to preserve it. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī takes fat cows as abundant harvests from repeated planting and lean cows as shortages eating saved food. For him green ears are stores and dry ears point to keeping grain unthreshed; the lean cows' failure to eat every fat cow allows a remaining reserve.
12:48/q06 Is Joseph's explanation a forecast, practical direction, or both? [* · interpretation] (turns on: How much of Joseph's statement is read as decoding the dream's future and how much as instructions to meet that future.)
  12:48/q06/p1 It forecasts seven fertile years followed by seven barren years and a further recovery year. — reasons: Qatāda says the first fourteen years are shown by the dream and the later abundant year was separately disclosed. The Study Quran describes an ensuing fifteen-year agricultural cycle. Al-Qummī reports Joseph interpreting plentiful years, famine years and then rain. Ṭabāṭabāʾī explicitly objects to treating the dream as merely this forecast.
  12:48/q06/p2 It sets out the practical policy of repeated cultivation, storage and rationing needed to survive famine. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī favors this as the dream's force for the king; planting repeatedly, preserving grain in ears and rationing reserves are required measures, and the symbols depict that work. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says Joseph's storage plan carries the population through drought, including use of scant produce grown during famine. The Study Quran says the first harvests should be stored for food in the second phase.
  12:48/q06/p3 Joseph's reply combines interpretation of meaning, practical advice and disclosure of the unseen further year. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān identifies these three kinds of speech and says the interpretation is by meaning rather than the dream's exact wording.
12:48/q07 How did Joseph know the further abundant year? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Whether Joseph's knowledge of the unasked-for year in 12:49 came from earlier divine teaching or a direct disclosure at this time.)
  12:48/q07/p1 He used knowledge God had previously taught him. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī reports this as one side of the exegetes' disagreement over Joseph's interpretation.
  12:48/q07/p2 He learned it through direct revelation. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī says the prediction of an abundant year not asked about in the dream supports direct revelation; Qatāda, reported by al-Rāzī, says God granted Joseph knowledge of that additional year, while the preceding fourteen years are indicated by the dream.
12:48/q08 Why are the years said to eat the stored food? [يَأْكُلْنَ · rhetoric] (turns on: Whether 'eating' is explained through inhabitants' action, the verb's sense of exhausting, or a developed image of predatory years.)
  12:48/q08/p1 Eating is attributed figuratively to the years because their inhabitants eat during them. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī and al-Thaʿlabī cite poetry calling day heedless and night asleep for people's acts; al-Wāḥidī and Ibn al-Jawzī also cite a sleeping night. Al-Zamakhsharī describes a figurative grammatical subject. Ibn ʿAṭiyya calls it metonymy and cites 27:86, 10:67 and 40:61; al-Samīn cites the day in 10:67. Al-Bayḍāwī says the attribution also matches the dream. Al-Ālūsī says it expresses what people undergo; Ibn ʿĀshūr locates the loss in the years as its time. Abū Ḥayyān compares describing a day as seeing. Al-Baghawī calls it an expansive metaphor.
  12:48/q08/p2 'Eat' means use up, exhaust or destroy the stored food. — reasons: Ibn al-Anbārī, reported by al-Wāḥidī, and al-Ṭabrisī cite the Arab idiom that a journey ate a camel's flesh; al-Wāḥidī also cites a line by Dhū al-Rumma. Al-Ālūsī reports this explanation from some commentators. Ibn ʿĀshūr cites Q 4:2 for consumption, while still calling attribution to years rational metaphor. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says drought may be described as consuming and drying vegetation.
  12:48/q08/p3 The wording personifies the harsh years as ravenous predators attacking people and their provisions. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī relates this image to the sense of shidād as attacking things and the following description of devouring.
12:48/q09 What variant reading is reported for 'they eat'? [يَأْكُلْنَ · readings]
  12:48/q09/p1 Al-Sulamī reads yaʾkulūn, 'they eat,' in the masculine plural, referring to people. — reasons: Abū Ḥayyān reports al-Sulamī's third-person reading and says it speaks of people eating rather than addressing the listeners.
12:48/q10 What is the food 'put forward for them'? [مَا قَدَّمْتُمْ لَهُنَّ · referent]
  12:48/q10/p1 It is the harvest and provisions gathered and stored in the fertile years for consumption in the coming famine. — reasons: Al-Farrāʾ glosses it as crops prepared in advance. Al-Ṭabarī, Qatāda, al-Māwardī, Ibn Kathīr and others specify earlier stores. Ibn ʿAṭiyya includes even scant produce obtained during famine in what sustains the people. Al-Bursawī says grain was stored in its ears. Ibn Kathīr says drought sowing yields no return, which makes the prior stores necessary. Al-Iṣlāḥī says the drought destroys the stores and leaves very little.
12:48/q11 Why does the verse say 'for them' of the years? [لَهُنَّ · rhetoric]
  12:48/q11/p1 The pronoun reinforces the personification of the years or corresponds to the dream's imagery. — reasons: Al-Ālūsī offers both explanations for lahunna.
12:48/q12 What is the little amount excepted from consumption, and what is it for? [إِلَّا قَلِيلًۭا مِّمَّا تُحْصِنُونَ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the exception identifies an unspecified protected remainder, seed needed for later planting, or a little withdrawn from stores for present eating.)
  12:48/q12/p1 A small part of the harvest remains protected in storage as a reserve. — reasons: Maqātil, al-Ṭabarī, Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatāda and others gloss the exception as the little saved or secured. Al-Māwardī reports protected storehouses. Al-Nasafī says the people keep only a small part of what they save. Al-Rāzī describes it as a protected remnant, although his wording says only a little of the reserves will be consumed. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the exception urges ample stockpiling.
  12:48/q12/p2 The little preserved is seed for sowing after the famine, protecting the next harvest. — reasons: Al-Māwardī calls reserving seed a further possibility. Al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Bursawī, al-Ālūsī and Kur'an Yolu give this purpose. Ṭabāṭab
