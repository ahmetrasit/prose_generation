12:43/q29 What does it mean to 'cross' or interpret a dream? [تَعْبُرُونَ · meaning]
  12:43/q29/p1 It is to pass from dream images to their eventual meaning or outcome. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj, al-Azhari, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Kashshaf, Abu Hayyan, al-Nasafi, al-Alusi, al-Tabataba'i, and Study Quran derive the sense from crossing a river to the far bank; al-Azhari says the interpreter examines both sides and traces beginning to end. Al-Baydawi calls it passage to corresponding psychic meaning, al-Alusi external or inward reality; ta'wil names the destination. Al-Samin cites al-Kashshaf's gloss. The author of al-Nazm compares adjectival usage in 4:90 and 3:7, and replacement of verb by adjective in 7:193.
12:43/q30 Which verb form for interpreting dreams is established? [تَعْبُرُونَ · meaning] (turns on: Established linguistic usage versus the Bedouin poetic attestation of the doubled form.)
  12:43/q30/p1 The light, ungeminated 'abara is the established and stronger usage. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf and al-Nasafi say authorities reject the doubled verb, ta'bir, and mu'abbir; al-Samin transmits al-Kashshaf, and Ibn Ashur cites his preference. Al-Alusi agrees on the lighter form's strength but acknowledges evidence for the doubled form.
  12:43/q30/p2 The doubled form is attested, although some reject it. — reasons: Al-Mubarrad cites a Bedouin line using dream-interpreter wording; Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, al-Alusi, and Ibn Ashur transmit it. Al-Alusi says the light form remains more established.
12:43/q31 What work does the lam in 'li-l-ru'ya' do? [لِلرُّءْيَا · grammar] (turns on: Whether lam directly marks an object, strengthens fronting, gives direction, or belongs to a different underlying predication or verb.)
  12:43/q31/p1 It clarifies or specifies the object of interpretation. — reasons: Al-Zajjaj and transmitters gloss 'if you interpret, namely the dream'; al-Samin compares 12:20 and proposes 'I mean the dream.' Al-Kashshaf, al-Nasafi, and al-Baydawi also permit this analysis. Al-Zarkashi gives haita laka at 12:23 as another example of a clarifying lam, glossing it as 'come or approach so that I may speak to you.'
  12:43/q31/p2 It emphasizes or strengthens a fronted object and weakened delayed verb. — reasons: Ibn al-Anbari calls it emphatic; Ibn Atiyya links it to fronting. Abu Hayyan prefers strengthening when the object precedes, not when it follows. Al-Samin says such additions normally need fronting or a derived governor, noting exceptions or containment with 11:107 and 27:72. Ibn Ashur adds foregrounding the dream and cadence. Al-Zarkashi describes the lam as transitive, strengthening a weak verb whose object precedes it, and also cites 23:60. Al-Suyuti likewise calls it emphatic or strengthening when the governing element is weak or delayed.
  12:43/q31/p3 It means 'toward,' directing interpretation to the dream. — reasons: Ibn al-Anbari offers this as an alternative.
  12:43/q31/p4 It is a lam of attachment linking interpreters to the vision. — reasons: Ahmad ibn Yahya gives the sense 'interpreters of the dream.' Al-Zarkashi reports that Basran grammarians call this the lam of annexation, while Ibn al-Anbari calls it instrumental; he also cites 31:14 and 7:62.
  12:43/q31/p5 It belongs to an understood or included verb meaning undertake the dream's interpretation. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf suggests volunteering for the task; al-Samin says ta'burun contains a verb governing lam; Abu Hayyan reports al-Zamakhshari's understood verb; al-Baydawi allows a lam-taking verb. Al-Alusi reports an eagerness reading.
  12:43/q31/p6 The phrase with lam is the predicate of kana, denoting competence in visions. — reasons: Al-Kashshaf, al-Razi, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, and al-Samin report this; ta'burun is another predicate or a circumstantial clause. Al-Alusi finds these alternatives strained.
  12:43/q31/p7 It is an extra lam with no independent meaning. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi reports this as a twelfth use of lam and includes 'you interpret the dream' under it, also mentioning 7:154.
  12:43/q31/p8 It is an instrumental lam. — reasons: Al-Zarkashi reports Ibn al-Anbari's description of this lam as instrumental, alongside the Basran description as a lam of annexation; he mentions 31:14 and 7:62.
12:43/q32 Why is 'for the dream' placed before 'interpret'? [لِلرُّءْيَا تَعْبُرُونَ · rhetoric]
  12:43/q32/p1 It foregrounds the dream and maintains the verse's cadence. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says the definite article is generic; grammarians also link the position to the strengthening lam.
12:43/q33 What is the predicate of 'if you are' in this phrase? [إِن كُنتُمْ لِلرُّءْيَا تَعْبُرُونَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether ta'burun alone completes kana or the prepositional phrase predicates interpretive competence.)
  12:43/q33/p1 Ta'burun is the straightforward predicate of kuntum. — reasons: Abu Hayyan prefers the apparent syntax over al-Zamakhshari's more complex parses.
  12:43/q33/p2 Li-l-ru'ya is the predicate, with ta'burun a second predicate or circumstance. — reasons: Al-Zamakhshari's analysis is reported by al-Razi, al-Nasafi, Abu Hayyan, and al-Samin; it conveys competence in dreams. Al-Alusi considers the analysis strained.
12:43/q34 Why did the court fail to interpret the king's dream? [أَفْتُونِى فِى رُءْيَٰىَ إِن كُنتُمْ لِلرُّءْيَا تَعْبُرُونَ · links]
  12:43/q34/p1 They called it confused dreams and said it exceeded their interpretive skill. — reasons: Al-Suddi and Ibn Abi Hatim describe consulted specialists. Al-Razi says they did not deny dream interpretation itself and implied a more skilled interpreter might succeed. Al-Qurtubi and al-Durr report the dismissal. Al-Razi likens a confused dream to a bundle of unrelated plants; al-Harawi derives adghath from bundled grass. Ata and Ibn Abbas call such dreams false, Mujahid a jumble or frightening terrors, Abu Ubayda uninterpretable. Al-Tabataba'i distinguishes tangled transformations from clearer symbolic dreams. Bint al-Shati notes that the Qur'an uses plural ahlam for mixed-up dreams and that the courtiers called this king's clear ru'ya confused dreams at 12:44.
12:43/q35 How does the king's dream connect to Joseph's story and other dream reports? [* · links] (turns on: These are distinct links drawn from the narrative rather than competing explanations.)
  12:43/q35/p1 A dream begins Joseph's ordeal and another brings release and eventual vindication. — reasons: Al-Nasafi draws the symmetry; al-Tabataba'i says the passage leads to release, rule, and renewed investigation proving innocence. He lists Jacob's explanation of Joseph's dream, Joseph's own and the prisoners' and king's dreams, and waking predictions taught by God, citing 12:4, 12:36, and 12:43. Islahi traces the prisoners' dreams to the opportunity to interpret the king's.
  12:43/q35/p2 The story illustrates God's unexpected direction of human affairs. — reasons: Asad contrasts Genesis' romantic telling with the Qur'anic lesson that people can dislike what benefits them and love what harms them, citing 2:216; Islahi says God's plans generate their own means.
  12:43/q35/p3 Royal dream consultation was a recognized Egyptian and ancient court practice. — reasons: Ibn Ashur says priestly rules made dream interpretation an Egyptian science, cites the prisoners' request in 12:36 and the Torah's Pharaoh summoning magicians and sages; he reports Kisra summoning the soothsayer Satih after an alarming dream near the Prophet's birth, through Abd al-Masih.
12:43/q36 How does 'eating' describe what the lean cows did? [يَأْكُلُهُنَّ · meaning]
  12:43/q36/p1 Al-akl can mean swallowing. — reasons: Al-Damghani gives swallowing as a sense of al-akl.
12:43/q37 What does al-Baqillani say about the passage's compact Qur'anic expression? [* · rhetoric]
  12:43/q37/p1 Its brevity remains clear and full, displaying an integration of expression beyond human rhetorical ability. — reasons: Al-Baqillani says this passage's brevity increases clarity and fullness, mentioning 27:31–33. He also argues that even a few rare, distinguished words are hard for humans to combine smoothly, and cites the varied but integrated tellings of Moses' story as another illustration of Qur'anic expression.
12:43/q38 What other Qur'anic uses of lam are listed alongside discussion of this lam? [لِلرُّءْيَا · grammar]
  12:43/q38/p1 Elsewhere lam may mean at or with, that, lest, consequence, cause or purpose, or in. — reasons: Ibn al-Jawzi reports these six further uses from some exegetes, illustrated respectively by 20:108; 3:179, 8:33, and 14:46; 16:55, 29:66, and 30:34; 6:53, 10:88, and 28:8; 76:9; and 59:2. Consequence denotes an outcome that was not the intended purpose.
12:43/q39 How are the adjacent hamzas in 'al-mala'u aftuni' classified? [ٱلْمَلَأُ أَفْتُونِى · readings]
  12:43/q39/p1 They form a damma-then-fath pair, one of five Qur'anic categories of adjacent hamzas. — reasons: Ibn al-Jazari says six vowel pairings are possible but five occur in the Qur'an. He places al-mala'u aftuni in the damma-then-fath group of eleven agreed sites, also including 27:32, with two disputed Nabi' forms in al-Ahzab. For comparison he counts seventeen agreed open-then-kasra sites plus two disputed Zakariyya idh sites, one open-then-damma site at 23:44, and fifteen agreed kasra-then-fath sites including 12:76 and 67:16–17.
12:43/q40 How is the first-person ya in 'inni ara' read? [إِنِّىٓ أَرَىٰ · readings]
  12:43/q40/p1 The Medinans, Ibn Kathir, and Abu Amr open the ya. — reasons: Abu Amr lists inni ara sab' among seven first-person-ya forms read with an open ya by these readers.
