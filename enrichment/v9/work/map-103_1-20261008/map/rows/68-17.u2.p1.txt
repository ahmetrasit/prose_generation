  68:17/q09/p8 People living shortly after Jesus was raised. — reasons: Some exegetes date them before Christianity spread in Yemen; Ibn ʿĀshūr reports the proposed chronology near the Abyssinian conquest and Year of the Elephant.
68:17/q10 How many heirs were there? [أَصْحَٰبَ ٱلْجَنَّةِ · reports] (turns on: Different numerical reports.)
  68:17/q10/p1 Three brothers or sons. — reasons: Al-Baghawī and Bursawī give three heirs; ʿIkrima's report via Ibn ʿĀshūr calls them three wealthy sons.
  68:17/q10/p2 Five sons, four supporting the plan and a moderate brother objecting. — reasons: Ṭabāṭabāʾī relays Ibn ʿAbbās's account of the unusually abundant harvest and the dissenting fifth brother.
68:17/q11 What had the father given to the poor? [أَصْحَٰبَ ٱلْجَنَّةِ · reports] (turns on: Accounts differ over surplus, gleanings and an owed share, which may coexist.)
  68:17/q11/p1 He kept his family's annual food and gave the surplus to the poor. — reasons: Al-Zajjāj also records this alongside gleanings; Qatāda describes the old man's sons objecting during his lifetime. Ibn Kathīr says the eventual loss covered crop, profit and charity.
  68:17/q11/p2 He let the poor collect gleanings, missed fruit, fallen dates, grapes and what harvesting tools or wind left. — reasons: Muqātil, al-Farrāʾ, al-Baghawī, al-Bayḍāwī and others specify what pickers, sickles or wind missed. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says one account had the father bring the poor along at harvest; al-Wāḥidī says he announced it to them.
  68:17/q11/p3 He paid God's due or gave a prescribed share to its rightful recipients. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās describes the poor's obligatory due; al-Qurṭubī says the Yemenite father paid God's due; Ibn ʿAbbās's report in Ṭabāṭabāʾī says each rightful person received a share.
68:17/q12 Why did the heirs plan to exclude the poor? [أَقْسَمُوا۟ · reports] (turns on: Different reports emphasize household need or rejection of the father's charitable practice.)
  68:17/q12/p1 They feared their many dependents would be left short by continued giving. — reasons: Muqātil says they thought food plentiful yet had many dependents; al-Farrāʾ says wealth had shrunk. Al-Wāḥidī says this excuse ended in ruin; later reports describe fear of poverty.
  68:17/q12/p2 They judged their father's charity foolish and resolved to keep its benefit for themselves. — reasons: ʿIkrima and Ibn ʿAbbās report their contempt for their father; Qatāda says they opposed him while he lived. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says they judged his generosity mistaken.
68:17/q13 How does 'when they swore' attach, and why mention an oath? [إِذْ أَقْسَمُوا۟ · grammar] (turns on: The notes give both syntactic attachment and differing rhetorical functions of the oath.)
  68:17/q13/p1 Idh is governed by balawnā. — reasons: Al-Samīn and al-Ālūsī give the syntactic attachment.
  68:17/q13/p2 The oath shows collective commitment to the plan, perhaps overcoming one brother's dissent. — reasons: Ibn ʿĀshūr infers a hesitant brother from 68:28; a report says he warned them to fear God and recall punishment, but the others secured participation by oath. Elmalılı likens the opening oath to an agreement that governs a shared enterprise's profit and loss. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ describes the garden owners' oath as sincere.
  68:17/q13/p3 The oath warns against overconfident vows made without regard to knowledge or power. — reasons: Elmalılı links it to the earlier 'frequent swearer'; Iṣlāḥī says the owners trusted the garden's past survival of weather. Jishumī faults swearing to deny the poor.
68:17/q14 What did the oath resolve to do? [أَقْسَمُوا۟ لَيَصْرِمُنَّهَا · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the oath is read only as intended harvest or also as an ominous literal cutting of the garden.)
  68:17/q14/p1 Harvest all the fruit secretly before the poor arrived, leaving them no share. — reasons: Muqātil says they would avoid announcing harvest as their father did; al-Ṭabarī, al-Baghawī and others describe dawn gathering. Al-Ālūsī says cutting was planned when ripe; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ stresses that everything would be taken. Ibn ʿĀshūr suggests the oath bound a hesitant brother.
  68:17/q14/p2 Their confident oath came true as a cutting of the orchard itself, to their harm and without their hand. — reasons: Elmalılı distinguishes their intended gathering from the literal words 'cut the garden,' then says the sworn event occurred unexpectedly in the ruin.
68:17/q15 How is layasrimannahā related to the oath? [لَيَصْرِمُنَّهَا · grammar]
  68:17/q15/p1 It is the oath's response in reported third person; direct quotation would be first-person plural. — reasons: Al-Samīn supplies hypothetical lanasrimannahā; al-Ālūsī says the wording reports rather than quotes; Abū Ḥayyān calls it the oath response.
68:17/q16 What does ṣarm or layasrimannahā mean? [لَيَصْرِمُنَّهَا · meaning] (turns on: Whether the word is explained by its ordinary harvest sense or its force of complete severance.)
  68:17/q16/p1 Cutting or harvesting fruit, especially date clusters, or gathering the crop. — reasons: Al-Ṭabarī cites Imruʾ al-Qays using ṣaram for severing a relationship; Jishumī adds ṣarām and jadhādh for harvest and ṣarm for separation between companions. Al-Naḥḥās distinguishes jadhādh and says the verb means cut and gather; al-Wāḥidī and al-Qurṭubī explain aṣrama as palms ready for cutting. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ notes later figurative extension.
  68:17/q16/p2 An emphatic complete severing of the garden that excludes the poor. — reasons: Biqāʿī hears finality in the expression; Elmalılı notes that their literal oath named cutting the garden itself.
68:17/q17 When was the harvest planned, and how does muṣbiḥīn work? [مُصْبِحِينَ · grammar] (turns on: Whether 'entering morning' emphasizes dawn itself or the dark period immediately before it.)
  68:17/q17/p1 At the beginning of morning or dawn, before the poor learned of it. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās and al-Samīn call muṣbiḥīn circumstantial; al-Samīn links it to the verb's subject and compares 37:137 and a proverb about a journeyer arrived by morning. Ibn ʿAṭiyya simply glosses entering morning. Al-Dāmghānī specifies dawn after the night has passed and relates the morning language to 68:20 and 18:42. Ibn al-Jawzī gives the basic sense of aṣbaḥa as reaching morning, while also recording its separate sense of kindling a lamp.
  68:17/q17/p2 In late-night or predawn darkness while keeping the action secret. — reasons: Al-Wāḥidī calls the dimness sadfa; al-Farrāʾ says late-night darkness; al-Zamakhsharī and al-Qurṭubī also put it before dawn.
68:17/q18 What did they fail to except from their plan? [أَقْسَمُوا۟ · interpretation] (turns on: Whether the omitted exception concerns God's will in the oath or the poor's share of the harvest.)
  68:17/q18/p1 They did not say 'if God wills' when vowing about the future. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī explains that the conditional expression functions as an exception because their meanings coincide; al-Rāzī calls this the majority view and connects it to overconfidence. Jishumī says one cannot be sure a future statement will occur. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ rejects this as the verse's apparent gloss.
  68:17/q18/p2 They intended to make no exception for the poor and leave none of the crop. — reasons: Al-Rāzī reports this alternative; Bint al-Shāṭiʾ prefers the apparent story of taking everything at dawn over the divine-will formula.
68:17/q19 What legal and moral lessons are drawn from the planned deprivation? [أَقْسَمُوا۟ لَيَصْرِمُنَّهَا · law] (turns on: The notes draw distinct consequences for harvest rights, intention and future oaths.)
  68:17/q19/p1 The poor had a claim on the harvest; harvesters should give those present a share. — reasons: Al-Naḥḥās calls payment an obligatory due. Al-Qurṭubī prefers a distinct harvest right based on 6:141, separate from zakāt, and favors forbidding night harvest when used to deprive the poor.
  68:17/q19/p2 Resolve to do evil is already blameworthy and can draw punishment before the act. — reasons: Jishumī says taking property and denying rights in fact would be worse; the owners were punished for resolve.
  68:17/q19/p3 Future-directed speech should include an exception because fulfillment is uncertain. — reasons: Jishumī treats the owners' omitted exception as support for this requirement.
68:17/q20 What happened to the orchard after the oath? [أَصْحَٰبَ ٱلْجَنَّةِ · reports] (turns on: Reports differ on the garden's physical fate and the origin of Ṭāʾif's name.)
  68:17/q20/p1 It burned or was blighted overnight and appeared black, ruined or reduced to ashes. — reasons: Muqātil says it perished; al-Māwardī gives ashes; al-Baghawī says the heirs found it black; al-Nasafī calls it a blight. Al-Zamakhsharī reports ṭayf beside ṭāʾif for the calamity and refers to 18:42; he lists ṣarīm as cut, blackened, dry, empty or sandy. The owners initially thought they had lost their way, then admitted deprivation through their wrongdoing.
  68:17/q20/p2 Gabriel swept the garden around the Kaaba and set it at what became Ṭāʾif. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī reports this as an explanation for the town's name and fertility.
  68:17/q20/p3 Ṭāʾif's name came instead from a man of Ṣadaf who built a wall around the town. — reasons: Al-Qurṭubī transmits al-Bakrī's competing etymology.
68:17/q21 How do later phrases in the story describe the owners' conduct and response? [* · interpretation] (turns on: Different later expressions locate the action, the warning, the confession and the hoped-for restoration.)
  68:17/q21/p1 Their secret departure aimed to deny the poor but left them deprived themselves. — reasons: Al-Zamakhsharī explains ḥard through withholding good, withholding milk, determination or speed; he cites a line of poetry and swift birds called ḥarād. Another reading makes ḥard their garden's name or confidence in success; an interpretation of ʿalā ḥard allows only anger and mutual blame.
