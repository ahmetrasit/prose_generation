# 78:14: 16 questions

## 78:14/q01 What does the plural in “We sent down” convey?  [وَأَنزَلْنَا · rhetoric]
- 78:14/q01/p1 It signals the majesty and collectivity of the Divine Essence, Names, and Attributes. — Bursevi explains the divine plural in these terms. Samarrāʾī observes that the signs through 78:14 mostly use the divine first-person plural and compares the surah's wording at 78:37–38; he does not give a separate explanation of the plural. [2 notes: BURSEVI; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 78:14/q02 What are the muʿṣirāt?  [ٱلْمُعْصِرَٰتِ · referent]
turns on: The immediate source of rain ('from' clouds or sky) versus winds as the means of drawing it out; some explanations turn on whether the name describes imminent rainfall, squeezing, relief, or whirlwinds.
- 78:14/q02/p1 Rain-bearing clouds, heavy with water and ready to release it. — Al-Tabari, al-Nahhas, al-Qurtubi, and Ibn Kathir favor clouds because rain comes from them and the verse says 'from'; Ibn Kathir cites 30:48. Al-Farra, Ibn Qutayba, al-Razi, al-Zamakhshari and others liken impending rain to a girl approaching menstruation; Abu al-Najm's verse is cited by al-Tha'labi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin and al-Alusi. Al-Azhari disputes al-Farra's precise definition: Abu Ubayd's informants say a girl has reached maturity, al-Kisa'i says approaching twenty, and Ibn al-A'rabi says the moment of menstruation, relating it to seclusion/refuge. Al-Wahidi and Bursevi compare ready grain; al-Samin notes the active form suits imminence, since otherwise a passive participle would be expected for clouds pressed by winds, and reports al-Zamakhshari's entry-into-a-state explanation. Abu Ishaq derives the name from clouds squeezing out water; al-Mubarrad says they retain and release water gradually, with 'asr also meaning refuge. Ibn Kaysan links the term to relief and 12:49; al-Samin questions the form-IV derivation. Al-Azhari prefers rain clouds over whirlwinds, which do not bring rain. Al-Biqai adds that these clouds cool the sun and enable food and drink. Ibn Kathir reports al-Tabari's preference; Islahi says water-laden and rain-producing clouds do not differ essentially. Sibawayh cites a verse about Mayya's dwellings and rain clouds, and al-Jurjānī cites a poetic line in which al-muʿṣirāt disperse rain upon a deserted site. Al-Itqān explicitly identifies the muʿṣirāt as clouds. Ibn ʿAbbās, as reported by Bint al-Shāṭiʾ, says clouds press one another so water emerges and cites al-Nābigha's poetry. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ says that two clouds are not required by this gloss; rain-heavy clouds suffice. She cites al-Rāghib's definition as rain-saturated clouds, favors it from context and Quranic usage, and traces the root to pressure that extracts liquid, comparing 12:36, 12:49, and 2:266. Sibawayh cites a poem about a barren quarter in which al-muʿṣirāt are rain-bearing clouds that leave a wet stream. - 78:14/q02/p2 Winds that squeeze, form, drive, or draw rain from clouds. — Ibn Zayd cites 30:48 for winds raising clouds; al-Wahidi reports a rain-bringing wind, while al-Baghawi describes winds compressing clouds. Mujahid and others also describe whirlwinds or cyclonic winds. Al-Azhari, al-Tabrisi and Ibn al-Jawzi take min instrumentally as bi; al-Tabari objects that winds do not contain water, and al-Qurtubi prefers clouds as rain's immediate source. - 78:14/q02/p3 The heavens or sky from which water descends. — Al-Hasan, Qatada and other named authorities identify the heavens. - 78:14/q02/p4 Clouds specifically characterized by spiraling winds or whirlwinds. — Ibn Qutayba reports clouds possessing spiraling winds; al-Mu'arrikh and al-Mazini give the same description. Al-Azhari counters that whirlwinds themselves are not rain-bearing. Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports al-Rāghib's alternative that the clouds bring a whirlwind, but rejects it on the basis of the verse's context and Quranic usage, comparing 2:266 and 78:15–16. ## 78:14/q03 How is “from” construed in the rain phrase?  [مِنَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether min names the place water comes from or the winds that cause its release; the bi variant favors the latter.
- 78:14/q03/p1 It marks the source or starting point of the descending water. — Al-Tabari, al-Nahhas, al-Qurtubi and Ibn Kathir use the ordinary 'from' sense to support clouds; al-Samin calls it the starting point. Al-Alusi reports an origin construal even when discussing the wind reading. Bursevi says descent from sky can pass through clouds. [7 notes: SAMIN-DURR; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن الزبير وابن عباس والفضل وعبد الله بن يزيد وعكرمة وقتادة); IBNKATHIR-FULL+; NAHHAS+; QURTUBI-FULL+; TAB-FULL+]
- 78:14/q03/p2 It has an instrumental or causal force like bi, rain coming by means of winds. — Al-Wahidi, al-Baghawi's al-Azhari, al-Tabrisi, Ibn al-Jawzi and al-Tabatabai expressly explain min as bi for winds. Abu Hayyan calls it attributing rain to its cause; al-Samin distinguishes the causal bi reading from the starting-point min reading. Al-Alusi reports causal and origin construals. [8 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(مجاهد ومقاتل وقتادة); BAGHAWI-FULL(الأزهري); TABRISI(مجاهد وقتادة والكلبي); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس، مجاهد، عكرمة، قتادة، مقاتل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس، مجاهد، قتادة); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(ابن الزبير وابن عباس والفضل وعبد الله بن يزيد وعكرمة وقتادة); TABATABAI(قيل)]
- 78:14/q03/p3 Here min is adverbial (ẓarf), expressing the circumstance of descent from the clouds. — Ibn al-Jawzī calls min in the water-from-clouds phrase adverbial; he reports that some grammarians group this adverbial use among the meanings of bi. [2 notes: IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(بعضهم)]

## 78:14/q04 What preposition is read before al-muʿṣirāt?  [مِنَ ٱلْمُعْصِرَٰتِ · readings]
turns on: The codices' min is read as source; reported bi is generally taken as cause or instrument and often supports the wind interpretation.
- 78:14/q04/p1 The written codices read min al-muʿṣirāt. — Al-Qurtubi distinguishes the codices' min from reports of bi; al-Samin gives min a starting-point sense. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس وعكرمة); SAMIN-DURR]
- 78:14/q04/p2 Some readers have bi-l-muʿṣirāt instead. — Al-Tabari reports this from Ikrima and Qatada; Ibn Atiyya names Ibn al-Zubayr, Ibn Abbas, al-Fadl ibn Abbas, Qatada and Ikrima; Abu Hayyan adds Abd Allah ibn Yazid, and al-Samin names him. Al-Qurtubi names Ibn Abbas and Ikrima. Al-Jishumi compares 97:4. Durr reports Fadl ibn Abbas's codex, Ibn Abbas through Qatada, and Mujahid's bi reading. Al-Alusi calls bi causal or instrumental; Ibn Atiyya and Abu Hayyan say it supports winds, though al-Zamakhshari, reported by al-Samin, permits clouds too, like giving from or by one's hand. [14 notes: TAB-FULL(عكرمة,قتادة); JISHUMI(عكرمة); IBNATIYYA-FULL(ابن الزبير وابن عباس والفضل بن عباس وقتادة وعكرمة,ابن عباس ومجاهد وقتادة); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس وعكرمة); BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن الزبير، ابن عباس، الفضل بن عباس، عبد الله بن يزيد، عكرمة، قتادة); SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); DURR-FULL(قتادة,مجاهد); ALUSI-FULL(ابن الزبير وابن عباس والفضل وعبد الله بن يزيد وعكرمة وقتادة)]

## 78:14/q05 How is the rain brought down and sustained?  [وَأَنزَلْنَا مَآءًۭ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the notes describe wind transporting heavenly water, rain passing from heaven through clouds, or the sun raising terrestrial water into clouds.
- 78:14/q05/p1 Winds gather, carry, and drive water or water-filled clouds until rain falls. — Muqatil says winds draw water from the lowest heaven's sea of provisions (51:22), raise and join clouds, then release rain with wind. Ibn Masud says winds collect water and drive it into clouds until full like a milch camel; al-Tha'labi likens the falling scattered drops to water drawn from a pregnant she-camel. Durr gives Ibn Masud's full-udder image and heavy showers distributed by wind; Nasafi reports winds carrying sky water to clouds. [6 notes: MUQATIL; NAHHAS(ابن مسعود); THALABI; NASAFI-FULL; DURR-FULL(عبد الله بن مسعود); SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري)]
- 78:14/q05/p2 Rain descends from the heavens through clouds. — Qatada and al-Hasan report descent from the sky. Al-Samin explains the sky-to-cloud passage; Bursevi says this reconciles sky descent with cloud descent, and Nasafi relays a report of winds carrying sky water to clouds. [6 notes: TAB-FULL(الحسن,قتادة); NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الحسن، قتادة); BURSEVI; DURR-FULL(الربيع بن أنس)]
- 78:14/q05/p3 Celestial causes, including the sun lifting water from earth and sea, may produce rain clouds. — Bursevi offers this as another way to understand descent from the sky. [1 notes: BURSEVI]
- 78:14/q05/p4 Rain-heavy clouds yield water under pressure; they may press one another, but two clouds are not required. — Bint al-Shāṭiʾ reports Ibn ʿAbbās's explanation that clouds press one another until water emerges, supported by a line of al-Nābigha. She treats this as a gloss rather than a necessary two-cloud restriction and says a rain-saturated cloud suffices. [2 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن عباس); BINTSHATI-IJAZ+]

## 78:14/q06 What does thajjāj mean here?  [ثَجَّاجًۭا · meaning]
turns on: Whether thajj itself names continuous pouring, with abundance as a consequence, or means sheer abundance; al-Tabari disputes the latter as Arabic usage.
- 78:14/q06/p1 Rain pouring copiously, forcefully, or in successive continuous flows. — Mujahid, Qatada, Ibn Abbas and others gloss it as pouring; al-Tabari stresses continuous succession, like sacrificial blood, and cites the Prophetic phrase about loud talbiya and sacrifice. Al-Nahhas likewise favors repeated pouring with the hadith. Al-Tha'labi, al-Tabrisi, Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, al-Biqai, Bursevi, al-Alusi, Elmalili, Tabatabai and Islahi add abundance, force, rapidity or repeated waves. Al-Samin cites a poem for copious forceful flow, and Durr an unnamed poem about black clouds. Ibn Kathir cites the report of a woman with prolonged bleeding; al-Razi mentions Ibn Abbas's epithet for copious speech. Al-Zajjaj takes the water as pouring itself; al-Alusi dismisses an objection that clouds could only release a little water as relative comparison. Muqatil links the copious successive rain to clouds; al-Qurtubi says Ibn Zayd's 'abundant' gives the same meaning. Al-Itqān glosses thajjājan as pouring or flowing down. [51 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); MUQATIL; ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,ابن وهب); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); NAHHAS(ابن عباس); THALABI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; IBNATIYYA-FULL(النبي); TABRISI(قتادة,مجاهد); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(مقاتل); RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس,الزجاج,الكلبي ومقاتل وقتادة); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن زيد,ابن عباس ومجاهد,الزجاج,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ثعلب، الأزهري); SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري,الشاعر); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن جرير,مجاهد); BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,الربيع بن أنس,شاعر غير مسمى,عكرمة,مجاهد); BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ITQAN; NAHHAS+]
- 78:14/q06/p2 It means abundance of water or rain as such. — Ibn Zayd gives 'abundant'; al-Tha'labi attributes plentiful rain to him, and also reports Mujahid's abundant-rain gloss. Al-Tabari objects that Arabic thajj does not denote quantity alone; Ibn Kathir repeats that objection. Al-Qurtubi regards Ibn Zayd's gloss as the same meaning as copious pouring. [6 notes: NAHHAS(ابن زيد); THALABI(ابن زيد,مجاهد); TABRISI(مجاهد); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن زيد); IBNKATHIR-FULL(مجاهد); TAB-FULL(ابن وهب)-; IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن جرير)-]

## 78:14/q07 Can the verb behind thajjāj be transitive or intransitive?  [ثَجَّاجًۭا · grammar]
turns on: Whether the flowing water is the verb's subject or something poured by an agent; al-Alusi says the intransitive is more common.
- 78:14/q07/p1 It can be intransitive: the water flows or pours itself. — Al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, al-Biqai and Elmalili acknowledge this use. Al-Zajjaj's gloss makes the water pour itself. Al-Alusi prefers it as the more common construction. [9 notes: RAZI-FULL(الزجاج); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس ومجاهد,الزجاج); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ثعلب، الأزهري); SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 78:14/q07/p2 It can be transitive: someone pours the water. — Al-Razi, al-Qurtubi, Abu Hayyan, al-Samin, al-Biqai and Elmalili acknowledge this construction; al-Alusi attributes it to al-Zajjaj while preferring the intransitive sense. [7 notes: RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس ومجاهد); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ثعلب، الأزهري); SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI]

## 78:14/q08 What variant form is reported for thajjāj?  [ثَجَّاجًۭا · readings]
turns on: The reported final consonant or form varies, while the notes connect the forms with flowing water.
- 78:14/q08/p1 Al-Aʿraj reads thajjāḥan with ḥāʾ in place of jīm. — Abu Hayyan and al-Samin report the reading; al-Alusi relates it to mathājiḥ, places where water gathers or runs together. Al-Samin reports al-Zamakhshari's mathājih for spillways and yantajihu for water entering a valley. [4 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الأعرج,الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL(الأعرج)]
- 78:14/q08/p2 The form thajjāj and the reported mathājij both denote outflows. — Al-Baydawi reports both forms in his explanation of pouring and the pilgrimage saying. [1 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL]

## 78:14/q09 What does this rain sign establish in the surah?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes draw different lessons from rain: provision and resurrection, a reproductive image of creation, or a condition concerning the Hour.
- 78:14/q09/p1 Rain and its resulting growth show divine provision, power, and the certainty of resurrection and judgment. — Al-Biqai says cooling rain yields food and drink; Islahi says sky-earth harmony displays monotheism, providence and accountability, while the signs of mercy, wisdom and power require a fixed reckoning. Kur'an Yolu links the seven heavens, sun, rain clouds and growth to God's power to create the afterlife, citing 2:29 and 67:3. Ibn Ashur says the surah uses creation and humanity's first creation to prove resurrection, warn of punishment and contrast believers' reward; Muqatil says each sign is more astonishing than resurrection. [7 notes: BIQAI-FULL(مقاتل); IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 78:14/q09/p2 Sky, earth, water, and vegetation form a reproductive analogy. — Al-Biqai likens sky to male, earth to female, water to semen, and plants to offspring; he says springs and pools are implied. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 78:14/q09/p3 The Hour cannot come while any water remains. — Muqatil adds this consequence while explaining the rain from the lowest heaven's sea of provisions and cites 51:22. [1 notes: MUQATIL]

## 78:14/q10 How does this surah connect with al-Mursalāt?  [* · links]
- 78:14/q10/p1 Both use signs of providence and pointed questions to establish judgment and address deniers. — Islahi says the two surahs use providence across heaven and earth to establish a purposeful world's judgment, rewarding the God-fearing and punishing rebels. Their affirmative-question style reproaches deniers while glad tidings for believers are understood against the warning. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 78:14/q11 What names of this surah are reported?  [* · sciences]
- 78:14/q11/p1 Five names: al-Nabaʾ, ʿAmma yatasaʾalūn, ʿAmma, al-Tasāʾul, and al-Muʿṣirāt. — Ibn Ashur lists these five and says al-Itqan lists four. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 78:14/q12 Where and when was this surah revealed?  [* · sciences]
- 78:14/q12/p1 It is Meccan; Jabir ibn Zayd's order places it after al-Maʿārij and before al-Nāziʿāt. — Ibn Ashur reports the place and relative revelation order. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 78:14/q14 What does māʾ mean here, and what is its lexical derivation?  [مَآءًۭ · meaning]
- 78:14/q14/p1 It is rain in this verse; māʾ denotes life-sustaining flowing water and derives from m-w-h. — Ibn al-Jawzī defines water as a flowing substance sustaining animals and quenching thirst, and reports a definition calling it subtle, porous, and naturally seeking rest. He gives the root موه, diminutive مويه, plurals مياه and أمواه, and relational forms مائي and ماوي. He reports exegetes' identification of the water sent down from clouds here as rain, comparing 15:22 and 25:48. [3 notes: IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أهل التفسير)]

## 78:14/q15 How are ellipsis and case understood in poetic citations using al-muʿṣirāt?  [ٱلْمُعْصِرَٰتِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the cited noun is accusative under an omitted verb or nominative in an identifying nominal clause.
- 78:14/q15/p1 An accusative noun may be governed by an omitted verb understood from context, such as 'I recall' before 'dwellings'. — Sibawayh supplies أذكر in Dhu al-Rumma's verse about Mayya's dwellings and rain clouds; he contrasts accusative الديار with the nominative and adduces omitted verbs in ولا زعماتك, familiar prohibitions, poetic accusatives, and formulas such as انتهوا خيرا لكم (4:171). He reports al-Khalīl's explanation of the latter as an understood second action, and notes that a parallel imperative can be explicit. Al-Jurjānī likewise supplies أذكر before accusative ديار and says omission can heighten eloquence. Sibawayh says familiar context and prior mention make omitted verbs recoverable: the accusative dates after كليهما can imply 'give me,' and a stated command can be less fixed than the customary ellipsis. Al-Khalīl, as reported by Sibawayh, explains that an imperative to stop implies a further action, 'enter what is better.' Sibawayh supplies omitted wishes after ألا رجل, an understood verb for the snake poem's accusatives, and implied remembering or finding in poems by Ibn Qumayʾa, al-Qaṭāmī, Ibn al-Ruqayyāt, and ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz al-Kilābī. He says nominative and accusative forms can reflect different understood thoughts, and restricts the خيرا ellipsis to a command rather than a statement or question. [23 notes: SIBAWAYH(الخليل); JURJANI-DALAIL]
- 78:14/q15/p2 A nominative noun may form an identifying nominal clause, with a demonstrative, subject, or predicate supplied from context. — Sibawayh reads nominative الديار as identification of Mayya's dwellings and reports Tamim speakers' supplied predicates in كلاهما وتمرا and كل شيء. Al-Jurjānī supplies ذاك or هو in ربع قواء أذاع المعصرات به and تلك before دار in another line; he notes the common omission of a subject at a fresh start. His teacher rejects taking الربع as a substitute for الطلل because a substitute must equal or exceed its antecedent, whereas a quarter is larger than a ruin trace. Sibawayh says some Arabs take كلاهما as 'both are mine,' followed by an implied request for dates; nominative الديار identifies the dwellings and nominative ربع can imply 'that is a quarter' or 'it is a quarter.' He says Ibn Abī Rabīʿa's parallel shows nominative and accusative reflect different unstated thoughts, and nominative زيد or عمرو can identify the man being wished for after ألا رجل. [10 notes: SIBAWAYH; JURJANI-DALAIL(شيخنا)]
- 78:14/q15/p3 A nominative noun can be the subject of an omitted verb supplied from preceding speech, rather than part of an identifying nominal clause. — Sibawayh reads nominative ضارع after لبيك يزيد with an understood 'let one weep for him.' He says a valley poem implies سقاها كل أجش from the preceding prayer. He also reports a nominative reading of شركاؤهم in 6:137 on the pattern of ضارع. [3 notes: SIBAWAYH]
