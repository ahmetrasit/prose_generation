# 103:2: 21 questions

## 103:2/q01 What is the grammatical answer to the oath?  [إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَفِى خُسْرٍ · grammar]
- 103:2/q01/p1 The declaration that humanity is in loss answers the oath by time. — Jishumi identifies the oath wāw and this clause as its answer; Ibn al-Jawzi reports al-Zajjaj; Study Quran takes verses 2–3 as the answer and compares 95:5–6; Corpus Coranicum describes an opening oath followed by rebuke. Al-Suyuti classifies al-ʿAṣr as a Qur'anic oath followed by a proposition about human action or condition: humanity is in loss. [5 notes: JISHUMI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الزجاج); STUDYQURAN; CORPUSCORANICUM; ITQAN]

## 103:2/q02 Whom does al-insān designate?  [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · referent]
turns on: Whether the definite singular is generic, as the following exception suggests, or points to a particular unbeliever or occasion.
- 103:2/q02/p1 Humanity as a whole, with believers subsequently excepted. — Kaʿb includes Adam and his descendants; Abu Ubayda and al-Zajjaj call it a generic noun. The following exception proves collective scope for Jishumi, Razi, Samin, Elmalili and others. Bint al-Shati says limiting it to named Meccans would make that exception fail. Alusi says named losers are examples, not exclusive. Study Quran allows a historical emphasis on humanity's final phase; Elmalili especially includes the last era. Al-Zarkashi and al-Suyuti say the definite singular means every human; the following exception establishes its collective scope. Al-Zarkashi adds that replacing the generic article with 'all' preserves the sense. Ibn Ashur cites the explicit believer exception as a case of the Qur'an qualifying a broad proposition precisely. [36 notes: MUJAHID(كعب); MAJAZ; NAHHAS; THALABI; MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو عبيدة والزجاج); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); ELMALILI; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI(المفسرون); STUDYQURAN(Bg and Q); BURHAN+; IBNASHUR-FULL+; ITQAN+; JISHUMI+]
- 103:2/q02/p2 Disbelievers, whose exclusion is made clear by the next verse. — Wahidi reports Muqatil's reading of the unbeliever astray until death; the exception is Bagawi's reason to limit the term. Wahidi specifies one working outside God's obedience and forfeiting a place in Paradise. Ibn Abbas is transmitted by Abu Salih. Bint al-Shati objects that the exception cannot be taken from an exclusively named group. [8 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; BAGHAWI-FULL; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس عن أبي صالح); TABRISI; STUDYQURAN(Bg and Q); BINTSHATI-]
- 103:2/q02/p3 Abu Lahab in particular. — Muqatil says the verse was revealed about ʿAbd al-ʿUzza ibn ʿAbd al-Muttalib; Tustari says he lost all his days. Razi repeats Muqatil's report. Ibn al-Jawzi's Nuzhat al-aʿyun identifies the human in loss as Abu Lahab ibn ʿAbd al-Muttalib. [5 notes: MUQATIL; TUSTARI; RAZI-FULL(مقاتل); BINTSHATI(المفسرون); IBNJAWZI-NUZHA+; BINTSHATI-]
- 103:2/q02/p4 Abu Jahl in particular. — Jishumi reports this occasion; Razi mentions an unspecified raised report; Ibn Abbas identifies Abu Jahl ibn Hisham in Durr and Tabatabai. Thalabi's Prophetic report and a sermon give the same identification; Alusi treats it as illustrative. [8 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL; THALABI(النبي,علي بن عبد الله بن عباس); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); TABATABAI(ابن عباس); BINTSHATI(المفسرون); BINTSHATI-]
- 103:2/q02/p5 A group of Meccan opponents. — Ibn Abbas in Razi names al-Walid, al-As and al-Aswad ibn Abd al-Muttalib; al-Dahhak in Qurtubi gives two men named al-Aswad. Tabrisi names al-Walid and Abu Jahl as examples; Bint al-Shati lists reported names. [4 notes: RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(الضحاك عن ابن عباس); TABRISI; BINTSHATI(المفسرون); BINTSHATI-]

## 103:2/q03 How can singular al-insān be followed by an exception for many believers?  [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · grammar]
- 103:2/q03/p1 A singular generic noun denotes the human collective. — Abu Ubayda's Majaz compares 70:19–22, 69:47 and a line of al-Nabigha; Tabari reports the same construction elsewhere. Qurtubi compares generic al-nafs and its exceptions in a legal verse. Abu Hayyan and Samin infer generic scope from the exception. Al-Zarkashi and al-Suyuti explicitly infer collective reference from the believer exception; al-Zarkashi adds al-Hajjaj's sermon in which each hearer was addressed as 'O human,' and distinguishes a mentally conceived genus from its external instances (Ibn Babshadh). Their surrounding grammatical surveys distinguish generic al from relative and anaphoric uses, universal individuals from a class's qualities or essence, and definite nouns from other general forms such as kull, relative and conditional words, and possessive or contextually general indefinites. Al-Zarkashi also reports debates about definite plurals and genus versus species. Al-Suyuti calls the following exception specification of the general statement, not abrogation, and gives other form-for-referent substitutions. Ibn Ashur cites this exception as precise qualification. [35 notes: MAJAZ; TAB-FULL(آخرون); NAHHAS; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; BURHAN(ابن بابشاذ,الحجاج,العلماء,صاحب ضوء المصباح); ITQAN(ابن عصفور,قيل); IBNASHUR-FULL; BURHAN+; ITQAN+]
- 103:2/q03/p2 A definite singular with al does not itself carry universal force. — Al-Zarkashi reports Fakhr al-Din al-Razi and his followers denying that a singular noun made definite by alif-lam is general. Al-Zarkashi rejects this denial on the strength of the exception in 103:3; his reply says the thief wording in 5:38 instead denotes the essence of theft, reaching every thief across time. [1 notes: BURHAN(الإمام فخر الدين الرازي ومن تبعه)+; BURHAN-]

## 103:2/q04 What wording and vowel readings of this verse are transmitted?  [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ خُسْرٍ · readings]
turns on: Which transmitted wording or vocalization is used, and whether an explanatory addition belongs to canonical recitation.
- 103:2/q04/p1 The received codex reads the present wording, with a still sīn in khusr. — Thalabi prefers the general reciters and codices; Qurtubi says Ali's explanatory addition is not Qur'an for recitation under the Uthmanic codex. Jishumi, Abu Hayyan and Samin attest the still sīn as the majority form. [5 notes: JISHUMI(الأعرج); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR; QURTUBI-FULL+; THALABI+]
- 103:2/q04/p2 Khusr is pronounced with ḍamma on the sīn. — Reported from al-Aʿraj, Asim, Talha, Isa al-Thaqafi, Ibn Hurmuz, Zayd ibn Ali and an Asim transmission through Abu Bakr and Harun. Qurtubi compares the parallel vowel variants of ʿusr and yusr. [6 notes: JISHUMI(الأعرج); IBNATIYYA-FULL(عاصم والأعرج); QURTUBI-FULL(الأعرج وطلحة وعيسى الثقفي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجمهور); SAMIN-DURR]
- 103:2/q04/p3 An explanatory recitation adds that humanity remains in loss until time's end, sometimes also adding time's calamities. — Ali is cited by Tabari, Thalabi, Mawardi, Qurtubi and Elmalili; Thalabi also attributes the time's-end addition to Ibn Masud, and Tabari reports Qatada. The variants differ over whether calamities are included. Qurtubi rejects this as canonical Qur'an. [9 notes: TAB-FULL(علي,قتادة); THALABI(ابن مسعود,علي); MAWARDI-FULL(علي); QURTUBI-FULL(علي); ELMALILI; THALABI-; QURTUBI-FULL-]
- 103:2/q04/p4 Ibn Masud's codex says God created humanity in loss. — Ibn Atiyya records the wording of ʿAbd Allah ibn Masud's codex. [1 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL(عبد الله بن مسعود)]

## 103:2/q05 What is the force of inna and la-fī?  [إِنَّ لَفِى · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the phrase depicts an eventual outcome or a continuing condition.
- 103:2/q05/p1 Inna emphasizes the assertion and fī portrays the human as immersed and surrounded by loss. — Razi assigns emphasis to inna and describes loss encompassing a person from every side. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 103:2/q05/p2 La-fī can speak of an inevitable route toward future loss. — Razi compares 4:10, where eating orphans' property is expressed as fire already in bellies because that will be its outcome. [2 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 103:2/q05/p3 La-fī can speak of continual, inescapable loss in passing time. — For Razi each passing hour is capital rarely preserved: sin wastes it, permissible pursuits leave no enduring trace, and even worship may fall short of higher humility and knowledge. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 103:2/q06 What does the indefinite khusr convey?  [خُسْرٍ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether indefiniteness signals magnitude, a distinct or unrestricted category, or relative smallness.
- 103:2/q06/p1 It magnifies a loss whose full extent is known only to God. — Razi prefers this because sins oppose God and immense blessings; Baydawi, Bursevi and others also intensify the loss. Bint al-Shati cites Razi's awe explanation, but rejects treating intensity as its sole force. [9 notes: BAYDAWI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI; RAZI-FULL+; BINTSHATI-]
- 103:2/q06/p2 It marks a distinctive kind or range of loss, beyond ordinary financial or social losses. — Bursevi, Alusi, Elmalili and Tabatabai mention a particular kind; Ibn Ashur adds multiple kinds and dramatic universality, comparing afterlife loss. Bint al-Shati understands it as open-ended and unrestricted rather than simple amplification. [6 notes: BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; BINTSHATI+]
- 103:2/q06/p3 It can belittle human loss in comparison with Satan's. — Razi reports this consoling possibility: others are more disobedient, though he prefers magnification. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL; RAZI-FULL-]

## 103:2/q07 What does khusr mean, and what is lost?  [خُسْرٍ · meaning]
turns on: Whether loss is read as a lexical diminution, unbelief, punishment, squandered life, spiritual deprivation, bodily decline or loss across both worlds.
- 103:2/q07/p1 Loss, ruin, diminishment, or destruction of one's capital. — Abu Ubayda, Ibn Qutayba, Tabari, Suddi, Ibn Shajara, Akhfash and others give these overlapping lexical glosses; Ibn al-Jawzi says khusr and khusran are equivalent. Wahidi likens it to lost capital. Razi and Raghib note that loss may be predicated of a person or a transaction; Kashshaf compares the nominal forms. Bagawi and Ibn al-Jawzi identify the self as the greatest capital. Ibn Qutayba glosses loss as deficiency and says the excepted believers are not diminished. Ibn al-Jawzi says khusrān is conventionally a reduction of one's capital. Al-Damghani's sense list also includes decrease, without supplying an example in this extract. [26 notes: MAJAZ; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL; MAWARDI-FULL(ابن شجرة,السدي); QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI(الأخفش); BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(الأخفش); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(أهل المعاني); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الأخفش); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; TABATABAI(الراغب); DAMGHANI; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA+; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL+]
- 103:2/q07/p2 Misguidance and disbelief that persist until death without faith. — Mujahid glosses loss as misguidance; Wahidi reports Muqatil's worldly explanation, and Razi links the particular-unbeliever interpretation to misguidance and belief as profit. Muqatil says Abu Lahab remains in error until Fire. Al-Damghani applies the sense 'misguidance' directly to humanity here and cites 4:119; Ibn al-Jawzi reports the same gloss from the exegetes, also with 4:119. [7 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); RAZI-FULL; STUDYQURAN; IBNJAWZI-NUZHA(أهل التفسير); DAMGHANI+]
- 103:2/q07/p3 Punishment for sins, especially Fire and forfeited home, family or property in Paradise. — Farra, Mawardi and Qurtubi cite punishment, with Qurtubi comparing 65:9; Nahhas calls entry into Fire the greatest loss. Wahidi adds the forfeited Paradise station. Hasan adds void deeds; Jishumi sees lasting punishment as unsurpassable. Tabrisi and Qur'an 39:15 are used for loss of self and family at Judgment. Al-Damghani lists punishment as another sense of khusr, although this extract ends before its examples. [21 notes: FARRA; NAHHAS; MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(الفراء); WAHIDI-QT; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(الحسن); TABRISI; QURTUBI-FULL(الفراء); RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; STUDYQURAN; QUTB-ZILAL; DAMGHANI; CORPUSCORANICUM+]
- 103:2/q07/p4 Evil or deprivation. — Zayd ibn Aslam gives evil in Mawardi; Ibn Zayd gives evil in Qurtubi, which also reports deprivation and observes that the senses are close. [2 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(زيد بن أسلم); QURTUBI-FULL(ابن زيد)]
- 103:2/q07/p5 The lost capacity to serve God and attain spiritual nearness. — Razi says deprivation of serving the Lord is the central loss, beside which Paradise and Hell are secondary, citing 51:56. Qushayri distinguishes deeds whose loss is evident at the end from present loss of states: contraction after expansion, veiling after nearness and resort to concessions after a higher path. Qashani and Study Quran describe losing innate guidance or attachment to God. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; QASHANI; STUDYQURAN]
- 103:2/q07/p6 Decline of bodily strength with age, while believers' earlier deeds keep being recorded. — Ibrahim is transmitted by Thalabi, Bagawi and Qurtubi, who compare 95:4–6; Study Quran also reports a historical reading in which later generations decline from earlier ones. [4 notes: THALABI(إبراهيم); BAGHAWI-FULL(إبراهيم عن ابن عون); QURTUBI-FULL(إبراهيم); STUDYQURAN]
- 103:2/q07/p7 Loss in both worldly life and the Hereafter, culminating in loss of dignity and the soul. — Study Quran cites 35:39, 22:11 and 39:15, noting that a person may lose what is dear without material poverty. Qutb makes earthly profit negligible beside God's pleasure, Paradise and human dignity; Abu Hayyan calls losing both worlds the greatest loss. [5 notes: STUDYQURAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL]

## 103:2/q08 Why does ordinary human life tend toward loss?  [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَفِى خُسْرٍ · interpretation]
turns on: How finite life and human choices produce loss or enduring gain.
- 103:2/q08/p1 Life and time are finite capital; misuse or failure to earn lasting good spends them irretrievably. — Bagawi calls self and lifetime the greatest capital. Razi says sin, permissible activity and even incomplete worship miss enduring gain; Elmalili compares every breath to an entrusted loan and cites 25:62, 53:39 and 52:21. Poems about each passing day, his elegy and the image of death consuming life sharpen the point. Tabatabai calls earthly life a test (13:26; 21:35) in which belief and deeds determine the afterlife (53:39–41; 30:44; 41:46). Islahi stresses rapidly consumed life. Bint al-Shati's broad al-insan usage is distinguished from al-nas and jinn contexts. [31 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; QASHANI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 103:2/q08/p2 Human desire, distraction and frailty draw people away from lasting good. — Sulami mentions neglect of God's rights, following desires and lack of fear. Razi says the afterlife's rewards are hidden whereas sensory appetites and anger draw people toward the visible world; Elmalili reports this. Biqai cites human injustice and ignorance (33:72) and notes that only a few are preserved from these defects. Qutb applies the loss to communities without righteous authority. [9 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم); RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI(Râzî); QUTB-ZILAL]
- 103:2/q08/p3 A person can turn life's expense into profit by using the present for faith, obedience and the afterlife. — Abu Hayyan contrasts trading away the afterlife for this world with buying it through this world. Elmalili reports the Sufi call to be the child of the moment and distinguishes it from worldly pleasure seeking; a final righteous act may compensate past waste. He denies the poem's blaming of time, locating corruption in human conduct. Qutb stresses the enduring stakes. [8 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ELMALILI; TABATABAI; QUTB-ZILAL]

## 103:2/q09 Who escapes loss, and to what extent?  [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَفِى خُسْرٍ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether exemption requires all four traits and whether a believer's sinful or incomplete action still entails a degree of loss.
- 103:2/q09/p1 The complete exception requires faith, righteous deeds, and mutual counsel in truth and patience. — Muhammad ibn Kaʿb says belief alone is insufficient and lists the successive conditions. Muqatil says those who do these things remain in the Gardens; Elmalili calls the four complete faith; Qutb calls them the single successful path in every age and the basis of a community. Al-Shafiʿi and others regard the surah as sufficient guidance. Tabari and Hasan compare another connected exception with this one. Ibn Qutayba says those who believe, do good and counsel truth and patience are not diminished. [13 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(الحسن); IBNABIHATIM(محمد بن كعب القرظي); ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Mehmet Âkif Ersoy); STUDYQURAN; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL+]
- 103:2/q09/p2 Believers who do righteous deeds are exempted from the loss. — Mujahid identifies the excepted believers; Muqatil and Thalabi also name belief and deeds. Razi says believers gain enduring happiness with their self and time. Ibn Ashur says good believers avoid loss when they do no opposite deed or repent; al-salihat includes commanded good and avoidance of sin. Islahi says deeds show faith has taken root, cites 4:136, and explains their outward, inward, worldly, religious, individual and collective scope through Farahi. [19 notes: MUJAHID(مجاهد); MUQATIL; TAB-FULL(مجاهد); THALABI; RAZI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Im ām Far āhī)]
- 103:2/q09/p3 Sinful believers incur a lesser, possibly temporary loss, though faith ultimately saves them. — Biqai distinguishes denial from losses proportionate to disobedience. Elmalili rejects the Murji'a claim that faith without action entails no loss; sinful believers whose evil outweighs good may experience Hell until cleansed. Ibn Ashur distinguishes eternal loss through denial from graded lesser losses and cites 11:114 on forgiveness of minor sins. Samarrai says mutual counsel prevents loss of further reward. [6 notes: BIQAI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; SAMARRAI-ASRAR; ELMALILI(Mürcienin)+]
- 103:2/q09/p4 Faith without action incurs no loss. — Elmalili attributes this to the Murji'a and explicitly rejects it in favor of graded loss for disobedient believers. [0 notes: ELMALILI(Mürcienin)-]

## 103:2/q10 How is the oath by time related to the human loss that follows?  [إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَفِى خُسْرٍ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the oath evokes life's finite capital, the late hour and reckoning, or time's warning events.
- 103:2/q10/p1 Time is the valuable, vanishing opportunity in which human gain or loss is determined. — Elmalili says time contains changes, wonders, blessing, harm and the chance to act; its passing consumes life unless used for faith and reform. He says the oath honors time rather than blaming it, and its senses of restraint and bestowal evoke both loss and gain. Kuranyolu similarly says God swears by precious time; Islahi relates it to responsibility for life's blessing. [11 notes: ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 103:2/q10/p2 Late afternoon evokes the closing human era or the end of life and the reckoning of what was earned. — Elmalili evokes creation late in the world's day, the era of Muhammad and the sorrow of returning without gain. Study Quran reads humanity in a final historical phase. Robinson relates the afternoon to merchants counting daily takings; Corpus Coranicum supports this through Q 104's contrast of wealth and damnation. [5 notes: ELMALILI; STUDYQURAN; CORPUSCORANICUM; CORPUSCORANICUM(Robinson)+]
- 103:2/q10/p3 Time's calamities and transformations warn that human loss continues to its end. — Elmalili explains Ali's interpretive dehr recitation as recurrent changes and calamities, while another view sees time's wonders as signs of the Creator's power. [2 notes: ELMALILI]
- 103:2/q10/p4 An oath-to-assertion link should be tested rather than inferred from analogy alone. — Bint al-Shati questions proposed links elsewhere: between the greatness of night and day and differing human striving, and between the falling star (53:1) and the Prophet's freedom from error. This note offers a methodological caution, not a specific alternative account of al-ʿAsr. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ+]

## 103:2/q11 What is the verse's relation to nearby sūras and other passages?  [* · links]
turns on: Which nearby warning or parallel supplies the strongest context for the verse.
- 103:2/q11/p1 It follows al-Takāthur's warning that worldly rivalry wastes life and obscures future accountability. — Abu Hayyan links 102:1,3 to distinction between believers and unbelievers. Ibn al-Zubayr, as quoted by Biqai, says accumulation narrows sight to the present; Biqai also connects human ignorance, ingratitude and imagined immortality across 100:6, 103:2 and 104:3. Elmalili, Islahi and Study Quran likewise link 102:1–2,8 with time's squandered blessing. [6 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(أبو جعفر بن الزبير); ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN]
- 103:2/q11/p2 Al-Tīn's human decline and exception parallel this verse, with bodily and spiritual emphases. — Ibrahim's aging account cites 95:4–6; Razi resolves an apparent reversal by assigning bodily states to al-Tin and the soul to al-Asr. Study Quran pairs 103:2–3 with 95:5–6. [5 notes: THALABI(إبراهيم); BAGHAWI-FULL(إبراهيم عن ابن عون); QURTUBI-FULL(إبراهيم); RAZI-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 103:2/q11/p3 Sūrah 104 likewise contrasts accumulated worldly wealth with final damnation. — Corpus Coranicum uses 104:1–9 to strengthen Robinson's link between reckoned earnings and eschatological loss; Biqai cites 104:3 alongside 103:2. [2 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM; BIQAI-FULL(أبو جعفر بن الزبير)]
- 103:2/q11/p4 The same pattern of a generic human term followed by an exception appears elsewhere. — Tabari reports Hasan's comparison with an exception after Hell; Majaz cites 70:19–22 and 69:47; Qurtubi cites 70:19,22 in discussion of a generic life. [4 notes: TAB-FULL(الحسن); MAJAZ; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 103:2/q11/p5 Other scriptures and Qur'anic passages also frame loss eschatologically. — Torrey compares Luke 9:24 and Syriac Peshitta ḥsar, and collects Qur'anic khusr usage; Corpus Coranicum notes Philippians 3:7–8 and the early Meccan 79:12. The commentator rejects a proposed hymn-like resonance and refers to criticism of Neuwirth's hypothesis, citing 93:1–2. [5 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Torrey)]

## 103:2/q12 What does the sūra as a whole teach through this declaration of loss and its exception?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the focus is the sūra's comprehensive program or the function of its opening contrast.
- 103:2/q12/p1 It concisely teaches the path of individual and communal salvation. — Shafiʿi is repeatedly reported to say reflection on this sūra would suffice; another report says it contains all Qur'anic sciences. Biqai maps its clauses onto knowledge of God and afterlife, worship, obstacles and patience, invoking Ghazali's six Qur'anic themes and a basmala contrast of transient creation and God's enduring mercy. Islahi calls it a condensed guide to personal and collective life; Kuranyolu and Qutb see the full duties and community identity in its brevity. [28 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(الشافعي); BIQAI-FULL(الشافعي,الغزالي); QUTB-ZILAL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Im ām Sh āfi‘ ī); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(İmam Şâfiî); STUDYQURAN(al-Shāfiʿī)]
- 103:2/q12/p2 Its opening separates the doomed from the saved before identifying saving conduct. — Biqai says prevention of harm precedes benefit and likens the saved human essence to what is extracted by pressing, linking the name al-ʿAsr with humanity's nobility and 95's best proportion. Qutb emphasizes one successful route across the ages. [7 notes: BIQAI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL]

## 103:2/q13 How is this sūra situated in revelation?  [* · sciences]
turns on: The differing reported settings, with most named authorities favoring Mecca.
- 103:2/q13/p1 It is Meccan, probably after Sūrah 94. — Abu Hayyan reports Ibn Abbas, Ibn al-Zubayr and the majority as Meccan; Ibn Kathir and Qutb affirm it. Tabatabai favors Meccan despite allowing either; Asad and Study Quran place it shortly after al-Sharh. [6 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس وابن الزبير والجمهور); IBNKATHIR-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN; TABATABAI+]

## 103:2/q14 What reports describe the sūra's recitation or reception?  [* · reports]

## 103:2/q16 How does affliction relate to human spiritual progress?  [* · inward]
- 103:2/q16/p1 God uses affliction to draw servants toward Himself, with the best tested first. — Qashani calls affliction God's whip and ranks those afflicted from prophets through saints and then other excellent people. [2 notes: QASHANI]

## 103:2/q17 What historical era does the oath and warning evoke?  [* · interpretation]
- 103:2/q17/p1 The era of Muhammad's mission opens a transformation from widespread ignorance and loss toward guidance. — Elmalili describes religious disorder and injustice before the mission, the Qur'an's first iqra revelation on the Night of Power, and the upright religion calling people to reform, Paradise and divine approval. He links this transition to al-Bayyina and reads the oath before the warning as meaningful. [4 notes: ELMALILI]

## 103:2/q18 How does al-insān differ from other words for humankind?  [ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ · meaning]
- 103:2/q18/p1 Al-insān can denote human beings in contrast with wild creatures, while al-nās denotes humanity broadly. — Bint al-Shati compares Qur'anic use of al-nas with al-ins in contexts paired with jinn, citing 49:13, 13:17 and 59:21. [1 notes: BINTSHATI]

## 103:2/q20 What claim did opponents make that this verse reverses?  [* · reports]
- 103:2/q20/p1 Opponents claimed Muhammad was in loss; the divine oath declares the opposite. — Razi transmits the report as an explanation of the oath's rebuttal. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 103:2/q21 How does this verse illustrate Ibn Ashur's account of Qur'anic style?  [* · rhetoric]
- 103:2/q21/p1 Its broad declaration is precisely qualified by the following exception for believers. — Ibn Ashur cites 103:2–3 as an example of carefully delimited Qur'anic propositions, unlike loose generalities that may overlook rare cases. He places this in a wider catalogue of Qur'anic innovations: a preserved recited book distinct from poetry and oratory, systematic topics, vivid narratives, reshaped reported speech, and adapted narrative names. Those other items describe his surrounding stylistic account rather than further claims about this verse. [8 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL+]
