# 103:1: 25 questions

## 103:1/q01 What does al-ʿaṣr in the oath designate?  [ٱلْعَصْرِ · referent]
turns on: Whether the unqualified word is inclusive or specifies a time of day, a prayer, a paired period, or a marked era; arguments use Arabic usage, poetry, report, context and analogous oaths.
- 103:1/q01/p1 Time or an age broadly, encompassing its changing periods and events. — Al-Tabari declines restriction without textual proof; al-Nahhas and Elmalılı likewise allow several Arabic senses. Ibn Abbas and others identify an age; Imru al-Qays and Ibn al-Sikkit's poetry attest the usage. Farāhī and Asad distinguish fleeting ʿaṣr from dahr; Cuypers calls it a general span without further grounds. Ibn Kathir calls this the well-known view. Bint al-Shati follows al-Tabari, Ibn Qayyim and most exegetes; Kur'an Yolu finds this best suited to the sūra. Al-Razi questions treating a reported expanded wording as Qur'an because that would invalidate prayer. Elmalılı cites a poet likening unseen time's motion toward death to a ship. Al-Damghani glosses al-ʿaṣr as time or its passage; al-Suyuti includes time as a whole among the oath's possible referents. Al-Ṭabarī explicitly includes evening, night, and day without restricting the oath. Ibn Kathīr says this span contains human acts, good and evil. ## 103:1/q02 What meanings and derivations does the word ʿaṣr have in Arabic?  [ٱلْعَصْرِ · meaning]
turns on: The sources distinguish its concrete pressing root, wide Arabic uses, bounded temporal force and the definite article's scope.
- 103:1/q02/p1 Its root denotes squeezing or pressing, extended to time, confinement, and the extraction of an essence. — Jishumi describes wringing cloth, grape extract, deferred prayer, rain-bearing clouds and a rising dust column. Tabarsi applies the pressed-events image to time. Bint al-Shati adds press equipment, lineage and the hardship that presses out a person's essence; she cites 12:36, 12:49, 78:14 and 2:266. Biqai connects the sūra name to extracting the saved essence. Bint al-Shati reads human life as pressed and refined by trials, exposing its essence; she cites the root's occurrences in Yusuf's dream and harvest year and the whirlwind in al-Baqara, calls this Qur'anic form unique, and rejects al-Raghib's derivation of temporal ʿaṣr from what remains after squeezing. Her criticism of proposed links in other oaths cautions against assuming a simple analogy for this image. [20 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI; ELMALILI; BINTSHATI; STUDYQURAN; BIQAI-FULL; BINTSHATI-IJAZ; BINTSHATI-IJAZ+]
- 103:1/q02/p2 The noun also spans an age, day or night, a part of day, prayer, group and rain. — Elmalılı adds Turkish senses of the current era and eighty or a hundred years and notes vocalizations ʿaṣr, ʿiṣr and ʿuṣr. Wahidi says these temporal senses are valid Arabic usage. Ibn ʿAṭiyya records variant vowel forms and distinguishes al-ʿaṣr for daytime from al-ʿashr for nighttime as reported by some scholars. Ibn ʿĀshūr lists eras named for a generation, ruler, prophet, or religion. [12 notes: NAHHAS; WAHIDI-BASIT; ELMALILI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; CORPUSCORANICUM; IBNATIYYA(ابن عباس,بعض العلماء); QURTUBI(ابن عباس وغيره); IBNASHUR]
- 103:1/q02/p3 Unlike dahr's vast or unlimited duration, ʿaṣr marks a bounded, passing period. — Asad describes dahr as unbounded; Elmalılı cites Raghib's range from the universe's beginning to end, and Farāhī stresses swift passage, supported by Imru al-Qays and ʿUbayd ibn al-Abras. Bint al-Shati reports Razi's suggestion that 'asr avoids unbelievers' exalted dahr. [8 notes: ASAD-NOTES; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Im ām Far āhī,Ḥam īd al-Dīn Far āhī); BINTSHATI(الرازي)]
- 103:1/q02/p4 The word's definite article can be generic or point to a particular known time. — Elmalılı says the generic article can gather all senses while the covenant article directs the first hearers to their own era; Ibn Ashur allows contextual indication, addition, or shared reference. Ibn ʿĀshūr treats the afternoon prayer as a conventional definite name, the Prophet's era as a reference to the present age, and general time as every age. [7 notes: ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; IBNASHUR]

## 103:1/q03 How does the opening oath work, and what does it emphasize?  [وَٱلْعَصْرِ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether wāw is explained as a conventional emphatic oath or as time's witness to the proposition.
- 103:1/q03/p1 The wāw introduces an oath whose answer affirms that humanity is in loss. — Jishumi identifies the oath particle and its answer; Elmalılı compares 93:1. Ibn Ashur says an oath confirms the assertion and its object displays God's creation, power and knowledge. Bint al-Shati says wāw magnifies what is sworn by and moves from perceptible reality to moral truth. Corpus Coranicum notes a possible pre-Islamic seer formula, a connection not yet studied adequately. Al-Zarkashi and al-Suyuti count oath openings as the fifth of the sūra-opening modes and list fifteen such sūras; al-Suyuti further classifies al-ʿAṣr as an oath followed by a proposition about human conduct or condition, namely humanity's loss. Al-Biqāʿī says the oath and emphatic answer announce that most people perish, a truth many deny in words or conduct. [13 notes: JISHUMI; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI; CORPUSCORANICUM; BURHAN; ITQAN; MAWARDI; BIQAI; IBNASHUR]
- 103:1/q03/p2 Time itself bears witness to humanity's loss. — Islahi gives wāw this witness force: history shows recompense, while the rapidly spent lifespan makes a person's deeds profitable or ruinous. [4 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR(Im ām Far āhī)]
- 103:1/q03/p3 A thematic link between an oath's object and its answer must be argued in each case, not assumed from other oaths. — Bint al-Shati questions proposed links between night and day and differing human striving, and between the falling star and the Prophet's freedom from error; these analogies cannot by themselves establish the link in al-ʿAṣr. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ]

## 103:1/q04 Why is time or afternoon singled out for the oath?  [وَٱلْعَصْرِ · interpretation]
turns on: The explanations appeal to divine signs, the day's closure, the prayer's status, the Prophet's era, historical recompense, or the value and moral use of time.
- 103:1/q04/p1 Time displays divine power and wondrous changes, so its oath invites reflection. — Alternating nights and days, shifting light and temperature, health and illness, wealth and poverty, and other events disclose God's power (Razi, Ibn Qayyim, Tabarsi). Razi cites 25:62 and 6:13. Bursevi relates the oath to dawn, sun, moon, night, day and forenoon oaths. Ibn Ashur adds the solar-earth cycle and daily social life. Bint al-Shati cautions against loading the verse with speculative philosophy and cites 28:71–72. Al-Rāzī argues that time is nobler than place when reading 6:12–13; Ibn ʿĀshūr adds the changing atmosphere and God's creative artistry and knowledge. [51 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(قيل); WAHIDI-BASIT(أهل المعاني); JISHUMI(ابن عباس والكلبي وأبو علي,الحسن وقتادة); KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(ابن عباس، الكلبي، الجبائي,الحسن، قتادة); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(ابن عباس وغيره); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عباس,قتادة); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(unnamed view); BINTSHATI(ابن القيم,الرازي); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; BAGHAWI(قيل); KASHSHAF; RAZI(أبو مسلم); QURTUBI; BAYDAWI; NASAFI; ABUHAYYAN(ابن عباس,قتادة); BIQAI; ALUSI(ابن عباس,قتادة); IBNASHUR]
- 103:1/q04/p2 Time is precious life capital; wasting it causes loss, while a final good act can gain lasting reward. — Mawardi cites the day's closing acts. Razi says a last moment of repentance can redeem a wasted life (25:62); al-Hasan compares closing markets to Judgment (21:1). Elmalılı cites elegies and poems about breath and passing days; Sufis urge afterlife-oriented use of the moment, while those denying afterlife seek worldly pleasure. ## 103:1/q05 How is al-ʿaṣr read?  [ٱلْعَصْرِ · readings]
turns on: Whether the transferred kasra is confined to pause or transmitted in connected recitation.
- 103:1/q05/p1 The received reading keeps ṣād vowelless in pause; Salām's reading gives ṣād kasra by vowel transfer. — Ibn Atiyya limits Salām's al-ʿaṣri and al-ṣabri to pause; Razi attributes the sound to breath or another impediment. Abu al-Fadl says transfer marks inflection and avoids two vowelless letters; Samīn gives poetic examples and also ḍamma transfer. A rajaz line in fī al-ʿaṣr supports the form. ## 103:1/q08 Does the word al-ʿaṣr have a theological implication about who causes events?  [ٱلْعَصْرِ · theology]
turns on: Whether the oath is read as correcting causal blame or as prompting inquiry into time's nature.
- 103:1/q08/p1 God acts through time; time itself does not cause calamity or loss. — Qashani invokes 45:24 and the ungraded 'God is time' report; Bursevi also quotes the prohibition on reviling time. Razi, Alusi and Elmalılı put blame on human conduct rather than the age. [14 notes: RAZI-FULL; QASHANI(النبي عليه السلام); BURSEVI(رسول الله); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; BINTSHATI(الرازي); RAZI; BAYDAWI; ALUSI]

## 103:1/q09 How does this opening connect with other Qur'anic passages?  [وَٱلْعَصْرِ · links]
turns on: The links draw on the preceding sūra, analogous oaths, parallels to the exception, and the sūra's own later clauses.
- 103:1/q09/p1 It follows al-Takāthur by exposing the loss caused by worldly distraction. — Jishumi says the prior warning against abundance leads to the demand for deeds; Abu Hayyan distinguishes believer and unbeliever. Biqai and Alusi continue the warning of 102; Study Quran cites 102:1 and 102:8 about wasted time and accountable blessings. [10 notes: JISHUMI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN; ABUHAYYAN; BIQAI; ALUSI]
- 103:1/q09/p2 It complements oaths by the forenoon and other day and night phases. — Zamakhshari, Nasafi and others pair afternoon with 93:1; Razi contrasts prior midmorning rising fortunes with afternoon decline. Bursevi cites oaths in 89, 91, 92 and 93; Corpus Coranicum observes this afternoon oath is unusual among early Meccan oaths. [18 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI(الحسن، قتادة); RAZI-FULL(أبو مسلم); NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قتادة); BURSEVI; ELMALILI(Katâde); CORPUSCORANICUM; BINTSHATI; KASHSHAF; RAZI(أبو مسلم); NASAFI; ABUHAYYAN(قتادة); ALUSI(قتادة); IBNASHUR]
- 103:1/q09/p3 The exception here adds mutual counsel to faith and works mentioned elsewhere. — Samarrai says counsel in truth and patience prevents further loss of reward. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR]
- 103:1/q09/p4 A verse about protected life uses this sūra as a parallel for a generic statement with an exception. — Qurtubi compares the generic definite al-nafs and its legally qualified exception to generic human statements in 103:1–3, citing 70:19 and 70:22. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL]
- 103:1/q09/p5 Its message distinguishes those saved from those ruined, framing the whole sūra. — Biqai maps the clauses to knowledge of God and afterlife, worship, doubt and patience, and says preventing harm precedes benefit. Qutb calls faith, work and mutual counsel the one path of success; Study Quran extends the decline image to each generation. Bint al-Shati links the opening's pressing image to the later contrast between human loss and salvation. [13 notes: BIQAI-FULL; QUTB-ZILAL; STUDYQURAN; BINTSHATI-IJAZ]
- 103:1/q09/p6 The oath by this age parallels oaths by the Prophet's life and the city where he lived. — Al-Rāzī and Ibn ʿĀshūr compare 15:72 and 90:2 to explain why the Prophet's era is honored. [2 notes: RAZI; IBNASHUR]
- 103:1/q09/p7 After al-Takāthur, it shows the state of those whom rivalry in abundance did not distract. — Al-Ālūsī connects the previous sūra's warning with the successful people described here. [1 notes: ALUSI]

## 103:1/q11 How many verses does the sūra have, and where is its first break?  [* · sciences]
turns on: The position of the first verse boundary; the reported total count is three.
- 103:1/q11/p1 It has three verses, with the oath as its own first verse. — Alusi calls the three-verse count undisputed; Neuwirth favors an independent oath in line with other openings. Al-Zarkashi counts al-ʿAṣr as a one-word opening verse by analogy with al-Fajr and al-Ḍuḥā and their rhyme despite different lengths, and notes that a single utterance can form a verse. Al-Suyuti reports scholars adding wa-l-ʿaṣr to standalone verses; the counters other than the later Medinans count this opening separately. [14 notes: ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس وابن الزبير والجمهور); QUTB-ZILAL; CORPUSCORANICUM(Neuwirth); BURHAN; ITQAN(المدني الأخير والباقون,غيره); BAGHAWI; KASHSHAF; QURTUBI; NASAFI; ALUSI; IBNASHUR]
- 103:1/q11/p2 Medina II joins the oath to verse 2 in its division. — Spitaler reports this verse division. Al-Suyuti reports that the later Medinan count instead makes wa-tawāṣaw bi-l-ḥaqq a separate verse while all still total three. Al-Mawsili's scheme distinguishes disagreements in internal breaks from disagreements in totals. [3 notes: CORPUSCORANICUM(Spitaler); ITQAN(المدني الأخير والباقون,الموصلي)]
- 103:1/q11/p3 Under Abu ʿAmr al-Dani's one-word rule, wa-l-ʿaṣr would not form a standalone verse. — Al-Suyuti reports al-Dani's assertion that mudhāmmatān is the only word that constitutes an āya on its own; the report does not itself supply an alternative three-verse division for al-ʿAṣr. [1 notes: ITQAN(أبو عمرو الداني)]

## 103:1/q12 When in the revelation sequence did this sūra come?  [* · sciences]
- 103:1/q12/p1 It followed shortly after Sūra 94. — Asad states this; Study Quran calls the sequence likely. Ibn ʿĀshūr places it after al-Inshirāḥ (94) and before al-ʿĀdiyāt (100). [3 notes: ASAD-NOTES; STUDYQURAN; IBNASHUR]

## 103:1/q13 What does the sūra as a whole teach?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes differ in emphasis between the sūra's moral summary, its extraction imagery, decline, and inward lessons.
- 103:1/q13/p1 It compresses the Qur'an's path of salvation: faith, good works, truth and patience avert universal loss. — Muhammad ibn Kaʿb says belief alone does not suffice; the four qualities follow in order. Sulami lists neglect of God's rights, desires and lack of fear as causes of loss. Qutb calls the sūra a concise Muslim constitution, while Biqai relays Ghazali's six Qur'anic themes and maps the clauses to them. Al-Shafi'i says reflection on this sūra would suffice; Akif's poem repeats its sequence. Ibn ʿĀshūr includes patiently purifying oneself and inviting others to truth, and reports that the sūra encompasses all Qur'anic sciences. [32 notes: IBNABIHATIM(محمد بن كعب القرظي); SULAMI(بعضهم); JISHUMI; BIQAI-FULL(الشافعي,الغزالي); QUTB-ZILAL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Mehmet Âkif Ersoy,İmam Şâfiî); STUDYQURAN(al-Shāfiʿī); IBNKATHIR-FULL(الشافعي); ALUSI-FULL(الشافعي); IBNKATHIR(الشافعي); ALUSI(الشافعي); IBNASHUR(الشافعي)]
- 103:1/q13/p2 Its imagery extracts the enduring saved essence from transient worldly life. — Biqai explains the name through extraction, describes the doomed and saved, and reads the basmala as contrasting perishing things with God's enduring mercy. [8 notes: BIQAI-FULL]
- 103:1/q13/p3 Each generation falls below its predecessor unless it follows the saving path. — Study Quran applies the decline reading both to individuals and to human history. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN; QUTB-ZILAL]
- 103:1/q13/p4 Affliction drives God's servants toward Him and falls on the spiritually best first. — Qashani describes affliction as God's whip and ranks prophets, saints and others by the order of its incidence. [2 notes: QASHANI]
- 103:1/q13/p5 Hearing, knowing and accompanying God's name call for wealth, self and spirit to be given to it. — Qushayri says hearing yields generosity, knowing ends preference for self, and companionship gives eternal life and the spirit's surrender. [3 notes: QUSHAYRI]

## 103:1/q16 What does the sūra's opening suggest about its literary form?  [* · rhetoric]

- 103:1/q16/p4 Across its three verses, the sūra uniquely has ten wāws among three-verse sūras. — Al-Suyuti cites Ibn Khalawayh's letter-count observation through the end of al-ʿAṣr. [1 notes: ITQAN(ابن خالويه)]

## 103:1/q20 How is the transition from al-ʿAṣr to the next sūra recited?  [* · readings]
turns on: The transmitted practice of a reader who ordinarily joins or pauses between sūras.
- 103:1/q20/p1 Some readers who generally join sūras make a brief pause at this transition. — Ibn al-Jazari reports a special sakt among several Hamza, Abu ʿAmr, Ibn ʿAmir, Yaʿqub and Warsh readers. - 103:1/q20/p2 Some readers who generally pause insert the basmala at this transition. — Ibn al-Jazari reports this practice from Ibn Ghalbun, the Hidaya author, Makki and others; it separates the same jarring phrase, and the special basmala presupposes pausing elsewhere. [3 notes: NASHR]
- 103:1/q20/p3 Most authorities give this transition no special treatment, and joiners do not insert an untransmitted basmala. — Ibn al-Jazari names Faris ibn Ahmad, Ibn Sufyan, Abu al-Tahir, Iraqi authorities and al-Dani among those making no distinction. ## 103:1/q22 What principle determines whether the opening of al-ʿAṣr is a separate āya?  [وَٱلْعَصْرِ · sciences]
turns on: Whether verse boundaries can be inferred by analogy and form or require transmitted specification.
- 103:1/q22/p1 An āya is a marked, separate unit of Qur'anic speech, understood through its boundaries or function as a sign. — Al-Suyuti relays al-Jaʿbari's sentence-based definition, another scholar's separated-portion definition, and explanations that the name āya marks a break or signifies proof of the messenger's truth. - 103:1/q22/p2 The actual verse boundaries are fixed by revelation and prophetic transmission, not inferred from definitions or analogy. — Al-Wahidi says a general definition could include less than a full verse but revelation fixed the divisions; others and al-Zamakhshari insist on transmitted specification. Al-Suyuti also cites a report about thirty verses in al-Ahqaf as evidence used in verse-counting discussion. [4 notes: ITQAN(ابن مسعود,الزمخشري,الواحدي,بعضهم)]
- 103:1/q22/p3 Rhyme and analogy with other one-word openings support counting wa-l-ʿaṣr separately. — Al-Zarkashi compares it with al-Fajr and al-Ḍuḥā despite their differing lengths; he also notes that a word can be a complete single utterance even when short. [2 notes: BURHAN]
