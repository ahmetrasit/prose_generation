# 89:1: 32 questions

## 89:1/q01 What does al-fajr mean as an Arabic word?  [ٱلْفَجْرِ · meaning]
turns on: Whether splitting is the root idea or an instance of a broader emergence.
- 89:1/q01/p1 Morning light breaking from night, or the time when it appears. — Tustari gives the outward sense as morning. Zajjaj, Mawardi, Tabarisi, Ibn Jawzi and Bint al-Shati describe darkness cleft by light. Ibn Jawzi relates the root to water bursting out and fājir breaking from obedience. Bint al-Shati cites six Quranic dawn uses, 2:187's white thread and 17:78's sequence after night; she also explains ghasaq as darkness mingled with first morning light or lingering evening light. Islahi distinguishes fajr's transition from subh, which extends beyond sunrise and is qualified in oaths (74:34; 81:18). Kuran Yolu gives brightness or daybreak time. Damghani glosses al-fajr simply as dawn. Mawardi describes light breaking over the eastern horizon, distinguishes the vanishing upright false dawn from the horizontally spreading true dawn, and cites a poet calling the latter truthful. Alusi derives the name from splitting the night and also glosses it as dawn's column or spreading light. Ibn Ashur describes the first eastern solar ray pushing back darkness before sunrise. [21 notes: TUSTARI; ZAJJAJ; MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; JISHUMI; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس، علي بن عبيد الله); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; BINTSHATI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR; DAMGHANI; MAWARDI; ALUSI; IBNASHUR]
- 89:1/q01/p2 The root broadly denotes emergence or outburst, physically or figuratively. — Bint al-Shati prefers this to al-Raghib's narrower splitting derivation. She cites springs, gushing and volcanoes, metaphorical emergence in goodness, generosity or evil, and the four pre-Islamic Fijar battle days. She counts 24 Quranic root uses, ten verbs mostly about water; 75:5 is the sole non-water verb. Nouns name rivers in 17:91, a spring in 76:6, and wickedness opposite piety in 91:8 and other passages. [6 notes: BINTSHATI(الراغب); BINTSHATI+]

turns on: The Kufan and Medinan grammarians give different colors; the latter invoke garment usage.

## 89:1/q04 Which dawn governs prayer and fasting?  [ٱلْفَجْرِ · law]
- 89:1/q04/p1 The horizontal true dawn; the earlier vertical false dawn is legally night. — Mawardi says the first narrow streak misleads and cites poetic al-musaddaq for truthful dawn. Jishumi, Bursevi, Alusi and Elmalili assign prayer and fasting rules only to spreading true dawn; Bursevi compares false dawn to a wolf's tail. Tabarisi distinguishes the two. Alusi says the legal role makes true dawn especially apt for the oath. Mawardi describes false dawn as an upright, narrow column that vanishes, leaving darkness before morning, and calls it misleading or the herald of morning; true dawn spreads horizontally and truthfully announces morning. Alusi says this legal distinction makes true dawn likelier as the oath's referent. [13 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(الشاعر); JISHUMI; TABRISI; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(علي وابن عباس وابن الزبير); ELMALILI; MAWARDI; ALUSI]

## 89:1/q05 How is the oath phrase grammatically construed?  [وَٱلْفَجْرِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the sworn object is named directly or an omitted head noun is supplied.
- 89:1/q05/p1 The oath wāw governs al-fajr in the genitive, directly swearing by dawn. — Nahhas gives the case analysis. Ibn al-Zubayr reports that God swears by dawn; Qurtubi counts five oaths. Ibn Ashur supplies the implicit oath verb, 'I swear by the dawn.' Bint al-Shati notes that oath-waw leads a clause to authenticate what is denied or mark acknowledgment and testimony. Tabari and Durr transmit Ibn al-Zubayr's statement that God swears by dawn; Qurtubi counts five oaths in the opening passage. [12 notes: NAHHAS; TAB-FULL(عبد الله بن الزبير); IBNABIHATIM(عبد الله بن الزبير); QURTUBI-FULL; DURR-FULL(عبد الله بن الزبير); IBNASHUR-FULL; BINTSHATI-IJAZ; TAB(عبد الله بن الزبير); MAWARDI; KASHSHAF; QURTUBI; DURR(عبدالله بن الزبير)]
- 89:1/q05/p2 An omitted noun makes it an oath by the Lord of dawn. — Samin reports this; Bint al-Shati attributes supplied genitives to Tabari, Naysaburi and Zamakhshari. [2 notes: SAMIN-DURR; BINTSHATI(الطبري)]
- 89:1/q05/p3 An omitted noun makes it an oath by dawn prayer or Quran of dawn. — Samin gives prayer and Bint al-Shati reports Quran of dawn as another completion. Alusi permits supplying an omitted noun for the dawn prayer, alongside a figurative construal naming the prayer itself. [3 notes: SAMIN-DURR; BINTSHATI(الطبري); ALUSI(ابن عباس وزيد بن أسلم)]

## 89:1/q06 Who may swear by a created dawn?  [وَٱلْفَجْرِ · law]
- 89:1/q06/p1 God may swear by any created thing; humans may swear only by God. — Maymun ibn Mihran draws this rule from the divine oath. ## 89:1/q07 Is al-fajr recited with verse-final tanwin?  [ٱلْفَجْرِ · readings]
turns on: Whether verse endings are pronounced like poetic pauses; grammarians also dispute the form's name.
- 89:1/q07/p1 Most readers leave al-fajr without tanwin. — Samin says they likewise leave al-watr and yasr without it. [1 notes: SAMIN-DURR(أبو الدينار الأعرابي)]
- 89:1/q07/p2 Abu al-Dinar al-Aʿrabi adds poetic pausal tanwin to al-fajr, al-watr and yasr. — Kashshaf says it substitutes for a lengthening letter and also reports Ibn Abbas's separate annexed layālin ʿashr. Abu Hayyan adduces a poem and grammatical discussion of open rhyme and ordinary pause. Ibn Khalawayh calls it tanwin al-tarannum, even on verbs and definite nouns. Samin notes some prefer tanwin tark al-tarannum and distinguish disputed tanwin al-ghali on closed rhymes; he cites 33:66-67, 33:10 and 13:9. Abu Hayyan reports Abu al-Dinar's tanwin on al-fajr, al-watr and yasr; Ibn Khalawayh and an unnamed poet illustrate rhyme-ending tanwin even on verbs and definite forms. Abu Hayyan compares unrestricted rhyme without tarannum, two prose stopping patterns, and treatment of verse endings as rhymes. [11 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(أبو الدينار الأعرابي); SAMIN-DURR(أبو الدينار الأعرابي,ابن خالويه); ABUHAYYAN(أبو الدينار الأعرابي,ابن خالويه,الشاعر)]

## 89:1/q08 What is the answer to the opening oath?  [وَٱلْفَجْرِ · grammar]
turns on: Whether verse 14 states the response, ensuing stories imply an omitted one, or the preceding sura supplies one.
- 89:1/q08/p1 The expressed answer is that the Lord is surely watchful (89:14). — Zajjaj, Tabarisi, Ibn al-Anbari and Razi identify it; Bint al-Shati reports Razi treating intervening material as parenthetical. Ibn Masud says the oaths affirm watchfulness. Kuran Yolu adds knowledge of deeds and requital. - 89:1/q08/p2 An unstated answer implies resurrection, judgment, punishment or requital. — Tabarisi suggests seizing oppressors or vindicating victims. Samin infers each person's recompense from earlier peoples and reports Zamakhshari's 'He will surely punish' from 89:6 and 89:13. Kuran Yolu gives resurrection and judgment. Bint al-Shati reports Ibn al-Qayyim's implicit answer in what befell Abraham and Muhammad. Al-Suyuti says an oath answer can be omitted because its objects already indicate the assertion, making the expression more forceful and concise; he reads the great pilgrimage rites and humble worship in these times as implying the honor of what Muhammad and Abraham brought. [7 notes: TABRISI; SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); BINTSHATI(ابن القيم); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Müfessirler); ITQAN]
- 89:1/q08/p3 The omitted answer is the end of al-Ghashiya: return to God for reckoning. — Samin reports the Shaykh's appeal to 88:25-26. Bint al-Shati reports Abu Hayyan and Muhammad Abduh arguing from printed adjacency; she rejects this because al-Ghashiya was revealed 58 suras after al-Fajr. ## 89:1/q09 Why is dawn chosen as an oath sign?  [وَٱلْفَجْرِ · rhetoric]
turns on: The sources stress divine order, resurrection, particular rites, moral symbolism and the oaths' evidentiary role.
- 89:1/q09/p1 Its ordered light and benefit show the Creator's sole power. — Jishumi calls these signs proof of the Creator; Razi notes that dawn sends people and animals seeking provision. Islahi says God alone brings forth and removes, grants respite and harmonizes opposing forces (21:22); 28:71-72 illustrates His power over perpetual night or day. Elmalili sees changing time as lordship. Qutb portrays creation joyfully waking and praying. Corpus Coranicum cites a likely authentic Umayya poem greeting the Lord each dawn, suggesting a cultic connotation. Razi says oath objects have religious benefit as signs of oneness, worldly benefit calling for thanks, or both; their perceived priority drives interpretations. He links dawn to 74:34, 81:18 and God as maker of daybreak (6:96). Ibn Ashur calls the first light a display of divine power and artistry; it releases people from death-like sleep into aware, useful work. He says the times sworn by display God's craftsmanship and include times of worship. [15 notes: RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); JISHUMI; ELMALILI; QUTB-ZILAL; CORPUSCORANICUM; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; RAZI; IBNASHUR]
- 89:1/q09/p2 Its daily return after dark images resurrection and judgment. — Razi and Bursevi compare dawn's spreading life with rising again. Biqai likens waking from sleep's lesser death to return, work and eventual recompense. Islahi invokes a waking supplication, 74:32-34, moral incompleteness without judgment and 68:35-36; ease and hardship prepare for 89:28. Kuran Yolu cites waking living things and 81:18. Razi compares creatures emerging at dawn with the dead rising from graves and calls it a lesson for reflection. ## 89:1/q26 Is al-Fajr classified as containing abrogation?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether the broad sura categories withstand the detailed abrogation cases.
- 89:1/q26/p1 One classification says it contains neither abrogating nor abrogated material. — Al-Suyuti reports this classification, which places al-Asr in a different category containing both. [1 notes: ITQAN(بعضهم)]
- 89:1/q26/p2 That four-part sura classification is open to question. — Al-Suyuti rejects uncritical use of it; his surrounding discussion distinguishes several kinds of changed obligation and three kinds of abrogation, including the debated nursing report and cases where a ruling ends but recitation remains. [11 notes: ITQAN(أبو موسى الأشعري,عائشة,مكي); ITQAN+; ITQAN(بعضهم)-]

## 89:1/q27 What added-letter spelling conventions appear in the surrounding writing discussion?  [* · sciences]
- 89:1/q27/p1 Some written alifs, yas, and waws are added by orthographic convention, perhaps reflecting older vowel notation. — Al-Suyuti lists extra alifs after waw and certain hamzas, extra ya in specified phrases, and extra waw in ulu and related forms; he reports al-Kirmani's older-script explanation. [5 notes: ITQAN(الكرماني)]

## 89:1/q28 Why are Quranic passages called ayat in the discussion that cites al-Fajr?  [* · sciences]
- 89:1/q28/p1 An ayah is a transmitted Quranic unit and a sign of its inimitable revelation. — Ibn Ashur says a Quranic portion can be explicitly complete, understood by ellipsis, or complete by inclusion; he calls ayah a Quranic designation grounded in the text's eloquence and challenge. He distinguishes Torah and Gospel clauses, explaining a narrator's 'ayah of stoning' as analogy, and notes kitab can mean the Quranic verses. [6 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 89:1/q29 How are disjoint-letter sura openings explained in the related opening-form discussion?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether a later explicit mention of the Quran is needed.
- 89:1/q29/p1 Their relation to later Quran references reflects the audience's attention and the weight of revelation. — Bint al-Shati reports al-Razi's explanations that mentioning the Quran draws attention to all of it and that some openings reflect its obligations; she finds these explanations forced. Al-Zarkashi asks about exceptions to the usual later Quran mention. [3 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الرازي,الزركشي); BINTSHATI-IJAZ-]
- 89:1/q29/p2 Every disjoint-letter sura contains a defense of the Quran and its inimitability. — Bint al-Shati reports Ibn Kathir's inductive explanation for the apparent exceptions. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(ابن كثير)]

## 89:1/q30 How is the sura titled in transmitted texts?  [* · sciences]
- 89:1/q30/p1 Surat al-Fajr, without the opening oath waw. — Ibn Ashur says Quran copies, commentaries and hadith books consistently use this title. [1 notes: IBNASHUR]

## 89:1/q31 Can al-fajr and the neighboring oath terms have more than one referent?  [ٱلْفَجْرِ · interpretation]
- 89:1/q31/p1 Shared terms can denote multiple things, with either one or several senses intended. — Al-Suyuti lists dawn, the ten nights, even and odd among examples of terms whose referents can be multiple; whether both senses are intended varies. [1 notes: ITQAN]

## 89:1/q32 Whom does the opening oath address?  [وَٱلْفَجْرِ · rhetoric]
- 89:1/q32/p1 The Prophet directly, while indirectly assuring deniers that the assertion will occur. — Ibn Ashur infers the address to the Prophet from the later reminders of God's acts against Ad and of divine watchfulness (89:6, 89:14). [1 notes: IBNASHUR]
