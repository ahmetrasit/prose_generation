# 96:10/q10 What does the denunciation of forbidding prayer teach? [* · interpretation]
turns on: The notes distinguish the immediate Abu Jahl occasion from the wording's broader moral reach and Qashani's inward reading.
- 96:10/q10/p1 Anyone who obstructs prayer, obedience, or other worship shares the warning.
  reasons: Razi and Alusi say Abu Jahl's occasion is particular but the warning general; Bursevi cites Abu al-Layth and Ibn al-Shaykh. Jishumi extends blame to stopping obedience or guidance and infers enjoining right. Alusi and Elmalili include verbal and practical obstruction, even distracting a worshipper; Elmalili includes prescribed and voluntary prayers and people like Bilal. Biqai sees envy and rebellion in denying God's due; Asad applies it to privatizing religion or dismissing metaphysical guidance, and Kuranyolu to tyrants obstructing social goodness, truth, and justice. Qashani calls the forbider veiled and self-satisfied; Islahi relates it to earlier rebellion.

# 96:10/q11 Does this warning forbid objecting to every prayer practice? [إِذَا صَلَّىٰٓ · law]
- 96:10/q11/p1 One may object to an accompanying wrong, such as a usurped place or disliked time, while avoiding direct prohibition of prayer itself.
  reasons: Ibn al-Shaykh, Alusi and Elmalili distinguish prayer from its unlawful circumstance; Ibn al-Shaykh says an invalid attribute does not invalidate underlying prayer. Ali avoided forbidding people praying before Eid, saying only that he had not seen the Prophet do so; Abu Hanifa likewise gave Abu Yusuf the prescribed praise after bowing without expressly forbidding his proposed forgiveness formula.

# 96:10/q12 How do the questions and clauses around this phrase function rhetorically? [* · rhetoric]
turns on: How much unstated warning or staged progression the repeated questions carry.
- 96:10/q12/p1 They invite astonished attention, rebuke the forbider, and warn that God sees him.
  reasons: Wahidi says repeated 'have you seen?' intensifies amazement; Kashshaf paraphrases it as 'tell me about' the forbider. Farra contrasts the guided worshipper with the denier; Ibn Atiyya says the closing 'Does he not know?' supplies a compressed threat for three conditional formulations. Qutb describes quick, broken touches that make listeners witness escalating ugliness; Islahi compares 'just look at him'. Alusi says separated clauses give each point independent force.
  holders (15 notes): TAB; WAHIDI-BASIT; WAHIDI-QT; KASHSHAF; ABUHAYYAN(ابن عطية، الفراء); ALUSI; QUTB-ZILAL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BINTSHATI

# 96:10/q13 Who is addressed by the surrounding 'have you seen?' questions? [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether consistent pronouns favor the Prophet or the discourse turns to the forbider or a general audience.
- 96:10/q13/p1 The Prophet is addressed.
  reasons: Razi relates his earlier prayer for Islam through Abu Jahl or Umar. Abu Hayyan prefers the Messenger because the pronouns stay consistent; Alusi cites al-Bahr's preference.
  holders (5 notes): RAZI; ALUSI(الإمام); ABUHAYYAN+
  notes: RAZI:96:10/r4 ABUHAYYAN:96:10/r6 ABUHAYYAN:96:9/r6 ALUSI:96:10/r47 ALUSI:96:10/r53
- 96:10/q13/p2 Anyone capable of seeing or understanding is addressed.
  reasons: Bursevi takes seeing as visual; Alusi records a group who address any capable hearer and another view addressing humanity as at 96:8. Ibn Ashur leaves the addressee unspecified and allows visual witnessing of the event.
  holders (4 notes): BURSEVI; ALUSI(جمع، قيل); IBNASHUR-FULL
  notes: BURSEVI:v10p365#2/r1 ALUSI:96:10/r26 ALUSI:96:10/r27 IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p446/r4
- 96:10/q13/p3 The disbelieving forbider is addressed.
  reasons: Abu Hayyan reports this reading: if the worshipper's prayer is guidance and a summons to piety, would the disbeliever still forbid him? The conditional pronouns then refer to the forbider; Alusi says others allow it.
  holders (3 notes): ABUHAYYAN(قيل); ALUSI
