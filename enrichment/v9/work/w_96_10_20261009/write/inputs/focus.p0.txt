# 96:10/q01 Who is the praying servant, and who forbids him? [عَبْدًا · referent]
turns on: The historical identification and Meccan chronology, versus the indefinite wording's general reach.
- 96:10/q01/p1 The servant is Muhammad and the forbider is Abu Jahl.
  reasons: Mujahid, Qatada, Ibn Abbas, and many commentators identify this pair; Ibn Atiyya calls it a consensus. Tabari places the prayer at the Maqam, and Ibn Kathir at the House. Razi says the prior context and following verses point to this specific man. Ibn Ashur says the threat could have been meant to reach Muhammad rather than said face to face.

# 96:10/q02 What does ʿabd signify here? [عَبْدًا · meaning]
turns on: Whether social subordination is included alongside religious servanthood.
- 96:10/q02/p1 God's servant, devoted to worship.
  reasons: Nöldeke and Schwally insist on God's servant rather than merely a human being; their social reading also draws on inner-textual signs of unequal rank at 96:17. Razi and Biqai emphasize complete devotion and service to the divine Master.
  holders (3 notes): RAZI; BIQAI-FULL; CORPUSCORANICUM(Nöldeke and Schwally)+
  notes: CORPUSCORANICUM:96:9-10:anmerkung/r2 RAZI:96:10/r7 BIQAI-FULL:v8p483/r7
- 96:10/q02/p2 The word admits both a worldly slave and a worshipper of God.
  reasons: Corpus Coranicum contrasts the worldly view of a slave with the believer's recognition that obedience to God can outrank obedience to an earthly master, with 96:19 as context.
  holders (1 notes): CORPUSCORANICUM
  notes: CORPUSCORANICUM:96:9-10:anmerkung/r3

# 96:10/q03 Why call him an indefinite 'servant' rather than address Muhammad directly? [عَبْدًا · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the indefiniteness primarily magnifies Muhammad, generalizes the worshipper, or has no special rhetorical force.
- 96:10/q03/p1 The indefinite servant magnifies Muhammad's perfect devotion and the outrage of obstructing prayer.
  reasons: Baydawi, Biqai, Bursevi, and Alusi say the form heightens the ugliness of forbidding a servant's service to his Master. Razi says an indefinite yet uniquely identifiable servant magnifies the Prophet, citing 97:1, 17:1 and 72:19; his account of a learned Jew and Ali cites 28:60 and 68:4. Razi also says it may portray the forbider's habit toward anyone. Bursevi notes the avoidance of direct 'you'.
  holders (10 notes): RAZI; BAYDAWI; BAYDAWI-FULL; BIQAI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI(قيل)
  notes: RAZI:96:10/r7 RAZI:96:10/r8 RAZI:96:10/r12 BAYDAWI:96:10/r2 BAYDAWI-FULL:96:9-14/r2 BIQAI:96:10/r1 BIQAI-FULL:v8p483/r7 BURSEVI:v10p365#2/r1 BURSEVI:v10p365#2/r2 ALUSI:96:10/r29
- 96:10/q03/p2 The generic designation makes the act itself astonishing regardless of which worshipper is targeted.
  reasons: Ibn Ashur says the wording deliberately generalizes Muhammad to anyone devoted to God, citing 17:5; its wonder lies in forbidding prayer itself.
  holders (2 notes): IBNASHUR; IBNASHUR-FULL
  notes: IBNASHUR:96:10/r1 IBNASHUR-FULL:v30p446/r5
- 96:10/q03/p3 The nunation may simply retain its ordinary indefinite sense.
  reasons: Alusi records this alternative without a further argument.

# 96:10/q04 What prayer is meant by 'when he prays'? [إِذَا صَلَّىٰٓ · referent]
turns on: The reported noon occasion, the date when five prayers became obligatory, and the verse's unrestricted wording.
- 96:10/q04/p1 The noon prayer.
  reasons: Mawardi holds this; al-Tabrizi is cited by Abu Hayyan and others. Elmalili says the report calls it noon prayer without claiming it was obligatory, and associates a first public prayer after Umar's conversion with it.
