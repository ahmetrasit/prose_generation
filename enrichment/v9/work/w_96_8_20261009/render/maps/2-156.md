# 2:156: 32 questions

## 2:156/q01 How is 'those who' parsed in relation to the patient?  [ٱلَّذِينَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the relative noun remains a modifier, is cut off for praise, or begins a new sentence.
- 2:156/q01/p1 It qualifies 'the patient' as an accusative adjective. — Wāḥidī reports it; Abū Ḥayyān lists it; al-Samīn prefers it. [3 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR+]
- 2:156/q01/p2 It is a new subject whose predicate is 'upon them are blessings' in 2:157. — Wāḥidī, Abū Ḥayyān, and al-Samīn place the intervening conditional in the relative clause. [3 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]
- 2:156/q01/p3 It is an accusative expression of praise. — Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn report the accusative cut-off for commendation. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]
- 2:156/q01/p4 It is nominative after an omitted 'they are', as a detached or resumed construction. — Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn supply hum; al-Samīn allows both cut-off and new resumption. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]

## 2:156/q02 What does 'when' imply about the occurrence of calamity?  [إِذَآ · grammar]
turns on: Whether idhā itself conveys repetition.
- 2:156/q02/p1 It indicates a general, recurring condition. — Abū Ḥayyān says idhā here denotes repetition and notes grammarians dispute whether the particle itself requires recurrence. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 2:156/q02/p2 The particle can mark a single occurrence. — Abū Ḥayyān reports the opposing grammatical view, though he reads this instance generally. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 2:156/q03 What counts as a 'calamity' here?  [مُّصِيبَةٌۭ · meaning]
turns on: Whether the word is restricted to severe personal injury or includes every painful loss.
- 2:156/q03/p1 Any unpleasant harm that befalls a believer, however small, including loss affecting self, family, or property. — Wāḥidī says it never names good fortune; al-Qurṭubī includes the slightest evil and cites illness, fatigue, sorrow, and anxiety in Muslim. The extinguished lamp, thorn, mosquito bite, and broken sandal strap illustrate breadth in Kashshāf, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Bayḍāwī, Abū Ḥayyān, and Ālūsī. Māwardī, Ṭabrisī, and Abū Ḥayyān specify person, family, and possessions. Bursawī derives it from reaching its target; Biqāʿī notes that the root striking a mark can describe what pleases or displeases although this occurrence is a loss. Ibn ʿĀshūr refers its lexical discussion to 4:72. [26 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; KASHSHAF-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL(النبي); QURTUBI-FULL(أبو سعيد وأبو هريرة,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); BAYDAWI-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(النبي); ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; JISHUMI; STUDYQURAN]
- 2:156/q03/p2 It is severe harm to the self. — Jishumī derives muṣība from iṣāba, as though the injury strikes a person; Ṭabrisī likewise speaks of hardship striking the soul. [2 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI]

## 2:156/q04 How are the forms and plurals of muṣība explained?  [مُّصِيبَةٌۭ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the plural hamza follows mistaken pattern analogy or a direct sound substitution.
- 2:156/q04/p1 The yāʾ in muṣība is a root letter replacing wāw; hamzated maṣāʾib arose by mistaken analogy. — Wāḥidī explains that speakers treated root yāʾ as a lengthening yāʾ, as in safīna/safāʾin and ṣaḥīfa/ṣaḥāʾif. He quotes Sībawayh calling the hamza an error, Abū ʿAlī preferring this account for its parallels, and Abū al-Ḥasan's comparable masīl plural. Al-Qurṭubī says Arab usage consistently hamzates the plural despite its original radical. The forms muṣāb, muṣāba, and muṣawba occur in al-Farrāʾ's poetry, al-Kisāʾī's Bedouin example, and al-Qurṭubī's verse about an unjust tribute; al-Qurṭubī adds the dialectal ṣāba for aṣāba. [11 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو الحسن,أبو علي الفارسي,الفراء,سيبويه); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الفراء والكسائي); QURTUBI-FULL(الشاعر); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو علي الفارسي)+]
- 2:156/q04/p2 The hamza of maṣāʾib directly replaces a kasra wāw. — Wāḥidī reports Abū Isḥāq's comparison with isāda; Abū ʿAlī rejects it because no parallel changes a noninitial kasra wāw in this way. [1 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو علي الفارسي)-]

## 2:156/q05 Why does the verse say that a calamity 'strikes them'?  [أَصَٰبَتْهُم مُّصِيبَةٌۭ · rhetoric]
turns on: The notes give complementary rhetorical effects, sound pairing and unassigned agency.
- 2:156/q05/p1 The verb and noun make a paronomastic pairing. — Abū Ḥayyān compares similarly paired words in 53:57 and 56:1. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 2:156/q05/p2 The wording leaves the agent unnamed so it covers harms from God and from people. — Al-Qāḍī in Rāzī and al-Muntakhab in Abū Ḥayyān distinguish divine causes from human ones; Rāzī illustrates fear by drowning, fire, lightning, or hostile Arabs, hunger by destroyed or seized wealth, property loss by disaster or enemy pressure, and death by dying or killing. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL(القاضي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(المنتخب)]

## 2:156/q06 What kind of hardship is in view in the passage?  [مُّصِيبَةٌۭ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the trials are read broadly or chiefly through the coming communal conflict.
- 2:156/q06/p1 It includes both private losses and afflictions inflicted by other people. — Rāzī distributes fear, hunger, property, and life loss between divine and human causes; Māwardī includes person, family, and property. Al-Qāḍī says duties remain in either case. [6 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; RAZI-FULL(القاضي)]
- 2:156/q06/p2 The immediate setting anticipates a collective trial of conflict for monotheism and battle. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī says the five verses came before the legislation of jihad, after opponents had exhausted other means; collective distress impairs common judgment and planning. The passage presents death in God's path as life and patience and prayer as protection. [5 notes: TABATABAI]

## 2:156/q07 How does 'they say' function in the construction?  [قَالُوٓا۟ · grammar]
- 2:156/q07/p1 The quoted declaration is the response to idhā within the relative clause. — Abū Ḥayyān specifies qālū innā lillāh as the conditional response. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 2:156/q08 How may 'innā lillāh' be pronounced?  [إِنَّا لِلَّهِ · readings]
turns on: Whether the kasra and frequent formulaic use license imāla here.
- 2:156/q08/p1 Imāla inclines its alif toward kasra in some transmissions from al-Kisāʾī. — Naḥḥās attributes permission to al-Kisāʾī; Jishumī, Ṭabrisī, Rāzī, al-Jazarī, and al-Suyūṭī locate the inclination in innā and/or lillāh. The following kasra, the divine name, and frequent use make the sequence nearly one word; al-Farrāʾ and al-Kisāʾī exclude innā before other names. Qutayba's transmission limits it to innā lillāh, not wa-innā ilayhi. Al-Jazarī surveys broader triggers: neighboring or underlying kasra and yāʾ, rhyme, resemblance, and frequent use; al-Suyūṭī reduces the causes to resemblance and indication and gives further examples. [24 notes: FARRA; NAHHAS(الكسائي); JISHUMI(الفراء والكسائي,الكسائي); TABRISI(الفراء,الكسائي); RAZI-FULL(الكسائي); NASHR(سيبويه,قتيبة عن الكسائي); ITQAN(ابن الجزري,الكسائي,سيبويه,صاحب المبهج)]
- 2:156/q08/p2 It may be pronounced with full tafkhīm. — Naḥḥās expressly permits it; Jishumī, Ṭabrisī, and Rāzī say other readers use it. [4 notes: NAHHAS; JISHUMI(الكسائي); TABRISI(الكسائي); RAZI-FULL(الكسائي)]

## 2:156/q09 What explains the form innā?  [إِنَّا · grammar]
turns on: Whether the apparent vowel is analyzed as morphological contraction or through formulaic fusion; Naḥḥās objects to the latter account.
- 2:156/q09/p1 It contracts from innanā by dropping one nūn for ease while retaining grammatical government. — Naḥḥās cites the same lightening in wa-innā ilayhi rājiʿūn; Abū Ḥayyān says dropping the second identical nūn preserves the particle's operation. [2 notes: NAHHAS; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 2:156/q09/p2 In this grief formula, the nūn is said to take kasra through the following lām. — Al-Farrāʾ says frequency fuses the expression like one word and compares kāfir and al-ḥamdu lillāh. Naḥḥās rejects this as mistaken: the nūn is not kasra, and an alif cannot itself be vowelled. [2 notes: FARRA; NAHHAS(الفراء)-]

## 2:156/q10 What do they acknowledge by saying 'we belong to God'?  [إِنَّا لِلَّهِ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the statement is explained as ownership and servitude or developed as inward surrender.
- 2:156/q10/p1 Their selves, families, possessions, and all they hold are God's property; they are His servants under His rightful disposal. — Zajjāj, Māwardī, Wāḥidī, Baghawī, Ṭabrisī, Rāzī, Abū Ḥayyān, Ibn Kathīr, and others say ownership entails accepting His judgment. Māwardī says He does not wrong them; Jishumī and al-Qāḍī distinguish God's own acts from justice sought against human harm. Qushayrī says the Creator has greater claim; Bursawī says His gifts are His favor and reclaimed things are stored for the servant. Biqāʿī says God encompasses all things. Ṭabāṭabāʾī argues human possession is only apparent by divine permission, citing 40:16; Study Quran relates the claim to 2:116, 2:255, and 2:284. Ali and al-Warrāq distinguish this ownership confession from the second clause's mortality. [43 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; IBNABIHATIM(عبد الصمد بن يزيد); MAWARDI-FULL; QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو بكر الوراق); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI(أمير المؤمنين); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL(أبو بكر الوراق,القاضي); QURTUBI-FULL; QASHANI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(الحرالي); BURSEVI; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(أمير المؤمنين عليه السلام); STUDYQURAN]
- 2:156/q10/p2 It is an inward surrender that witnesses God's power and accepts His allotment beyond personal choice. — Al-Qāsim says every ordeal hides blessing and even its blessing hides love's fire; neither piety nor disobedience reverses events. Qushayrī contrasts the one 'for God,' who remains patient, with the one 'by God,' who loses choice and judgment. Qāshānī sees God's continual disposal and manifestations of His attributes. [5 notes: SULAMI(القاسم); QUSHAYRI; QASHANI]

## 2:156/q11 What does 'to Him we return' mean?  [وَإِنَّآ إِلَيْهِ رَٰجِعُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether return denotes resurrection and final judgment, sole divine rule, human annihilation, subsistence through God, compensation, or an inward effacement; spatial motion is explicitly rejected.
- 2:156/q11/p1 People die and are raised in the Hereafter for God's judgment, reward, or punishment. — Zajjāj stresses resurrection and reward for patience; Māwardī includes punishment of wrongdoers; Abū Ḥayyān calls death the greatest calamity, making lesser losses bearable. Ibn Kathīr says no atom of good is lost. Elmalılı includes gathering and a new creation after barzakh, distinguishes the ultimate Hereafter from the interval, and says people die bearing earned deeds; he links the contented soul's return to 89:27–28. Study Quran cites 2:210, 2:281, 3:83, and 5:48 for the wider motif. [26 notes: TAB-FULL; ZAJJAJ; MAWARDI-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI; BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 2:156/q11/p2 It is arrival at the Hereafter where God alone openly holds power to judge, benefit, and harm. — Wāḥidī says temporary delegated human control ends. Jishumī reports the view that no other authority judges there. Rāzī compares returning sovereignty with returning power, not travel; Bursawī and Elmalılı also reject a spatial destination because God transcends place and direction. [6 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; JISHUMI(قيل); RAZI-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI(Râzî)]
- 2:156/q11/p3 It acknowledges human death, passing away, or annihilation. — Abū Bakr al-Warrāq, Ali, Abū Ḥayyān, Bursawī, and Ṭabāṭabāʾī contrast God's ownership in the first clause with human perishing in the second. Elmalılı lists this as one figurative possibility. [9 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو بكر الوراق); TABRISI(أمير المؤمنين); RAZI-FULL(أبو بكر الوراق); QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ELMALILI; TABATABAI(أمير المؤمنين عليه السلام)]
- 2:156/q11/p4 Return involves both essential passing away and continued existence solely through God. — Elmalılı prefers this third reading over annihilation alone or subsistence alone. Divine knowledge preserves the person's reality even if body or soul perishes; 28:88 says all but God's Face perishes. God as universal cause and Living Sustainer can renew bodily or spiritual forms endlessly; the soul may survive barzakh but resurrection is a distinct new creation. People return bearing good or bad deeds and endure in a manifestation of beauty or majesty, with divine beauty the highest aim. He invokes 12:21 and the contented and pleasing soul of 89:27–28, and disputes making independent soul survival the foundation of the Hereafter. [26 notes: ELMALILI; ELMALILI+]
- 2:156/q11/p5 Return is understood as compensation of the loss and abundant reward from God. — Abū Ḥayyān reports this as a distinct explanation. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل)]
- 2:156/q11/p6 Inwardly it means effacing the self in God and witnessing its annihilation in Him. — Qāshānī joins this reading to witnessing divine attributes through calamity. [2 notes: QASHANI]
- 2:156/q11/p7 It refers to the soul's own continued spiritual existence as the basis of the Hereafter. — Elmalılı reports philosophers using 'spiritual subsistence' this way, and reports the soul's continuation as an explanation of the interval after death; he objects that independent self-subsistence forgets God as First Cause. [2 notes: ELMALILI; ELMALILI-]
- 2:156/q11/p8 It is literal motion toward God in a place or direction. — Rāzī, Bursawī, and Elmalılı explicitly reject spatial movement as impossible for God. [0 notes: RAZI-FULL-; BURSEVI-; ELMALILI-]
- 2:156/q11/p9 Returning is a thing's restoration to a former state or ownership. — Jishumī links the root sense to 96:8; Ṭabrisī compares a house becoming someone's property again and calls it synonymous with returning and becoming. [2 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI]

## 2:156/q12 What relation do the two clauses bear to present and future affliction?  [إِنَّا لِلَّهِ وَإِنَّآ إِلَيْهِ رَٰجِعُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the contrast is temporal, ownership versus mortality, or a joined confession of servitude and resurrection.
- 2:156/q12/p1 The first accepts the present loss; the second accepts the future, including final reward and redress. — Rāzī says both entrust affairs to God and encompass vindication from a wrongdoer; Abū Ḥayyān reports the present/future reading. Rāzī also links the latter with self-humbling and contentment with promised afterlife reward. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل)]
- 2:156/q12/p2 The first confesses ownership; the second confesses death or annihilation. — Abū Bakr al-Warrāq, Ali, Abū Ḥayyān, and Ṭabāṭabāʾī give this pair of meanings; Ṭabāṭabāʾī notes a fuller version in al-Kāfī. [6 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو بكر الوراق); TABRISI(أمير المؤمنين); RAZI-FULL(أبو بكر الوراق); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; TABATABAI(أمير المؤمنين عليه السلام)]
- 2:156/q12/p3 Together they confess servitude and resurrection, giving refuge and consolation. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the words unite monotheism, servitude, resurrection, and certainty of God's final ownership; al-Qurṭubī calls them refuge and protection for the same reason. Kashshāf says the expression marks surrender and acquiescence. [6 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; RAZI-FULL]

## 2:156/q13 What response to calamity does the verse teach?  [قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا لِلَّهِ وَإِنَّآ إِلَيْهِ رَٰجِعُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the emphasis is inward patience, conduct under human-caused harm, or the breadth and timing of recitation.
- 2:156/q13/p1 Patient believers say the formula with sincere belief, submit to God's decree, and hope for reward. — Ṭabarī says they recognize every blessing as God's, affirm servitude and return, hope for reward, and fear punishment. Wāḥidī cites the Prophet that patience is at the first blow; Ālūsī also stresses the first shock. Bayḍāwī and Ālūsī say patience requires inward recognition of life's purpose, the greater remaining blessings, and the final return. Ibn ʿĀshūr says empty recitation gains no merit, but speaking conviction reinforces it and teaches others. Qushayrī describes progression from patience to gratitude, joy, and pride. Dawūd al-Ṭāʾī commends contentment and lack of grief because calamity earns reward. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk limits the promise to those who keep piety and obligations; al-Qāsim commends acceptance of allotment. Ibn Masʿūd says falling from the sky would be preferable to wishing that God's decree had not happened. [38 notes: TAB-FULL; IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس,الضحاك); SULAMI(القاسم); QUSHAYRI; WAHIDI-BASIT(النبي); KASHSHAF-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL(ابن مسعود,القاضي,داود الطائي); QURTUBI-FULL; BAYDAWI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL(الحرالي); DURR-FULL(أبو سعيد الخدري,ابن عباس,الضحاك,قتادة,مطرف بن عبد الله بن الشخير); ALUSI-FULL; ELMALILI; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 2:156/q13/p2 Where people cause the harm, the believer seeks God's justice while restraining anger and unlawful retaliation. — Jishumī and al-Qāḍī in Rāzī distinguish this from accepting a God-sent affliction as wise, good, and beneficial; the words commit the sufferer to God's just command in both cases. Al-Muntakhab in Abū Ḥayyān repeats the distinction; Ṭabrisī says God will repair just harm or secure justice against an unjust perpetrator. Rāzī cites 40:20 for God's right judgment. [10 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL(القاضي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(المنتخب); TABRISI]
- 2:156/q13/p3 The formula should be spoken for every harmful occurrence, including very small losses and remembered older ones. — The Prophet's extinguished lamp appears in Kashshāf, Ibn ʿAṭiyya, Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī, and Abū Ḥayyān; al-Qurṭubī and Ālūsī enumerate minor harms. The Commander of the Faithful, Ibn Mājah's report, and Ibn Kathīr say renewed istirjāʿ after a long time earns the original reward. [12 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(أمير المؤمنين); RAZI-FULL(النبي); QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNKATHIR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ALUSI-FULL(النبي); STUDYQURAN]

## 2:156/q14 Which parts of the response are obligatory or recommended?  [قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا لِلَّهِ وَإِنَّآ إِلَيْهِ رَٰجِعُونَ · law]
turns on: The notes address different prescribed acts: required submission, voluntary formula, and the added supplication.
- 2:156/q14/p1 Submission to God's command and decree, and continued duties, are obligatory; public verbal istirjāʿ is recommended. — Abū Bakr al-Rāzī distinguishes inner obedience from voluntary public utterance, which teaches others and displays steadfastness to disbelievers. Abū Ḥayyān repeats the distinction and says voicing it angers disbelievers by showing obedience and restraint. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL(أبو بكر الرازي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 2:156/q14/p2 After istirjāʿ, one should ask God for reward in the affliction and a better replacement. — Ālūsī expressly calls this recommended, citing Muslim's Umm Salama report. The reported prophetic instruction appears in Baghawī, Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr, and Rāzī. [6 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(أم سلمة); RAZI-FULL(أم سلمة); QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); IBNKATHIR-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); ALUSI-FULL(أم سلمة)]

## 2:156/q15 Why teach believers to voice this declaration?  [قَالُوٓا۟ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the stress falls on public teaching or the formula's consoling force.
- 2:156/q15/p1 Speech makes conviction vivid to the speaker and teaches witnesses to be steadfast. — Ibn ʿĀshūr calls it a taught motto that strengthens belief by making it perceptible; Abū Bakr al-Rāzī and Abū Ḥayyān say public recitation invites imitation and displays obedience to disbelievers. Jishumī likens it to reassuring the afflicted, 'I am yours.' [4 notes: JISHUMI; RAZI-FULL(أبو بكر الرازي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 2:156/q15/p2 Its wording intensifies acceptance and consoles the sufferer through God's just governance. — Jishumī says acknowledging an irresistible, just Lord prevents despair; Ṭabrisī says God repairs a deserved affliction or vindicates one unjustly harmed. [3 notes: JISHUMI; TABRISI]

## 2:156/q16 What rewards are promised to those who say it patiently?  [قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا لِلَّهِ وَإِنَّآ إِلَيْهِ رَٰجِعُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: The reports and commentators describe general blessings and specific forms of recompense.
- 2:156/q16/p1 They receive divine blessings or forgiveness, mercy, and guidance. — Ibn ʿAbbās lists all three from 2:157; Muqātil and Saʿīd b. Jubayr explain ṣalawāt as forgiveness, Muqātil comparing 9:103, while Saʿīd says mercy protects from punishment. ʿUmar calls blessing and mercy two equal portions with guidance as an extra topping. Al-Ṣādiq relates the three to those deprived of property by force; Biqāʿī says even minor calamities fall under the promise. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk connects eligibility with piety and fulfilling obligations. Ṭabrisī defines the promised guidance as attaining the true path. [15 notes: MUQATIL; IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس,الضحاك); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; RAZI-FULL(ابن عباس); QURTUBI-FULL(عمر); BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,الضحاك,سعيد بن جبير,قتادة); TABATABAI(الصادق عليه السلام,الله عز وجل); TABRISI]
- 2:156/q16/p2 God repairs the loss, gives a good outcome, and grants a pleasing righteous replacement. — Prophetic reports in Ibn Abī Ḥātim, Kashshāf, Ṭabrisī, Rāzī, and al-Durr agree in this threefold promise; Qurṭubī says the glad tidings can be replacement, abundant reward, or both, exemplified by Umm Salama. [6 notes: IBNABIHATIM(النبي); KASHSHAF-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); TABRISI(الحديث); RAZI-FULL(النبي); QURTUBI-FULL; DURR-FULL(رسول الله)]
- 2:156/q16/p3 Particular losses may earn Paradise, a House of Praise, or elevated ranks. — Abū Mūsā's report promises a House of Praise when a bereaved parent praises God and recites istirjāʿ; al-Tirmidhī calls it ḥasan gharīb and identifies Abū Sinān as ʿĪsā ibn Sinān. ## 2:156/q23 How do these words relate to the adjacent promises and tests?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the connection is read locally as patience and its reward or as a broader unit anticipating battle.
- 2:156/q23/p1 Verse 156 describes the patient promised glad tidings in 2:155 and prepares for the blessing, mercy, and guidance named in 2:157. — Rāzī says it explains how patience is practiced; Ibn al-Jawzī says forewarning makes despair less likely. Qurṭubī uses Umm Salama's replacement as an example of glad tidings. Ālūsī reads the omitted object of 'give glad tidings' as immense mercy and beneficence, while Bayḍāwī notes the omission. Biqāʿī distinguishes patience grounded in God, which earns divine companionship in 2:153, from lesser patience awarded safety and reward. Kuryolu says 2:155 renews patience and 2:156 specifies faith and submission; Study Quran links the formula with loss of souls. - 2:156/q23/p2 The five-verse unit leads from warning of battle through patient trust to divine reward and was revealed together. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī reads 2:153–157 as a connected unit, placed before jihad legislation; death in God's path is called life, while prayer and patience preserve resolve amid collective conflict. [6 notes: TABATABAI]

## 2:156/q24 What practice is associated with the formula in Muslim usage?  [قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا لِلَّهِ وَإِنَّآ إِلَيْهِ رَٰجِعُونَ · reports]
- 2:156/q24/p1 Muslims commonly recite it when they learn of someone's death. — Study Quran notes this established usage and relates it to the prior verse's loss of lives. [2 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 2:156/q25 How can reflection on calamity turn the heart toward God?  [مُّصِيبَةٌۭ وَإِنَّآ إِلَيْهِ رَٰجِعُونَ · inward]
turns on: The reflective paths differ in whether attachment is lost, its object disappears, God supplies something better, or the self witnesses divine action.
- 2:156/q25/p1 Self-directed reflection makes loss of an attachment redirect the heart from created things to God's remembrance. — Rāzī contrasts effort (taṣarruf) and divine attraction (jadhb). Adam lost the Garden to which he was attached; Jacob was separated from Joseph, his comfort. Rāzī says Muhammad's hoped-for Meccan supporters became his fiercest opponents and cites, without grading, a report that no prophet suffered more. Dawūd al-Ṭāʾī defines asceticism as not wishing to remain in the world and contentment as the best deed. [5 notes: RAZI-FULL(النبي,داود الطائي)]
- 2:156/q25/p2 The object of attachment may be removed altogether, leaving neither affliction nor favor and returning the servant to God. — Rāzī gives this as a second path after the attachment examples. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 2:156/q25/p3 God may give something better than hoped for, causing shame and a return to His mercy. — Rāzī gives this as the third path. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]
- 2:156/q25/p4 One may witness divine disposal and the lights of God's attributes through the ordeal. — Qāshānī describes surrender and self-effacement; Qushayrī sees patience deepen into gratitude, joy, and freedom from choice. [5 notes: QASHANI; QUSHAYRI]

## 2:156/q26 Why recall the Prophet's calamity amid one's own loss?  [مُّصِيبَةٌۭ · reports]
- 2:156/q26/p1 His loss was the greatest calamity for Muslims, so recalling it consoles lesser losses. — Al-Qurṭubī reports an instruction through Fiṭr ibn Khalīfa and ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Rabāḥ. He cites Abū ʿUmar: revelation ended, prophethood passed, Arab apostasy first appeared, and good diminished; Abū Saʿīd said their hearts had changed before dust was brushed from the grave. The continuation of Abū al-ʿAtāhiya's poem says calamities are universal and urges remembering Muhammad. [4 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(أبو سعيد,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 2:156/q27 How does this passage ground moral character and patience?  [* · theology]
turns on: The notes distinguish training through social outcomes, promised afterlife recompense, and knowledge of God's sole ownership.
- 2:156/q27/p1 The verse's pure monotheism gives an inward knowledge that removes the root of grief by recognizing God's sole ownership. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī calls this a third, distinct ethical path, with real effects beyond social approval or duty and recompense. He cites 20:8, 6:102, 32:7, 20:111, 30:26, 17:23, 41:53–54, and 53:42; self, powers, and acts depend on God. Kuryolu relates steadfast faith and surrender to dignity before enemies. [8 notes: TABATABAI; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]
- 2:156/q27/p2 Repeated fitting acts can establish virtues; social praise and worldly outcomes motivate the first ethical method. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī says practiced courage overcomes cowardice and identifies this path with Greek and other ethical writers: restraint wins esteem, greed brings humiliation, and learning and justice yield respect. He notes that the Qur'an does not rest moral choice on society's praise. [3 notes: TABATABAI]
- 2:156/q27/p3 Afterlife reward and punishment provide a second method of moral training. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī says Qur'anic promises of Paradise, measureless reward, and painful punishment use this path, citing 9:111, 39:10, 14:22, and 2:257. [1 notes: TABATABAI]

## 2:156/q28 Are virtues shared across societies despite disputes over their application?  [* · theology]
turns on: Whether morality rests on stable human nature or on each society's chosen plan.
- 2:156/q28/p1 Yes; societies generally value justice, temperance, courage, and wisdom, while classifying particular cases differently. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī locates modesty, protective indignation, contentment, and humility under these virtues. Autocrats counted a king's absolute power as a right; medieval French opinion reportedly deemed scholarship unsuitable to kings' duties. Societies with differing sexual or gender norms still praise fair rulers, modest observance of law, defense of sanctities, legal contentment, and deference to guides. He argues judgments ultimately rest on stable human nature, not a variable social plan. [8 notes: TABATABAI]
- 2:156/q28/p2 Moral goodness depends on each society's adopted social goal. — Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports this claim but rejects its confusion of natural human society with a hypothetical plan; contradictory plans would leave force rather than shared reasoning to govern. [0 notes: TABATABAI-]

## 2:156/q29 What do the cited reports say about collecting the Qur'an after Yamāma?  [* · sciences]
turns on: The accounts assign initiative or first collection to different people and vary in chain strength and meaning of 'gathering'.
- 2:156/q29/p1 Abū Bakr, prompted by ʿUmar after many reciters died at Yamāma, commissioned Zayd to collect the Qur'an. — Ibn Kathīr calls Hishām's report from his father sound and describes initial hesitation by Abū Bakr and Zayd. He says roughly three thousand reciters rallied by invoking the Companions of al-Baqara, while about five hundred reciters died, making written preservation urgent. Al-Suyūṭī says ʿUmar required two witnesses to confirm oral reception even with a written copy. [6 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبوه); ITQAN(الحسن)]
- 2:156/q29/p2 ʿUmar was the first collector or the first to order collection. — Al-Ḥasan's report says he learned a verse bearer died at Yamāma and ordered collection; al-Suyūṭī explains 'first' as first to propose it and calls the chain disconnected. [2 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL(عمر بن الخطاب); ITQAN(الحسن)]
- 2:156/q29/p3 Ali stayed home after Abū Bakr's pledge to collect a copy in revealed order. — ʿIkrima says Ali feared additions, Abū Bakr approved, and Ali vowed to wear his cloak only for prayer until done; ʿIkrima says humans and jinn could not reproduce its revealed order. Ibn Sīrīn's report says his copy contained abrogating and abrogated material but could not be obtained in Medina. Ibn Ḥajar calls a related interrupted report weak and explains 'gathering' as memorization if sound, preferring the report from ʿAbd Khayr. [4 notes: ITQAN(ابن حجر,عكرمة,محمد بن سيرين)]
- 2:156/q29/p4 A disconnected report names Sālim, freedman of Abū Ḥudhayfa, as first collector. — It says muṣḥaf was chosen over the Jewish term sifr; al-Suyūṭī interprets Sālim as helping at Abū Bakr's order. [1 notes: ITQAN(ابن بريدة)]

## 2:156/q31 Does truncation to one letter occur in the Qur'an?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether disconnected letters count as abbreviated divine names.
- 2:156/q31/p1 Yes; a report treats disconnected letters as abbreviations of divine names. — Al-Zarkashī defines iqtitāʿ as retaining one letter and dropping the rest, rejecting al-Mathal al-sāʾir's denial.
