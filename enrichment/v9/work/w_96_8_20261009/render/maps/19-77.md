# 19:77: 24 questions

## 19:77/q01 How is the hamza of araʾayta read?  [أَفَرَءَيْتَ · readings]
turns on: Whether interrogative رأيت retains, eases, elides, or prolongs its hamza.
- 19:77/q01/p1 The hamza is omitted or eased. — Al-Farrāʾ reports an omitted-hamza reading. Majāz cites Abū al-Aswad’s أريت as evidence; Qirāʾāt-ER says easing lightens pronunciation without changing the grammar. [4 notes: FARRA; MAJAZ(بعضهم); QIRAAT-ER]
- 19:77/q01/p2 The hamza is fully pronounced. — Majāz says some preserve interrogative رأيت unchanged and cites al-Mutawakkil al-Laythī’s أرأيت. [2 notes: MAJAZ(المتوكل الليثي,بعضهم)]
- 19:77/q01/p3 The hamza has an intermediate sound. — Qirāʾāt-ER describes a sound between full realization and elision, reflecting early dialectal variation. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER]
- 19:77/q01/p4 The hamza is prolonged into a long ā. — Qirāʾāt-ER says the prolongation creates an emphatic pause before the relative clause. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER]
- 19:77/q01/p5 A split pause-and-continuation form is transmitted. — Qirāʾāt-ER takes the paired forms as evidence that the hamza was a live phonological question. [1 notes: QIRAAT-ER]

## 19:77/q02 What does afaraʾayta ask the hearer to do?  [أَفَرَءَيْتَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether seeing functions as an idiom for reporting or an invitation to marvel.
- 19:77/q02/p1 It means 'tell me' or 'report to me'. — Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Nasafī explain it as report; Abū Ḥayyān says seeing yields knowledge and trustworthy report. Al-Burhān says the verb no longer denotes literal seeing and is grammatically transitive. Al-Ālūsī reports this as cause named for result; Abū al-Suʿūd objects to making it an imperative addressed to another person. [6 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURHAN; ALUSI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(أبو السعود)-]
- 19:77/q02/p2 It invites seeing or knowledge of an astonishing claim. — Al-Ṭabrisī, al-Biqāʿī, al-Bursawī, al-Ālūsī, and Ibn ʿĀshūr stress amazement; Ibn ʿĀshūr says knowing an extraordinary report is likened to sight. Abū al-Suʿūd prefers this to figurative 'tell me'. Iṣlāḥī hears the introduction as marking foolish grandiosity. [7 notes: TABRISI; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ALUSI-FULL(أبو السعود)+]

## 19:77/q03 How does the initial interrogative hamza behave?  [أَفَرَءَيْتَ · grammar]
- 19:77/q03/p1 It is the original initial question particle, with broader placement and rhetorical uses than hal. — Al-Burhān says it may precede إن, conjunctions, and other interrogatives; هل cannot take the same place and is more tied to verbs. Hamza may also convey denial, affirmation, or report; hamza with لم can remind or warn. Its treatment of أم distinguishes connected and disconnected forms. [6 notes: BURHAN]

## 19:77/q04 Must interrogative hamza imply an alternative?  [أَفَرَءَيْتَ · grammar]
turns on: Whether implicit alternatives are distinctive to hamza questions.
- 19:77/q04/p1 Every hamza question balances an expressed or implicit alternative. — Ibn al-Ṭarāwa asserts this as reported in al-Burhān. [1 notes: BURHAN(ابن الطراوة); BURHAN(الصفار)-]
- 19:77/q04/p2 No special alternative is required. — Al-Ṣaffār argues that every interrogative asks for certainty, including هل قام زيد, and that hamza questions without expressed alternatives occur, citing 19:77, 53:33, and 53:19; al-Burhān also cites 23:115. [2 notes: BURHAN(ابن الطراوة); BURHAN(الصفار)+]

## 19:77/q05 How is araʾayta constructed syntactically?  [أَفَرَءَيْتَ ٱلَّذِى · grammar]
- 19:77/q05/p1 The relative clause is its first object and the following question its second. — Al-Samīn gives this parsing. Abū Ḥayyān follows Sībawayh’s one direct object plus interrogative second object and reconciles other 'tell me' constructions with it. Al-Burhān says the idiom is grammatically transitive even if its sense implies a preposition. [3 notes: SAMIN-DURR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURHAN]

## 19:77/q06 Whom does the opening address?  [أَفَرَءَيْتَ · referent]
turns on: Whether the second-person form has a general or particular addressee.
- 19:77/q06/p1 Any hearer fit to consider the example. — Ibn ʿĀshūr treats the address as generally applicable. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 19:77/q06/p2 It may address the Prophet specifically. — Ibn ʿĀshūr allows this narrower addressee. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 19:77/q08 What are the signs he disbelieved in?  [بِـَٔايَٰتِنَا · referent]
turns on: Whether āyāt is confined to revealed verses or includes further proofs.
- 19:77/q08/p1 They are the verses of the Qurʾān, especially its resurrection passages. — Muqātil names Qurʾānic verses; Ibn ʿĀshūr includes passages about resurrection. Abū Ḥayyān includes the Qurʾān and resurrection proofs. [3 notes: MUQATIL; IBNASHUR-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 19:77/q08/p2 They include God’s proofs beyond the Qurʾān. — Al-Ṭabrisī explains the signs as Qurʾānic or other divine proofs. [1 notes: TABRISI]

## 19:77/q09 How does the fāʾ link this opening to what precedes?  [أَفَرَءَيْتَ · links]
turns on: Whether the immediate antecedent is the previous story, the worldly-status boast, or the resurrection denial.
- 19:77/q09/p1 It introduces this disbeliever's story after the previous story. — Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, and al-Samīn take the fāʾ as a sequence marker. [4 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR]
- 19:77/q09/p2 It links to the prior boast about the better gathering and worldly standing. — Al-Ālūsī says this is the more apparent context, comparing 19:73; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī links the same boast to trust in pagan prosperity. [2 notes: TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 19:77/q09/p3 It branches from the earlier resurrection denial. — Ibn ʿĀshūr points to 19:66; al-Rāzī says the passage resumes the deniers’ mockery after arguments for resurrection. Al-Biqāʿī also sees the resurrection context; al-Ālūsī allows it as an alternative. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL; RAZI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]

## 19:77/q10 What does the wording of la-ūtayanna assert?  [لَأُوتَيَنَّ · grammar]
- 19:77/q10/p1 It means 'I shall certainly be given' and answers an implicit oath. — Al-Thaʿlabī and al-Wāḥidī give the passive sense; al-Wāḥidī, al-Nasafī, and al-Samīn identify an implied oath. Al-Samīn makes the oath clause the complement of qāla. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī adds that the oath lām and emphatic nūn make the claim forceful. [6 notes: THALABI; WAHIDI-BASIT; NASAFI-FULL; SAMIN-DURR; TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL]

## 19:77/q12 Is the boast mockery or a sincere belief?  [وَقَالَ لَأُوتَيَنَّ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the emphatic wording and ensuing challenge answer a taunt or a seriously held expectation.
- 19:77/q12/p1 It is a mocking taunt against resurrection or Paradise. — Muqātil says al-ʿĀṣ did not believe promised reward and punishment. Al-Kalbī, Muqātil, and most debt reports depict a deferred-payment taunt; Iṣlāḥī says the reply is sarcastic because the boast is childish. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī argues the later serious challenge cannot answer mere mockery. [25 notes: MUQATIL(العاص); YAHYA-SALLAM(قتادة); TAB-FULL(ابن عباس,قتادة); ZAJJAJ(خباب); THALABI(الكلبي ومقاتل); MAWARDI-FULL(الكلبي); WAHIDI-ASBAB(الكلبي ومقاتل); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس وجماعة أهل التفسير,مقاتل والكلبي); WAHIDI-WAJIZ; JISHUMI(مقاتل والكلبي); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(العاصي بن وائل); TABRISI(الكلبي); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الكلبي ومقاتل,الماوردي); BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; STUDYQURAN(Ṭ, Ṭs, W); TABATABAI-]
- 19:77/q12/p2 It is a serious, emphatic claim that disbelief brings wealth and power. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites the oath, unconditional promise, and following question about unseen knowledge or covenant. He says children make little sense in a Paradise-debt taunt because early Muslims were not known to teach procreation there. [7 notes: TABATABAI; TABATABAI+]
- 19:77/q12/p3 He expresses overconfident entitlement to wealth and leadership. — Iṣlāḥī says he treats blessings as personal competence or inherited right and projects that status into the afterlife. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 19:77/q14 What does walad or wuld denote?  [وَوَلَدًا · meaning]
turns on: Whether vowel differences mark synonyms, number, collective force, or a wider kinship sense.
- 19:77/q14/p1 The forms can mean the same thing: offspring, whether one child or children. — Al-Ṭabarī, al-Farrāʾ, al-Zajjāj, and others cite poetry, paired dialect forms, and a proverb for equivalence. Al-Layth says walad covers singular or collective and either sex; Ibn al-Sikkīt allows one or several. Ibn Kathīr says both readings share a meaning. [16 notes: ZAJJAJ; FARISI-HUJJA(الفراء); WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو علي); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL(أبو علي,بعضهم); RAZI-FULL(حمزة والكسائي); QURTUBI-FULL(حمزة والكسائي,قيس); NASAFI-FULL(حمزة وعلي); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الأعمش وطلحة والكسائي وابن أبي ليلى وابن عيسى الأصبهاني); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(حمزة والكسائي والأعمش وطلحة وابن أبي ليلى وابن عيسى الأصبهاني); QIRAAT-ER]
- 19:77/q14/p3 Walad is collective and wuld refers to one child in a Qays usage. — Al-Ṭabarī reports Qays distinguishing the forms; al-Wāḥidī and al-Jishumī describe the fatḥa form as collective and the rounded form as singular. - 19:77/q14/p5 Walad means a son or daughter; wuld can include one's family and children. — Abū al-Ḥasan, as cited by al-Fārisī, and al-Akhfash, as cited by Ibn ʿAṭiyya, make this distinction; the latter also says family or parent. [2 notes: FARISI-HUJJA(أبو الحسن); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الأخفش)]

## 19:77/q15 What related uses clarify the root of walad?  [وَوَلَدًا · meaning]
turns on: Which lexical derivation or related usage is brought to bear.
- 19:77/q15/p1 Birth-related words describe where a person was born or raised. — Al-Jishumī contrasts a foreign-born woman raised among Arabs with one born in Muslim lands. Al-Qutaybī defines talīda as born abroad and raised in Arab lands; Ibn Shumayl says talīd and mawlūd may both denote those born in one's household. [3 notes: JISHUMI(ابن شميل,القتبي)]
- 19:77/q15/p2 Another proposed derivation invokes secure upbringing; mawlid can mean newly coined speech. — Al-Jishumī reports this etymology and extended usage. [1 notes: JISHUMI]

## 19:77/q16 What is the significance of pairing wealth and children?  [مَالًۭا وَوَلَدًا · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the pairing chiefly signals goods, trials, or presumptuous control of reward.
- 19:77/q16/p1 They are emblematic worldly goods and can also be trials. — Al-Fārisī cites Qurʾānic pairings of wealth with sons or children at 18:46, 64:15, and 64:14. Study Quran adds 9:69 and 17:6 and stresses the trial side. Asad reads the passage as criticism of material values divorced from morals; Iṣlāḥī adds inherited entitlement. [4 notes: FARISI-HUJJA; STUDYQURAN; ASAD-NOTES; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 19:77/q16/p2 The claim wrongly treats a powerless creature as able to secure gifts from the All-Powerful. — Al-Biqāʿī says the Hereafter boast first denies the Almighty’s power and then attributes power to the creature. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 19:77/q17 What does the passage teach about boastful claims and divine response?  [* · theology]
turns on: How the boast's lack of warrant contrasts with covenant, divine power, and enduring goods.
- 19:77/q17/p1 God reproaches and threatens the boastful speaker; his supposed assurance lacks unseen knowledge or covenant. — Al-Jishumī calls it reproach and threat. The Bukhārī versions say revelation rebuts the claim, with 19:80 foretelling punishment and a solitary return. Iṣlāḥī says the reply is disparaging. [6 notes: JISHUMI; BUKHARI(خباب); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; TABATABAI(الأكثر)]
- 19:77/q17/p2 A believer may hope for fulfillment because believers have a covenant with God. — Al-Qushayrī draws this implication and says God does not break His covenant. [1 notes: QUSHAYRI]
- 19:77/q17/p3 Worldly material success cannot establish moral or religious worth. — Asad says the passage criticizes equating wealth and children with success while leaving moral concerns aside; Abū Ḥayyān contrasts transient luxury with enduring good deeds. [2 notes: ASAD-NOTES; ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 19:77/q18 How does this verse connect with the neighboring argument?  [* · links]
turns on: Which adjacent theme carries the narrative forward.
- 19:77/q18/p1 It resumes the resurrection denier's delusion and mockery. — Al-Rāzī says resurrection was established and deniers answered before this example. Ibn ʿĀshūr ties it to 19:66, and al-Biqāʿī says the consequences of the Day have just been depicted. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 19:77/q18/p2 It continues the preceding contest over worldly status and better gatherings. — Al-Ālūsī prefers the link with 19:73 over 19:66; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī sees the same conviction that paganism brings worldly success. Asad relates the criticism to 19:73–75. [4 notes: TABATABAI; ASAD-NOTES; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 19:77/q18/p3 The narrative contrasts worldly boasting with increased guidance and lasting good deeds. — Abū Ḥayyān points to 18:46 as a parallel for enduring goods replacing transient luxury. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 19:77/q19 How does the boast connect to the later 'until they see what is promised'?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the later time clause closes the boast across intervening text or belongs to the nearer contrast.
- 19:77/q19/p1 The unbelievers' worldly taunt continues until victory or Judgment reveals their worse standing. — Al-Zamakhsharī links the boast to that endpoint. Abū Ḥayyān calls it very remote because of a long interval and two parenthetical sentences, citing Abū ʿAlī’s syntactic objection. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); ABUHAYYAN-FULL-]
- 19:77/q19/p2 The later clause contrasts their better station and assembly with rivals worse in place and forces. — Al-Zamakhsharī’s other construction takes the seen object as promised punishment or the Hour. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري)]

## 19:77/q20 What event prompted the revelation?  [* · reports]

## 19:77/q21 How are the Khabbāb occasion reports evaluated?  [* · reports]
turns on: Whether the transmitted occasion or the contextual force of the ensuing argument governs its use.
- 19:77/q21/p1 The Khabbāb account is well established and fits the verse's explicit wording. — Al-Wāḥidī and al-Qurṭubī cite al-Bukhārī and Muslim; al-Ṭabrisī calls it sound, al-Tirmidhī grades a route ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ, and al-Ālūsī invokes the canonical collections. Al-Qurṭubī prefers the afterlife sense on this basis. ## 19:77/q22 What role may Arabic poetry play in explaining Quranic wording?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether poetry is admitted as linguistic evidence or thought to challenge the text.
- 19:77/q22/p1 Arabic poetry provides evidence for Quranic vocabulary and usage. — Ibn ʿĀshūr reports ʿUmar urging consultation of Arab poetry, Ibn ʿAbbās calling it the Arabs’ register and citing it for obscure words, and ʿIkrima citing a line for zanīm. He recounts Ibn al-Aʿrābī answering Ibn al-Rāwandī’s challenge about 'garment of piety' through Arabic usage. [4 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(ابن عباس,عكرمة,عمر)]
- 19:77/q22/p2 A report from Aḥmad disapproves of explaining the Qurʾān with a poetic line. — Ibn ʿĀshūr calls the reported response puzzling; if authentic, he thinks it may reject poetry used to challenge Quranic wording, as some unbelievers did. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL(أحمد بن حنبل)]

## 19:77/q23 How much of the Qurʾān did the Prophet explain directly?  [* · sciences]
- 19:77/q23/p1 Direct Prophetic explanations are relatively few. — Ibn ʿĀshūr says Ibn ʿAṭiyya’s report from ʿĀʾisha about limited explanation concerns unseen matters and generalities requiring revelation. [1 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 19:77/q24 How does the following kallā answer the boast?  [* · links]
- 19:77/q24/p1 It rebukes the claimant and warns him to stop imagining this reward. — Al-Zamakhsharī says kallā means he is mistaken in his wish and must desist. [1 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL]
