# 27:29: 17 questions

## 27:29/q01 Who is the woman speaking, and what is her background?  [قَالَتْ · referent]
turns on: The reports give different genealogical and biographical detail.
- 27:29/q01/p1 She is Bilqis, an Arab queen of the Himyarite people of Tubbaʿ. — Muqatil names Tubbaʿ ibn Abi Sharahil and says her people were Arabs. Wahidi repeats Muqatil. Kashshaf calls her Arabic-speaking, literate, and descended from Tubbaʿ ibn Sharahil; Biqai calls her literate and of Tubbaʿ. Alusi also gives descent from Yaʿrub ibn Qahtan and Tubbaʿ, describes the refined, separately lettered Himyari musnad script and its restricted teaching, and reports Ibn Khaldun's view that Arabic writing passed from Tubbaʿ and Himyar through Hira to the Hijaz. Names and genealogical wording vary. [7 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); KASHSHAF-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(ابن خلدون)]
- 27:29/q01/p2 She is Bilqis daughter of Sharāḥbīl, a Yemeni woman from a royal family who took rule after her people died. — Yahya transmits this from Qatada. [1 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(قتادة)]

## 27:29/q02 How did the queen address and involve her court?  [قَالَتْ يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلْمَلَؤُا۟ · interpretation]
turns on: The notes describe either the act of summoning and consulting, or the manner in which she spoke.
- 27:29/q02/p1 She gathered or summoned her leading people and told them about the letter, seeking their counsel. — Muqatil says she addressed her nobles. Zuhayr says she addressed her people. Yazid ibn Ruman says a letter unlike earlier kings' letters led her to summon Yemen's leaders. Ibn Atiyya says the chiefs represented everyone. Tabrisi says she called the nobles the assembly. Abu Hayyan says she took the letter and summoned leaders. Biqai allows either her people generally or only notables. Alusi connects the consultation to the next verse. Ibn Kathir says she opened and read the letter first. [9 notes: MUQATIL; IBNABIHATIM(زهير بن محمد,يزيد بن رومان); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 27:29/q02/p2 Her address conveyed submission, fear, or apprehension at the letter. — Mujahid says she sensed its nobility and was apprehensive; Nasafi describes a submissive, fearful manner. Baghawi and Kashshaf say its seal made her tremble and submit. Ibn Kathir says she was astonished and alarmed. [5 notes: IBNABIHATIM(مجاهد); NASAFI-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL]

## 27:29/q03 Who are the malāʾ?  [ٱلْمَلَؤُا۟ · meaning]
- 27:29/q03/p1 They are the leading and eminent people of her community, its dignitaries or representatives. — Baghawi calls them leading and eminent; Ibn Atiyya says they represent the rest. Bursevi says they fill eyes with awe and hearts with majesty and gives the plural أملاء. Tabrisi calls them nobles. [4 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL; IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; BURSEVI]

## 27:29/q04 How large was the queen's council or command structure?  [ٱلْمَلَؤُا۟ · reports]
turns on: The transmitted numbers of rulers or chiefs and men under each differ.
- 27:29/q04/p1 It had 313 members, each commanding ten thousand men. — Thaʿlabi attributes this to Qatada, Muqatil, and al-Thumali; Baghawi to Qatada and Muqatil. Bursevi also gives 313 as one reported count. [3 notes: THALABI(قتادة ومقاتل والثمالي); BAGHAWI-FULL(قتادة ومقاتل); BURSEVI]
- 27:29/q04/p2 It had twelve thousand commanders, each commanding one hundred thousand fighters. — Baghawi gives this account while describing the hoopoe waiting near her throne; Bursevi gives twelve thousand as another count, without the troop figure. [2 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL; BURSEVI]
- 27:29/q04/p3 She had one thousand chiefs, each commanding one hundred thousand. — Ibn Abi Hatim transmits this from Ibn Abbas. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس)]
- 27:29/q04/p4 She had one hundred thousand subordinate kings, each with one hundred thousand men. — Baghawi transmits this from Ibn Abbas and explains that a qil is a king under a supreme monarch. [1 notes: BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن عباس)]
- 27:29/q04/p5 She gathered 310 rulers. — Tabrisi gives this count. [1 notes: TABRISI]

## 27:29/q05 How is the yāʾ of innī read here?  [إِنِّىٓ · readings]
- 27:29/q05/p1 Nāfiʿ and Abū Jaʿfar, the two Medinan readers, pronounce the final yāʾ open. — Nasafi calls it the Medinan reading. Nashr lists this among ten loci, with 11:54 and other listed verses. Ibn Mujahid counts 27 possessive yāʾs in the sūra, six disputed; Nashr also discusses a class with ḍamma followed by kasra and its agreed and disputed instances. [5 notes: NASAFI-FULL; NASHR(المدنيان,نافع، أبو جعفر); IBNMUJAHID]

## 27:29/q06 Why does she open with the emphatic innī?  [إِنِّىٓ · rhetoric]
- 27:29/q06/p1 It marks the importance of what she is about to report. — Alusi says the emphatic opening signals the importance of the judgment. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]

## 27:29/q07 What is conveyed by the passive ulqiya, with no deliverer named?  [أُلْقِىَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the passive highlights an extraordinary delivery, leaves an unknown or unimportant bird unmentioned, or serves the queen's political presentation.
- 27:29/q07/p1 It presents the letter's arrival as strange and unexpected. — Biqai stresses the oddity of a letter cast to her; Ibn Kathir says she saw a feat beyond any king's power when a bird brought the letter and withdrew politely. [3 notes: BIQAI-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL]
- 27:29/q07/p2 The agent is omitted because she did not know who cast it, or considered the bird insignificant or irrelevant. — Abu Hayyan suggests ignorance or deliberate belittling if she saw the bird. Alusi adds that the agent could simply be unimportant. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL; ALUSI-FULL]
- 27:29/q07/p3 She withheld the deliverer's identity as a political display of access to hidden channels. — Alusi reports Ibn Arabi in the Fusus: the omission made her court think she could receive things by unknown means and gave her precedence. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الشيخ الأكبر)]

## 27:29/q08 How was the letter delivered to her?  [أُلْقِىَ إِلَىَّ كِتَٰبٌۭ · reports]
turns on: Reports differ over whether she slept or sat in court, how the bird gained access or her attention, and where the letter landed.
- 27:29/q08/p1 The hoopoe entered her locked sleeping chamber through an opening and put the letter on her chest or between her breasts. — Qatada says she locked the doors with keys beneath her head and found the letter on her belly or between her breasts. Kashshaf places the chamber at Maʾrib and puts the letter on her chest. Razi, Tabrisi, Ibn Jawzi, Durr and Alusi give the sleeping chest version; Alusi says Solomon perfumed and sealed it. The precise resting place varies. [8 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(قتادة); KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; TABRISI(قتادة); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(قتادة); DURR-FULL(قتادة); ALUSI-FULL(رُوي,قيل)]
- 27:29/q08/p2 The hoopoe dropped it into her lap while she sat among her court or looked up at it. — Al-Suddi says it fell from high above into her lap, and she feared it might be from God. Wahidi's Muqatil says the bird flapped while people watched and dropped it when she raised her head. Kashshaf and Alusi describe assembled leaders and soldiers; Ibn Jawzi says the bird carried it in its beak and its seal frightened her and her troops. [5 notes: IBNABIHATIM(السدي); WAHIDI-BASIT(مقاتل); KASHSHAF-FULL; IBNJAWZI-ZAD(مقاتل); ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
- 27:29/q08/p3 The hoopoe cast it to her at her throne, where it was read or read aloud. — Al-Dahhak says it was cast opposite her throne. Ibn Abbas says it landed at the throne and was read aloud, identifying Solomon and the basmala. Baghawi says the hoopoe waited near the throne while she summoned her council. [3 notes: TAB-FULL(الضحاك); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL]
- 27:29/q08/p4 It was dropped on her neck while she slept or reclined. — Wahidi attributes the sleeping neck version to Qatada; Baydawi describes the bird entering through a locked house's window and dropping it on her neck. [2 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(قتادة); BAYDAWI-FULL]
- 27:29/q08/p5 The hoopoe blocked the sun at her worship window, drawing her attention before casting the letter. — Tabrisi attributes the account to Wahb and Ibn Zayd: she used a sun-facing window to prostrate at sunrise. Alusi also reports it. [3 notes: TABRISI(وهب وابن زيد); ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
- 27:29/q08/p6 The hoopoe pecked her awake and frightened her before delivery. — Kashshaf, Razi, and Alusi preserve this alternative. [3 notes: KASHSHAF-FULL; RAZI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]

## 27:29/q09 What intervening action does the narrative leave unstated?  [قَالَتْ أُلْقِىَ إِلَىَّ · links]
- 27:29/q09/p1 The hoopoe took and delivered Solomon's letter; the queen received or read it and then addressed her council. — Zajjaj and Wahidi say the context supplies the journey and delivery. Ibn Atiyya and Tabatabai add her reading and assembly. Tabrisi, Razi, Qurtubi, Burhan and Alusi similarly infer the messenger's delivery; Alusi says omission conveys prompt obedience. Samarrai cites this as understood ellipsis and compares an explicative fā after omitted steps in other stories. Kuran Yolu says Solomon sent it with the hoopoe to verify its report and ordered it to return with her answer. [12 notes: ZAJJAJ; WAHIDI-BASIT(أبو إسحاق); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BURHAN; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; SAMARRAI-ASRAR-MUH; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR]

## 27:29/q10 In what language was Solomon's letter, and how could the queen read it?  [كِتَٰبٌۭ · reports]
turns on: Whether Solomon inferred Saba's language from the hoopoe's report, and whether Bilqis read the script herself or needed a translator.
- 27:29/q10/p1 It was in the land's language, likely Arabic, and the literate queen could read it. — Alusi notes that the hoopoe identified Saba as Arab, so Solomon could infer its queen was Arab. He says her apparent Arab identity and reading favor Arabic; Solomon may have known it or used a multilingual interpreter. Kashshaf describes her as Arabic-speaking and literate; Baghawi says she could read. Alusi reports her descent and the Himyari musnad writing tradition. [7 notes: ALUSI-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; BAGHAWI-FULL]
- 27:29/q10/p2 It was in Solomon's own language and script, and a translator informed her of its contents. — Alusi reports this possibility and says a translator belongs among a king's needs. He favors a non-Arabic letter because an Arabic one would imply Solomon already knew her circumstances; he also records the counterargument that the hoopoe's report identified Saba as Arab. [3 notes: ALUSI-FULL; ALUSI-FULL+; ALUSI-FULL-]

## 27:29/q11 Did she recite the letter's text or summarize it?  [كِتَٰبٌۭ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the ensuing words preserve the letter's concise wording and order or give the queen's account of its sense.
- 27:29/q11/p1 Her account reproduces a concise letter: sender and address, basmala, then the demand to abandon arrogance and submit. — Ibn Atiyya describes its eloquent sequence. Kashshaf gives a fuller reported address from Solomon son of David to Bilqis, a greeting to those following guidance, and the demand, and says prophetic letters were brief, perfumed with musk, and sealed. Islahi allows that the ancient letter may have contained only the words she reported, comparing Muhammad's concise letters. Itqan, citing al-Tibi, calls the heading, content, and request compressed into few words; it also reports the view that equal wording and meaning counts as concision. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ITQAN(الطيبي)]
- 27:29/q11/p2 She summarized the letter's meaning, identifying Solomon and its opening rather than reading every word aloud. — Ibn Atiyya offers summary as an alternative to verbatim sequence. Islahi says ancient royal letters and decrees conventionally began with sender and recipient. Abu Hayyan sees movement from an unspecified report to identification of sender and content; Kashshaf says the basmala resumes and explains the message. [6 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; KASHSHAF-FULL]

## 27:29/q12 Why did she call the letter karīm?  [كِتَٰبٌۭ كَرِيمٌ · interpretation]

- 27:29/q12/p1 It was sealed, a seal being the honor and completion of a royal letter. — Farra says he was told this; Zuhayr says kings seal letters and exchange none unsealed. Reports attributed to the Prophet say a letter's honor is its seal; Ibn Abbas versions cite this verse. Anas says non-Arabs accepted only sealed letters, so Muhammad adopted a silver signet engraved Muhammad, Messenger of God; a related account assigns the adoption to Solomon. Ibn al-Muqaffa calls an unsealed letter to a friend contempt; Bursevi cites a warning that an unsealed letter is defeated and a view that Solomon's miraculous ring showed sovereignty and frightened her. Baghawi and Kashshaf say Bilqis trembled on seeing the seal. Sulami also describes ornamentation and title. Tabatabai calls this conjectural. [46 notes: FARRA; IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; IBNQUTAYBA-MUSHKIL; SULAMI; THALABI(أنس,ابن المقفع,الضحاك,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم); MAWARDI-FULL(السدي); WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس والضحاك,النبي); IBNABIHATIM(زهير بن محمد); BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن عباس,عطاء والضحاك); KASHSHAF-FULL(ابن المقفع); IBNATIYYA-FULL(رسول الله); TABRISI(ابن عباس); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL(النبي); BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم,قيل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الحديث); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,زهير بن محمد); BURSEVI(أنس,ابن عباس,الأسئلة المقحمة,النبي عليه السلام); ALUSI-FULL(ابن المقنع,ابن عباس وقتادة وزهير بن محمد,حديث); STUDYQURAN(Q, Ṭ); TABATABAI(بعضهم)-]
- 27:29/q12/p2 Its contents, wording, or purpose were good, fine, gentle, or excellent. — Muqatil calls it fine; al-Suddi and Qatada say its contents were good. Zajjaj and Wahidi prefer this sense, comparing a pleasant or honorable station in 26:58 and 44:26. Qurtubi prefers the gentle exhortation to worship God, tact, and absence of insult or obscurity, relating 16:125 and 20:44; Abu Hayyan likewise stresses a mild tone. Tabrisi includes elegant handwriting, wording, and eloquence; Nasafi also mentions handsome binding. Ibn Jawzi al-Dimashqi calls it awe-inspiring. Elmalili says it merited reverence; Ibn Jawzi Nuzha lists excellence or superiority among Quranic senses, and Damghani lists excellence, forbearance, and worth in one's estimation. Qurtubi calls karīm the highest praise of a letter, as of the Qur'an in 56:77. Bilqis had read it before council, says Farra. Islahi cites the Torah, Talmud and later Quranic verses for her prior esteem of Solomon. Biqai also praises the sender and messenger. ## 27:29/q13 What consequence or inward meaning is drawn from honoring the letter?  [كِتَٰبٌۭ كَرِيمٌ · inward]
turns on: The inward reports emphasize either blessing through reverence or an immediate inward compulsion to submit.
- 27:29/q13/p1 Honoring it led Bilqis toward guidance, faith, and affirmation, and reverence can bring later blessing. — Sulami transmits Qasim. Bursevi cites the Najmiyya reading of faith in the Presence of the Generous and compares the magicians' respectful treatment of Moses at 20:65. Bursevi contrasts Khosrow, who tore Muhammad's letter and whose kingdom God tore apart as punishment for disbelief and defiance. [4 notes: SULAMI(القاسم); BURSEVI(التأويلات النجمية)]
- 27:29/q13/p2 The blessed book captured her heart, leaving only submission rather than reply or speech. — Sulami reports this from an unnamed reader. [1 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم)]

## 27:29/q14 What does the story imply about royal correspondence and rule?  [* · interpretation]
turns on: These are separate reported implications, one about royal communication and one about governance.
- 27:29/q14/p1 Bird delivery of royal letters appears to have been customary among kings. — Islahi infers this from the queen's and courtiers' lack of amazement. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 27:29/q14/p2 A people governed by a woman will not prosper. — Yahya transmits Abu Bakra's report of the Prophet's saying alongside the account of Bilqis. [1 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(أبو بكرة)]

## 27:29/q15 How is the written form of al-malāʾ explained?  [ٱلْمَلَؤُا۟ · grammar]
turns on: The reports explain different graphic features of the word: its extra alif and the seat of the final hamza.
- 27:29/q15/p1 The rasm adds an alif after the wāw in the three Naml instances of al-malāʾ by analogy with the alif after a pronominal wāw. — Nashr gives the graphic analogy. [1 notes: NASHR]
- 27:29/q15/p2 A final hamza written with wāw or yāʾ signals eased pronunciation at pause. — Nashr prefers this over explanations that the spelling strengthens the hamza, marks its vowel, or treats it like a medial hamza in connected speech. [1 notes: NASHR+]

## 27:29/q16 Where does the queen's encounter fit in the sūra's account?  [* · links]
- 27:29/q16/p1 The account of Solomon's miraculous host and his encounter with the Queen of Sheba culminates in her faith in the one God. — The Study Quran's introduction places this verse within that arc. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]

## 27:29/q17 What methodological claims about Qur'anic inimitability are reported alongside this verse?  [* · rhetoric]
turns on: Bint questions the evidential basis for claims about lost books and the method used to discuss inimitability.
- 27:29/q17/p1 Inquiry should avoid speculative historical chains and contentious technical detours. — Bint al-Shati criticizes polemical proliferation and al-Rafi'i's conjectural method, including judgments about lost books and extravagant praise by supporters. [2 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ]
- 27:29/q17/p2 Al-Jahiz's lost book may have only begun establishing eloquence because rhetoric was not yet a discipline. — Al-Rafi'i conjectures this; Bint criticizes its speculative basis. [0 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الرافعي)-]
- 27:29/q17/p3 Al-Wasiti built on al-Jahiz, and Abd al-Qahir built Dalāʾil al-Iʿjāz on al-Wasiti. — Al-Rafi'i proposes this chain although the two earlier books are lost; Bint presents it among unsupported conjectures. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الرافعي); BINTSHATI-IJAZ-]
- 27:29/q17/p4 Ibn Suraqa's lost book cannot have been useful, since a useful book would have survived. — Al-Rafi'i infers this from non-survival; Bint identifies it as unsupported. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ(الرافعي); BINTSHATI-IJAZ-]
- 27:29/q17/p5 Al-Rafi'i first discussed inimitability within Tārīkh Ādāb al-ʿArab, then published that treatment separately for usefulness and accessibility. — Bint gives this publication history. [1 notes: BINTSHATI-IJAZ]
