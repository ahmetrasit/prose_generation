# 26:89: 15 questions

## 26:89/q01 What does the exception say benefits a person on that Day?  [إِلَّا مَنْ أَتَى ٱللَّهَ بِقَلْبٍۢ سَلِيمٍۢ · grammar]
turns on: Whether man is disconnected, an object or subject of yanfaʿ, or a figurative substitute for wealth and children; whether an omitted noun is required and whether the reading covers the poor and childless.
- 26:89/q01/p1 The exception is disconnected: wealth and children do not benefit, whereas a person arriving with a sound heart benefits from its soundness. — Al-Wāḥidī calls it a separate statement. Abū Ḥayyān prefers the apparent syntax and denies that an omitted noun is needed; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī likewise says it covers rich, poor, parents, and childless alike. The latter rejects connected readings for restricting benefit to wealthy parents and rejects an unnecessary ellipsis. The Shaykh in al-Samīn and Abū Ḥayyān in al-Ālūsī also deny the ellipsis. Ibn ʿĀshūr objects that disconnectedness alone leaves the grammatical difficulty unresolved. [8 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ); ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان); TABATABAI; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; TABATABAI+; SAMIN-DURR-; ALUSI-FULL(الكشف)-; IBNASHUR-FULL-]
- 26:89/q01/p2 The exception is disconnected but an implied 'state of soundness' supplies the item that benefits. — Al-Zamakhsharī says a state must be supplied because soundness is not the same kind as wealth and children; al-Samīn defends this by comparing the impossible bare exception 'horses neigh except camels.' Al-Kashf says Abū Ḥayyān instead changes the antecedent from wealth to the whole sentence. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī objects that this ellipsis is needless. [7 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR(الزمخشري); ALUSI-FULL(الزمخشري,الكشف); TABATABAI(بعضهم); SAMIN-DURR+; ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; SAMIN-DURR(الشيخ)-; ALUSI-FULL(أبو حيان)-; TABATABAI-]
- 26:89/q01/p3 The exception is connected to the people benefited: only the person with a sound heart can benefit from wealth spent well and righteous children. — Al-Rāzī says the heart directs spending in obedience and teaching children religion, while remaining free of their temptation. Al-Nasafī makes man the object of yanfaʿ; Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn report this free-object parse. Al-Samīn also allows an omitted general object or excluded person and cites a poetic line about escaping only with a sword sheath and loincloth. Ibn ʿĀshūr insists the person is benefited rather than benefactor, with an implied general benefactor and rhetorical abstraction: the heart causes its owner's benefit. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī objects that this reading overlooks poor or childless people. Muqātil, Ibn Kathīr, Bursawī, and al-Biqāʿī connect benefit to faith and good use of possessions. [21 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT; QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(بعضهم); SAMIN-DURR; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; BURSEVI(ليث); ALUSI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; TABATABAI(بعضهم); TABATABAI-]
- 26:89/q01/p4 The exception is connected to wealth and children belonging to a sound-hearted person; their charity and righteous upbringing benefit. — Al-Samīn says one subject-substitute analysis lets children predominate over wealth, giving the righteous child who benefits self and others; another supplies a possessive before man. Abū al-Baqāʾ gives a subject-substitute analysis involving predominance and intercession, but al-Samīn says it conflates the two routes. Al-Ālūsī describes man as nominative with an implied noun. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī rejects the connected restriction to wealthy parents. [5 notes: SAMIN-DURR(أبو البقاء); ALUSI-FULL(قيل); TABATABAI(بعضهم); SAMIN-DURR-; TABATABAI-]
- 26:89/q01/p5 The exception treats worldly wealth and children as affluence, while the sound heart is the only enduring religious wealth. — Al-Rāzī contrasts worldly possessions and children with religious wealth; al-Zamakhsharī, al-Nasafī, al-Samīn, and al-Ālūsī give the same figurative connection. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī notes the implied noun and figurative use of affluence, and rejects restricting the contrast to possessors of worldly riches. Thawbān reports the Prophet naming a remembering tongue, grateful heart, and righteous spouse the best wealth after the hoarding verse. [7 notes: RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL(ثوبان,قيل); TABATABAI(بعضهم); TABATABAI-]
- 26:89/q01/p6 The connected exception substitutes the sound heart for wealth and children altogether. — Al-Rāzī's illustration answers a question about Zayd's wealth and sons by affirming soundness of heart instead. Al-Zamakhsharī gives a state-based connected exception and a corresponding rhetorical illustration; al-Ālūsī repeats the Zayd example and also permits connection through an implied condition, illustrated by a greeting that is a painful blow. [6 notes: RAZI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري); SAMIN-DURR; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI-]

## 26:89/q02 What nuance does أَتَى add to coming to God?  [أَتَى · meaning]
turns on: Whether Arabic lexical usage and Qurʾānic examples support a special ease in atā.
- 26:89/q02/p1 Atā denotes an easy arrival, while jāʾa is broader and may describe more difficult arrivals. — Al-Rāghib draws the distinction. Al-Sāmarrāʾī notes derivatives of atā for a well-traveled path and easy water channels, and cites strenuous Qurʾānic arrivals with jāʾa, including 23:27, 50:19, 18:71, 18:74, and 19:27, 88–90. [3 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT(الراغب الأصفهاني)]
- 26:89/q02/p2 The lexicons generally treat atā and jāʾa as equivalents without this stated distinction. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī observes that lexicographers gloss each verb by the other. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT]

## 26:89/q03 When does coming to God occur in the inward reading?  [أَتَى ٱللَّهَ · inward]
- 26:89/q03/p1 It is arrival at a moment of watchfulness before God. — Baḥr relates this to the heart originally created as a mirror for divine attributes, later tarnished by attachment to the created world. [1 notes: BURSEVI(بحر)]

## 26:89/q04 What is the sound heart?  [بِقَلْبٍۢ سَلِيمٍۢ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether soundness names minimum saving faith, broader moral purification, detachment from creation, or a special inward state.
- 26:89/q04/p1 It is free of shirk, grounded in sincere monotheistic faith. — Qatāda, Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, al-Ḥasan, Ibn Zayd and others give this core gloss. Ibn Zayd expressly says no one is free of sins. Ibn ʿAbbās phrases it as testimony that there is no god but God; al-Muqātil says this makes wealth and offspring useful at resurrection. Ibn Kathīr adds sincere devotion. Ibn Sīrīn's reported concern about al-Ḥajjāj asks whether he would die with this monotheistic heart despite his sins. Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the wording supports a general sense while freedom from shirk remains paramount. [23 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM(قتادة); ABDURRAZZAQ(قتادة); IBNQUTAYBA-GHARIB; TAB-FULL(ابن زيد,قتادة); IBNABIHATIM(ابن عباس,الحسن,عبد الرحمن بن زيد,مجاهد); SULAMI; WAHIDI-WAJIZ; MAWARDI-FULL(الحسن); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الحسن وابن زيد); IBNKATHIR-FULL(ابن عباس,مجاهد، الحسن); DURR-FULL(ابن عباس,عون,قتادة); STUDYQURAN(IK); IBNATIYYA-FULL]
- 26:89/q04/p2 It is free of shirk, doubt, disbelief, and hypocrisy, with certain and sincere faith. — Al-Wāḥidī attributes the composite gloss to Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, al-Kalbī, Muqātil, Qatāda, al-Ḥasan and most interpreters; he infers certainty and sincerity. Al-Ḥasan and Mujāhid in al-Ṭabrisī include doubt. Al-Nasafī cites 2:10 for diseased unbelieving and hypocritical hearts. Al-Ālūsī says this transmitted sense best fits the context; faith is necessary for benefit from possessions. Kuranyolu adds belief in the articles of faith and spiritual health. [11 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس، مجاهد، الكلبي، مقاتل، قتادة، الحسن، وأكثر المفسرين); TABRISI(الحسن ومجاهد); NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عباس ومجاهد وقتادة وابن سيرين وغيرهم); ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(İbn Kesîr); DURR-FULL(مجاهد)]
- 26:89/q04/p3 It is certain that God is real, the Hour is coming, and the dead will be raised. — Muḥammad or Ibn Sīrīn gives this threefold confession in al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Abī Ḥātim, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr and Study Quran. [5 notes: TAB-FULL(محمد); IBNABIHATIM(محمد بن سيرين); QURTUBI-FULL(محمد بن سيرين); IBNKATHIR-FULL(محمد بن سيرين); STUDYQURAN(Q)]
- 26:89/q04/p4 It is free from doubt about the truth. — Mujāhid gives this gloss in several transmissions; Study Quran adds sincerity. [5 notes: TAB-FULL(مجاهد); IBNABIHATIM(مجاهد); MAWARDI-FULL(مجاهد); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(مجاهد); STUDYQURAN(Ṭ)]
- 26:89/q04/p5 It is the healthy heart of a believer, unlike the sick heart of a disbeliever or hypocrite. — Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyib cites the sickness in hearts at 2:10. Ibn ʿĀshūr takes the sick heart as the sound heart's metaphorical opposite; Study Quran reports the same spiritual-health image. [8 notes: THALABI(سعيد بن المسيب); WAHIDI-BASIT(سعيد بن المسيب); BAGHAWI-FULL(سعيد بن المسيب); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(سعيد بن المسيب); IBNKATHIR-FULL(سعيد بن المسيب); STUDYQURAN(Th); IBNASHUR-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 26:89/q04/p6 It is broadly pure of blameworthy traits and endowed with good qualities. — Al-Ḍaḥḥāk calls it sincere, pure and devoted; al-Qurṭubī prefers this capacious account because it gathers the narrower views. Al-Rāghib defines salāma as freedom from outward and inward afflictions. Asad calls it protected from evils. [8 notes: TAB-FULL(الضحاك); IBNABIHATIM(الضحاك); MAWARDI-FULL(الضحاك); QURTUBI-FULL(الضحاك); TABATABAI(الراغب); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Esed)]
- 26:89/q04/p7 It is free from sins and moral corruption. — Al-Māwardī says heart health produces health in the limbs. Al-Ṭabrisī reports freedom from corruption and sins; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī identifies wrongdoing as shirk and disobedience, invoking 20:111 and its humbled faces. Al-Sulamī also reports a narrower threshold of freedom from major sins. Another reported formulation joins freedom from shirk and sins to submission to God's judgment, loyalty to His allies, opposition to His enemies, and obedient worship. [6 notes: SULAMI; MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI(قيل); TABATABAI; ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]
- 26:89/q04/p8 It is free of ignorance, false beliefs, vile character and worldly desires, with knowledge and excellent character. — Al-Rāzī prefers this definition by analogy with bodily health: knowledge and fine character constitute the heart's proper health. Al-Ālūsī cites the Imam and says righteous deeds follow from the heart's effect on limbs. Kuranyolu and Study Quran relay al-Rāzī's preference; al-Biqāʿī adds preservation of original God-given nature, Islam, good action and Sunna. Ibn ʿĀshūr identifies the heart with inward cognition and its health with freedom from shirk and psychological purification. [6 notes: ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); STUDYQURAN(R); BIQAI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Râzî)+; RAZI-FULL+]
- 26:89/q04/p9 It is free of innovation and established in the Sunna. — Sahl and Abū ʿUthmān al-Nīsābūrī are among the reported authorities. Al-Tustarī also includes entrusting affairs to God and contentment with decree; Kuranyolu adds freedom from arrogance caused by wealth and children. [9 notes: TUSTARI; SULAMI(سهل); THALABI(أبو عثمان النيسابوري); BAGHAWI-FULL(ابن عثمان النيسابوري); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الثعلبي); QURTUBI-FULL(أبو عثمان السياري); IBNKATHIR-FULL(أبو عثمان النيسابوري); BURSEVI(قشيري); KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(Şevkânî)]
- 26:89/q04/p10 It is free of attachment to wealth, children, worldly love and even lawful worldly things. — Al-Ḥusayn ibn al-Faḍl and al-Ḥasan say wealth and children must not injure it. Al-Ṣādiq and al-Kāshifī appeal to the Prophetic saying that love of the world is the root of every sin. Ibn ʿAṭiyya and Abū Ḥayyān include detachment even from permitted things; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports the saying separately. [9 notes: THALABI(الحسين بن الفضل); IBNATIYYA-FULL; TABRISI(الصادق); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الحسين بن الفضل); QURTUBI-FULL(الحسن); ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI(الكاشفي); TABATABAI(الصادق عليه السلام,النبي صلى الله عليه وآله وسلم)]
- 26:89/q04/p11 It contains nothing besides God and seeks nothing but Him. — Ibn ʿAṭāʾ excludes other preoccupations, joys and aims; Sufyān says it meets God with nothing else within it. Al-Junayd speaks of love of God alone; al-Qushayrī excludes greed and even hope for the afterlife. Al-Wāsiṭī includes freedom from ostentation and annihilation from created things; Kharrāz links this to Abraham's and Joseph's prayers for Islam in 2:128 and 12:101. The al-Kāfī report adds that shirk or doubt ruins the heart and renunciation empties it for the Hereafter. Abu Hayyan says wording supports the breadth but freedom from shirk is the broader governing sense. Abū Yazīd's journey through deserts and spiritual realms ends in his requesting and receiving the state of wanting nothing. [15 notes: SULAMI(أبو سعيد الخراز,ابن عطاء,الجنيد,الشبلي,الواسطي,بعضهم); IBNATIYYA-FULL(سفيان); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(سفيان); BURSEVI(أبو يزيد,قشيري); ALUSI-FULL(سفيان); TABATABAI(المسؤول); STUDYQURAN(Su)]
- 26:89/q04/p12 It is safe from an evil divine decree and content with God's decree. — Al-Wāsiṭī and al-Qāsim give safety from an evil decree; al-Warrāq says contentment extends to loved and disliked outcomes. Sahl combines entrusting affairs to God and contentment. Another account makes contentment without inward opposition the final sign of successive certainty, gnosis and witnessing. Another account ties serenity to certainty and abandoning personal will as states change or remain. [8 notes: SULAMI(أبو بكر الوراق,القاسم,الواسطي,بعضهم,سهل); TUSTARI; BURSEVI(بعضهم)]
- 26:89/q04/p13 It is untroubled by the afflictions and passing thoughts of created existence. — Ibn ʿAṭāʾ describes a heart empty of passing inspirations; al-Wāsiṭī says it has not tasted the bitterness of spiritual ailments. In al-Bursawī's quotation, Ibn ʿAṭāʾ excludes any created affliction. [3 notes: SULAMI(ابن عطاء,الواسطي); BURSEVI(ابن عطاء)]
- 26:89/q04/p14 It is untouched by the created world because its state with God is sound. — Al-Wāsiṭī points to Abraham's tests involving himself, family and son: none altered him, and all else became insignificant. [1 notes: SULAMI(الواسطي)]
- 26:89/q04/p15 It is free of turning away from God. — Al-Wāsiṭī gives this answer when explicitly asked what a sound heart is. [1 notes: SULAMI(الواسطي)]
- 26:89/q04/p16 It has no hatred toward the Prophet's companions and family. — An anonymous view specifies the Companions; Taysīr expands the list to the Prophet's family, wives and Companions. [2 notes: SULAMI; BURSEVI(تيسير)]
- 26:89/q04/p17 It does not curse. — Hishām's father gives this gloss; ʿUrwa tells his sons that Abraham cursed nothing and had a sound heart. [2 notes: IBNABIHATIM(أبوه); QURTUBI-FULL(عروة)]
- 26:89/q04/p18 It has no deficiency in original aptitude and no veils from the soul's embodied attributes. — Al-Qāshānī states this inward account. Baḥr likens the originally pure heart to a divine mirror tarnished by worldly attachment. [2 notes: QASHANI; BURSEVI(بحر)]
- 26:89/q04/p19 It embodies sacred purity and divine attributes rather than looking toward creation. — Baḥr says the exception points to taking on God's character and attributes. [1 notes: BURSEVI(بحر)]
- 26:89/q04/p20 It harms no one, bears no grudge over harm, and expects no repayment for kindness. — Abū al-Qāsim al-Ḥakīm gives these as three signs of piety, loyalty and sincerity. Al-Māwardī also reports sincere conduct toward people; another reported gloss excludes envy and betrayal. [3 notes: MAWARDI-FULL(عبد الرحمن بن أبي حاتم); BURSEVI(أبو القاسم الحكيم,الكاشفي)]
- 26:89/q04/p21 It is a heart protected from fear at resurrection. — Al-Māwardī links this possibility to the preceding glad tidings received when seeing God. [1 notes: MAWARDI-FULL]
- 26:89/q04/p22 It is a heart free of the original signs of wretchedness, passion and affliction, departing with God pleased. — An unnamed authority maps this course from entering life through standing before God. [1 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم)]
- 26:89/q04/p23 Its soundness has levels, progressing from freedom from shirk to freedom from desires, ostentation, self-admiration, and remembrance of anything besides God. — Abū ʿUthmān names four levels. Another unnamed authority traces scripture certainty, gnosis, witnessing and annihilation, with contentment as the sign. [2 notes: SULAMI(أبو عثمان,بعضهم)]

## 26:89/q05 Can سَلِيمٍ mean 'stung,' and what does that imply?  [سَلِيمٍۢ · meaning]
turns on: Whether salīm is taken through the lexical usage for a bite victim or through safety and submission.
- 26:89/q05/p1 Salīm can mean one bitten or stung; the heart is agitated by fear of God, separation, or longing. — Al-Junayd uses the Arabic expression for a snakebite victim; the anonymous al-Sulamī reading stresses restless grief and supplication over separation. Bursawī's cited poetry says nearness can hurt more than distance because it brings fear of loss, while distance allows hope of reunion. Al-Ālūsī repeats fear of separation. [10 notes: SULAMI(بعضهم); THALABI(الجنيد); IBNATIYYA-FULL(الجنيد); IBNJAWZI-ZAD(الجنيد); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL(الجنيد); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الجنيد); BURSEVI(جنيد); ALUSI-FULL(الجنيد); ABUHAYYAN-FULL(الزمخشري)-; ALUSI-FULL-]
- 26:89/q05/p2 Salīm is the one who is safe, submits, yields and surrenders. — Al-Rāzī reports this alternate interpretation. [1 notes: RAZI-FULL]

## 26:89/q06 Why is the heart singled out?  [بِقَلْبٍۢ · rhetoric]
- 26:89/q06/p1 The heart governs the limbs; its soundness yields righteous action and its corruption corrupts them. — Al-Māwardī, al-Ṭabrisī and al-Qurṭubī give the causal argument; al-Ṭabrisī says bodily corruption follows corrupt inward intention. Al-Rāzī answers an objection that the verse makes tongue and limbs dispensable: their failure proves an unsound heart. Kashf al-Asrār cites the report comparing the heart to a morsel of flesh. Ibn ʿĀshūr says the final state at arrival is decisive. [7 notes: MAWARDI-FULL; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; BURSEVI(كشف الأسرار); ALUSI-FULL(الإمام); IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 26:89/q07 What did the sound heart signify for Abraham?  [بِقَلْبٍۢ سَلِيمٍۢ · links]
turns on: Whether the link is a second scriptural description or a scheme of prophetic spiritual ranks.
- 26:89/q07/p1 God honored Abraham by explicitly describing him with a sound heart elsewhere. — Al-Rāzī and al-Nasafī cite 37:83–84, and Study Quran cites 37:84. [3 notes: RAZI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; STUDYQURAN]
- 26:89/q07/p2 Abraham's soundness is his special rank of intimacy among prophetic virtues. — Ibn Ṭāhir sets Adam's self-reproach and forgiveness, Abraham's soundness and intimacy, and Muhammad's steadfastness and love in sequence; he calls steadfast nearness and witnessing the highest character and cites 7:23, 37:83, 11:112, 42:15 and 68:4. [1 notes: SULAMI(أبو بكر بن طاهر)]

## 26:89/q08 Who speaks the line about the Day and the exception?  [* · referent]
turns on: Whether the eschatological clause and its exception remain within Abraham's prayer or are God's comment on it.
- 26:89/q08/p1 The Day clause and exception continue Abraham's speech. — Abū Ḥayyān prefers the apparent reading: Abraham reports what God taught him about his people and Resurrection. Al-Zarkashī specifically contrasts this with God's speech in 37:84. [2 notes: BURHAN; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+]
- 26:89/q08/p2 God describes the Day that Abraham feared after Abraham's own speech. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya separates the 'day wealth and children do not benefit' clause from Abraham's speech. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية)]
- 26:89/q08/p3 God adds the sound-heart exception as completion and explanation of Abraham's prayer. — Iṣlāḥī argues that, if parentage itself brought benefit, Āzar would have benefited from Abraham as son. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 26:89/q09 How does the exception bear on Abraham's prayer for his father?  [* · links]
turns on: Whether the exception is connected to believers' possessions, and what follows for a prayer concerning an unbelieving father.
- 26:89/q09/p1 It points to a prayer for his father's guidance to faith, since posthumous intercession for an unbeliever would be futile. — Al-Ālūsī draws this from faith's being necessary for benefit. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī reports the argument but says it depends on a connected exception and on the rejected claim that Āzar was Abraham's biological father; 9:114 is mentioned. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI(بعضهم)-]
- 26:89/q09/p2 With a disconnected exception, Abraham prayed before his father's death. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī prefers this because 21:28 limits intercession to those God approves. [1 notes: TABATABAI+]
- 26:89/q09/p3 Abraham's father remained outside the granted requests. — Kuranyolu reports exegetes' proofs that all Abraham's requests were granted except the one for his father. A report in al-Durr describes the unbelieving father Āzar transformed into a filthy beast and cast into Hell despite Abraham's appeal not to be shamed. [2 notes: KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(müfessirler); DURR-FULL]

## 26:89/q10 What is the relation of this verse to Abraham's narrative and the surrounding sūra?  [* · links]
turns on: How Abraham's example is related to the preceding divine favors and the sūra's sequence.
- 26:89/q10/p1 Abraham's argument moves from God's exclusive creative and saving acts to sincere prayers and the Resurrection scene. — Ibn al-Jawzī says only the performer of creation, guidance, provision, healing and forgiveness can be divine. Al-Nasafī traces Abraham's worship question, exposure of idols' inability to harm, benefit or hear, rejection of ancestral imitation, recital of God's favors, supplications, reward and punishment, and polytheists' remorse. Ibn ʿĀshūr says five spiritual requests answer five bodily gifts at 26:78–82, with the prayer for his father inserted in the plea against disgrace. [7 notes: IBNJAWZI-ZAD; NASAFI-FULL; IBNASHUR-FULL]
- 26:89/q10/p2 The account continues the sūra's preaching pattern and fills the interval after Noah before Moses. — Iṣlāḥī says it has the preceding anecdote's purpose and recurring ending; Abraham is the most prominent messenger between Noah and Moses. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 26:89/q11 How does the verse's contrast resemble another Qurʾānic contrast?  [* · links]
- 26:89/q11/p1 It parallels worldly wealth and children as adornment versus lasting good deeds. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī cites 18:46 and observes that here the lasting benefit is assigned to a sound heart. [1 notes: TABATABAI]

## 26:89/q12 What reports illustrate sound hearts and the people of Paradise?  [* · reports]
turns on: The two reports use different images for Paradise's people.
- 26:89/q12/p1 People enter Paradise with hearts like birds, pure of sin and defect. — Al-Qurṭubī cites Abū Hurayra's report in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim and allows that the likeness also suggests little worldly experience. [1 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]
- 26:89/q12/p2 Most people of Paradise are simple-minded in avoiding disobedience, with an inborn turn to good and charitable opinions of others. — Al-Qurṭubī calls Anas's Prophetic report sound. Al-Azharī explains the simple as disposed to good and unaware of evil; al-Qutabī says sound hearts and good opinions dominate them. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(الأزهري,القتبي,النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم)]

## 26:89/q13 How are good and evil precedents relevant to Abraham's supplications?  [* · links]
- 26:89/q13/p1 Priority in good deeds is prized, while initiating evil brings continuing blame. — Ibn ʿĀshūr cites Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ as first to shoot in God's cause and a hadith that Adam's first son shares every unjust killing because he began it; no grading is supplied. [2 notes: IBNASHUR-FULL]

## 26:89/q14 What can āya mean and how is a Qurʾānic verse defined?  [* · sciences]
turns on: Whether the word's original sense is group, wonder or sign, and what marks a counted verse boundary.
- 26:89/q14/p1 Āya can mean a group. — Abū ʿAmr al-Shaybānī cites the phrase for people going out with their group; al-Jaʿbarī includes 'group' as a possible source of the verse name. [2 notes: BURHAN(أبو عمرو الشيباني,الجعبرى)]
- 26:89/q14/p2 Āya can mean a marvel or wonder. — Al-Zarkashī cites a poet who calls an unequaled beauty a wonder. [1 notes: BURHAN(شاعر)]
- 26:89/q14/p3 Āya can mean a sign or trace, and a Qurʾānic verse is a sign of Muhammad's prophethood. — An Arab idiom speaks of no sign left in a ruined house; another view says the counted unit proves the messenger true and challengers unable to match it. Al-Jaʿbarī gives 'sign' as another name origin. [3 notes: BURHAN(الجعبرى,العرب,قيل)]
- 26:89/q14/p4 A verse is a transmitted bounded Qurʾānic unit, identified by prophetic instruction. — One view applies this to verse and sūra boundaries and calls the verse a group of letters distinct in sense. Al-Jaʿbarī describes a unit of clauses with a beginning and end within a sūra; another view requires separation from both neighbors and no counterpart elsewhere. Al-Wāḥidī's view sees interruption of one passage from another but recognizes transmitted limits prevent arbitrary shorter units. [4 notes: BURHAN(الجعبرى,الواحدي وبعض أصحابنا,بعضهم,غيره)]

## 26:89/q15 How is the word āya derived?  [* · grammar]
turns on: The proposed underlying form and sound change.
- 26:89/q15/p1 It derives from ayya on the faʿla pattern. — Sībawayh says movement of yāʾ and opening the preceding vowel yield āya. [1 notes: BURHAN(سيبويه)]
- 26:89/q15/p2 It derives from āyiyah with deletion of the first yāʾ. — Al-Kisāʾī says deletion avoids the gemination found in dābbah. [1 notes: BURHAN(الكسائي)]
