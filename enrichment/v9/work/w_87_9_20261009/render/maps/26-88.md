# 26:88: 16 questions

## 26:88/q01 Which earlier day does يَوْمَ specify, and how is it parsed?  [يَوْمَ · grammar]
- 26:88/q01/p1 It is an appositional substitute for the earlier day when people are raised. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Samīn, al-Ālūsī, and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī identify 26:87 as the antecedent; al-Ālūsī says the repetition magnifies the day's terror and prepares for 26:89. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Samīn stress that an apposition shares its governing verb with its antecedent, a constraint relevant to the dispute over the speaker. [11 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; SAMIN-DURR(ابن عطية); ALUSI-FULL(ابن عطية); TABATABAI]

## 26:88/q02 Whose speech includes the description of this day and the following verses?  [يَوْمَ · interpretation]
turns on: Where Abraham's speech ends, especially whether apposition to the earlier day permits a change of speaker; Iṣlāḥī places God's completion at the following exception.
- 26:88/q02/p1 Abraham continues speaking through this day and the following account. — Abū Ḥayyān calls this the apparent reading and says Abraham relates what God taught him about his people and Resurrection. Al-Ālūsī extends Abraham's speech through the statement that there is a sign in this (26:103). Abū Ḥayyān, al-Samīn, al-Ālūsī, and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī argue that apposition to the earlier day requires the same governing verb and therefore the same speaker. [6 notes: ALUSI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL+; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عطية)+; SAMIN-DURR+; TABATABAI+]
- 26:88/q02/p2 Abraham's speech ends before this day; God describes the day and continues the account. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya treats the day as God's description of the day on which Abraham feared disgrace; al-Samīn reports a continuation of God's speech through following verses. Al-Ālūsī reports this as Ibn ʿAṭiyya's view but rejects it. Its opponents say the appositional phrase cannot change speaker while sharing the earlier phrase's governing verb. [2 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL(ابن عطية); SAMIN-DURR(ابن عطية); ABUHAYYAN-FULL-; SAMIN-DURR-; ALUSI-FULL(ابن عطية)-; TABATABAI-]
- 26:88/q02/p3 God supplies the following exception about the sound heart as a completion of Abraham's prayer. — Iṣlāḥī says that if having a child could benefit a parent, Āzar would have benefited from Abraham as a son. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 26:88/q03 What is the inward sense of this day?  [يَوْمَ · inward]
- 26:88/q03/p1 It is the approach to the divine presence for acceptance of God's grace. — Bursevī reports this reading from Baḥr. [1 notes: BURSEVI(بحر)]

## 26:88/q04 What object of يَنفَعُ is understood?  [يَنفَعُ · grammar]
- 26:88/q04/p1 The object is omitted because it is understood: wealth and children benefit no one. — Al-Wāḥidī supplies the understood person as the object; al-Ṭabrisī likewise presupposes an omitted object when discussing the following exception. [2 notes: WAHIDI-BASIT; TABRISI]

## 26:88/q05 How can the following exception be parsed with يَنفَعُ?  [يَنفَعُ · grammar]
turns on: Whether the relative clause substitutes for the understood object or is parsed as an exception.
- 26:88/q05/p1 The following relative clause is accusative as a substitute for the omitted object of 'benefit'. — Al-Ṭabrisī explains the resulting sense as no person being benefited by wealth or children except the person who comes to God. [1 notes: TABRISI]
- 26:88/q05/p2 The following relative clause is accusative as an exception. — Al-Ṭabrisī offers this as the alternative syntactic analysis. [1 notes: TABRISI]

## 26:88/q06 What sort of benefit do wealth and children fail to give on that day?  [يَنفَعُ مَالٌۭ بَنُونَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether the denial includes good works and righteous children without qualification, or leaves a benefit for Muslims whose deeds and children are righteous.
- 26:88/q06/p1 They cannot secure, ransom, defend, or otherwise help a person at Resurrection. — Muqātil says neither averts punishment after death. Al-Ṭabrisī says wealth cannot ransom its owner from hardship and children cannot bear a parent's sins; Abū Ḥayyān contrasts their worldly power to ransom and defend. Ibn Kathīr says even earth-filling gold or all people offered as ransom would fail. Al-Biqāʿī adds that wealth cannot purchase an intercessor or powerful defender, sons cannot support or console, and lesser things are still less useful. Bursevī extends the denial even to wealth spent on good works and righteous sons capable of intercession. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī explains that worldly social bonds and assigned causal power are conventional and severed when Resurrection discloses reality (6:94; 23:101; 37:25–26); this extends to learning, craft, and beauty. The Study Quran cites the recurring theme in 3:10, 3:116, 9:55, 31:33, 34:35–37, 58:17, 60:3, and 64:15. The Prophet's answer about Ibn Judʿān says his hospitality and kin care will not benefit him because he did not seek forgiveness for his sin on Judgment Day. [11 notes: MUQATIL; TABRISI; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; DURR-FULL(رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم); BURSEVI; TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]
- 26:88/q06/p2 Believers can benefit from good deeds and righteous children, though disbelievers' wealth and children cannot help without sound hearts. — Bursevī reports al-Layth: disbelievers boasted of wealth and children, while Muslims may benefit from their deeds and from a righteous child whether the child dies first or later prays for the parent. ʿAlī, as reported by Ibn Abī Ḥātim, contrasts wealth and children as the world's harvest with deeds as the afterlife's harvest, while allowing that God grants both to some people. [2 notes: BURSEVI(ليث); IBNABIHATIM(علي)]

## 26:88/q07 Why is wealth called مَالٌ?  [مَالٌۭ · meaning]
- 26:88/q07/p1 It is so called because it draws people toward itself. — Ibn Abī Ḥātim reports this explanation from al-Tharī, using the sense of inclining. [1 notes: IBNABIHATIM(الثرى)]

## 26:88/q08 What does بَنُونَ designate here?  [بَنُونَ · referent]
turns on: Whether the word is confined to actual sons or extends to all possible helpers.
- 26:88/q08/p1 It means sons in its ordinary sense. — Al-Ālūsī explicitly gives the ordinary sense as his own explanation before mentioning a broader alternative. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL]
- 26:88/q08/p2 It stands for helpers or supporters more generally. — Abū Ḥayyān reports this broad interpretation; al-Ālūsī reports it as another view. Al-Qurṭubī explains sons as supporters: if one's own son cannot help, nobody else can. [3 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); ALUSI-FULL]

## 26:88/q09 What grammatical form is بَنُونَ?  [بَنُونَ · grammar]
- 26:88/q09/p1 It is appended to the sound masculine plural pattern and loses its nūn in an iḍāfa construction. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī gives بنو آدم and بني آدم as examples of the nūn's loss in construct forms. [1 notes: SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 26:88/q10 How is a name such as بَنُونَ shortened in vocative truncation?  [بَنُونَ · grammar]
- 26:88/q10/p1 Only its final nūn is dropped, preserving a name of at least three letters. — Sībawayh says paired jointly added suffixes are removed together, as in مسلمان, but a name like بنون cannot be shortened below three letters; its remainder may count as an unaugmented name. He illustrates other vocative truncations with عثمان to عثم, مروان to مرو, and أسماء to أسم, citing matching poetry. [2 notes: SIBAWAYH]

## 26:88/q11 Why are wealth and sons named as examples?  [مَالٌۭ بَنُونَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the choice emphasizes worldly adornments, the strongest human support, or the immediately preceding mention of Abraham's father.
- 26:88/q11/p1 They are the chief adornments and sources of status, security, and comfort in worldly life. — Ibn ʿAṭiyya says the verse negates worldly attachments and names their greatest adornments. Abū Ḥayyān and al-Ālūsī report the same wider sense of all worldly goods and attractions; Abū Ḥayyān cites 18:46. The Study Quran calls these sources of worldly status, security, and comfort. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī extends the examples to all conventionally valued means to material ends, including learning, craft, and beauty. [5 notes: IBNATIYYA-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL(قيل); ALUSI-FULL(قيل); TABATABAI; STUDYQURAN]
- 26:88/q11/p2 Sons are named because a son is the strongest likely supporter, so the failure of sons implies the failure of other helpers. — Al-Qurṭubī draws that inference; al-Biqāʿī similarly says lesser means are even less useful after wealth and sons fail. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL; BIQAI-FULL]
- 26:88/q11/p3 The mention of sons recalls Abraham's father: Abraham could not benefit him. — Al-Qurṭubī reports this explanation from an unnamed authority because Abraham's father was just mentioned; Iṣlāḥī likewise says Āzar's having Abraham as a son could not save him. [2 notes: QURTUBI-FULL(قيل); ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 26:88/q12 How does the following sound-heart exception qualify this verse?  [* · links]
- 26:88/q12/p1 A sound heart free of associationism matters where wealth and children fail. — Yaḥyā ibn Sallām transmits Qatāda through Saʿīd defining the sound heart as free of associationism. Bursevī reports al-Layth that disbelievers' boasts of wealth and children cannot help without sound hearts; al-Ālūsī says this day's description prepares for the following exception in 26:89. [3 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM(قتادة); BURSEVI(ليث); ALUSI-FULL]

## 26:88/q13 What does Abraham's prayer and this day show about divine and family help?  [* · interpretation]
- 26:88/q13/p1 People need God's support to face the terrors of Resurrection. — Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī infers this from Abraham's plea not to be disgraced: weak human constitution cannot face that day without divine aid. [1 notes: TABATABAI]
- 26:88/q13/p2 Abraham's father could not be saved through his son, and the request concerning him was the exception to Abraham's granted prayers. — Iṣlāḥī uses Āzar as the case showing that a child cannot benefit a parent; Kur'an Yolu reports that exegetes offer various proofs that all Abraham's requests were granted except the one concerning his father. [2 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR; KURANYOLU-TEFSIR(müfessirler)]

## 26:88/q14 How is the Abraham account placed in the sūra's sequence?  [* · links]
- 26:88/q14/p1 It serves the same preaching purpose as the preceding account and ends with the recurring verses. — Iṣlāḥī makes this comparison with the preceding anecdote. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 26:88/q14/p2 It fills the gap after Noah in the previous sūra, since Abraham was the most prominent messenger between Noah and Moses. — Iṣlāḥī gives this placement within the sequence of prophetic accounts. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]
- 26:88/q14/p3 The sūra can be classed as a digression because its discourse returns afterward to prophets and earlier communities. — Al-Suyūṭī reports this classification from some scholars. [1 notes: ITQAN(بعضهم)]

## 26:88/q15 What narrative wording presents Resurrection as certain?  [* · rhetoric]
- 26:88/q15/p1 Past-tense verbs describe future Resurrection events to present them as certain. — Abū Ḥayyān identifies أتى, أزلفت, and برزت after the rebuke of the idolaters. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]

## 26:88/q16 What manuscript spelling conventions are cited alongside this verse?  [* · readings]
turns on: Whether the yāʾ in malayh and malayhim is additional or represents the hamza.
- 26:88/q16/p1 The cited spellings use added or omitted alifs and yāʾ or wāw forms to represent hamzas and connected pronunciation. — Ibn al-Jazarī says mayah, mayatayn, malayh, and malayhim have an added alif before a yāʾ representing hamza; some initial hamzas follow their eased connected form, combining two pronunciations. He says unabbikum writes a dammated hamza with wāw after alif, while other double or triple hamzas may use a single alif; hāʾulāʾ joins the alerting particle, omitting its alif, as in yā ayyuhā. For Ṭā Hā's yābna umm, the wāw joins the word and the following alif belongs to ibn, not the vocative particle. Al-Sakhāwī cites the Damascus codex; Ibn al-Jazarī says he saw the spelling there despite an erasure mark and locates that codex at the northeastern Mashhad ʿAlī of the Umayyad Mosque, formerly at al-Kushk mosque according to trusted teachers. [9 notes: NASHR(أبو الحسن السخاوي); NASHR+]
- 26:88/q16/p2 The yāʾ in malayh and malayhim is an added letter. — Ibn al-Jazarī attributes this view to al-Dānī, al-Shāṭibī, and their followers, and argues instead that the yāʾ is the hamza's written form. [0 notes: NASHR-]
