# 26:73: 14 questions

## 26:73/q01 How are the two alternatives joined to the preceding question?  [أَوْ يَنفَعُونَكُمْ أَوْ يَضُرُّونَ · grammar]
- 26:73/q01/p1 They are coordinated alternatives under the single preceding interrogative, 'do they hear you?' — Sībawayh cites the full Qur'anic sequence as an example of hal followed by coordinated alternatives in one inquiry; al-Naḥḥās explicitly coordinates this clause with 'do they hear you?' [2 notes: SIBAWAYH; NAHHAS]

## 26:73/q02 What hearing or response is at issue in the preceding question?  [أَوْ يَنفَعُونَكُمْ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether 'hearing you' requires an omitted noun for the worshippers' invocation or can directly mean hearing their speech; the grammarians dispute the force of Zuhayr's poetic analogy.
- 26:73/q02/p1 Whether the idols hear or answer the worshippers' invocation; one grammatical account supplies an omitted 'invocation.' — Muqātil speaks of answering prayers; Yaḥyā b. Sallām of hearing supplication for a desired benefit or removal of harm; Qatāda of answering worshippers when called; al-Ṭabrisī of hearing and answering supplications; al-Rāzī of hearing requests before granting aid. The Basran grammarians reported by al-Ṭabarī posit an omitted duʿāʾ and compare Zuhayr's omission of ḥakamāt before al-abaq. [6 notes: MUQATIL; YAHYA-SALLAM; TAB-FULL(بعض نحويي البصرة); IBNABIHATIM(قتادة); TABRISI; RAZI-FULL]
- 26:73/q02/p2 The idols are asked whether they hear the worshippers themselves, meaning their speech, without a noun being omitted. — The other Arab grammarians reported by al-Ṭabarī say Arabs naturally use 'I heard Zayd' for hearing his speech. They reject the Basran analogy: Zuhayr's preceding mention of bridles licenses the later reference to a bridle without its noun. [1 notes: TAB-FULL(بعض من أنكر ذلك من أهل العربية); TAB-FULL(بعض نحويي البصرة)-]

## 26:73/q03 What benefit might the idols supposedly give?  [يَنفَعُونَكُمْ · interpretation]
turns on: The notes specify different hoped-for benefits: a return for devotion, provision and relief, nearness through devotion, or harm to an enemy.
- 26:73/q03/p1 They might benefit people in return for worship, obedience, or prayers. — Muqātil ties benefit to worship and answered prayer; al-Kalbī to obedience; al-Wāḥidī's report from Muqātil to worship; Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, al-Būrsawī, and al-Ālūsī likewise relate benefit to worship. Al-Ālūsī says the intensity of the cult presupposes expected benefit or protection from harm. [8 notes: MUQATIL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي,مقاتل); IBNJAWZI-ZAD; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL]
- 26:73/q03/p2 They might provide sustenance or remove hardship. — Al-Ṭabarī specifies provision as the hoped-for reward of worship; Ibn ʿAbbās, as reported by al-Wāḥidī, names provision and relief from hardship; al-Baghawī reports provision as an explanation of benefit; al-Qurṭubī includes provision and control of good and evil. [4 notes: TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); BAGHAWI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL]
- 26:73/q03/p3 Devotion to them might bring worshippers nearer to their goal. — Abū Ḥayyān describes the supposed benefit as being brought nearer through devotion. [1 notes: ABUHAYYAN-FULL]
- 26:73/q03/p4 Even a thing unable to hear could benefit a worshipper by injuring an enemy. — Al-Biqāʿī compares fire, which can be used for benefit or to harm an enemy despite being unable to hear. [1 notes: BIQAI-FULL]

## 26:73/q04 Why is benefit raised before harm?  [يَنفَعُونَكُمْ يَضُرُّونَ · rhetoric]
- 26:73/q04/p1 Benefit comes first because people principally seek benefit for themselves. — Al-Biqāʿī calls benefit the worshipper's primary aim; al-Sāmarrāʾī says people seek benefit for themselves, explaining why 'benefit' has a specified object while 'harm' is unrestricted. [2 notes: BIQAI-FULL; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]

## 26:73/q05 What does يَضُرُّونَ mean as a word?  [يَضُرُّونَ · meaning]
- 26:73/q05/p1 It means to cause harm in the ordinary sense. — Al-Dāmghānī gives this ordinary sense of ḍarr. [1 notes: DAMGHANI]

## 26:73/q06 What harm might the idols supposedly cause or prevent?  [يَضُرُّونَ · interpretation]
turns on: Whether harm is retaliation against the worshipper for abandoning worship, a general injury to avert, harm to an enemy, or harm to any person turning away; al-Ālūsī disputes the last scope.
- 26:73/q06/p1 They might punish worshippers who neglect, disobey, or abandon their worship. — Muqātil, al-Kalbī, al-Wāḥidī's Muqātil report, al-Baghawī, al-Ṭabrisī, Ibn al-Jawzī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥayyān, and al-Būrsawī connect harm to withheld worship; al-Ṭabarī specifies loss of wealth or destruction of worshippers and their children. Al-Ālūsī supplies 'you' as the object when worship is abandoned. [12 notes: MUQATIL; TAB-FULL; WAHIDI-BASIT(الكلبي,مقاتل); BAGHAWI-FULL; TABRISI; IBNJAWZI-ZAD; BAYDAWI-FULL; NASAFI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL+]
- 26:73/q06/p2 They might cause harm generally or protect worshippers by averting it. — Yaḥyā b. Sallām speaks of seeking removal of harm; Ibn ʿAbbās in al-Wāḥidī asks whether idols can bring harm; al-Rāzī describes the worshipper's hope of averting it; al-Qurṭubī asks whether idols control good and evil; al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī says worship seeks protection from injury. Al-Sāmarrāʾī's general wording includes harm to worshippers. [6 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; WAHIDI-BASIT(ابن عباس); RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; TABATABAI; SAMARRAI-ASRAR]
- 26:73/q06/p3 They might injure an enemy of the worshipper. — Al-Biqāʿī uses fire as an example of a nonhearing thing capable of harming an enemy; al-Sāmarrāʾī says the unspecified harm can reach a worshipper's enemies as well as worshippers. His other explanation says one may wish harm for an enemy or fear it for oneself. [3 notes: BIQAI-FULL; SAMARRAI-ASRAR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 26:73/q06/p4 They might harm anyone who turns away from their worship. — Al-Ālūsī reports this as an alternative but says it conflicts with the apparent contextual sense. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]

## 26:73/q07 Why is the object of يَضُرُّونَ left unspoken?  [يَنفَعُونَكُمْ يَضُرُّونَ · rhetoric]
turns on: Whether the omitted object is the addressed worshippers, any person who abandons worship, or deliberately unrestricted; the accounts also cite cadence and the contrast with the explicit 'you' after benefit.
- 26:73/q07/p1 The omitted object is 'you,' carried over from the preceding clause, and the omission suits the cadence. — Al-Ālūsī supplies 'harm you' for leaving idol worship and expressly cites the verse's rhyme or cadence as the reason for dropping the object. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL+]
- 26:73/q07/p2 The omission leaves harm general, including injury to worshippers or their enemies; it also suits the cadence. — Al-Sāmarrāʾī gives both cadence and unrestricted scope, contrasting the specified benefit sought for oneself with possible harm to oneself or an enemy. His other note explains that one fears harm for oneself but may wish it for an enemy. [2 notes: SAMARRAI-ASRAR; SAMARRAI-LAMASAT-HALAQAT]
- 26:73/q07/p3 The unspoken object may be anyone who abandons their worship. — Al-Ālūsī reports this alternative and argues that it conflicts with the apparent context, which speaks to the worshippers themselves. [1 notes: ALUSI-FULL(قيل)]

## 26:73/q08 What does Abraham's challenge establish about worshipping the idols?  [* · interpretation]
- 26:73/q08/p1 Idols cannot hear, benefit, or harm; worship that expects such powers from them has no sound basis. — Yaḥyā b. Sallām explicitly denies all three capacities. Al-Ṭabrisī says religion must rest on proof, otherwise Abraham would not argue this way. Al-Rāzī explains that worshippers seek help from one who hears requests and grants benefit or averts harm. Al-Qurṭubī and Abū Ḥayyān say worship makes no sense if neither benefit nor harm follows; al-Būrsawī says worship must bring benefit or avert harm; al-Ālūsī says intense devotion presupposes that expectation. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī identifies this as Abraham's second objection. Al-Islāḥī says the questions expose the idols' baselessness. [10 notes: YAHYA-SALLAM; TABRISI; RAZI-FULL; QURTUBI-FULL; ABUHAYYAN-FULL; BURSEVI; ALUSI-FULL; TABATABAI; ISLAHI-TADABBUR; BIQAI-FULL]

## 26:73/q09 What did the people concede in their reply?  [* · interpretation]
- 26:73/q09/p1 Their idols neither hear them nor benefit or harm them. — Al-Ṭabarī says context supplies this omitted reply; Ibn Kathīr and al-Biqāʿī state the concession directly. Al-Islāḥī describes the people as having no answer to Abraham's questions. [4 notes: TAB-FULL; IBNKATHIR-FULL; BIQAI-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 26:73/q10 Why did the people continue idol worship despite the challenge?  [* · interpretation]
- 26:73/q10/p1 They followed their forefathers' practice and would not abandon it. — Ibn Kathīr says they concede the idols' incapacity yet follow their ancestors; al-Islāḥī calls inherited tradition their only remaining basis and says they refuse to leave it. [2 notes: IBNKATHIR-FULL; ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 26:73/q11 When did Abraham pose these questions in the reported sequence of events?  [* · reports]
- 26:73/q11/p1 After he had conclusively conveyed the truth through scriptural and intellectual arguments. — Al-Islāḥī places the reported exchange after those arguments had already established the truth. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 26:73/q12 How does Abraham's account serve this sūra's preaching purpose?  [* · links]
- 26:73/q12/p1 Like the preceding anecdote, it advances the same preaching purpose and ends with the recurring verses. — Al-Islāḥī draws both parallels between Abraham's narrative and the preceding anecdote. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 26:73/q13 How does Abraham's account relate to the preceding sūra's narrative sequence?  [* · links]
- 26:73/q13/p1 It fills the gap after Noah because Abraham was the most prominent messenger between Noah and Moses. — Al-Islāḥī connects this account with the sequence in the previous sūra. [1 notes: ISLAHI-TADABBUR]

## 26:73/q14 What other Qur'anic exchange does this idol worship recall?  [* · links]
- 26:73/q14/p1 The Israelites' vow to remain devoted to their idol in 20:91 and Moses' confrontation in 20:97. — The Study Quran draws the parallel to both verses in the account of Aaron, the Israelites, and Moses. [1 notes: STUDYQURAN]
